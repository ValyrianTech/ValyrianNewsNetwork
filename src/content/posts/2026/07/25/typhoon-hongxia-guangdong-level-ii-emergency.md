# Typhoon Hongxia: Guangdong Activates Level II Emergency

Guangdong Province has activated a Level II emergency response as Typhoon Hongxia—the 12th named storm of the 2026 Pacific typhoon season—accelerates toward China’s southeastern coast, with authorities forecasting landfall between Huilai and Shenzhen on the night of July 25 to the morning of July 26.

The Guangdong Provincial Flood Control Headquarters upgraded its wind prevention response from Level III to Level II at 09:00 local time on July 25, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/local/20260725/7316d8112f3f432c8e5e52559c79e81c/c.html), while the Guangdong Water Resources Department concurrently elevated its flood and typhoon response to the same level. The escalation follows a 48-hour period of intensifying preparations across multiple government agencies, reflecting the growing threat posed by a storm that strengthened to typhoon intensity by 05:00 on July 25. As of 08:00 that morning, the storm’s center was located approximately 380 kilometers southeast of Shanwei City in the northern South China Sea, moving west-northwest at 20 kilometers per hour with maximum winds expected to reach 12 to 14 grade—typhoon to strong typhoon level.

## Why This Matters

Guangdong is China’s most populous province, home to more than 126 million people, and its largest provincial economy with a GDP exceeding 13 trillion yuan. The Pearl River Delta region—encompassing major industrial and manufacturing hubs such as Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Huizhou—lies in the storm’s projected path, raising concerns about significant economic disruption alongside potential loss of life.

China employs a four-tier emergency response system for natural disasters, where Level I represents the most severe category and Level IV the least. The activation of Level II—up from Level IV just 48 hours earlier—signals a major emergency situation requiring province-wide mobilization of resources and personnel.

## Coordinated Multi-Agency Response

The rapid escalation of emergency levels across multiple agencies within two days demonstrates China’s layered disaster management framework in action. On July 23, the China Meteorological Administration activated a Level III emergency response covering typhoon, rainstorm, and severe convection alerts. The same day, the Guangdong Maritime Safety Administration began implementing traffic restrictions on vessels transiting through the southern exit of the Taiwan Strait and those traveling east-north through the Pearl River Estuary, in coordination with maritime authorities from Fujian, Hainan, Guangxi, and Hong Kong.

By July 24, the National Flood Control Headquarters had activated a Level IV response for both Guangdong and Fujian and dispatched a working group to the province. The Ministry of Natural Resources followed suit, activating a Level IV marine disaster emergency response for the two coastal provinces and issuing storm surge blue alerts and wave yellow alerts for July 25 through 27, as [China News Service](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/gn/2026/07-24/10665937.shtml) reported.

At the local level, the Shantou Maritime Bureau had already activated a Level III typhoon emergency response by the evening of July 23, guiding 134 vessels to implement typhoon preparedness plans.

## Heavy Rainfall and Flooding Risks

The Guangdong Hydrological Bureau has warned that from July 25 to 28, rivers in the northern, central-eastern, and eastern coastal areas of the province could experience water level rises of three to eight meters. The main streams of the North River (Beijiang), East River (Dongjiang), and Han River (Hanjiang) are expected to see significant rises, with some smaller and medium rivers in these basins at risk of major flooding.

The province’s meteorological bureau forecasts that eastern Guangdong, Heyuan, Meizhou, Huizhou, and eastern Shaoguan will experience heavy to torrential rainfall—with some areas facing extremely torrential downpours. Major cities including Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Foshan are expected to see rain transitioning to heavy downpours as the storm moves inland.

According to the [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260725/2eb0da1e57a74f5f9869888383ea070b/c.html) report on the Water Resources Department’s response, officials have been instructed to “strictly implement 24-hour duty shifts and leadership on-duty systems, closely monitor typhoon dynamics, strengthen frontline inspections and risk management of water conservancy projects, pre-position rescue forces and materials for key vulnerable areas, and fully prepare typhoon defense measures in advance to safeguard the safety of people’s lives and property.”

## What to Watch For

Several uncertainties remain as Typhoon Hongxia approaches. The exact landfall location varies slightly between forecasts—ranging from Huilai to Shenzhen, or alternatively from Zhuhai to Raoping. The storm’s final intensity at landfall will depend on sea surface temperatures and atmospheric conditions in the final hours before it reaches the coast.

The post-landfall trajectory will determine the extent of inland rainfall and the severity of flooding in mountainous areas, particularly in Heyuan, Meizhou, and Shaoguan, where the risk of landslides is elevated. With Guangdong still recovering from severe flooding earlier in the season—including a dam failure at Guangxi’s Liulan Reservoir on July 6 that prompted a State Council investigation—the province’s disaster management infrastructure faces a critical test.

As Typhoon Hongxia barrels toward one of China’s most densely populated and economically vital regions, the next 24 hours will be decisive in determining the scale of the impact.
