# China's Micro Nursing Homes Ease Family Care Burden

At the gates of a residential complex in Bishan county, Chongqing, 90-year-old Luo slouches in her wheelchair while 82-year-old Erge shuffles forward unsteadily, supported by a grey-haired neighbor. This small group, cared for by former restaurant owner Lei Changming and her cousin Zhang Juanjuan, represents an emerging solution to one of China's most pressing social challenges: how to care for an aging population of 323 million.

As reported by [Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-07-25/weekend-long-read-the-micro-nursing-homes-saving-chinas-exhausted-families-102467565.html), micro nursing homes — small-scale, community-embedded facilities typically housing 5 to 15 elderly residents in residential apartments — are gaining traction across China. They offer an affordable, localized alternative to both large institutional nursing homes and traditional home care, blending into residential neighborhoods while providing professional care.

## China's Aging Crisis by the Numbers

By the end of 2025, China's population aged 60 and above reached 323 million, equivalent to roughly 23 percent of the total population. Projections from Caixin research suggest that by 2035, the number of disabled elderly requiring care could exceed 50 million, with family care costs breaking 1 trillion yuan. The demographic pressure is immense, and the traditional model of family-based care is buckling under the strain.

## A New Model Emerges from a Restaurant Closure

Lei Changming shut her last surviving restaurant in March 2026 and pivoted to elderly care alongside her cousin Zhang Juanjuan. By late March, they had signed with a company operating a residential shared eldercare model, and their seven-bed facility officially opened in early May 2026 in Bishan, a small city in southwestern China.

The homelike environment is a deliberate feature. "In our place, you can sleep as late as you want," Lei told Caixin, emphasizing the flexibility that distinguishes their model from institutional care. The relationship between caregivers and residents is informal and warm — "It's not so much employer-employee as familiar acquaintances in daily life," the Caixin report notes.

A [photo essay](https://photos.caixin.com/2026-07-18/102465756.html) by Caixin photographer Zhang Ruixue documents daily life at the facility: group outings to a mountain reservoir waterfall, shared meals, and the quiet companionship of elderly roommates living together as an improvised family.

## The Cultural Barrier: Filial Piety Under Strain

Traditional Chinese filial piety (孝道) creates strong social pressure to care for aging parents at home. Sending an elderly parent to a nursing home is often viewed as unfilial. But this cultural norm, combined with the legacy of the one-child policy, has created an unsustainable burden on the "sandwich generation."

The phenomenon of "younger elderly" — those aged 60 to 70 — caring for "older elderly" aged 80 and above is increasingly common. The Caixin report highlights the case of 65-year-old Wang Yaoguo, who has been caring for his 90-year-old bedridden mother-in-law for six years. His blood pressure spiked to 180 due to stress. A friend warned him not to let caregiving become "another tragedy," recounting a case where two younger-elderly caregivers collapsed while the older recipient remained alive.

A separate report by [China News](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/sh/2026/06-15/10640744.shtml) found that in rural areas, even elderly people living in dilapidated housing often refuse to enter nearby nursing homes. "No one ever thought of sending their mother to a nursing home. Sending them would be called 'unfilial,'" the report quotes local residents. The cultural resistance remains powerful, though economic necessity is slowly changing attitudes.

## Government Policy: Building a Framework

China's elderly care strategy follows the "9073" model: 90 percent home-based care, 7 percent community-based care, and 3 percent institutional care. This framework is driving demand for community-embedded solutions like micro nursing homes.

In December 2024, the Central Committee and State Council issued landmark "Opinions on Deepening the Reform and Development of Elderly Care Services," published in January 2025. As outlined by the [State Council](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/202501/content_6996776.htm), the policy sets ambitious targets: building a basic elderly care service network by 2029, with universal basic services for all seniors by 2035. A key component is the "15-minute elderly care service circle" policy, which aims for full national coverage. By 2027, 90 percent of urban communities are targeted to have such service circles.

## Analysis: Promise and Challenges

The micro nursing home model offers several advantages over both home care and large institutions. Facilities are lower cost, provide a homelike environment in familiar neighborhoods, allow flexible routines, and deliver personalized care. For families like Wang Yaoguo's, they could represent a lifeline.

However, significant challenges remain. The model lacks standardized regulations, faces neighborhood resistance in some communities, operates under unclear licensing requirements, and struggles to achieve profitability with only a handful of beds. Questions about quality control and scalability persist: Can this model be expanded beyond isolated cases to meaningfully address the needs of 323 million seniors?

## What's Next

As China's population continues to age rapidly — with the disabled elderly population expected to exceed 50 million by 2035 — the pressure to find sustainable care solutions will only intensify. Micro nursing homes represent a grassroots innovation that aligns with government policy goals, but their long-term impact will depend on whether regulators can create a framework that allows them to scale safely and profitably.

For now, in a residential complex in Chongqing, seven elderly residents are finding a new kind of home — one where they can sleep as late as they want, go on outings with friends, and receive care that feels less like a medical service and more like family.
