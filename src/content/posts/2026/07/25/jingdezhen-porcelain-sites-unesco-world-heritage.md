# Jingdezhen Porcelain Sites Join UNESCO World Heritage List

In a landmark decision for global cultural heritage, the "Jingdezhen Handicraft Porcelain Industry Sites" have been inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List during the 48th Session of the World Heritage Committee in Busan, South Korea, on July 25, 2026. The designation makes Jingdezhen China's 61st World Heritage site, recognizing more than a millennium of continuous porcelain craftsmanship that shaped global art and trade.

According to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260725/fb71f80c0fa745f5b39cbc802499696c/c.html), the National Cultural Heritage Administration confirmed the successful inscription, which represents the culmination of 11 years of dedicated application work and nearly half a century of conservation efforts.

## The Porcelain Capital

Located in northeastern Jiangxi Province, Jingdezhen has been known as China's "Porcelain Capital" for over 1,000 years. The heritage property comprises five component parts spanning 1,979 hectares, with a buffer zone of 5,545.7 hectares, [China Daily](https://global.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202607/25/WS6a643171a310986e2b46745d.html) reports. These include the Town Area Porcelain Production Center, the Hutian Ancient Kiln Site, the Gaoling Porcelain Clay Mining Site, the Changling Porcelain Stone Mining Site, and the Jiaotan Firewood Production Area, collectively containing 15 groups of heritage elements and 45 individual heritage points.

What makes Jingdezhen truly exceptional is the completeness of its industrial ecosystem. The inscribed sites illustrate a full production chain — from raw material extraction at kaolin clay and porcelain stone mines, through transportation via historic waterways and wharves, to production at urban kilns and fuel supply from dedicated forest areas.

## Technological and Artistic Innovation

The UNESCO listing, inscribed under cultural criteria (ii), (iii), (iv), and (vi), recognizes Jingdezhen's extraordinary contributions to technological innovation, artistic achievement, and industrial organization in porcelain production from the 10th to the 19th centuries.

As [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/world-heritage/committee-2026) notes in its official description, Jingdezhen potters developed the "binary formula" — mixing kaolin with porcelain stone — which produced porcelain of unmatched quality. The city also pioneered kiln designs that evolved from the gourd-shaped kiln to the highly efficient egg-shaped kiln, capable of firing different types of porcelain simultaneously by drawing on ceramic traditions from both northern and southern China.

"The city combined ceramic traditions from northern and southern China, incorporated pigments introduced from the Middle East and Europe, and blended domestic and foreign decorative aesthetics," said Wei Qing, author of the World Heritage nomination dossier, as quoted by China Daily. The most famous product of this cross-cultural exchange was blue-and-white porcelain, which combined Chinese porcelain-making techniques with Persian cobalt blue pigment.

"Jingdezhen porcelain became a medium for sharing Chinese civilization and culture with the world," Wei added.

## From Revolution to Evolution

The timeline of Jingdezhen's porcelain production spans multiple dynasties — from the bluish-white wares of the Song Dynasty (960–1279), through the revolutionary blue-and-white ceramics of the Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368), the painted porcelain of the Ming Dynasty (1368–1644), and the famille-rose and enamel wares of the Qing Dynasty (1644–1911).

Wang Guangyao, a ceramic archaeologist at the Palace Museum in Beijing, told China Daily: "It is this spirit of innovation that has kept the kilns of Jingdezhen burning."

The city's global reach is evidenced by archaeological discoveries at the Guanyinge kiln site, where Kraak porcelain — ceramics produced specifically for European markets during the Ming and Qing periods — has been found, confirming Jingdezhen's role as a major center for export-oriented production that fueled the "chinoiserie" style across 17th and 18th century Europe.

## Heritage as Urban Renewal

Beyond its historical significance, the World Heritage designation has already demonstrated transformative power for Jingdezhen itself. Wang Tongmao, director of the Jingdezhen Municipal Leading Group Office for World Cultural Heritage Nomination, noted that the application process helped authorities better understand how the city's porcelain industry evolved into China's ceramics capital, providing "a stronger foundation for future conservation efforts."

Deng Chao, head of the World Cultural Heritage Department at China's National Cultural Heritage Administration, highlighted a broader lesson from Jingdezhen's experience. "The World Heritage application has helped Jingdezhen transform itself from a declining industrial city into a vibrant center where traditional craftsmanship and modern industry coexist," he told China Daily.

Deng emphasized that "it is also important to identify and nurture local cultural resources, make them the cornerstone of regional economic and social progress, so that heritage conservation can go hand-in-hand with urban development."

## A New Chapter

With its inscription on the UNESCO World Heritage List, Jingdezhen joins an elite group of global cultural treasures. As China's 61st World Heritage site, its recognition underscores not only the artistic beauty of its porcelain but the complete industrial and social ecosystem that sustained nearly a millennium of continuous production and innovation.

The designation also carries forward-looking implications. As Jingdezhen balances heritage conservation with modern development, its model of using cultural preservation as a catalyst for economic revitalization offers valuable lessons for historic industrial cities worldwide. The world will now watch how this ancient porcelain capital continues to evolve — its kilns still burning, its craftsmanship still thriving — as a living UNESCO World Heritage site.
