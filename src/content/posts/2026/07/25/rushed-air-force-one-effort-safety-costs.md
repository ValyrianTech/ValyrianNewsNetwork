# Rushed Air Force One Effort Led to Safety Risks, Costs

A New York Times investigation has revealed that President Donald Trump's push to accelerate the delivery of a new Air Force One led to ballooning costs, compromised safety features, and a subsequent press freedom battle after the Justice Department subpoenaed journalists covering the story. The project — originally budgeted at $3.3 billion — has now ballooned to an estimated $5.7 billion, according to a 2026 audit by the Government Accountability Office (GAO).

## Background: A Decades-Old Fleet

The effort to replace the presidential aircraft fleet, which has served since the 1970s, began in earnest in 2017 when the Trump administration issued a White House directive accelerating the procurement process. In 2018, Boeing was awarded a contract worth approximately $3.9 billion to build two new Boeing 747-8 aircraft. However, according to the [NYT investigation](https://www.newsdirectory3.com/trumps-aircraft-delivery-push-led-to-higher-costs-and-safety-trade-offs/), the push for speed came at a significant cost.

Just one year later, in 2019, the contract was restructured with approval from the Department of Defense, streamlining approval processes but reducing the number of required safety reviews. A source familiar with the procurement process told the Times: "The emphasis on delivering the planes quickly created pressure to bypass standard review steps, which affected the final product's safety and functionality."

## Two Separate Aircraft Programs

A critical distinction in the story involves two parallel aircraft programs. The original Boeing contract for two purpose-built 747-8 aircraft has faced repeated delays, with delivery now projected for mid-2028. Separately, a Boeing 747-8 was donated by the government of Qatar as an "unconditional" gift valued at approximately $400 million. This aircraft was retrofitted and unveiled by Trump in June 2026 as the new Air Force One, entering service shortly thereafter.

The Qatari-donated aircraft became the center of a security scare in early July 2026. Trump flew to the NATO summit in Ankara, Turkey, on the new plane but returned on the older aircraft after US officials detected a credible threat from Iranian proxy forces targeting the president. As [reported by CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-iran-proxies-credible-threat-air-force-one-turkey/), the Secret Service recommended the switch after intelligence indicated a plan to fire a missile at the president's aircraft. The older Air Force One jets are equipped with laser-based antimissile defense systems that the newer aircraft lacked at the time.

## Safety Trade-Offs and Cost Overruns

Specific safety features were deprioritized or removed entirely to meet the accelerated timeline. According to the investigation, these included the removal of certain defensive systems and the delay of cybersecurity upgrades. The aircraft notably lacked advanced antimissile capabilities — a gap that became acutely apparent during the Turkey security incident.

A former Air Force official with knowledge of the project stated: "There was a trade-off between speed and ensuring all systems were fully integrated. Some features were deprioritized to meet the timeline." The White House has since announced that the new aircraft will undergo additional upgrades in the fall, with one official stating the plane "will be maxed out" over the next month.

The GAO's 2021 audit had already flagged issues, finding that "certain aspects of the procurement process lacked transparency." The broader context of defense contract overruns is stark: a 2023 analysis by the Federation of American Scientists found that 68% of major defense contracts experience cost overruns, with political pressure cited as a contributing factor in 32% of cases.

## The Press Freedom Battle

The controversy took a darker turn when the Trump administration's Justice Department issued subpoenas to five New York Times journalists — Julian E. Barnes, Eric Lipton, Eric Schmitt, Adam Goldman, and Tyler Pager — who reported on the Air Force One security concerns. The DOJ sought to compel grand jury testimony and phone records, including records from some journalists' relatives.

As [reported by the BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwyrjl04vnro), the DOJ withdrew the subpoenas on July 23, 2026, during a court hearing before Judge Arun Subramanian, after conceding procedural missteps. The judge expressed strong opposition to the government's tactics, stating: "Subpoenas are the last step, not the first step, but the last step."

David McCraw, senior vice president and deputy general counsel for the Times, said in a statement covered by [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2026/07/justice-department-plans-to-withdraw-subpoenas-for-new-york-times-journalists-who-reported-on-trumps-new-air-force-one-1237001870/): "Today's proceeding was an important affirmation of our country's commitment to a free press. We are pleased that the government finally conceded that the subpoenas violated the law, but they should never have been issued in the first place."

A DOJ spokesperson insisted the leak investigation would continue: "Make no mistake, this investigation remains ongoing, and we will pursue justice against those threatening national security by leaking classified information, a serious federal crime." Judge Subramanian left the door open for the DOJ to reissue subpoenas if they follow proper procedures.

## The Iran Threat Dimension

The security incident in Turkey highlighted the real-world consequences of the rushed development. According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/jul/24/trump-air-force-one-iran-threat), the threat from Iranian proxy forces was specific and credible — involving a plan to fire a missile at the president's aircraft. Trump has since stated that the US is "locked and loaded" and "ready to go" in response to the ongoing conflict with Iran.

## Analysis: A Pattern of Political Pressure

The Air Force One case fits a broader pattern identified by watchdogs. As a representative from the Project On Government Oversight noted: "This case reflects a broader pattern where political goals overshadow technical and fiscal prudence." A defense analyst not affiliated with the project observed: "When you have a president pushing for a timeline, it creates a dynamic where other considerations can be secondary. This isn't unique to this case, but the scale of the Air Force One project makes it a significant example."

The Federal Aviation Administration certified the aircraft, stating that "the agency's safety standards were met, and the aircraft underwent rigorous testing before deployment." However, the FAA's involvement was limited to regulatory compliance, with the Air Force retaining primary responsibility for design and safety.

## What's Next

Several questions remain unanswered. Will the DOJ reissue subpoenas through proper channels? What specific safety systems were removed in the rushed process, and will they be restored? How will the Iran threat affect future use of the new aircraft? And will the original Boeing-built aircraft ever be completed, or will the Qatari-donated plane serve as the permanent replacement?

The controversy also raises ongoing ethical questions about foreign governments donating assets to sitting US presidents. The NYT investigation, based on internal documents and interviews with current and former officials, ensures that the balance between presidential ambition, national security, and fiscal responsibility in the Air Force One program will remain under scrutiny for years to come.
