# Trump Orders Warning Signs Outside Smithsonian Museum

President Donald Trump signed an executive order on July 24 directing federal officials to install warning signs outside the Smithsonian Institution's National Museum of American History, alleging that the museum presents "inaccurate information" about the nation's past. The order directs Interior Secretary Doug Burgum to work with the National Park Service to place temporary signage on public walkways leading to the museum, informing visitors of the findings of a recent White House report and guiding them toward what the administration considers "accurate information regarding America's history."

## Background: A Broader Campaign

This action represents the latest escalation in the Trump administration's campaign to reshape how American history is presented in federal cultural institutions. In March 2025, Trump signed Executive Order 14253, titled "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History," which targeted what the administration called "divisive, race-centered ideology" at the Smithsonian and directed a review of all National Park Service monuments and markers. According to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_14253), that order prohibited expenditure on exhibits that "degrade shared American values" or "divide Americans based on race" and mandated the review of interpretive materials across NPS sites.

The impact was swift and wide-ranging. By early 2026, educational content had been removed or flagged for removal at more than 25 National Park Service sites, including exhibits on slavery at the President's House in Philadelphia, references to climate change at Fort Sumter, and materials related to Native American history at sites including Grand Teton and Little Bighorn. A federal court in June 2026 issued a preliminary injunction ordering the administration to restore removed signs and exhibits, finding that the removals set "a dangerous precedent of censorship and sanitization."

## The Executive Order

Trump's latest directive, titled "Restoring Trust in the Smithsonian Institution," cites a 162-page White House Domestic Policy Council report released on July 4, 2026, titled "Saving America's Story: How Ideological Capture at the Smithsonian Institution's National Museum of American History Erases Our Heritage." The report accused the museum of adopting "an ideological framework that no longer treats the American story as a shared national inheritance to be taught or celebrated, but as a political instrument to divide, dispirit, and discourage our citizens," according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/trump-administration/trump-orders-signage-warning-visitors-inaccurate-information-smithsoni-rcna589156).

The [White House fact sheet](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/07/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-restores-trust-in-the-smithsonian-institution/) on the order states that "the Smithsonian is failing to tell America's story with honesty, seriousness, and pride" and that the institution cannot be trusted to properly steward the over $1 billion it receives annually in federal funding. The order directs federal officials to use "all available authorities" to enforce compliance with its policies, including through funding mechanisms.

The report recommended that the museum post signage above every entrance reading: "Warning: the exhibits in this museum were prepared by people who don't want you to love your country," as [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-smithsonian-museum-warning-signs-inaccurate-information/) reported. The executive order also mandates temporary exhibits or signage honoring the 56 signers of the Declaration of Independence, accusing the museum of failing to "appropriately honor" them during the nation's 250th anniversary year.

## Smithsonian and Congressional Response

The Smithsonian Institution declined to comment on the executive order. Smithsonian Secretary Lonnie Bunch, however, had previously defended the institution, stating that the White House report "is not a fair characterization of the work and totality of the National Museum of American History." He emphasized the institution's commitment to "scholarship, nonpartisanship, independence, accuracy, and integrity."

The executive order came just days after a heated House Oversight Committee hearing on July 21, where Republicans grilled National Museum of American History Director Dr. Anthea Hartig about the museum's exhibits. Rep. Tim Burchett (R-TN), the committee chairman, said the museum had "become infected by a woke ideology," according to [USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/nation/2026/07/25/executive-order-smithsonian-national-museum-american-history/91048449007/). Hartig defended the museum's approach, testifying that "We tell the truth about the times that the country fell short of its own founding ideals. Telling our story fully is a form of respect for the American people, and a commitment to constantly being in pursuit of a more perfect union."

## Legal and Political Implications

The executive order raises significant questions about the role of the federal government in shaping historical narratives at publicly funded cultural institutions. The Smithsonian receives more than $1 billion in annual federal funding, giving the administration substantial leverage over the institution's operations. Legal challenges to the March 2025 executive order are ongoing, and the new order is likely to face similar court battles. A federal court in February 2026, in a case involving removed slavery exhibits at the President's House in Philadelphia, quoted George Orwell's "Nineteen Eighty-Four" in its ruling, stating that the government does not have the power to "dissemble and disassemble historical truths."

## What to Watch For

As of July 25, it remained unclear whether warning signs had actually been installed outside the museum. The National Park Service and Interior Department had not responded to inquiries about implementation timelines. The order mandates temporary signage on NPS-maintained walkways, but the specific content and design of the signs have yet to be disclosed.

The controversy reflects a broader cultural battle over how American history should be taught and presented — whether museums should present a predominantly celebratory narrative of national achievement or a more comprehensive account that includes the nation's failures alongside its successes. As the United States approaches its 250th anniversary celebrations, this debate is likely to intensify, with the Smithsonian museums at the center of the storm.
