# Japan Names First Director of National Intelligence Bureau

Japan's Cabinet has approved the launch of a new National Intelligence Bureau on July 31 and appointed Kazuya Hara as its first director, marking the most significant overhaul of the country's intelligence apparatus since World War II. The move, [reported by Kyodo News](https://mainichi.jp/english/articles/20260724/p2g/00m/0na/034000c), establishes a centralized intelligence framework under a newly created National Intelligence Council chaired by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, as Tokyo ramps up counterespionage capabilities amid what officials describe as an increasingly severe international security environment.

## A New Intelligence Architecture

The National Intelligence Bureau is an upgrade to Japan's existing Cabinet Intelligence and Research Office, which has long served as the country's central intelligence coordination body. According to [Jiji Press](https://jen.jiji.com/jc/eng?g=eco&k=2026072400709), the new bureau will be staffed by 700 personnel and its director will hold a position on par with the secretary-general of the National Security Secretariat, reflecting the elevated status of intelligence coordination within the government.

The bureau will serve as the implementing body for the National Intelligence Council, a new central command center chaired by Prime Minister Takaichi and comprising nine other Cabinet members with intelligence-related responsibilities. The council is scheduled to hold its first meeting on July 31, the same day the bureau officially launches, and is expected to begin formulating Japan's first-ever comprehensive national intelligence strategy.

## Kazuya Hara: A Seasoned Security Professional

Hara, 58, currently serves as director of Cabinet Intelligence, a role he has held since June 2023. His career includes extensive service at the National Police Agency, where he served as chief of Saitama prefectural police and head of the NPA's Security Bureau, as well as a stint as executive secretary to former Prime Minister Shinzo Abe, [NTV News 24 Japan reported](https://www.ntv.co.jp/englishnews/articles/2021e1ekje5mf7zf520l.html). This background places him at the intersection of law enforcement, public security, and high-level political intelligence work.

Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara praised the appointment at a July 24 press conference, stating that Hara "has vast experience on intelligence" and that the government "hopes he will fully perform his duty." Kihara described the bureau's launch as "significant in today's increasingly challenging international environment" as it will "enable the government to make sound policy decisions based on higher-quality, more timely intelligence."

## Legislative Foundation and Next Steps

The new framework was established under legislation enacted in May 2026 that created the legal basis for both the National Intelligence Council and the National Intelligence Bureau. After the council begins its work, the government plans to convene an expert panel in summer 2026 to develop Japan's first national intelligence strategy. The government aims to submit further legislation to the ordinary parliamentary session in 2027, including proposals for a system to register foreign government agents engaged in lobbying activities, effectively creating a foreign agent registration framework similar to those in other advanced democracies.

## Geopolitical and Domestic Reactions

The expansion of Japan's intelligence capabilities has drawn sharp criticism from Chinese state media. [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_33656812), a Chinese news outlet, framed the development as part of Japan's broader "remilitarization" strategy, a framing that reflects longstanding tensions over Japan's evolving security posture. [China Daily previously reported](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202606/03/WS6a1f6cb6a310d6866eb4c1ad.html) that critics within Japan have warned the moves "repeat prewar mistakes," drawing parallels to the country's pre-World War II intelligence and security apparatus, including the notorious Special Higher Police.

Domestically, the new intelligence framework has raised significant concerns about civil liberties. Japanese commentators and opposition media have warned of increased surveillance of citizens, the potential for arbitrary use of power by the government, and unresolved questions about how to ensure democratic oversight to protect basic human rights from excessive intelligence activities. These concerns echo broader debates about the balance between national security and individual freedoms that have intensified under the Takaichi administration's push to strengthen security legislation.

## Regional and Strategic Context

The establishment of the National Intelligence Bureau reflects a broader trend under Prime Minister Takaichi's administration to strengthen security capabilities in response to what Tokyo perceives as an increasingly dangerous regional environment. Recent flashpoints include China conducting military firing exercises within Japan's exclusive economic zone and an alleged Russian spy departing Japan, incidents that have reinforced the government's argument for enhanced intelligence capabilities.

For Japan's allies, particularly the United States, the enhanced intelligence capabilities are likely to be welcomed as they could facilitate deeper intelligence-sharing and security cooperation. However, the move is certain to heighten tensions with China and may be viewed warily by Russia. Neighboring countries such as South Korea may hold mixed reactions.

## What to Watch For

In the coming months, the expert panel on intelligence strategy will shape Japan's first comprehensive national intelligence doctrine. The 2027 parliamentary session will be critical, particularly for proposed counterespionage legislation and the foreign agent registration system. How Japan balances enhanced security with democratic safeguards and civil liberties protections will be a defining question as these new institutions take shape.
