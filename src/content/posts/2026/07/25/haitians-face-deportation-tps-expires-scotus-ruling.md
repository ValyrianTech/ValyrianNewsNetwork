# Haitians Face Deportation as TPS Expires After SCOTUS Ruling

More than 350,000 Haitian nationals across the United States are set to lose their Temporary Protected Status on Monday, July 27, following a Supreme Court ruling that cleared the way for the Trump administration to terminate the program for Haiti. Families with U.S.-born children now face an agonizing choice between leaving their children behind or taking them to a Haiti crippled by gang violence and political chaos.

## The Supreme Court Ruling

In a 6-3 decision on June 25, the Supreme Court allowed the administration to end TPS for approximately 350,000 Haitians and 6,000 Syrians, ruling that the president has the legal authority to terminate the program. The administration has long argued that TPS, established by the Immigration Act of 1990, was intended as a temporary measure and has become "de facto amnesty," according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/23/nx-s1-5884947/haitians-protected-status-tps).

A lower federal court in Washington, D.C., is expected to implement the Supreme Court’s ruling on Monday, after a 10-day extension granted by the Department of Homeland Security expires. After that date, beneficiaries lose both their work authorization and deportation protections.

## An Impossible Choice for Families

For the estimated 50,000 U.S.-born children of Haitian TPS holders, the ruling creates a humanitarian crisis within a crisis. Parents who have lived and worked legally in the United States for more than a decade are suddenly confronting the prospect of family separation.

Josiane Valsaint, a TPS holder and mother of two U.S.-born children ages 10 and 12 in San Diego, told NPR she cannot fathom what will happen to her children if she is detained. “Who’s going to take care of my kiddos? They’re not adults yet. They were born here,” she said. Taking her children to Haiti, where armed gangs control vast swaths of the country, is not an option she can consider.

Another mother, identified only as Sandra, told NPR that her teenage son expressed suicidal thoughts after the Supreme Court ruling, terrified of being sent to a country he has never known. “I cried. I’m supposed to protect them, and I feel like I’m failing,” she said.

Guerline Jozef, executive director of the San Diego-based Haitian Bridge Alliance, said family separation is nearly inevitable. “They cannot take those children back to Haiti. Not because Haiti is not beautiful. But because right now, in July 2026, it is impossible for them to be safe there,” Jozef told NPR.

The Department of Homeland Security has stated that Immigration and Customs Enforcement does not separate families and that parents may either take their children or designate a safe person to care for them. Advocates argue this offers little comfort given the impossible choices involved.

## Deportation Flights Begin

[The Haitian Times](https://haitiantimes.com/2026/07/21/us-informs-haiti-of-upcoming-deportations/) reported that the U.S. Embassy has informed Haiti’s government to expect weekly deportation flights carrying approximately 250 people in total, equating to roughly 1,000 deportees per month. Jean Négot Bonheur Delva, director general of Haiti’s National Office of Migration, confirmed the flights will begin after July 24.

Haiti, however, has announced no national plan for receiving or reintegrating deportees. Deportees currently receive approximately $77 in travel assistance—hardly enough to begin anew in a country where more than 85% of the capital, Port-au-Prince, is under gang control. Between July 4 and July 9 alone, gang attacks killed at least 61 people, including 14 children, and displaced thousands.

## Economic Impact

Haitian TPS holders contribute an estimated $5.9 billion annually to the U.S. economy and pay $805 million in federal and payroll taxes and $755 million in state and local taxes each year, according to [FWD.us](https://www.fwd.us/news/haitian-tps-holders-make-the-u-s-stronger/). Some 200,000 are currently in the U.S. workforce, working as nursing assistants, agricultural workers, cooks, delivery drivers, and caregivers. Forcing them out of their jobs would drive up costs for everyday Americans across multiple industries.

Florida, home to roughly 158,000 Haitian TPS holders—the largest concentration in any state—stands to lose $2.6 billion in annual economic contributions. Massachusetts, New York, and Ohio also face significant economic disruption.

## Political Landscape and What’s Next

House Bill 1689, which would extend TPS protections for Haitians, passed the House in April with bipartisan support but was blocked by Senate Republicans. No further legislative action is expected in the near term.

Massachusetts Attorney General Andrea Campbell has published emergency planning guides to help parents designate temporary guardians for their U.S.-citizen children. Advocacy groups like the Haitian Bridge Alliance are scaling up legal support and contingency planning.

The ruling represents a key milestone in the Trump administration’s broader mass deportation agenda and could set a precedent for ending TPS for other nationalities, including roughly 1 million people from Venezuela and other countries currently holding protected status.

As Monday’s deadline approaches, the Haitian community and advocacy organizations are bracing for the fallout. “We are asking them to launch a diplomatic offensive to avoid the catastrophe that is looming,” said Lucnas Etienne, president of the Haitian political organization Zansèt Ayiti, urging the Haitian government to negotiate with U.S. officials.

For families like Valsaint’s, the wait for a resolution—or a miracle—continues. “Families stick together,” she said, her voice catching. “We can’t go back to Haiti.”
