# WNBA Turmoil Clouds Historic All-Star Celebration

The WNBA has never been bigger. Viewership is soaring, investment is pouring in, and player salaries are at an all-time high following a landmark collective bargaining agreement. But as the league gathered in Chicago this weekend for its 30th-season All-Star Game at the Obama Presidential Center, a convergence of controversies over race, gender, pay equity, and player safety [threatened to overshadow](https://apnews.com/article/wnba-allstar-race-caitlin-clark-angel-reese-df5ab15ce147704b31aa5880226c402f) the celebration.

“It is a moment to be celebrated without a doubt,” said Ketra Armstrong, sport management professor and director of the Center for Race and Ethnicity in Sport at the University of Michigan. “We want to celebrate what’s great. But sometimes, the progress can overshadow, still, where the perils are.”

## A League at a Crossroads

The WNBA’s 2026 season marks both a triumph and a tipping point. Players unanimously approved a new, seven-year collective bargaining agreement in March — a deal widely described as “transformational” — that runs through 2032 and significantly improves compensation. The league expanded from 13 to 15 teams with the addition of the Portland Fire and Toronto Tempo, and viewership numbers continue to climb.

Yet this explosive growth has laid bare long-simmering tensions. Issues of race, gender, and representation — persistent undercurrents throughout the league’s history — have intensified as women’s sports have claimed a larger share of the national spotlight.

## A Season of Flashpoints

The discord reached a fever pitch in the weeks leading up to the All-Star break, with a series of incidents testing the league’s institutional capacity.

**The Thomas-Clark Incident** — On June 24, Phoenix Mercury forward Alyssa Thomas made contact with her fist to the throat of Indiana Fever guard Caitlin Clark during a game. The WNBA retroactively reviewed the play, deemed it a “non-basketball act,” and issued Thomas a Flagrant Foul 2 penalty and a one-game [suspension](https://www.espn.com/wnba/story/_/id/49178397/mercury-thomas-suspended-1-game-shot-clark-throat). Thomas, who said the contact was accidental, later reported receiving death threats and racial slurs in the [aftermath](https://apnews.com/article/phoenix-mercury-alyssa-thomas-suspension-ba1abf1ec70873006fa0a6d973fbb3e3) of the incident.

“People are sending racial slurs and all types of stuff,” Thomas told reporters. “There’s a difference between trolling and there’s a difference between hatred.”

Clark, who left the game with a back injury, expressed frustration over the constant off-court noise. “It’s basketball,” she said in an ESPN interview. “When I walk out of the locker room I shouldn’t be fearful of what the conversation is going to be. It’s emotionally exhausting. It’s draining.”

**The Brondello-Reese Controversy** — On July 18, Toronto Tempo coach Sandy Brondello was caught on a live microphone referring to Atlanta Dream forward Angel Reese as a “protected species” while arguing with officials. The WNBA [suspended](https://www.cbssports.com/wnba/news/sandy-brondello-angel-reese-apology-tempo-dream-wnba/) Brondello for one game without pay. 

Brondello, a native Australian, said the phrase is commonly used in Australian sports to express frustration over officiating. But in the United States, it was viewed as racially charged. She apologized publicly, and Reese accepted, expressing gratitude for the league’s swift action.

“It’s a much bigger platform now. Everything that happens is news,” said David Berri, a sports economist at Southern Utah University. “There were hard fouls 10 years ago. Nobody paid any attention to that.”

**Online Harassment and Player Safety** — The league’s player safety crisis has deepened. Las Vegas Aces guard Chelsea Gray publicly shared a racist message she received. WNBA veteran Brianna Turner disclosed that her former team, the Indiana Fever, fired a male employee for sexual harassment last season, adding that players were encouraged not to discuss the matter.

Commissioner Cathy Engelbert met with player committees in the week before the All-Star Game to discuss expanding protections against online abuse. But players have voiced frustration with what they describe as insufficient action from league leadership.

**Transgender Athlete Debate** — Indiana Fever forward Sophie Cunningham added to the discourse with comments about transgender athletes, stating she wanted to “protect young girls in sport who shouldn’t have to go against biological men.” The remarks drew both support and backlash, with Turner defending transgender athletes in response.

## Growth Magnifies the Stakes

David Berri argued that the scrutiny is an inevitable consequence of the league’s rising profile. “This is what a sports league is,” he said. “If a sports league is going to get attention, you’re going to get attention for these things as well.”

Since entering the league, Clark — a white player who shattered NCAA scoring records — has become a lightning rod for debates about race, attention distribution, and representation in women’s sports. Her outsized media profile compared to Black stars has intensified racial dynamics within a league predominantly composed of Black women.

“There’s a lot to celebrate,” Armstrong said, “but race still matters. Race will always matter. It just depends on how we make it matter.”

## What Comes Next

The All-Star Game has come and gone, but the underlying issues facing the WNBA remain unresolved. The league must navigate a path forward that addresses player safety, racial equity, and governance while maintaining its extraordinary growth trajectory.

Key questions loom: Will Engelbert and league leadership implement concrete measures to combat online harassment? Can the WNBA address the racial dynamics that have intensified with Clark’s rise? How will the league handle the transgender athlete debate internally and in its public messaging?

What is clear is that the WNBA’s moment in the sun comes with challenges that no amount of record-breaking viewership can solve alone. As Armstrong put it, progress and peril are traveling together — and how the league manages both will shape its future for years to come.
