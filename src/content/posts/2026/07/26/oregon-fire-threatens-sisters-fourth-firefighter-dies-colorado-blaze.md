# Oregon Fire Threatens Town as Fourth Firefighter Dies from Colorado Blaze

As vicious winds and tinder-dry conditions fanned a major wildfire near the central Oregon town of Sisters, federal officials confirmed that a fourth firefighter has died from injuries sustained in a separate Colorado blaze weeks earlier—underscoring the escalating toll of the 2026 wildfire season across the Western United States.

The lightning-sparked Akawa Butte Fire, which ignited July 16 near Sisters, has burned approximately 27,000 acres and was designated the highest-priority wildfire in the nation for firefighting resources, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/wildland-firefighters-death-colorado-utah-41f7f9576a4b65a58709ca7807233a33). Nearly 5,000 homes were under Level 1, 2, or 3 evacuation orders, with about 30 people sheltering at Sisters High School.

## A Community on Edge

Sisters, a city of roughly 3,000 residents, faced an especially dangerous day on Saturday as winds gusting up to 30 mph threatened to push flames into new areas. Firefighters managed to make progress strengthening control lines, and officials expressed cautious optimism that the city would be spared once the worst winds subsided.

“It’s going to take a while to put it out entirely. But as long as it stops moving, I think that’ll get people to relax a little bit,” Sisters Mayor Jennifer Letz told [OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/07/24/akawa-butte-fire-oregon-sisters/). She added that the city was not in immediate danger and that business was continuing as usual.

The Akawa Butte Fire is just one of multiple blazes across Oregon. The Bench Fire near the Warm Springs Reservation grew to over 6,900 acres with zero containment, triggering Level 3 “Go Now” evacuation orders and closing Highway 26, as [KPTV](https://www.kptv.com/2026/07/25/wildfire-updates-new-go-now-orders-hagen-fire-dayville-residents-level-2-alert-shingle-fire/) reported. The Shingle Fire in Grant and Wheeler counties reached an estimated 21,849 acres, prompting Governor Tina Kotek to invoke the Emergency Conflagration Act—the eighth such invocation in July alone.

“This is all hands on deck here in Oregon,” Kotek said. “We’re seeing August conditions because of drought, because of weather, and this is a moment where Oregonians have to come together to defend our communities.”

Rita MacDonald, a Sisters resident of 35 years, captured the weary resignation felt by many. “I think it’s gotten to be just about every summer we have at least one major fire, and we all just sort of hold our breath, and hopefully it won’t happen,” she told OPB.

## A Tragic Milestone in Colorado

Across the Rocky Mountains, the wildfire crisis claimed another life. Nathan Matthews, 43, of Lincoln, Nebraska, died on Friday, July 24, from severe burn injuries he suffered during the Knowles Fire burnover incident in Mesa County, Colorado, on June 27, according to the [U.S. Department of the Interior](https://www.doi.gov/pressreleases/fourth-firefighter-dies-following-knowles-fire-burnover-colorado).

Matthews was a member of the Rifle Helitack crew—firefighters deployed by helicopter into remote terrain to attack fast-growing blazes. He was among five crew members who deployed emergency fire shelters when flames trapped them during initial attack operations. Three firefighters—Emily Barker, 38, of Clinton Township, Michigan; Nick Hutcherson, 27, of Glendale, Arizona; and Sydney Watson, 27, of Warrior, Alabama—died at the scene. Matthews had been receiving medical treatment for his burns in the weeks since.

“This is another heartbreaking loss for our wildland fire family,” said U.S. Wildland Fire Service Chief Brian Fennessy in a statement. “We mourn the loss of Nate while continuing to grieve the deaths of Emily, Nick and Sydney. Our hearts are with their families, friends, crewmates and everyone impacted by this tragedy.”

A serious accident investigation team is reviewing the circumstances surrounding the burnover incident, which could lead to changes in wildland firefighting protocols, the Interior Department said. The identity and condition of the fifth firefighter involved have not been released.

## A Season of Unprecedented Severity

The twin crises in Oregon and Colorado are unfolding against the backdrop of what experts call an exceptionally severe wildfire season. Months of dry weather, record-low snowpack, high temperatures, and extreme drought have created conditions ripe for large, fast-moving fires across the Western U.S. and Canada.

Aaron Whiteley, an incident commander for the Oregon Department of Forestry, told OPB that Oregon has seen over 1,000 fires and 500,000 acres burned so far in 2026—more than all of 2025. Canada, too, is battling 926 active fires, 82 of them out of control, involving 300 aircraft and 5,300 personnel, AP News reported.

Ben Duda, emergency manager for the Deschutes County Sheriff’s Office, summed up the stakes near Sisters: “We don’t have the margin for error when we’re getting this close to town.”

## The Road Ahead

As firefighters continue to battle blazes across multiple states, the outlook remains sobering. Governor Kotek warned that the fire season is far from over, and resources are stretched thin. The deaths of Matthews and his three colleagues serve as a grim reminder of the dangers wildland firefighters face every day.

“We remain committed to supporting those affected and honoring the legacy of all four firefighters who made the ultimate sacrifice in service to our nation,” Fennessy said.

For residents of Sisters and communities across the West, the question is not whether fire will come, but how prepared they will be when it does.
