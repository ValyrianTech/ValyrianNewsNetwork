# Self-Dressing Robotic Clothes Help Seniors Stay Independent

A team of researchers from South Korea's KAIST and Stanford University has developed robotic clothing that can dress a person in roughly ten seconds, offering a potential breakthrough for seniors and others with limited mobility. The system, called SWAG (Self-Wearing Adaptive Garment), uses soft air-powered robots built directly into the fabric, eliminating the need for bulky external robotic arms.

## The Challenge of Getting Dressed

Getting dressed ranks among the basic activities of daily living that determine whether someone can live independently. When mobility declines due to arthritis, shoulder problems, balance issues, or recovery from surgery, tasks like pulling on a shirt or stepping into pants can become exhausting or impossible. According to [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/tech/self-dressing-clothes-help-older-adults), many older adults require assistance from family members or professional caregivers for this deeply personal routine, reducing their autonomy and increasing reliance on others.

Stanford researchers have identified dressing assistance as a critical use for future home robots, noting that assistive technology could help older adults remain in their homes as caregiving needs grow.

## How SWAG Works

The SWAG system takes a fundamentally different approach from traditional dressing robots. Instead of placing a rigid mechanical arm next to the person, the team built flexible robotic tubes called "subvines" into the garment itself. As [Reuters](https://tech.yahoo.com/science/articles/south-korea-us-team-unveils-030031030.html) reports, air pressure causes these soft structures to unfold and grow along the body using an eversion mechanism — turning the fabric inside out as it advances, much like ivy climbing a wall.

This design keeps friction low because the material unrolls onto the skin rather than dragging across it. The system can adapt to a moving wearer without requiring complex control algorithms or cameras, making it simpler and potentially safer than conventional robotic alternatives.

## A Rainy Bike Ride That Sparked an Idea

The concept originated when KAIST postdoctoral researcher Nam Gyun Kim was riding his bicycle and it began to rain. "I thought it would be helpful if a raincoat could be put on automatically," Kim told Reuters. That moment led him to explore how clothing could move into place on its own, eventually resulting in the SWAG system.

Kim serves as the lead author of the paper published in IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters. The research team also includes Professor Jee-Hwan Ryu of KAIST and Professor Allison Okamura of Stanford's CHARM Lab, both veterans of soft growing robot research.

## Performance and Recognition

The system can put on a full suit in approximately ten seconds, with individual demonstrations showing a jacket taking about 13 seconds, pants roughly 21 seconds, and a sleeve prototype around 14 seconds. The researchers tested sleeves, jackets, and trousers, all built around the same unfurling mechanism.

The academic community has taken notice. According to [TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/kaist-stanford-swag-self-wearing-dressing-robot), the paper won the Best Paper Award at the ICRA 2026 conference, selected from over 1,700 published papers. Professor Ryu noted that this marks the second consecutive year his group has claimed the honor, highlighting how quickly soft growing robots have moved from curiosity to credible technology.

## Potential Applications

While the primary focus is on older adults and people with disabilities, the researchers see broader applications. Semiconductor cleanroom workers who frequently put on protective clothing, emergency responders who need to suit up quickly, and healthcare workers applying protective coverings could all benefit from the technology.

## What Remains to Be Solved

The current system remains a research prototype connected to a pneumatic base station, with no consumer price or release date announced. Key challenges include making the garments portable, accommodating different body shapes, addressing laundering and maintenance, and conducting clinical trials with people who have real mobility impairments. The team also notes that automated removal of the clothing remains a future goal.

## Looking Ahead

Despite these hurdles, SWAG represents a philosophical shift in assistive technology: instead of bringing a robot to the clothing, the robotics are built into the clothing itself. For millions of older adults facing reduced independence, a garment that helps guide itself into place could mean more privacy, less reliance on caregivers, and greater confidence starting each day.
