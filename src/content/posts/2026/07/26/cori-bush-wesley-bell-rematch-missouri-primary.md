# Bush-Bell Rematch Tests Democratic Party's Direction in Missouri

ST. LOUIS — The Democratic primary rematch between former U.S. Rep. Cori Bush and incumbent Rep. Wesley Bell in Missouri's 1st Congressional District has become one of the most closely watched contests of the 2026 midterm cycle, pitting the party's progressive wing against its moderate establishment in a race with national implications.

With early voting already underway and the Aug. 4 primary fast approaching, the race is shaping up as a referendum on the Democratic Party's direction — particularly on the influence of outside spending, the Israel-Gaza war's impact on American politics, and the enduring legacy of the Ferguson protests that launched both candidates' political careers.

## A Rematch Rooted in Ferguson

Both Bush and Bell trace their political origins to the 2014 Ferguson protests that erupted after the police shooting of Michael Brown. But they have taken sharply divergent paths since then, and their rematch encapsulates a broader struggle within the Democratic Party over how to create change.

Bush, a nurse and pastor who became a frontline protest organizer, channeled her activism into a congressional run and won in 2020 by toppling a 50-year political dynasty, becoming Missouri's first Black congresswoman. Bell, by contrast, pursued institutional change — winning election to the Ferguson City Council and later serving as St. Louis County Prosecuting Attorney, where he [implemented police reform](https://apnews.com/article/cori-bush-wesley-bell-a2d8c60a470a03791e61c1e8aafc0dad).

In 2024, Bell defeated Bush by less than six percentage points in the Democratic primary, a loss Bush attributed largely to the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC), which spent millions through its United Democracy Project super PAC to defeat her.

## National Stakes and Outside Spending

AIPAC has poured more than $1.2 million against Bush in 2026 through the United Democracy Project, while New Democrat Majority has spent an additional $1 million against the progressive challenger. The outside spending has drawn sharp criticism from voters and activists who question the role of money in the race.

"It doesn't seem like it should be big money should be influencing this race," said Allison Johnson, an undecided voter quoted in AP News.

The race has drawn national attention as a test of AIPAC's electoral power. Former Rep. Jamaal Bowman, a close Bush ally who was himself defeated by an AIPAC-backed challenger in 2024, framed the contest in stark terms: "Cori is the knockout blow to AIPAC."

## Competing Visions

The candidates offered starkly different visions during a joint appearance on St. Louis Public Radio's "Politically Speaking Hour" on July 24, as reported by [STLPR](https://www.stlpr.org/government-politics-issues/2026-07-24/rep-wesley-bell-cori-bush-missouri-first-district-primary-listen-live).

Bell, who serves on the House Armed Services and Foreign Affairs committees, has emphasized his effectiveness as a lawmaker and his ability to work across the aisle. He rallied with former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi on July 24, a powerful signal of establishment support.

"She's not a serious candidate," Bell said in an interview with the AP. "I wake up every day working for St. Louis, and the folks in St. Louis know that I'm going to wake up working for them. And then we've got an opponent, Cori, who wakes up thinking about Cori. And we've seen it over and over. And that's why she was fired."

Bush has framed the race as a grassroots movement against powerful interests. Speaking outside the Old Courthouse where the Dred Scott case was argued, she told supporters: "We're standing here saying, send back that descendant, send her back to the place that enslaved people built. This is our moment."

Addressing pro-Palestinian activists and Black Lives Matter organizers at a rally near the Gateway Arch, Bush declared: "It's not that they're necessarily scared of me. They're scared of what comes with me. They're scared of y'all."

## The Israel Factor

The Israel-Gaza war has deepened divisions within the Democratic Party, and the Missouri race has become a flashpoint. Bush has been a vocal critic of Israel's military campaign and U.S. military aid to Israel, while Bell has defended the U.S.-Israel alliance.

"I think it's important that we stand with our allies," Bell said, adding: "What we've learned in Iraq, what we learned in Afghanistan, is that we can't do it alone. We need our allies and we also need to be transparent with the American people."

Bush has challenged Bell to confront anti-Muslim sentiment within the community and to "stand up and fight for the liberation of the Palestinian people."

## What's at Stake

The Aug. 4 primary will determine the Democratic nominee in the reliably blue 1st District, meaning the outcome will effectively decide who represents the district in Congress. But the race's significance extends far beyond Missouri.

This rematch will signal which faction of the Democratic Party is gaining momentum heading into the 2026 midterm elections and potentially the 2028 presidential cycle. It will test whether the party's base has shifted toward the anti-war, pro-Palestinian stance that Bush champions, or whether voters still favor the moderate, institution-focused approach embodied by Bell.

For voters in the district, local concerns also loom large — including recovery from a 2025 tornado that devastated the St. Louis metropolitan area, police reform, and economic development. But the flood of outside money and the national spotlight have made this race about much more than local issues.

As former Ferguson Mayor Ella Jones, who backs Bell, put it: "He's the best person for the job. When he was on council, he and I worked together to get the police reform done."

Activist DeRay McKesson, a lead organizer during the 2014 Ferguson protests who supports Bush, offered a different view: "I like Cori, I think she is the best choice and has a clear commitment to St. Louis. She's not afraid to challenge power."

With early voting underway and election day on Aug. 4, the people of Missouri's 1st District will deliver their verdict — and the nation will be watching.
