# Charleroi Halts Greening Projects Amid Climate Funding Crisis

The city of Charleroi has suspended multiple urban greening and energy renovation projects, citing a lack of budgetary visibility and reduced financial support from the Walloon Region. The decision, reported by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/charleroi-met-des-projets-de-vegetalisation-sur-pause-le-casse-tete-du-financement-des-villes-face-au-rechauffement-climatique-11750375), underscores the growing dilemma facing municipalities across Belgium and Europe as they grapple with the escalating costs of climate adaptation.

## A City Caught Between Ambition and Austerity

Charleroi had embarked on an ambitious greening program under its "Plan Places" initiative, designed to transform concrete-dominated public squares into green, permeable spaces with trees, play areas, and fountains aimed at combating urban heat islands. The Place Destrée in Gilly, completed in 2024, served as a flagship example — converting a vast parking lot into a nature-friendly sanctuary for residents.

Yet that momentum has stalled. Of the 17 square renovations the city had planned, only 10 will now be completed. Several energy renovation projects for public buildings, including a school in Gilly, have also been put on hold. The first wave of projects was funded through a combination of municipal funds, regional subsidies, and European recovery plan financing. For the next phase, the city says the money is no longer guaranteed.

Mayor Thomas Dermine did not mince words: "Given the budgetary context, given that the Walloon Region has put a brake on financing for large cities, we had to press pause." The city also made €40 million in cuts to secure €48 million from the regional Plan Oxygene, a debt relief mechanism for struggling municipalities.

## A Clash of Interpretations

The Walloon government contests the notion that support has been withdrawn. The office of the Walloon Minister for Nature argues that the recovery plan was built on exceptional, non-recurring European funds that were never intended to be permanent. It maintains that several support mechanisms remain available to municipalities, including a €46 million envelope dedicated to green spaces, and that projects already underway under the recovery plan continue.

This standoff reflects a deeper tension: who bears the cost of adapting cities to a rapidly warming climate? For Charleroi, the answer is increasingly unclear.

## The Human Cost of Inaction

The debate is not academic. Extreme heat is already claiming lives in Belgium. Mayor Dermine revealed earlier this year that a 22-month-old girl died of hyperthermia during the June 2026 heat wave. He noted that a local funeral director had reported 3.5 times more deaths than normal during the extreme heat period.

The Walloon Air and Climate Agency (AWAC) has projected that a +2°C warming scenario could generate €174 million per year in heat-related health costs alone. Against this backdrop, every delayed greening project represents not just an aesthetic loss but a potential public health failure.

Marie-Charlotte Debouche, policy campaign officer at the nature protection association Natagora, framed the dilemma with clarity: "We have no clear vision of how governments plan to finance adaptation. But we have a very clear vision of the cost of inaction." Natagora has previously estimated the annual cost of climate inaction in Wallonia at €577 million.

## What's Next for Charleroi and Beyond

Charleroi is not alone. According to research cited in the RTBF report, 88% of Walloon municipalities facing financial distress are concentrated in the nine largest cities — precisely those most in need of climate adaptation investments. The financing gap threatens to widen as heat waves intensify and budgets tighten.

All eyes are now on the Walloon Nature Restoration Plan, expected in September 2026 under a European Union obligation. Natagora hopes the plan will establish binding targets for restoring urban green spaces, increasing tree canopy cover, and, crucially, providing the financial mechanisms to make adaptation a reality.

For now, Charleroi's pause stands as a cautionary tale. Cities across Belgium and Europe are being asked to prepare for a hotter future with the tools — and budgets — of the past. The question Mayor Dermine and his counterparts are increasingly asking is simple: if not now, when? And if not with public funding, how?
