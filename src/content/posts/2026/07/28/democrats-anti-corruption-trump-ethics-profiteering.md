# Democrats Push Anti-Corruption as Trump Profiteering Swells

A half-century after Watergate reshaped how Americans view corruption in Washington, President Trump has shattered ethical norms in ways that have enriched himself and his family by an estimated $4 billion during the first year of his second term. Now, as the 2026 midterm elections approach, Democrats are making a calculated bet: that voters will trust them to clean up a political system they believe has been co-opted against their interests.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/28/nx-s1-5869808/family-trump-profit-corruption-voters-ethics-democrats), the party is rolling out a platform centered on government accountability, aiming to capitalize on deep public concern over presidential profiteering and the erosion of traditional ethical norms in the executive branch.

## The Scale of the Problem

The numbers are staggering. A tally from [The New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/08/18/the-number) estimates that Trump and his family have pulled in roughly $4 billion during his first year back in office. The president increased his net worth by more than $2 billion since returning to office, according to a Senate Democratic report released July 20.

That report, titled "The Cost of Corruption: How Trump Turns Power into Profit at Americans' Expense," highlights a litany of self-enrichment schemes: crypto ventures that helped Trump pocket more than $1 billion, defense contracts awarded to tech startups in which Trump's sons had invested, and the acceptance of a $400 million luxury jet from Qatar's royal family.

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer described Trump as "without question the most corrupt president in American history" when announcing the anti-corruption initiative.

Rep. Jamie Raskin, the top Democrat on the House Judiciary Committee, put it even more starkly: "Watergate was like a Cub Scout prank compared to what we're living through."

## Voters See a Systemic Crisis

Democrats' anti-corruption messaging may have more resonance than in past cycles, thanks to a broad public consensus that corruption is endemic. A [Brennan Center for Justice poll](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/analysis-opinion/poll-voters-want-solutions-government-corruption) conducted in April and May found that 92% of voters — including 90% of Republicans, 93% of Democrats, and 93% of independents — believe corruption is a big problem in U.S. politics and government.

"Voters are defining corruption expansively," said Marina Pino, an attorney with the Brennan Center's Elections and Government Program, noting that Americans tie corruption directly to kitchen-table issues. Eighty-eight percent of voters say corruption is responsible for big problems that government has failed to solve, like the high cost of living.

## The Credibility Gap

Yet Democrats face a significant challenge: convincing a cynical electorate that they are the ones to fix the system. [NPR focus groups](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/16/nx-s1-5893599/pennsylvania-focus-groups-trump-corruption) of Pennsylvania swing voters revealed a deeply divided audience. Half of the 12 participants said they were "troubled" by Trump's earnings. The other half waved it off with weary cynicism.

"When it comes to ethics, moral and financial, I think that just about every politician is corrupt," one participant said. "So, the fact that he's a known huckster, I mean, we've known that for years about him."

This sentiment — that "all politicians are corrupt" — is perhaps the biggest obstacle Democrats face. If voters believe the entire system is broken, how do you persuade them to trust the party that has been part of that system?

## What Democrats Are Proposing

Democrats are pursuing a two-track approach. The first track targets Trump directly. Raskin has [introduced the Protecting Our Democracy Act](https://raskin.house.gov/2026/5/raskin-garcia-and-morelle-unveil-landmark-legislation-to-curb-trump-administration-s-historic-abuses-of-power-profiteering-from-public-office), legislation that would codify constitutional prohibitions on foreign and domestic emoluments, prevent presidents from profiting off foreign influence, and prohibit presidents from accepting payments from pardon recipients or appointees.

The second track targets systemic reform. Democratic congressional candidate Paige Cognetti, running in Pennsylvania's 8th district, has proposed an eight-point anti-corruption plan that includes a ban on congressional stock trades, 18-year term limits for Congress and Supreme Court justices, and restrictions on former lawmakers becoming lobbyists.

Sen. Jon Ossoff of Georgia has framed the issue in broader terms, arguing that "corruption in America runs a lot deeper than Donald Trump." At a Savannah rally in June, he declared: "American politics is coin operated. Money goes in, favors come out."

## Republicans Fight Back

National GOP campaign teams are working to undercut Democratic credibility on corruption. Bernadette Breslin, press secretary at the National Republican Senatorial Committee, pointed to campaign donations from billionaires and criminal charges faced by Democratic members.

House Republicans have also advanced limited restrictions on stock trading and focused their messaging on waste, fraud, and abuse in federal programs — a narrower definition of corruption that avoids the structural critique Democrats are making.

## The Midterm Stakes

With the 2026 midterms approaching, anti-corruption is emerging as a central campaign theme for Democrats, tied directly to affordability. The party has announced numerous groups and policy agendas focused specifically on anti-corruption measures.

But the legislative reality is sobering: even if Democrats retake both chambers of Congress, Trump would retain veto power over any anti-corruption bills. Democratic oversight and investigations would be the primary tools, not legislation.

The deeper question — and the one that may ultimately determine the outcome of the midterms — is whether voters who have come to expect corruption from both parties can be convinced that one party actually means to fix it. As one Pennsylvania swing voter put it: "The fact that he's a known huckster… we've known that for years." Overcoming that kind of deep-seated cynicism may be Democrats' toughest challenge yet.
