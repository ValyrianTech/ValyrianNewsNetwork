# Betye Saar, Pioneering Assemblage Artist, Dies at 99

Betye Saar, the pioneering American artist whose assemblage works transformed racist memorabilia into powerful symbols of Black liberation, died Sunday morning at her home in Los Angeles. She was 99 years old, four days shy of her 100th birthday, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/betye-saar-artist-died-age-los-angeles-92190e7326fce49d9c4ad3add89022f3).

A central figure of the 1960s Black Arts Movement, Saar pioneered the medium of assemblage—sculptures made from found objects—reclaiming Jim Crow-era collectibles and transforming them into totems of resistance. Her work explored Black identity, spirituality, and the African diaspora across seven decades.

## From Design to Revolutionary Art

Born Betye Irene Brown on July 30, 1926, in Los Angeles, Saar earned a B.A. in design from UCLA in 1947. She began her adult life as a social worker before shifting her focus to fine art at age 35 after taking a printmaking elective, according to her [Wikipedia biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betye_Saar). Inspired by Joseph Cornell's 1967 exhibition and Simon Rodia's Watts Towers, which she watched being built as a child, Saar began creating assemblages that blended found objects with political commentary.

The assassination of the Rev. Martin Luther King Jr. in 1968 marked a turning point. As Saar later told the [Hammer Museum](https://hammer.ucla.edu/now-dig-this/artists/betye-saar), King's death "triggered a rage within me, and the mystic transformed into the warrior."

## 'The Liberation of Aunt Jemima' and a Legacy of Reclamation

Saar's most famous work, "The Liberation of Aunt Jemima" (1972), exemplifies her approach. She took a derogatory mammy figurine—a stereotype born from the Jim Crow era—and placed a rifle in one hand and a hand grenade under her skirt, transforming the image into a revolutionary symbol.

"I had a lot of hesitation about using powerful, negative images such as these—thinking about how white people saw Black people, and how that influenced the ways in which Black people saw each other," Saar said, according to AP News. "What saved it was that I made Aunt Jemima into a revolutionary figure."

The piece became an enduring icon of the Black feminist movement. In 2020, nearly 50 years after Saar created the work, Quaker Oats announced it would retire the Aunt Jemima brand, acknowledging the logo was "based on a racial stereotype."

## Institutional Recognition After Decades

Though Saar received a solo exhibition at the Whitney Museum of American Art in 1975, major institutions exhibited her work only sporadically for decades—a reflection of the broader exclusion of Black women artists from mainstream recognition. The turning point came in 2019, when the Los Angeles County Museum of Art and the Museum of Modern Art mounted concurrent solo shows, an extraordinary honor.

In spring 2026, Saar was inducted into the American Academy of Arts and Letters. She received the W.E.B. Du Bois Medal from Harvard University in 2022 alongside Kareem Abdul-Jabbar, and held honorary doctorates from multiple institutions.

"She had boundless energy for making art. I think she had a lot to say and was tired of the art world being dominated by white males," Angela Robinson Witherspoon, director of the 2023 documentary "Betye Saar: Ready to Be A Warrior," told AP News.

## A Living Legacy

Saar's influence extended well beyond her own practice. She taught at UCLA and the Otis Art Institute in the 1980s, mentoring a generation of Black artists including renowned painter Kerry James Marshall. Her daughters Alison and Lezley both became accomplished artists in their own right.

The [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/artanddesign/2026/jul/27/betye-saar-death) noted that Saar's "symbolically rich body of work has evolved over time to demonstrate the environmental, cultural, political, racial, technological, economic, and historical context in which it exists," according to Roberts Projects, the Los Angeles gallery that represents her.

In July 2025, Saar announced the Betye Saar Legacy Group, a partnership with nine curators to safeguard her archive and provide access for future scholarship. At the time of her death, exhibitions of her work continued in Los Angeles and New York.

Saar is survived by her three daughters, Tracye, Alison, and Lezley. Her work remains on view in major museum collections worldwide, ensuring that generations to come will encounter the artist who turned the detritus of American racism into enduring art.
