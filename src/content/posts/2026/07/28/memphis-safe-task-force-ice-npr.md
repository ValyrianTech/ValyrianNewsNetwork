# Memphis 'Anti-Crime' Task Force Quietly Became ICE Crackdown

MEMPHIS, Tenn. — When President Donald Trump announced the Memphis Safe Task Force in the fall of 2025, federal and local officials described it as an anti-crime initiative targeting violent offenders. Immigration enforcement was never publicly mentioned as a goal. But an investigation by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/27/nx-s1-5896284/national-guard-memphis-immigration-task-force) has found that the task force quietly transformed into an immigration crackdown, with the vast majority of arrests involving individuals with no criminal convictions.

## Context

The task force, launched alongside Tennessee Gov. Bill Lee (R), deployed more than a dozen federal agencies and hundreds of Tennessee National Guard members to work with local and state law enforcement. Crime in Memphis was at a [25-year low](https://www.psrmemphis.org/federal-data-poses-questions-about-the-public-safety-need-of-memphis-immigration-arrests/) when the operation began, having fallen steadily since 2023 after hitting a near-record high. Trump called the Memphis task force a "replica" of the D.C. Safe and Beautiful Task Force, which also carried a significant immigration enforcement component.

## Key Developments

Data from the Institute for Public Service Reporting (PSR) reveals the scale of the operation's immigration focus. During the task force's first 17 days, 91% of ICE street arrests in Shelby County involved people with no criminal record. From Sept. 29, 2025, through April 1, 2026, [891 ICE arrests](https://www.psrmemphis.org/immigration-arrests-surge-under-trumps-memphis-safe-task-force/) were recorded in Shelby County, with 536 ending in deportation. More than 80% of those arrested had no criminal convictions. Immigration arrests surged sixfold after the task force began operations.

"All roads lead to immigration enforcement with this administration," said Chioma Chukwu, executive director of American Oversight, a nonpartisan watchdog tracking the task forces. "This administration has used crime as a pretext for furthering and advancing its immigration enforcement agenda."

Task force operations have centered on traffic stops in Memphis's two main Hispanic neighborhoods. Maria Oceja, co-founder of Vecindarios 901, a rapid-response network tracking ICE activity, recalled predicting the shift from the outset. "I was like, they're not coming to Memphis for just crime. I'm pretty sure this is going to be an ICE operation," she said.

When Stephen Miller, the White House immigration architect, visited Memphis in October 2025 for the task force rollout, he told assembled law enforcement officers: "You are unleashed." The hotline Oceja's organization runs was overwhelmed with calls that very first evening, requiring four people to answer simultaneously through the night for months.

The impact on Memphis's immigrant community — roughly 10% of the city's population and its fastest-growing demographic segment — has been severe. "There was a spike of fear and panic," said Mauricio Calvo, president and CEO of Latino Memphis. "Kids were not going to school, people were not showing up to work." Mutual aid groups report families too afraid to leave their homes. Amber Hampton runs a food pantry from her garage, delivering groceries to families who limit their outdoor activities to essentials.

The task force has no set end date, and its presence remains visible: National Guard troops stationed in grocery store parking lots, convoys of unmarked SUVs lining thoroughfares.

## The Yasser Lopez Case

The detention of 18-year-old Yasser Lopez became a flashpoint. The high school soccer player and Nicaraguan immigrant with no criminal record was pulled over in a traffic stop on Feb. 20, 2026, while on his way to a soccer game. ICE agents took him into custody. He was held for 63 days without bond at the West Tennessee Detention Facility.

His classmates staged a walkout in protest, and the [ACLU of Tennessee](https://www.aclu-tn.org/press-releases/aclu-of-tennessee-statement-free-yasser-lopez/) took up his case. On April 24, U.S. District Judge Sheryl H. Lipman ordered his release, [ruling](https://www.psrmemphis.org/federal-judge-orders-dhs-to-release-high-school-soccer-player-yasser-lopez/) that his arrest and detention without bond violated his due process rights. "This is the new norm," Lopez said after his release. "This is what has been happening all around the city. Now, it just happened to me."

## Analysis

The Memphis operation stands apart from similar federal interventions in Chicago and Minneapolis because of the cooperation at the state level. Tennessee's Republican governor fully embraced the task force, making local pushback difficult. Memphis Mayor Paul Young (D) said he is not supportive but cannot control federal actions. "There's no mayor in America that can control the immigration aspects of this," Young said.

In March 2026, Trump visited Memphis and declared the task force a success. Gov. Lee said the operation had "created a generational change," thanking Miller directly while focusing entirely on crime reduction and making no mention of immigration enforcement. The White House maintains that the task forces "have yielded tremendous results."

Ronnie Davis, a Memphis barber who initially welcomed the task force, now expresses unease. "They are only touting the good things," he said. "But had I been Hispanic, I'd be terrified. If I was Haitian, I'd be terrified."

## What's Next

The Memphis Safe Task Force continues operating with no scheduled end date, and Trump has indicated plans for similar task forces in other Republican-led states. With more than 10,900 total arrests and 1,800 firearms seized by the U.S. Marshals Service, the administration points to public safety gains. But critics argue the mission's stated purpose has been obscured by what they call a sprawling immigration enforcement operation that has sown fear through Memphis's immigrant communities — a reality they say is far from what residents were sold.
