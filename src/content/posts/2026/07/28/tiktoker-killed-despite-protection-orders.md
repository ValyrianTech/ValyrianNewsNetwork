# TikToker Killed Despite Multiple Protection Orders

Sara Gilson, a 43-year-old TikTok influencer from Owasso, Oklahoma, was allegedly fatally shot by her estranged husband Jeremiah "Shawn" Duffey, 48, on July 23, 2026, in an apparent murder-suicide — despite having obtained multiple protective orders against him over a five-year period. The case has drawn widespread attention to the failures of the domestic violence protection system, as the restraining orders Gilson secured apparently could not prevent the tragedy.

According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/influencer-sought-multiple-protection-orders-husband-s-accused-killing-rcna589422), court records show Gilson had sought protective orders against Duffey dating back to 2021. Two earlier orders were dismissed when she failed to appear at hearings — a common barrier in domestic violence cases where victims often abandon legal proceedings due to fear or coercion.

## A History of Protective Orders

Gilson's most recent emergency protective order was filed on June 10, 2026, and granted through August 24, 2026. The order required Duffey to stay 100 yards away from Gilson and her home. In her application, Gilson explicitly warned the court that Duffey had a firearm on his person and had threatened suicide. She also informed authorities that he had "went on the run."

The June 10 filing coincided with a separate, but related, emergency protective order filed by the mother of a 15-year-old girl who played on the youth traveling basketball team that Duffey coached under the online alias "Duff Daddy." The mother alleged that Duffey had been caught kissing and fondling the teenager by another coach, and that over time the man had sent inappropriate messages, invited the girl to his hotel room during tournament trips, and offered money "to keep her quiet."

Owasso police subsequently referred a charge of lewd molestation of a minor to the U.S. Attorney's Office in Tulsa, as Duffey was a member of the Osage Nation and the alleged incident occurred in Indian Country, triggering federal jurisdiction.

## The Night of the Tragedy

On July 23, 2026, at approximately 11:16 PM CT, a 911 call came from Gilson's home in Collinsville, Oklahoma, a suburb of Tulsa. Dispatchers reported hearing a woman screaming and crying, followed by a loud bang that sounded like a gunshot. When dispatchers attempted to call back, there was no answer. Moments later, a second call came from a neighbor's home. The caller was Gilson's son from a previous marriage, who told dispatchers that his stepfather had shot his mother.

Responding officers arrived to find Gilson and Duffey both dead from apparent gunshot wounds to the head, in what police have described as a murder-suicide. Owasso police confirmed they had received no reports that Duffey had violated the protective order prior to the killing.

## Social Media and Public Accusation

The case also carries a striking digital dimension. On July 11, 2026 — less than two weeks before her death — Gilson posted a viral TikTok video that both captured and complicated her situation. The video used a popular "Netflix documentary" trend, with text reading: "Preparing for when Netflix drops a documentary about my soon to be ex husband who I just found out is a pedophile." Her caption read: "I wish I was joking."

Gilson's public accusation on social media preceded what would become a murder-suicide, highlighting the dangerous intersection of viral content and real-world threats. In her final TikTok post on July 21, she shared a photo of herself on a boat with the words: "May whatever the f*** that was never find me again," captioned "Please God."

## Systemic Failures and Unanswered Questions

Gilson's case has become a flashpoint for advocates who argue that protective orders, while essential, are only as effective as the systems that enforce them. Key failures include:

- **Firearm access:** Despite Gilson's explicit warning to the court that Duffey had a firearm, the protective order did not prevent him from accessing a gun. Research has shown that orders explicitly restricting firearm access can reduce the likelihood of domestic violence homicide.
- **Enforcement gaps:** Owasso police stated they received no reports of violations, but Gilson's final protective order had been in effect for only six weeks at the time of her death.
- **Cross-reporting failure:** The two protective orders filed on the same day — one by Gilson, one by the 15-year-old's mother — were handled separately, with no indication of coordinated enforcement.

## A Community in Mourning

Gilson leaves behind two children from a previous marriage. A [GoFundMe campaign](https://www.gofundme.com/f/support-saras-children-after-tragic-loss) organized by Brandon Johns has raised over $32,000 for the children's immediate needs, counseling, and education costs. "Sara was an incredible mom. Her children were the center of her world, and everything she did was for them," the fundraiser states.

## What's Next

The case raises urgent questions about the effectiveness of domestic violence protective orders, particularly when firearms are involved and when the accused faces separate allegations of child sexual abuse. The U.S. Attorney's Office in Tulsa has not yet commented on the status of the lewd molestation charges following Duffey's death. Advocates are calling for policy reforms that would strengthen enforcement of protective orders, improve coordination across jurisdictions, and close loopholes that allow firearms to remain in the hands of those subject to such orders.
