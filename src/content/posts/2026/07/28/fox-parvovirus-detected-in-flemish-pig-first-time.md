# Fox Parvovirus Detected in Flemish Pig for First Time

For the first time in Belgium, the fox parvovirus has been detected in a pig, the Flemish animal health organization Dierengezondheidszorg Vlaanderen (DGZ) [confirmed](https://www.dgz.be/nieuws/fox-parvovirus-vlaams-varkensbedrijf) on July 23, 2026. The discovery, made in the Flanders region, marks the latest expansion of a virus that has already affected pig populations in the Netherlands and Germany. Authorities have stressed that the virus poses no danger to humans and currently presents no threat to food safety.

## A Virus New to Belgian Livestock

The fox parvovirus is genetically related to a protoparvovirus first identified in fox feces in the Netherlands in 2012, and subsequently in Croatia in 2016. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/07/28/fox-parvovirus-varkens-vlaanderen-virus/), the virus has since crossed from wildlife into domestic pig populations, appearing in the Netherlands in late 2024 before spreading to Germany and Denmark.

The virus was detected in Flanders using Pathosense technology on a spleen sample from a piglet submitted for autopsy at DGZ. The sample came from a piglet showing characteristic symptoms, including bulging eyes, cross-eyedness, skin redness, and hair loss. While these symptoms can be striking, infections are generally mild, do not lead to death, and resolve on their own after several weeks.

Industry publication [VILT](https://vilt.be/nl/nieuws/nieuw-virus-met-opvallende-symptomen-nu-ook-aangetoond-op-vlaams-varkensbedrijf) reports that symptoms have been observed on at least 80 percent of Dutch pig farms since the virus first emerged there in December 2024. The rapid spread has drawn attention from veterinary authorities across Europe.

## No Risk to Humans or Food Supply

The Federal Agency for the Safety of the Food Chain (FAVV) has stated that the virus currently poses no danger to food safety, though the standard rule applies that sick animals should not enter the food chain. The virus is not transmissible to humans, and public health authorities have issued no alerts.

This reassurance is particularly significant for Flanders, a major pig-producing region with a high concentration of farms in West-Flanders. The detection of a novel virus has raised concerns among livestock farmers already navigating multiple animal health challenges, including African Swine Fever outbreaks elsewhere in the European Union.

## Uncertain Transmission Routes

Details about how the fox parvovirus spreads remain under investigation. The Dutch Animal Health Service (GD) has noted that multiple transmission routes are possible, including from sows to fetuses and horizontal transmission between animals. Because of limited information, only general hygiene and animal health advice can currently be offered to farmers.

Parvoviruses are known for their extreme environmental resistance. They can survive dry heat up to 90°C and remain viable in organic material for up to one year. This resilience makes containment particularly challenging once the virus establishes itself in a region.

## Scientific Context and Response

The identification of the fox parvovirus as the causative agent of the observed symptoms was detailed in a scientific paper published on July 6, 2026, in the CDC journal Emerging Infectious Diseases. The study, led by Dr. Tijs J. Tobias of the Royal GD and Utrecht University, found that the detected parvovirus is genetically distinct from Porcine Parvovirus type 1 (PPV1), meaning existing vaccinations provide little to no cross-immunity. The authors noted that final proof of causality is still needed.

DGZ has advised farmers to monitor piglets for the distinctive symptoms and to contact their helpdesk or regional veterinarian Caroline Bonckaert if suspected cases are observed. The organization will work with individual farms to determine whether additional investigation is warranted.

## Looking Ahead

The detection of fox parvovirus in Belgium underscores the constant risk of wildlife-to-domestic-animal disease spillover, particularly in regions with intensive livestock farming. Veterinary authorities are expected to enhance surveillance programs to track the virus's spread, while researchers continue investigating transmission routes and potential long-term implications for the European pig farming sector.

As the situation develops, the key message from Belgian authorities remains clear: the virus is mild, self-limiting, and poses no threat to human health or food safety. Vigilance, however, will be essential to prevent further spread.
