# China Toughens Insider Trading Laws in Landmark Overhaul

China's top judicial authorities have jointly issued a sweeping revision to the country's insider trading rules — the first major overhaul in 14 years — significantly expanding the scope of criminal liability while sharply increasing monetary thresholds for prosecution. The new interpretation, released jointly by [the Supreme People's Court and the Supreme People's Procuratorate](http://society.people.com.cn/n1/2026/0727/c1008-40768246.html), took effect on July 27, 2026.

## Why This Matters: 14 Years in the Making

The original judicial interpretation on insider trading had been in effect since June 1, 2012, operating under an earlier legal framework that no longer reflected the realities of China's vastly expanded capital markets. Since then, China passed a revised Securities Law in 2019 and the Futures and Derivatives Law in 2022 — both introducing stricter regulatory requirements. Yet the criminal enforcement framework had remained frozen, creating a growing misalignment between administrative regulation and judicial enforcement.

Perhaps more critically, insider trading patterns themselves have evolved dramatically. Over recent years, violations have become more concealed, pre-positioned, and circle-based, exploiting regulatory blind spots — particularly in the early-stage "intention phase" of mergers, acquisitions, and major investments, where controlling shareholders could leak preliminary plans to close associates before any formal decision was made.

## Three Pillars of the New Framework

The revision, documented as Fa Shi [2026] No. 13, introduces three core changes that legal experts and market analysts have described as precisely targeting the most significant regulatory blind spots.

### 1. Forward Shift of the Sensitive Period

Under the previous framework, the "sensitive period" for identifying inside information was too narrow to capture early-stage information leakage. The amendment now stipulates that the time when a controlling shareholder, actual controller, or relevant decision-maker discloses preliminary intentions to form inside information to close associates — or conducts transactions based on such preliminary intentions — shall be considered the initial time of the proposal.

According to an in-depth analysis by [21st Century Business Herald](https://m.21jingji.com/article/20260725/herald/d19c4fe09fc1a16db6ea47c95b7cff9f.html), this change closes a "significant regulatory blind spot" where core insiders could exploit the time gap between early-stage planning and formal decision-making to pass information to connected parties for preemptive trading.

### 2. Tightened Exemption Defenses

The revision fundamentally tightens all four categories of exemption defenses that previously allowed market participants to avoid insider trading liability. Three key changes stand out:

- **Acquisition defenses**: Listed company acquisitions must now demonstrate a legitimate purpose, preventing what analysts call "dark warehouse" trading — where entities use acquisition status to mask insider trading
- **Pre-set transactions**: Must be genuine and lawful, closing loopholes where parties used虚假 (fraudulent) contracts or trading plans to circumvent the law
- **Disclosure requirements**: Any claimed "public disclosure" must genuinely ensure fair protection for all investors, not selective disclosure to favored parties

### 3. Tiered Sentencing Thresholds Raised Substantially

Perhaps the most technically significant change involves the monetary thresholds for criminal prosecution, which have been raised substantially — by approximately 3.3 to 8 times the previous levels — to reflect inflation and market growth since 2012:

- **"Serious circumstances"**: Transaction amount ≥ 2 million yuan (up from ~600,000), margin ≥ 1 million yuan, profit/loss avoidance ≥ 500,000 yuan
- **"Extremely serious circumstances"**: Transaction amount ≥ 20 million yuan, margin ≥ 10 million yuan, profit/loss avoidance ≥ 5 million yuan

As [Sina Finance reported](https://finance.sina.com.cn/stock/y/2026-07-27/doc-inikevrp3242274.shtml), interviewed lawyers noted that while the thresholds are higher, the tightened defenses and earlier trigger points mean more conduct may now fall within the scope of criminal liability — resulting in a net widening of the enforcement net.

## Market and Enforcement Implications

The amendment takes effect immediately, meaning all ongoing and future insider trading cases will be adjudicated under the new framework. The forward shift of the sensitive period creates retrospective risk for cases involving preliminary-stage information leakage that were previously in a legal gray zone.

For companies engaged in M&A, restructuring, and major transactions, the implications are immediate and significant. Internal information management protocols must be reviewed to ensure compliance with the stricter framework. Market participants can no longer rely on acquisition exemptions as a safe harbor for trading on non-public information.

The timing is also notable: as [a legal analysis on Bilibili](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1yA3L61EmU/) by Beijing criminal defense lawyer Zhang Jianhua highlighted, the release coincides with a broader anti-corruption push in China's financial sector. Just days before the announcement, former CSRC Vice Chairman Fang Xinghai was placed under investigation for suspected serious violations of discipline and law.

## What to Watch For

The key questions going forward center on enforcement. How will courts apply the new rules to cases already under investigation? Will the tightened defenses lead to an initial surge in convictions as previously borderline cases fall within the scope of criminal liability? And perhaps most importantly, how will market behavior change in response to the earlier trigger point for insider information identification?

What is clear is that China's capital markets have entered a new enforcement era — one where the gap between administrative regulation and criminal enforcement has been substantially closed, and where the early-stage "gray zones" of information leakage that have long plagued market integrity are now squarely in prosecutors' sights.
