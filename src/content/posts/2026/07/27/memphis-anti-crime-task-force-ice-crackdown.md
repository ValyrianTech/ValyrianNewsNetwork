# How Memphis' Anti-Crime Task Force Turned Into an ICE Crackdown

When President Donald Trump announced the Memphis Safe Task Force in September 2025, he framed it as a decisive strike against violent crime. But an [investigation by NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/27/nx-s1-5896284/national-guard-memphis-immigration-task-force) reveals that the operation quietly evolved into a large-scale immigration enforcement machine, arresting thousands of people — the vast majority of whom had no criminal convictions.

## Background: From Crime Fighting to Immigration Enforcement

The task force was born from "Operation Viper," a secret FBI undercover mission that netted 500 arrests and 100 indictments before any public announcement. On September 12, 2025, Trump signed an order sending the National Guard alongside hundreds of federal agents — including personnel from the FBI, DHS, ICE, DEA, ATF, and U.S. Marshals — to Memphis. Gov. Bill Lee of Tennessee praised the deployment as an opportunity for "generational change" in public safety, though crime in Memphis was already at a 25-year low.

By the end of September, more than 700 federal agents and roughly 300 state troopers had arrived, operating alongside over 1,900 Memphis police officers. The stated mission was to combat "street and violent crime."

## The Data: Immigration Arrests Surge

Within the first two weeks, the task force made 803 arrests. But a clear pattern emerged: of the ICE "street arrests" in Shelby County during the first 17 days of operations, 91% involved individuals with no criminal convictions, according to data analyzed by the Institute for Public Service Reporting. ICE street arrests nearly tripled after the task force launched — from 6.4 per week to 18.5 per week.

By March 2026, the task force had made over 7,100 total arrests. Of the 802 ICE arrests in Shelby County between September 29 and March 10, 88% were of people with no criminal convictions, and 73% had neither criminal convictions nor pending charges. The data also revealed that at least 90 children were arrested by ICE in Tennessee during this period, 34 of them under the age of 10.

The arrest rate represented a sixfold increase in ICE street arrests: from 0.9 per day before the task force to 5.4 per day afterward. Across Tennessee, more than 10,000 ICE arrests occurred in the first 14 months of Trump's second term — a threefold increase over the previous administration.

## Community Impact: Fear and Disruption

The effect on Memphis' immigrant communities was immediate and severe. Mauricio Calvo, president and CEO of Latino Memphis, told NPR that the task force created "a spike of fear and panic. Kids were not going to school, people were not showing up to work."

Maria Oceja, co-founder of Vecindarios 901, a local rapid-response network, said she predicted the crackdown from the start. "I was like, they're not coming to Memphis for just crime. I'm pretty sure this is going to be an ICE operation," she recalled. Her organization's hotline received so many calls on the first evening that four people had to answer simultaneously throughout the night — a pattern that lasted for months.

Hunter Demster, another co-founder of Vecindarios 901, said the traffic stops that led to many arrests seemed concentrated in Hispanic neighborhoods. "It was very evident that this was an immigration operation at its core," he said. "It's about targeting Black and brown folk."

## A Teen's Ordeal: The Case of Yasser Lopez

The human cost of the operation was exemplified by the case of Yasser Lopez Soza, an 18-year-old high school student. Lopez was arrested in February 2026 while riding as a passenger to a soccer game. Immigration agents detained him after a traffic stop, and he was held without bond for 63 days at a detention facility.

A U.S. district court judge later found that his arrest and detention violated his due process rights and ordered his release. His classmates staged a walkout in protest, and the ACLU of Tennessee took up his case.

"But then I remembered that this is the new norm," Lopez told NPR through a translator. "This is what has been happening all around the city. Now, it just happened to me."

## Legal Challenges and Mission Creep

The National Guard deployment faced legal challenges from the start. In October 2025, local officials sued Gov. Bill Lee, arguing the deployment violated the Tennessee Constitution. A chancery court ruled in November that Lee had exceeded his authority, finding no "grave emergency" or "disaster" as required by state law. The state appealed, however, keeping the Guard in place while the legal battle continues.

Chioma Chukwu, executive director of American Oversight, a nonpartisan watchdog, sees the Memphis operation as part of a broader pattern. "All roads lead to immigration enforcement with this administration," she told NPR. "This administration has used crime as a pretext for furthering its immigration enforcement agenda."

The White House defended the task force, with spokesperson Abigail Jackson pointing to results without providing evidence. Emily Williams, a spokesperson for the U.S. Marshals Service, which leads the task force, stated that immigration enforcement is "only one aspect" of the mission, adding, "If you are in the country illegally, you are committing a crime."

## Local Cooperation and Continued Presence

Unlike cities such as Chicago or Minneapolis, where federal immigration enforcement faced local resistance, Memphis has benefited from strong state-level cooperation. Gov. Lee has been a vocal supporter, and Mayor Paul Young, a Democrat, acknowledged his limited ability to push back. "My goal is to make sure that I can control what I can control," Young told NPR. "And there's no mayor in America that can control the immigration aspects of this."

Ten months after its launch, the Memphis Safe Task Force remains in operation with no set end date. National Guard troops are still stationed in grocery store parking lots and along tourist areas, and convoys of unmarked SUVs line thoroughfares. Mutual aid groups continue to deliver groceries to families too afraid to leave their homes.

## What's Next

The task force's long-term future remains uncertain. Trump has signaled plans to replicate the model in other Republican-led states, while legal questions over the National Guard's role remain unresolved. For immigrant communities in Memphis, the period of heightened fear has become the new reality — one that, as Yasser Lopez put it, has touched nearly everyone.
