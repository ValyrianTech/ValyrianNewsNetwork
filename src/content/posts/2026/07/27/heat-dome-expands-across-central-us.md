# Heat Dome Engulfs Central US, Endangering 80 Million

A massive heat dome is expanding across the central United States, placing approximately 80 million people under heat advisories and excessive heat warnings from Texas to North Dakota, as forecasters warn of dangerously high temperatures that could persist well into next week.

The National Weather Service has issued extreme heat warnings across a vast swath of the country, spanning the Desert Southwest, Gulf Coast, Midwest, and Great Plains. Temperatures are running 10 to 15 degrees Fahrenheit (5.6-8.3°C) above normal for this time of year, with some areas of the southwestern desert potentially reaching 120°F (48°C), according to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/hot-weather-temperatures-climate-heat-dome-65d34e4c520472c7bb1f46cd133b7952).

## What Is Driving the Extreme Heat?

The current conditions are being caused by a heat dome — a high-pressure atmospheric system that acts as a lid, trapping heat in place over a region for days or even weeks. The high pressure compresses and warms the air while preventing cooler air from moving in, creating a dome of hot air that intensifies over time.

"It looks like it is going to be pretty much stationary in that position as we go through the week," said Bob Oravec, a meteorologist with the National Weather Service, as reported by the AP.

Compounding the danger, overnight low temperatures are not expected to drop sufficiently, allowing heat stress to accumulate as the heat wave lingers. High humidity across much of the affected region further exacerbates conditions, making it feel even hotter.

## Scorching Temperatures Across the Region

Specific temperature forecasts paint a stark picture. Western Nebraska is under an extreme heat warning with temperatures forecast to reach 108°F (42°C). Las Vegas is experiencing near-record highs around 114°F (45°C). The Denver area faces the potential for record-breaking temperatures, while Houston is enduring its first prolonged triple-digit stretch of summer, with heat index values reaching up to 112°F.

In Springfield, Missouri, heat index values have climbed as high as 115°F, according to the National Weather Service. The worst pockets of heat are forecast to become particularly severe across Texas and parts of the Gulf Coast by Wednesday.

## Life Under the Heat Dome

The extreme weather is reshaping daily life for millions. At the Museum of the Fur Trade near Chadron, Nebraska, employee Hannah Lovelace noted that fur hats and pelts in the gift shop were not selling in the oppressive heat. "I'm sure no one would be willing to wear it on a day like this," she told the AP, adding that loose-fitting, period-correct fabric shirts remained popular among visitors headed to historical reenactments.

In Las Vegas, HVAC repair technician Kristian "Flaco" Santos spends hours on rooftops and in attics where temperatures can be 15 degrees warmer than the already scorching air outside. "It is brutal," Santos said. "I'm not Superman, but I do, I do take care of myself when I'm in the heat." He wears long-sleeved shirts and hoodies for sun protection, drinks water with electrolytes, and takes frequent breaks.

In response to the conditions, cooling centers have opened in cities like Denver, and the Indianapolis Motor Speedway offered water refill stations and cooling areas during the Brickyard 400 on Sunday.

## A Summer of Record Heat

This heat dome marks the third major heat event to hit the United States in the past month. An early July heat dome forced communities in the eastern US to cancel Independence Day celebrations, disrupting the country's 250th anniversary. A mid-July heat wave then struck the Southwest and Great Plains, bringing 115°F (46°C) temperatures to Montana and breaking multiple temperature records.

The extreme weather is not limited to the US. As The [Guardian reports](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/jul/25/scorching-us-heat-wave), a June heat wave in Europe caused an estimated 5,700 excess deaths in France. Wildfires in Spain and France have forced 250,000 people to evacuate, and parts of the United Kingdom are also burning amid hot, dry conditions. Across the US, firefighters are currently battling 88 active fires.

## The Climate Connection

Scientists say the pattern of increasingly frequent and severe heat waves is consistent with long-standing climate projections. Andy Pershing, chief program officer at Climate Central, a climate change communications nonprofit, explained to the AP that rising carbon dioxide levels from burning fossil fuels are making hotter weather more common and more extreme.

"We are experiencing exactly what the climate models in the 1990s said we should experience now," Pershing said. "That is more frequent, more severe heat waves, bigger temperature anomalies, more humidity, higher overnight temperatures. These are all part and parcel of the climate story."

The continental United States recorded its second-hottest first half of the year on record in 2026, according to data from the National Centers for Environmental Information, with June ranking as the 18th warmest June on record.

## What Lies Ahead

The NWS expects the heat dome to remain largely stationary through the week, with some possible but temporary relief toward next weekend. Some areas, including the Mid-Atlantic, Northeast, and much of the Pacific Northwest, are expected to be spared the worst of the conditions.

Officials are urging residents in affected areas to take precautions: seek air conditioning or cooling centers, drink plenty of water, and limit time in direct sunlight. Children, older adults, pregnant people, and those with pre-existing medical conditions remain especially vulnerable to heat-related illnesses, including heat stroke — the deadliest weather phenomenon in the United States.

As the nation endures its third major heat event in a single month, the question of how communities adapt to an increasingly volatile climate has never been more urgent.
