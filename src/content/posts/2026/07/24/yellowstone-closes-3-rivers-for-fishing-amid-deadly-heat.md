# Yellowstone Closes 3 Rivers for Fishing Amid Deadly Heat

Yellowstone National Park has imposed emergency fishing closures on three of its most iconic rivers — the Madison, Firehole, and Gibbon — after water temperatures soared to lethal levels, killing trout in one of the park's most visited areas. The [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/yell/learn/news/26015.htm) announced the full 24-hour closures on July 15, citing peak water temperatures of 80 to 86 degrees Fahrenheit, conditions it described as "lethal to trout."

## A Crisis Escalates in Days

The closures unfolded rapidly as a severe heat wave settled over the northern Rockies. On July 9, park officials implemented partial restrictions, allowing fishing only from sunrise to 2 p.m. on the three rivers. But as temperatures continued to climb — with air temperatures forecast to reach around 90°F — and reports of dead fish emerged from the Firehole River, the NPS escalated to full closures less than a week later.

According to the NPS press release, the Firehole and Madison rivers and their tributaries, along with the Gibbon River downstream of Norris Campground, are now under temporary full closures. The measures are designed to "protect the park's native and wild trout fisheries and will remain in effect until conditions improve."

Yellowstone Lake and other lakes remain open to fishing from sunrise to sunset, and all other rivers and streams not listed as closed are unaffected.

## Why Warm Water Is Deadly to Trout

Trout, a cold-water species, are especially vulnerable to heat stress. Water temperatures above 68°F are considered dangerous, and the park recorded daytime peaks of 80–86°F — far beyond the species' tolerance threshold.

Steven J. Cooke, a biology professor at Carleton University who studies fish physiology, explained the science behind the closures in an interview with [Fox News Digital](https://www.foxnews.com/travel/urgent-yellowstone-fishing-closures-hit-3-rivers-water-turns-deadly). "Fish are ectotherms, which means that their body temperature is the same as the water temperature," Cooke said. "At high temperatures, trout struggle to obtain enough oxygen and may not recover upon release."

Cooke, who serves on the board of the nonprofit Keep Fish Wet, warned anglers that such disruptions are likely to become more frequent. "Anglers need to get used to such closures, as they will become more common," he said. He advised that improving fish-handling practices — minimizing fight time and eliminating air exposure — can help fish survive stressful conditions and allow continued fishing opportunities.

## Broader Context: A July of Record-Breaking Heat

The Yellowstone fishing closures are the latest consequence of an extraordinary July heat wave that has shattered temperature records across the United States. According to [WeatherOnThisDay](https://weatheronthisday.com/trends/july-2026-heat-recap), more than 1,500 daily temperature records were broken in the first half of July 2026 alone.

The Western United States was hit particularly hard. A heat dome parked over the northern Rockies and Great Plains drove temperatures in eastern Montana to 116°F on July 13. Wyoming recorded 139 daily heat records in just four days, while Montana added 118. These extreme conditions pushed river temperatures in Yellowstone well beyond the tolerance range of native trout.

The closures fit a broader pattern of warming waters threatening freshwater ecosystems as extreme heat events become more frequent nationwide.

## Yellowstone's Fishing Heritage at Risk

Yellowstone has been a premier fishing destination for more than 150 years. Its waters support rainbow, brown, and cutthroat trout populations that draw anglers from around the world. The park's regulations are designed to balance recreational fishing with conservation of native fish abundance and genetic integrity.

In an ironic twist, Yellowstone had extended the 2026 fishing season on these three rivers earlier this year, opening them weeks earlier to allow anglers a longer season during more optimal water conditions. The decision inadvertently exposed more anglers to a season disrupted by extreme heat.

## Looking Ahead

The NPS said staff will continue monitoring water temperatures daily. The weather forecast calls for slightly cooler temperatures and isolated thunderstorms in the coming days, which could bring some relief. However, the closures will remain in effect until conditions improve, and additional restrictions may be imposed if necessary.

For now, anglers planning a visit to Yellowstone this summer are urged to check current fishing restrictions before heading out. The situation serves as a stark reminder that as global temperatures rise, even America's most cherished natural landmarks are feeling the heat.

As Cooke noted, adapting to these changes will require both flexible management and a commitment from anglers to do their part in protecting fish populations during increasingly challenging conditions.
