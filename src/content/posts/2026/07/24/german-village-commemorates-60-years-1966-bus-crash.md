# German Village Commemorates 60 Years Since Deadly Bus Crash That Killed 28 Children from Brussels

A German village is holding a solemn commemoration ceremony on Saturday to mark 60 years since one of the deadliest bus crashes in post-war European history — a tragedy that claimed the lives of 28 children from Brussels. The event, organized by Annie Cuypers, whose 17-year-old brother Paul was among the victims, takes place at the bridge near Niederbrechen where the bus plunged 12 meters onto the road below on July 25, 1966.

## The Crash

In the early morning of July 25, 1966, a Belgian tour bus carrying 43 passengers — 38 children and youths aged 12 to 18 returning from a summer camp in Tyrol, Austria, along with five adults — was traveling on the A3 autobahn near Limburg an der Lahn in Hesse, Germany. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/07/24/zestig-jaar-na-zware-busramp-duits-dorp-herdenkt-zaterdag-28-br/), the bus struck a concrete barrier, broke through the bridge railing, and fell onto the road below. The cause is believed to have been driver fatigue, though a blown tire has also been suggested as a possible factor.

The crash killed 33 people in total: 28 children, the bus driver, trip organizer Leopold Ceuster, his wife Sophie Van Messem, and two other adults. Only 10 people survived. The tragedy claimed more victims than the well-known 2012 Sierre bus crash in Switzerland, which killed 22 children and 6 adults.

## A Rescue with Limited Means

The rescue operation was hampered by the limited equipment available to firefighters in 1966. As [the Niederbrechen Fire Department](https://www.feuerwehr-niederbrechen.de/news/2016-07-24-und8222vor-50-jahren-25-juli-1966-bei-einem-schweren-busunglueck-an-der-autobahnbruecke-niederbrechen-und8211-werschau-sterben-33-personen-darunter-28-kinderund8220.html) documented on the 50th anniversary, workers from a nearby brickworks were among the first on the scene. Firefighters had only axes and hacksaws — not the hydraulic rescue tools available today — to cut through the wreckage. Seventeen people were declared dead at the scene; others died en route to or in hospitals in Limburg, Diez, and Hadamar.

Willi Kremer, then a 19-year-old firefighter and later fire chief of Niederbrechen, recalled the chaos in a 2018 interview with the [Frankfurter Neue Presse](https://www.fnp.de/hessen/erinnerungen-busunglueck-1966-niederbrechen-10421428.html): "It was completely different from today. We didn't have that equipment, the jaws of life for example, that firefighters use today to cut open a vehicle. And at first we thought the brickworks was on fire."

## The Human Toll

The children on the bus came from families working at government institutions including the police, ministries, and the Sabena airline. The trip was organized by "Jeugd en Vakantie" (Youth and Holiday), run by Leopold Ceuster, a former resistance fighter who organized nature trips for children from families who were not well-off financially.

Annie Cuypers' brother Paul attended Sint-Jorisinstituut school in Brussels and served as an altar boy at St. Michael's Cathedral. He was buried in Testelt near Scherpenheuvel-Zichem, the family's home region. "He never quite found his way in Brussels," Cuypers told VRT NWS, explaining that the family had only moved to the capital three years before his death.

## A Friendship Born from Tragedy

Remarkably, the disaster gave rise to a lasting bond between the fire departments of Niederbrechen and Enghien (Edingen), Belgium. Some children on the bus were children of Belgian firefighters. Weeks after the crash, contact was established, and the two brigades have maintained a regular exchange ever since, meeting approximately every three years. The Niederbrechen fire station even has a corner dedicated to the accident, featuring an exhibition that includes the old firefighter uniform of Annie Cuypers' uncle.

## The 60th Anniversary

Saturday's ceremony at the crash site brings together family members, a firefighter who participated in the original rescue, and a delegation from the Enghien fire department. A memorial stone with plaques in German, French, and Dutch has marked the site since shortly after the tragedy.

For Annie Cuypers, now 75, this commemoration carries particular urgency. "I haven't found many survivors anymore," she said. "Their families have often passed away too. Everyone is still welcome, of course. I'm already 75, so this might be the last time we can do this."

## A Tragedy Remembered

While the 2012 Sierre bus crash remains vivid in Belgian memory, the 1966 Niederbrechen crash — which was actually deadlier — has received less attention in recent decades. Saturday's ceremony represents a final chapter in the living memory of the disaster, as survivors and close family members grow fewer with each passing year. The commemoration ensures that the 28 children from Brussels who lost their lives on a summer morning 60 years ago will not be forgotten.
