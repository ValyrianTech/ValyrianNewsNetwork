# Haitian Families Brace for Separation as TPS Expires

More than 350,000 Haitian nationals in the United States are set to lose their Temporary Protected Status (TPS) starting Monday, July 27, following a U.S. Supreme Court ruling that allowed the Trump administration to terminate the decades-old humanitarian program. The decision leaves thousands of mixed-status families—including an estimated 50,000 U.S.-born children with at least one Haitian TPS parent—facing an agonizing choice: leave their American children behind, or take them to a country the U.S. State Department warns Americans not to visit.

## The End of a Humanitarian Lifeline

TPS was first granted to Haitians in 2010 after a catastrophic 7.0-magnitude earthquake killed an estimated 200,000 to 300,000 people and displaced millions. The program allows foreign nationals from designated crisis-ridden countries to live and work legally in the United States, provided conditions in their home country make safe return impossible.

Over the past 16 years, Haiti has lurched from one crisis to another: Hurricane Matthew in 2016, the assassination of President Jovenel Moïse in 2021, and an escalating gang war that now sees armed groups control an estimated 85% of Port-au-Prince. The U.S. State Department currently maintains a Level 4 "Do Not Travel" advisory for Haiti, citing kidnapping, crime, civil unrest, and poor healthcare infrastructure. Commercial flights to the capital's main airport were suspended after planes were struck by gunfire.

Despite these conditions, the Trump administration argues that Haiti has improved sufficiently to justify ending TPS, and that the program was always intended as temporary. In an emailed statement to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/23/nx-s1-5884947/haitians-protected-status-tps), a DHS spokesperson said: "For too long, TPS has been allowed to function as a de facto amnesty program. The Trump administration is restoring integrity to the immigration system."

## The Supreme Court Clears the Path

The Supreme Court ruled on June 25 that it had no power to review DHS termination decisions, effectively ending legal challenges that had blocked previous attempts to rescind TPS. Justice Elena Kagan dissented, citing racism as a factor in the administration's decision and referencing President Trump's inflammatory comments about Haitian immigrants. A lower federal court in D.C. is expected to implement the ruling on Monday, allowing immigration agents to begin arresting and detaining those who have lost their protections.

A bipartisan bill (H.R. 1689) that would extend TPS protections for Haitians through January 2029 passed the House in April with support from Florida Republicans including Reps. Carlos Gimenez, Maria Elvira Salazar, and Mario Diaz-Balart. But [Senate Republicans blocked the legislation](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/1689/all-actions) the week of July 20, leaving hundreds of thousands of people in legal limbo.

## Families Face Impossible Choices

For families with U.S.-born children, the end of TPS presents a cruel dilemma. Josiane Valsaint, a Haitian TPS holder in San Diego and mother of two American children ages 10 and 12, told NPR she cannot fathom taking them to Haiti. "Who's going to take care of my kiddos?" she asked. "They're not adults yet. They were born here." Her voice caught as she added: "We can't go back to Haiti."

Guerline Jozef, executive director of the Haitian Bridge Alliance, a San Diego-based nonprofit that supports Haitian immigrants, said family separation is the most likely outcome. "They cannot take those children back to Haiti," Jozef told NPR. "Not because Haiti is not beautiful. Not because there's no hope. But because right now, in July 2026, it is impossible for them to be safe there."

The DHS spokesperson insisted that "ICE does not separate families" and said parents can choose to be removed with their children or designate a caretaker. But advocates and TPS holders dispute this characterization, noting that removing children to a country in the grip of a gang war is itself a form of separation from safety.

## A Healthcare Crisis Brewing in Florida

Beyond the humanitarian toll, the end of TPS for Haitians threatens to cripple Florida's healthcare system. An estimated 35,000 Haitian TPS holders work in the state's healthcare sector as nurses, certified nursing assistants, and home health aides, according to [Mother Jones](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/07/floridas-nursing-homes-are-bracing-for-life-without-haitian-tps-workers/). Their annual economic contribution to Florida's economy is estimated at $2.6 billion.

Margarette Nerette, a vice president for 1199SEIU Florida, the state's largest healthcare worker union, said her phone has not stopped ringing. "I don't know how we're going to survive this," she told Mother Jones. "The workers are suffering, but the patients are going to suffer more." At one nursing home, roughly 30 of its 100 employees are TPS holders.

The Florida Health Care Association, which represents hundreds of assisted living facilities and nursing homes, has formally requested an exemption from DHS for Haitian TPS workers in healthcare, warning that losing them would "overwhelm our local hospitals and the entire healthcare system." Florida already ranks last in the nation for home health worker availability, according to the Home Care Association of Florida.

## Deportations Are Expected to Begin

Haiti's top migration official, Jean Négot Bonheur Delva, told local reporters that based on conversations with the U.S. embassy, Haiti expects approximately 1,000 deportees per month—about 250 people on each of two weekly flights, as reported by [The Haitian Times](https://haitiantimes.com/2026/07/21/us-informs-haiti-of-upcoming-deportations/). The Haitian government has not announced a national plan to receive or reintegrate returning citizens, and advocates have urged the administration to negotiate a suspension of deportations.

Nina, a Haitian TPS holder and certified nursing assistant in Florida who spoke on condition of anonymity, described the despair spreading through her community. "I try to keep a smile on my face, but my world is upside down," she said. "Right now is not the right time. Going back right now is a death sentence."

## What Lies Ahead

As Monday's deadline approaches, advocates are exploring legal options and urging Congress to revive the stalled TPS extension bill. Sui Chung, executive director of Americans for Immigrant Justice, described a pervasive "sense of terror and disbelief" among TPS holders who cannot understand how they can be returned to a country considered too dangerous for American travelers.

For Sandra, a Haitian TPS holder in San Diego whose teen son expressed suicidal thoughts over the prospect of being sent to Haiti, the waiting is unbearable. "I cried," she said. "I'm supposed to protect them, and I feel like I'm failing."

Whether through legislative action, further legal challenges, or the mercy of immigration enforcement discretion, the coming days will determine the fate of hundreds of thousands of people—and the American children who call this country their only home.
