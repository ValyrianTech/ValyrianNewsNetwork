# Correspondent: Ceuta Migrant Push Is an Organized Operation

Almost 49,000 migrants crossed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta in the space of 24 hours, and [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/correspondent-sven-tuytens-dit-is-een-georganiseerde-operatie~1785485718798/) correspondent Sven Tuytens says the mass movement was no accident. Reporting from the Morocco–Ceuta border, Tuytens describes an organized operation that Moroccan security services knowingly allowed — a diplomatic pressure tactic aimed at Spain and the European Union.

At least 19 people died during the surge, most of them drowning near the breakwater that marks the frontier, VRT NWS reported; BBC News put the toll at 18 and noted that officials did not rule out it rising. The influx, which began on 30 July, is already the largest recorded in the enclave's modern history. Ceuta, one of two Spanish cities on Africa's northern coast, has a population of roughly 83,600, according to [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cx2kp639yx4o) — meaning the arrivals could outnumber more than half of the city's residents. Officials estimate that at least 7,000 of those who crossed are minors.

## A Border Left Open

The defining detail, Tuytens says, is that the normally heavily guarded border post near the Moroccan town of Fnideq was simply open. "Also now it is an organized operation," he told VRT NWS. "Images show people arriving in trucks. The Moroccan security services knew what was happening and simply allowed it." His colleague Samira Jadir, reporting from Fnideq, said the whole city seemed empty at one point because so many people had headed for the crossing.

Rumors on social media and messaging platforms such as Discord and Facebook groups — claiming that the border had opened — helped draw thousands to Fnideq, VRT NWS reported, though where the rumors originated remained unclear. The scale of the movement overwhelmed Ceuta's reception system. Government leader Juan Vivas warned that there was "no more room for newcomers," with around 1,000 people waiting at a temporary reception center. Spain dispatched 60 soldiers to support the Guardia Civil, along with police reinforcements that included divers, a ship and drones. Several hundred migrants also entered Melilla, the EU's other African enclave, forcing Spain to close the Beni Enzar crossing — Melilla's only operational land border with Morocco.

## Morocco Reasserts Control

By Friday morning, Moroccan authorities had largely closed the door again. Forces deployed water-cannon vehicles and checkpoints around Fnideq and Bab Sebta, intercepting groups of mostly young men and busing some away from the border, [Hespress](https://en.hespress.com/142934-spain-says-49000-reached-ceuta-as-morocco-reinforces-border-amid-continued-crossings.html) reported. Overnight clashes left several vehicles torched. Hundreds of migrants who had already crossed into Ceuta began voluntarily returning to Morocco via the same breakwater route, with the flow shifting direction by Friday midday. Madrid and Rabat, the Moroccan outlet added, agreed to strengthen coordination and arrange "as soon as possible" the delivery and return of everyone who entered the enclave illegally.

The rapid reassertion of control is central to Tuytens' argument. "That is a clear signal," he said. "Morocco uses the migrants as a diplomatic pressure tool to get things done from Spain and the EU." Morocco, he noted, did nothing to stop people at the height of the surge and halted the flow only once the message had been sent.

## Competing Official Explanations

Spain and Morocco reject the suggestion of state orchestration. Spanish Interior Ministry sources told [Infobae](https://www.infobae.com/espana/agencias/2026/07/30/interior-se-alinea-con-marruecos-y-achaca-la-crisis-de-ceuta-a-las-redes-de-trafico-de-migrantes/) that human-trafficking networks were "instrumentalizing" a Supreme Court ruling of 8 July that bars the summary return — the so-called "hot returns" — of migrants who reach Ceuta or Melilla by sea, using false social-media messages to encourage mass crossings. Madrid described Morocco's cooperation as "exemplary," with Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska thanking Moroccan authorities for their "efforts." Morocco's embassy in Madrid, for its part, attributed the mass entries to "criminal organizations."

The gap between the official line and on-the-ground reporting has fueled a sharp European debate. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, who visited Ceuta on 31 July and was met with jeers from demonstrators, called the influx an "attack" and a "violation of Spain's territorial integrity." European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called the images "unacceptable" and assigned two Commissioners to support Spain, while EU Commissioner Magnus Brunner offered Frontex assistance. Italy and Finland went further, proposing that Spain be suspended from the Schengen zone; Italy later clarified that it meant border controls at the Italy–Spain border. Spain summoned the Italian ambassador, with Foreign Minister José Manuel Albares saying he expected "European solidarity and not partisan demagoguery" from an ally. France announced stronger controls at its border with Spain, while Sweden urged Madrid to restore control and the Netherlands called on Morocco to act.

## A Repeat of 2021?

For Tuytens and other analysts, the parallel with May 2021 is unmistakable. Then, Morocco allowed around 8,000 people to cross into Ceuta in two days after a dispute over Spain's decision to give medical treatment to Polisario Front leader Brahim Ghali, the BBC noted. That crisis ended only after Madrid shifted its position on the disputed Western Sahara, aligning more closely with Morocco's autonomy plan. The BBC also reported that Sánchez had recently traveled to Algeria — which backs Sahrawi independence — a move that may have re-angered Rabat.

The timing is also striking. Until 15 July, no migrant had reached Ceuta by sea in 2026; in all of 2025 there were only four such arrivals, and in 2024 there were 28, VRT NWS reported. The sudden appearance of thousands at the border within hours — some in trucks — therefore signals organization rather than spontaneity, even if the precise motive remains unconfirmed. "We just don't know yet exactly why Morocco is doing this," Tuytens acknowledged. Spanish media have framed the crisis around the "triangle" of Sánchez, Algeria and Morocco, suggesting the migration surge is being used as leverage in a longer diplomatic game.

The human cost is visible on both sides of the fence. "Not Morocco!" migrants shouted as they entered, many begging not to be sent back, [El País](https://english.elpais.com/spain/2026-07-31/not-morocco-thousands-of-migrants-enter-ceuta-and-beg-not-to-be-sent-back.html) reported. One returnee told Reuters: "People are dying here. I beg you, please, if you haven't come yet, don't come." Another migrant, 24-year-old Yassine, said he could not go back: "I have nothing there, Morocco is death."

## What to Watch

The immediate priority for Madrid is the return of the estimated 49,000 arrivals, a process that has already begun. Spain and Morocco have agreed on rapid returns, and Moroccan security forces have demonstrated that they can stop crossings when they choose to. The larger question is diplomatic: whether Rabat will use further pressure to extract concessions on Western Sahara — and whether the EU can maintain internal solidarity while its external border is tested at the continent's only land frontier with Africa.

The coming days will reveal whether the influx was a one-off signal or the start of a sustained campaign. For Ceuta, a city of 83,600 now hosting tens of thousands of additional people, the recovery will take far longer.
