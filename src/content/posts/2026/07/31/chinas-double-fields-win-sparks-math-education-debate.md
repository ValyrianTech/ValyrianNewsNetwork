# China's Double Fields Win Sparks Math Education Debate

On July 23, 2026, at the opening ceremony of the International Congress of Mathematicians (ICM) in Philadelphia, the International Mathematical Union announced the 2026 Fields Medal winners: Wang Hong, Deng Yu, John Pardon, and Jacob Tsimerman. For China, the result was historic: Wang and Deng became the first mathematicians holding Chinese nationality to receive the prize, and the first time two Chinese mathematicians won in the same cycle. The double win has set off a searching debate among Chinese educators about how to build on the achievement, [People's Daily reported](http://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2026/0731/c1004-40771323.html).

## A Historic First

The Fields Medal, often called the Nobel Prize of mathematics, is awarded every four years to at most four mathematicians under the age of 40. According to [ScienceNet](https://news.sciencenet.cn/htmlnews/2026/7/568755.shtm), the 2026 announcement marked a breakthrough for Chinese mathematics. Before Wang and Deng, only two mathematicians of Chinese descent had won the award—Shing-Tung Yau in 1982 and Terence Tao in 2006. Wang is also the third woman in the award's roughly 90-year history to become a Fields Medalist, after Maryam Mirzakhani and Maryna Viazovska.

The framing of the achievement differs across media. ScienceNet emphasized that Wang and Deng are the first mathematicians holding Chinese nationality to win, while international coverage such as [Yicai Global](https://www.yicaiglobal.com/news/chinese-mathematicians-wang-hong-deng-yu-win-2026-fields-medal) noted that Shing-Tung Yau remains "the first Chinese winner" of the Fields Medal, counting his 1982 prize. The difference reflects how outlets define nationality. The fact that both laureates completed their advanced training abroad—Wang earned her PhD at MIT, Deng at Princeton—has sharpened the discussion in China about whether its universities can eventually produce such breakthroughs at home.

## Breakthroughs Behind the Prize

Wang Hong, 35, was born in Guilin, Guangxi. She entered Peking University in 2007 through the national college entrance examination, initially in the School of Earth and Space Sciences, and transferred to the School of Mathematical Sciences in her second year. With Joshua Zahl of the University of British Columbia, she proved the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture, a century-old geometric problem about the space required to rotate a needle in all directions. The conjecture sits beneath several foundational questions in harmonic analysis, making its proof one of the most anticipated results in modern mathematics.

Deng Yu, 37, from Shenzhen, entered Peking University the same year as an International Mathematical Olympiad gold medalist admitted through the recommendation system. With collaborators Zaher Hani and Ma Xiao, he rigorously derived the Boltzmann equation from the hard-sphere dynamics of rarefied gases, a major step toward answering Hilbert's Sixth Problem, posed at the 1900 ICM. The work showed how the microscopic jostling of particles inevitably produces the irreversible behavior of fluids.

In a [ScienceNet interview](https://news.sciencenet.cn/htmlnews/2026/7/568774.shtm), Wang Hong reacted to the award with characteristic understatement: "Winning the award is nice. But problems I can't solve, I still can't solve." She described years as a self-described "nobody" at Peking University, a period of struggle that she credits with teaching her the value of persistence. After earning her PhD at MIT, she joined NYU's Courant Institute and was later appointed a tenured professor at the Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques—the first woman to hold a permanent professorship there.

## What the Double Win Means for Chinese Mathematics

For many in China, the significance runs deeper than two medals. Zhang Jiping, former dean of Peking University's School of Mathematical Sciences and an academician of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, called the result "a historic breakthrough for Chinese mathematics, a milestone." In the original People's Daily interview, Zhang noted that both winners had arrived at Peking University by different routes but shared an unusual capacity for sustained effort. He predicted that, with state support and patient cultivation, China could produce a Fields Medal winner trained entirely at home in less than 20 years.

The double win is also rare in the award's history. Only the United States, France, Britain, and Russia had previously produced two same-nationality laureates in a single cycle. A [Guangming Daily article carried by People's Daily Online](http://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2026/0726/c1004-40768034.html) pointed to the wider ecosystem behind the result: this year, 14 mathematicians affiliated with Peking University were invited to give 45-minute talks at the ICM, up from eight in 2018 and 13 in 2022. The article traced that success to decades of training reforms, small discussion groups, strong mentorship, and an unusual degree of freedom for students to choose their own paths.

## The Debate Over Domestic Training

The harder question is whether China can move from producing elite undergraduates to nurturing world-class research without relying on graduate training abroad. Wang Hong's formative doctorate was at MIT; Deng Yu transferred to MIT as an undergraduate and earned his PhD at Princeton. Zhang Jiping acknowledged the gap but insisted that the foundation laid at Peking University mattered deeply. He pointed to the earlier "Golden Generation" of graduates from the late 1990s and early 2000s and the newer "Platinum Generation" that includes Wang and Deng, saying the labels are less important than the encouragement they offer.

The interview also touched on persistent flaws in China's research culture. Zhang warned that "titles-only" and "papers-only" evaluation remains entrenched, and that honors should naturally follow first-class work rather than becoming targets in themselves. He called for wider channels for selecting young talent and for more international cooperation, arguing that mathematics is the most international of disciplines and cannot thrive in isolation.

## Analysis: A Milestone, Not a Finish Line

The double win is a landmark, but it also exposes how much of China's elite mathematical research is still produced outside the country. Both laureates did their advanced training abroad—Wang at MIT, Deng at Princeton—a pattern that underscores the distance yet to be covered. The challenge for Chinese institutions is to create environments where young researchers can take on high-risk, long-horizon problems—precisely the kind of work that produced the Kakeya proof and the Boltzmann equation derivation.

The response in China has been unusually self-aware. Rather than simply celebrating, the People's Daily interview asked directly what the achievement means for the future of Chinese mathematics. The emerging answer is twofold: sustained national investment in basic science has created a strong pipeline of undergraduates, but the ecosystem must now reward patience, originality, and international exchange over short-term publication metrics.

## What's Next

In the coming years, observers will watch whether China can translate its expanding undergraduate talent into independent research leadership. Zhang Jiping's prediction of a domestically trained Fields Medal winner within two decades offers a clear benchmark. So too does the question of whether reforms to evaluation, admissions, and international collaboration will follow the fanfare.

For mathematicians, the proof's completion is a new beginning rather than an ending: related conjectures in harmonic analysis now come into sharper view. If China's mathematics community takes the same stance, the 2026 double win may be remembered not as a peak but as the moment the climb began.
