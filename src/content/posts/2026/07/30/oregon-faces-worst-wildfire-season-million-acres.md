# Oregon Faces Worst Wildfire Season as 1.6 Million Acres Burn

Oregon is on track for its most destructive wildfire season in history, with more than 1.6 million acres already burned before the traditional peak of fire season has even arrived. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/29/nx-s1-5910624/oregon-is-on-track-for-its-worst-wildfire-season-yet), 30 large fires are actively burning across central and eastern Oregon, stretching firefighting resources to critical levels and forcing thousands of residents from their homes. The National Interagency Fire Center has declared Preparedness Level 5 — the highest possible — indicating that federal firefighting resources are nearly exhausted.

## A Season Shaped by Climate and Drought

The scale of the 2026 wildfire season reflects a convergence of environmental factors that scientists say are being amplified by climate change. Oregon recorded its lowest snowpack on record during the winter of 2025-2026, and persistent drought now affects nearly half of the state's counties. When a major lightning storm swept through central and eastern Oregon in mid-July, it ignited dozens of fires simultaneously — bringing little rain but strong winds that rapidly fanned the flames into massive, fast-moving fire complexes.

"When you get these warmer temperatures, basically the atmosphere just sucks all the moisture out of the landscape much quicker after winter, like during spring and summer," Larry O'Neill, Oregon's state climatologist at Oregon State University, told NPR. "We're holding our breath right now that the weather conditions will moderate, though that's not looking likely."

The fires are predominantly burning in rangeland and grassland areas rather than forests, fueled in part by invasive grasses that have increased fire risk across the region. August-level fire conditions were already present in July, and with the peak of wildfire season still ahead, officials warn that the situation could worsen significantly.

## Major Fires and Human Impact

Among the largest blazes are the Big Grass Fire (approximately 301,000 acres southwest of Jordan Valley), the Rowe Creek Complex (over 300,000 acres in Wheeler County), and the Hay Creek Complex (over 200,000 acres in north-central Oregon). The Akawa Butte Fire near Sisters has been designated the highest national priority, burning over 27,000 acres with only 5 percent containment.

The human toll is mounting rapidly. More than 33,000 Oregonians were under some level of evacuation order as of July 27, according to state officials. On the Warm Springs Reservation, the Bench Fire destroyed at least six homes and prompted emergency evacuations. Red Cross shelters have been opened in Madras and other affected communities, while major thoroughfares including Highway 26, Highway 20, and Interstate 84 have been closed intermittently, stranding travelers and cutting off access to affected areas.

For residents of rural communities, the fires are exacting a heavy economic toll. Mary Thompson, a resident of Harper near the Coleman Creek Fire, described the anguish of watching a lifetime of work face destruction. "It's hard, it's really hard," she told NPR. "But I don't know what they're going to do once the fires are over with, 'cause there won't be any grounds to feed 'em."

Terry Barnes, a stranded camper at the Owyhee Reservoir, echoed the sense of dislocation. "We've certainly never been trapped where we couldn't go home," he said.

Catherine Randall, evacuated from the town of Huntington as the Tartar Fire approached, described the painful calculus of fleeing with only what she could carry. "I just grabbed my computer and a couple other things. I figure you know you take a quick look around and it's either going to be there when you get back or it's not," she said.

## Political Pressures and Federal Funding Disputes

The wildfire crisis is unfolding against a backdrop of political tension over federal firefighting support. Oregon Governor Tina Kotek, a Democrat, declared a statewide wildfire emergency on June 16, well before the current fires exploded, and has invoked the Emergency Conflagration Act more than ten times in recent weeks.

However, FEMA has approved only three of five federal Fire Management Assistance Grant requests from Oregon, denying aid for the East Evans Creek Fire and the Rowe Creek Complex. Oregon's U.S. Senators, Ron Wyden and Jeff Merkley, have demanded that FEMA explain its criteria by August 10, and the state has 30 days to appeal the denials.

The funding dispute comes amid broader cuts to federal land management agencies. Since 2025, the Trump administration's DOGE team has eliminated approximately 10,000 positions at the U.S. Forest Service and Bureau of Land Management, including wildfire prevention staff — reductions that critics say are now compounding the crisis.

State Fire Marshal Mariana Ruiz-Temple has urged vigilance, noting that "the last two and a half weeks have been extremely challenging for our firefighters and support staff." Jessica Neujahr of the Oregon Department of Forestry added that while the state is at "all hands on deck" status, "there's only so many firefighters that we have."

## Approaching Historic Records

With 1.6 million acres already burned — more than five times the total for the entire 2025 season of 324,000 acres — Oregon is rapidly approaching the all-time record of 1.9 million acres set in 2024. The 10-year annual average is approximately 680,000 acres, meaning the current season has more than doubled that figure before August.

Dense smoke from the fires has prompted air quality advisories as far away as the Boise, Idaho metropolitan area, home to more than 700,000 people. The economic damage is expected to be severe, particularly for ranching communities that rely on federal grazing land — much of which is now charred and may take years to recover.

## What to Watch For

Oregon's traditional wildfire season typically extends through August and September, and forecasters see no immediate relief. The Pacific Northwest is also bracing for one of the strongest El Niño events on record, which could further exacerbate dry conditions.

O'Neill, the state climatologist, warned that the immediate forecast offers no reprieve. The same conditions driving Oregon's fires — warmer temperatures sucking moisture from the landscape — are being observed from the Pacific Northwest to Western Europe, suggesting a pattern that may intensify in the years ahead.

As the fires continue to burn, the central questions remain: Can Oregon's firefighting force sustain this pace through September without critical burnout? Will the political debate over federal land management and climate policy shift in the wake of what is shaping up to be a record-shattering season? And for the thousands of evacuated Oregonians waiting to return home, the answer cannot come soon enough.
