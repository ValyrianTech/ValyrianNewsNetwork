# Iowa's Water Crisis Becomes Central Issue in Governor's Race

Water contamination and rising cancer rates have emerged as defining issues in Iowa's 2026 gubernatorial election, reshaping a political landscape long dominated by agricultural interests. Both Democratic nominee Rob Sand and Republican nominee Zach Lahn have made water quality central to their campaigns, reflecting growing voter concern over nitrate pollution from farm runoff and its potential link to the state's elevated cancer rates.

## Background: A State in Crisis

Iowa has the second-highest rate of new cancer cases in the United States behind only Kentucky, and is one of only a few states where cancer rates continue to rise. The 2026 annual report from the Iowa Cancer Registry confirms this ranking. The state recorded 36 nitrate-related drinking water violations in 2025, more than double the number reported in 2024, according to the Iowa Department of Natural Resources as reported by [Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/health/2026-03-13/annual-report-finds-iowa-cancer-rates-remain-some-of-the-highest-in-the-nation).

Nitrate, a compound formed when nitrogen fertilizer breaks down in soil, has been linked to multiple health problems including cancer and blue baby syndrome. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's legal limit for nitrate in drinking water is 10 milligrams per liter, though some researchers argue this standard is outdated. Agriculture accounts for roughly 93% of the total nitrogen in Iowa's waterways, a direct consequence of the state's agricultural dominance where approximately 85% of land is used for row-crop farming.

## Key Developments: Nitrate Spikes and Water Bans

For two consecutive summers, nitrate levels in the Des Moines and Raccoon Rivers spiked above the federal limit, triggering lawn watering bans affecting approximately 600,000 people in the Des Moines metro area. All restrictions were lifted in late July 2026 after nitrate concentrations dropped below the EPA limit, but Central Iowa Water Works has announced plans to expand its nitrate removal facility, as detailed by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/27/nx-s1-5900895/water-cancer-iowa-governor-midterms-democrats-sand-maha-lahn).

The Iowa Nutrient Reduction Strategy, a voluntary program launched in 2013, aimed to reduce nitrogen and phosphorus loads by 45% compared to 1980-1996 baseline levels. However, adoption of key conservation practices remains low — only about 17% of crop acres use cover crops, and nitrogen levels show no significant change after more than a decade, according to research covered by NPR.

## The Political Landscape: A Cross-Party Issue

Democratic candidate Rob Sand, currently serving as state auditor, has proposed tax incentives for landowners to convert acreage into wetlands, which naturally filter water before it reaches rivers and streams. He has also called for funding the Iowa Water and Land Legacy (IWILL) fund, a conservation trust fund approved by voters in 2010 that has never received legislative appropriations in 16 years. Sand participated in a cancer roundtable with survivors and advocates in Des Moines on July 28, where he discussed radon mitigation, tobacco taxes, and state funding for cancer research, as reported by [Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/health/2026-07-28/democrat-rob-sand-iowa-cancer-rates-governor-race).

Republican nominee Zach Lahn, a farmer who won a crowded GOP primary in June by defeating Trump-backed Congressman Randy Feenstra, has made water quality and cancer rates central themes of his campaign. Lahn was endorsed by the MAHA-PAC, a super PAC aligned with Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s "Make America Healthy Again" movement. Politico reported that the water quality issue is blurring traditional party lines in Iowa's competitive open-seat race, as covered in their in-depth feature [Politico](https://www.politico.com/news/magazine/2026/07/19/iowa-water-democrats-agriculture-00992753).

Both candidates have pledged to fund the IWILL trust fund, a position that signals shifting political calculus on environmental issues in a state that has marched to the right over the past decade, with a Republican-controlled state legislature.

## Analysis: The Lobe Rangers and the Limits of Voluntary Action

A nonpartisan nonprofit called the Lobe Rangers, founded by three north-central Iowa farmers — Zack Smith, Matt Bormann, and James Hepp — has been advocating for stronger water quality regulations. The group argues that the state's reliance on voluntary conservation measures has failed to produce meaningful results. Their name references the Des Moines Lobe, a flat, tongue-shaped region in north-central Iowa where roughly 80% of land is used for intensive row-crop agriculture.

At their first annual field day near Rockwell City on July 25, attended by approximately 200 people including lawmakers, water utility representatives, and candidates, the Lobe Rangers outlined their position. Co-founder Zack Smith stated that 13 years after the Nutrient Reduction Strategy launched, adoption rates remain insufficient to achieve targeted reductions, and that relying on voluntary change alone has not delivered results.

The Lobe Rangers support funding the IWILL fund and creating a system of incentives and disincentives, where farmers who opt out of certain conservation practices would provide funding for those who adopt them. As documented by [Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/agriculture/2026-07-28/lobe-rangers-water-quality-nutrient-reduction-strategy), the group has built an online following through videos about agriculture's role in protecting water quality and has agreed to meet with both gubernatorial candidates without making an endorsement.

Matthew Helmers, director of the Iowa Nutrient Research Center at Iowa State University, noted at the field day that achieving the state's nutrient reduction targets would require approximately 60% of corn and soybean acres to adopt cover crops, along with millions of acres treated by wetlands, saturated buffers, and bioreactors. Current adoption levels remain far below those targets.

## Implications and Forward Look

Iowa's gubernatorial race may serve as a bellwether for how water quality and environmental health issues play in agricultural states. The emergence of a Republican candidate championing water quality, traditionally a Democratic issue, signals potential shifts in voter priorities and party alignments influenced by the MAHA movement.

David Cwiertny, director of the University of Iowa's Center for Health Effects of Environmental Contamination, has noted that the EPA has acknowledged the science linking elevated nitrate levels to long-term health risks but has not updated its drinking water standards. If the EPA revises its standards, it would have significant implications for Iowa's water utilities and agricultural practices.

The general election in November will determine whether the next governor can translate campaign promises into legislative outcomes in a state where voluntary conservation approaches have dominated for more than a decade and where the gap between voter intent and legislative action — exemplified by the 16-year unfunded IWILL trust fund — remains wide.
