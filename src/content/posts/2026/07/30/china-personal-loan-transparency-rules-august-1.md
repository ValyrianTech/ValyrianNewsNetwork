# China's New Personal Loan Rules Mandate Full Cost Disclosure

New regulations requiring full transparency in personal loan pricing take effect across China on August 1, mandating that all lending institutions present borrowers with a clear, itemized breakdown of every cost associated with their loans. The rules, jointly issued by the National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) and the People's Bank of China (PBOC) in March, mark a significant shift toward consumer protection in the country's multi-trillion-yuan personal lending market, as reported by [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20260730/63599f1e5f2442c6bd6f435e3d6a6096/c.html).

## Why the Rules Were Needed

China's personal loan market has grown rapidly in recent years, fueled by fintech platforms, consumer finance companies, and internet-assisted lending. However, this growth exposed widespread issues of opaque pricing. Some lenders advertised low nominal interest rates while burying guarantee fees, service fees, and insurance charges in complex fee structures, making it nearly impossible for ordinary borrowers to calculate the true cost of borrowing. In auto finance and home renovation lending, "zero interest" marketing often masked high handling fees that pushed effective costs far above advertised rates.

According to [regulators' own assessment](https://www.nfra.gov.cn/cn/view/pages/ItemDetail.html?docId=1251645&itemId=915&generaltype=0), these practices "easily trigger financial consumption disputes, affect the effectiveness of interest rate policy, and weaken the quality and efficiency of financial services to the real economy."

## What the Regulation Requires

Formally titled the Regulations on Explicit Comprehensive Financing Cost for Personal Loan Business, the rules require every lender to present borrowers with a standardized financing cost disclosure table before a loan contract is signed. The table must itemize the loan principal, all interest and fees, the entity collecting each charge, and the annualized cost using the internal rate of return (IRR) method established by the PBOC. It must also disclose contingent costs such as overdue penalties and default interest.

For in-person loan processing, borrowers must sign the disclosure table before signing the loan contract. For online lending, the table must appear in a pop-up window with a mandatory reading period, and borrowers must confirm before proceeding. Under the "new loans, new rules" principle, only loans originated after August 1 are subject to the requirements; existing loans are grandfathered.

The regulation applies across all lending channels — commercial banks, consumer finance companies, auto finance firms, trust companies, micro-lenders, and other regulated institutions — covering consumer loans, mortgages, business loans, auto loans, and decoration loans.

## Financial Institutions Prepare for Compliance

Banks across China have been racing to overhaul their systems and processes ahead of the deadline. Hengfeng Bank (Evergrowing Bank) announced on July 24 that it will implement full compliance from August 1, requiring all personal loan customers to sign the new disclosure table. Jilin Bank published its disclosure template with a ceiling of 18% annualized for normal-performance comprehensive financing costs. Zheshang Bank developed an independent calculation engine to ensure its cost estimates match official benchmarks, and has implemented pop-up displays with mandatory reading timers.

China Post Consumer Finance, a major consumer finance company, recently published its Comprehensive Financing Cost Disclosure Table and explicitly stated in its announcement that "the comprehensive annualized financing cost disclosed in this table truly reflects the full cost of this loan; there is no suppressed interest rate, split fees, hidden costs, or other misleading practices."

## Expert Views: A Reshaping of Market Competition

Analysts say the regulation marks a fundamental shift in China's lending landscape. Lou Feipeng, a researcher at China Postal Savings Bank, noted in comments carried by [Securities Daily](https://finance.sina.com.cn/jjxw/2026-07-27/doc-inikevrp3213847.shtml) that in the short term, institutions need IT system upgrades and contract revisions, while tech platforms must redesign fee models "from traffic monetization to value-based service fees." In the long run, he said, the market will favor "institutions that can provide real value, price risk accurately, operate efficiently, and have independent risk control."

Wang Pengbo, chief analyst at BOTONG Consulting, said the transparency of costs will standardize industry competition. "The past approach of relying on marketing rhetoric to attract customers will be constrained," he said. "In the future, the focus of competition among institutions will shift more toward risk control capabilities, customer service, and product experience."

Tian Lihui, a finance professor at Nankai University, identified two scenarios where cost opacity has been most acute: internet-assisted lending, where complex fee structures hide true costs, and auto finance and renovation lending, where "zero interest" gimmicks mask high fees. He argued the regulation will reshape "the competitive logic of the personal loan market," forcing a shift from information asymmetry toward refined pricing and differentiated service.

Dong Ximiao, chief economist at China Merchants Bank, said the rules will "effectively curb hidden fees and unreasonable price hikes, forcing financial institutions and lending platforms to further optimize services and enhance capabilities, returning to healthy competition."

## Broader Implications

The regulation is part of a broader push by Chinese regulators to strengthen consumer financial protection. It builds on the PBOC's 2021 requirement for annualized interest rate disclosure and the NFRA's 2024 Personal Loan Management Measures, along with 2025 rules tightening oversight of internet-assisted lending.

Industry observers point to several potential long-term effects: industry consolidation favoring efficient operators with genuine cost advantages; more informed consumer borrowing decisions; and a possible template for transparency requirements in other financial products. Open questions remain about enforcement consistency across provinces and whether smaller institutions will have the capacity to comply fully.

As August 1 approaches, China's personal loan market is poised to enter what analysts describe as an era of "comprehensive financing cost transparency," where borrowers for the first time have a unified yardstick to measure the true price of credit.
