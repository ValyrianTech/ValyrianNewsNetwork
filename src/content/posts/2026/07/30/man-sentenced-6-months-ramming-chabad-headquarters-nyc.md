# Man Sentenced to 6 Months for Ramming Chabad Headquarters in NYC

A New Jersey man has been sentenced to six months and one week in prison for deliberately driving his car into the Chabad Lubavitch World Headquarters in Brooklyn, a case that has ignited debate over whether the attack was a hate crime or the desperate act of a man struggling with mental illness. Dan Sohail, 36, of Carteret, New Jersey, was sentenced on July 29 in Brooklyn federal court and is expected to be released in approximately one week, having already served six months in pretrial detention.

## The Attack

On January 28, 2026, shortly before 9:00 p.m. local time, Sohail drove a 2011 Honda Accord to a side entrance of the Chabad Lubavitch World Headquarters at 770 Eastern Parkway in the Crown Heights neighborhood of Brooklyn. According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/man-sentences-ramming-chabad-lubavitch-rcna589937), he exited his vehicle, removed barriers set up to protect the site, waved passersby away, and then rammed the entrance. He reversed and drove into the doorway four more times — five impacts in total — knocking the door off its hinges and destroying his car’s front bumper.

The NYPD bomb squad found no explosives or weapons in the vehicle. Remarkably, no one was injured, despite the building being busy with people gathered for prayer at the time. NYPD officers already stationed nearby took Sohail into custody at the scene.

The attack occurred on Yud Shvat, the 75th anniversary of Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson’s assumption of leadership of the Lubavitch movement — a significant Chabad holiday with elevated attendance at the headquarters, as noted in the [Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Chabad_headquarters_ramming_attack) on the incident.

## Sentencing and the Court’s Reasoning

Sohail pleaded guilty in mid-May 2026 to one count of intentionally damaging religious property under federal statute 18 U.S.C. § 247, which carries a maximum penalty of three years in prison and a $250,000 fine. Prosecutors had sought an additional eight months beyond time served — for a total of 14 months — arguing that a stiffer sentence was necessary as a deterrent amid a dramatic surge in antisemitic hate crimes.

In their court filings, prosecutors noted that "a staggering" 80 of 146 hate crime incidents across New York City since the start of 2026 were based on crimes against Jews and Jewish institutions.

Judge Eric N. Vitaliano, however, declined the prosecution’s request. Citing a psychiatric report, the judge stated that Sohail’s mental health challenges "played an important role" in his decision-making. He also ordered Sohail to pay $20,000 in restitution.

Vitaliano said he was "somewhat saddened" to see that the Justice Department wanted him to be influenced at sentencing by what he described as a "mistaken initial impression among the wider public that the Jan. 28 attack was a hate crime." The judge noted that prosecutors ultimately charged Sohail with damaging religious property — not a hate crime — and criticized the government for relying on public perception rather than the actual charges.

## Hate Crime or Mental Health Crisis?

The case has highlighted a complex intersection of criminal intent, mental illness, and rising antisemitism. Defense attorney Mia Eisner-Grynberg described the attack as "an irrational cry for help," painting a portrait of a man who had been seeking belonging within the Jewish community. According to court papers, Sohail had attended Chabad events, found the rabbinical students "warm and welcoming," and had even danced with them. He followed their encouragement to visit Israel but became erratic and extremely anxious upon his return.

Meanwhile, authorities had initially investigated the incident as a possible hate crime. According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/chabad-headquarters-car-crash-new-york-06e8ef680526ec95ea33276cdf293f7e), NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch called the headquarters "a sacred place for many Jews in New York City and around the world," and the attack prompted enhanced security around houses of worship citywide.

## Broader Context and Historical Significance

The Chabad Lubavitch World Headquarters is the central site of the Chabad movement, one of the largest Hasidic Jewish organizations globally, with replicas of its distinctive red-brick facade found worldwide. The site has a history of violence: it was at the epicenter of the 1991 Crown Heights riots, and in 2014, a disturbed man entered the synagogue and stabbed a rabbinical student before being shot dead by police.

The leniency of Sohail’s sentence — substantially below both the statutory maximum and the prosecution’s recommendation — has drawn mixed reactions. Critics may argue that a six-month sentence for deliberately ramming a car into a synagogue five times on a major religious holiday is far too lenient given the broader context of rising antisemitic attacks. Supporters of the sentence may contend that the mental health evidence justifies a treatment-focused approach rather than punitive incarceration.

## What’s Next

Sohail is expected to be released from custody within the coming week. The $20,000 restitution payment will go to the Chabad Lubavitch Headquarters for damages sustained during the attack. The case continues to fuel broader debate about how the justice system balances deterrence, accountability, and compassion when hate crimes and mental illness intersect — a debate with no easy answers as antisemitic incidents continue to rise across the United States.
