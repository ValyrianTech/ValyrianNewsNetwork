# Trump Touts Tariffs in Michigan; Costs Worry His Supporters

MILFORD, Mich. — President Donald Trump traveled to the General Motors Proving Ground in Milford, Michigan, on July 27 to hold a rally touting his tariff policies as a triumph for American manufacturing. But behind the enthusiastic crowd and the president's rosy declarations, federal data and even the concerns of some of his own supporters paint a far more complicated picture.

Speaking ahead of Michigan's August 4 primary, Trump declared that "Michigan is thriving" and that "car companies are doing better than they've ever done" thanks to his trade measures, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-michigan-midterms-tariffs-automakers-ae38aca90cb320f6bb8fd778f2c90bea). Yet the reality on the ground tells a different story.

## The Gap Between Rhetoric and Reality

Since Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs were announced in April 2025, Michigan has lost 8,300 manufacturing jobs, according to Bureau of Labor Statistics data. Auto parts manufacturing employment in the state has declined by approximately 4,000 jobs year-over-year through June 2026 — a 3.5% drop — only partially offset by the addition of 500 vehicle manufacturing jobs.

Nationally, auto employment fell from 971,000 jobs in June 2025 to 946,600 in June 2026. General Motors, which hosted the president at its proving ground, reported $2.7 billion in profits for 2025 — a sharp decline from the $10 billion it earned in 2023. GM's U.S. sales of 2.8 million vehicles in 2025 remained below the 3 million mark it consistently hit in the mid-2010s.

Trump claimed the trade deficit had fallen by 71%, but the most recent data shows the deficit is actually up nearly 14% year-over-year as of May 2026.

## Rising Costs Hit Home

Inflation has moderated to 3.5% nationally as of June 2026, but prices in the Detroit region are up 4% year-over-year. Midwestern consumers are feeling the pinch: food prices are up 3%, housing costs have risen 3.3%, and fuel has climbed 4.6%. The national average gas price jumped 15 cents, with most states now averaging over $4 per gallon — a situation compounded by the ongoing military conflict with Iran.

Energy costs are another pressure point. According to the Michigan Advance, 82% of Michigan's electric customers — roughly 4.2 million people — are paying more due to the combined effect of tariffs and cancellations of federal grants.

## Even the Faithful Feel the Strain

At the rally, the crowd was adoring. But interviews with attendees revealed a more nuanced sentiment.

Lisa Scherer, a 64-year-old former business owner who drove five hours from Ohio to attend, acknowledged that high gas prices are "hurting a lot" and that it is "very rough" to afford things. Living on $1,100 per month in Social Security benefits, she said of Trump: "He's forgot about the people on a fixed income. But I look at it like, I guess, no pain, no gain."

Roy Parks, a 49-year-old handyman from Milford, echoed the sentiment: "You may not like it at first, but it's a short-term pinch, short-term pain, but long-term good. It works out in the end."

Joe Miskovich, 57, who owns an aerospace business, urged patience: "The American public needs to look at the bigger picture. Yes, granted, there might be a little higher increase in price. But the long-term effect is going to be absolutely positive."

Not all supporters expressed worries. Rose Stroud, 75, of Commerce, Michigan, declared: "Trump is, like, No. 1. He's the best. He thinks about the people, about what they need."

But the pattern of supporters simultaneously defending the president while acknowledging economic pain signals a potential vulnerability as the November midterms approach.

## Political Stakes in a Battleground State

Michigan remains a critical swing state. Trump won it in 2016 and again in 2024, but Biden carried the state in 2020 by 154,188 votes. The August 4 primary will test whether Trump's endorsements carry weight with voters feeling economic strain.

Trump has endorsed Congressman John James for governor and former Representative Mike Rogers for Senate. The Democratic Senate primary between Representative Haley Stevens and progressive Abdul El-Sayed has drawn national attention.

Veteran Michigan pollster Bernie Porn offered a blunt assessment, telling AP: "When I was listening to him, I said, 'What planet are you on?'" Porn characterized Trump as "an anchor around Republicans," warning that some Republican voters may lack motivation to turn out in November. "I doubt if Michigan is going to be very different," he said.

Michigan Democratic Party Chair Curtis Hertel was more direct: "Michiganders are tired of Donald Trump and his Republican allies making America more expensive, less prosperous, and less secure."

## The Trade Policy Paradox

The core tension in Trump's trade strategy is difficult to ignore. His tariffs aim to revive domestic manufacturing by penalizing foreign imports, yet they have simultaneously raised costs for consumers and businesses while disrupting the deeply integrated cross-border supply chains that Michigan's economy depends on.

Michigan's supply chains are heavily intertwined with Canada — a relationship built over decades of trade barrier reductions. The USMCA, negotiated during Trump's first term, was allowed to lapse. Now, the administration has invoked the Tariff Act of 1930 to threaten 50% tariffs on many Canadian imports.

Some automakers are responding with long-term investments. GM announced a $4 billion plan to shift production from Mexico to the U.S. starting in 2027. Ford is building a $3 billion BlueOval Battery Park, though it currently employs only 500 workers. Toyota has pledged $3.6 billion to move Tacoma production from Mexico to Texas over four years. But these benefits remain years away.

## A Defining Test for Trump's Economic Message

The Michigan rally underscored a fundamental question heading into the midterms: Can Trump's tariff-centric economic message hold together when the data — and the lived experience of many voters — tells a different story?

For now, many supporters remain willing to trust the president's vision, framing higher prices as necessary short-term pain for long-term gain. But with the August 4 primary serving as an early gauge of voter sentiment, the answer may soon become clearer. The question of whether patience will outlast pocketbook pain could define not just Michigan's elections, but the broader political landscape heading into November.
