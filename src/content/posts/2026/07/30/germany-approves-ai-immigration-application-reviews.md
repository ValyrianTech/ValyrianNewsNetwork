# Germany Approves AI for Immigration Application Reviews

The German Cabinet under Chancellor Friedrich Merz approved a draft bill on July 29, 2026, that would permit immigration authorities to use artificial intelligence to assist in processing asylum, visa, and residence permit applications. The proposed [KI-Migrationsverwaltungsgesetz (KIMVG)](https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-de/aktuelles/kuenstliche-intelligenz-asylverfahren-2448638) — or AI Migration Management Act — aims to address persistent backlogs and staffing shortages that have long strained Germany’s immigration system.

## Context and Background

Germany’s migration administration has faced “a steadily increasing volume of applications for years,” according to the draft bill, with existing personnel and organizational capacities proving insufficient to process procedures within reasonable time frames. The legislation builds on a key points paper published by the German government in November 2025 on the use of AI in visa and migration procedures.

The bill comes just weeks after the EU’s comprehensive asylum reform took effect on June 12, 2026, introducing stricter rules including faster asylum procedures and more consistent deportations. This German AI legislation can be seen as a national complement to those EU-wide reforms.

## What the Law Would Allow

Under the proposed law, three key authorities — the Federal Office for Migration and Refugees (BAMF), local immigration offices, and the Foreign Office — would be authorized to deploy AI systems. Authorities could use application data to train, validate, and test AI models, and completed cases could be analyzed across files to identify decision patterns.

The legislation would permit AI to compare applicant statements against publicly available online information when there are “reasonable doubts” about the accuracy of claims. However, the German government has emphasized that processing of insights from the “core area of private life” is prohibited, with any such recordings required to be deleted immediately.

## Human Oversight Maintained

The German government has stressed that AI serves only as a support tool. Vice Government Spokesman Steffen Meyer stated that the goal is “to make asylum and residence administrative procedures more efficient, uniform, higher quality, and safer.” The government insists that individual reviews and final decisions remain entirely the responsibility of human caseworkers, and that discriminatory algorithms are to be excluded.

## Civil Society Criticism

The draft has drawn sharp criticism from human rights organizations. Wiebke Judith, legal expert at Pro Asyl, called the legislative proposal “highly dangerous,” arguing that fundamental rights, transparency and effective legal protection are not sufficiently safeguarded. She warned that “especially when it comes to protection from persecution, the state must not rely on intransparent, algorithmically controlled decision-making processes.”

Algorithm Watch, a nonprofit that monitors algorithmic systems, accused the government of using people in migration procedures as “guinea pigs” for untested AI systems with high error rates. The German Caritasverband warned about risks of errors when using publicly accessible online data, while municipal associations welcomed the goal of relieving pressure on immigration offices but called the government’s cost calculations unrealistic and demanded financial guarantees for training and IT infrastructure.

## Broader Implications

The proposed law is among the first national-level legislation in Europe explicitly authorizing AI in migration management, potentially setting a precedent for other countries grappling with similar challenges. The law must comply with EU data protection and non-discrimination frameworks, which critics argue it fails to do adequately.

For asylum seekers and migrants, the legislation could mean faster processing times and reduced uncertainty — but also raises risks of AI-driven errors or biases affecting life-changing decisions, along with privacy concerns stemming from automated internet checks.

## What’s Next

The draft bill now proceeds to the parliamentary process, with debate expected in the Bundestag and Bundesrat in autumn 2026. The legislation is almost certain to face legal challenges from civil society organizations on constitutional and human rights grounds, potentially reaching the German Federal Constitutional Court.

As reported by [CCTV News](https://news.cctv.com/2026/07/30/ARTICtaTNrdGzLugxIGJ1p85260730.shtml), the proposal represents a significant step in Germany’s ongoing efforts to modernize public administration — but the debate over where efficiency ends and fundamental rights begin is far from over.
