# Wisconsin EF3 Tornado Causes Widespread Damage, No Deaths

A powerful EF3 tornado tore through northeastern Wisconsin's Fox Cities region on Monday, July 27, leaving a 12-mile path of destruction across Appleton, Menasha, Neenah, and Fox Crossing. Remarkably, no fatalities have been reported — a outcome officials attribute to the storm's early afternoon timing, when most residents were awake and able to take shelter after warnings had been issued.

According to [the Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/wisconsin-tornado-weather-c528fec31f227f13721f92fb7d93194e), the tornado touched down around 1 p.m. CDT and remained on the ground for more than 20 minutes, packing estimated wind speeds of up to 140 mph. The National Weather Service classified it as an EF3 — the strongest tornado to strike Outagamie and Winnebago counties since an EF4 touched down in April 1984.

## Devastation Across the Fox Cities

The tornado cut through a densely populated region home to more than 100,000 people, approximately 100 miles northwest of Milwaukee. Hundreds of buildings were damaged or destroyed, including homes, schools, and a fire station. Over 10,000 customers lost power, with roughly 5,100 We Energies customers affected in Winnebago County and about 5,700 in Outagamie County as of Tuesday evening.

Dozens of people were injured. ThedaCare Regional Medical Centers in Appleton and Neenah treated 40 patients, while Ascension St. Elizabeth Hospital in Appleton cared for approximately 30, all with minor injuries. Gold Cross Ambulance transported 60 people to hospitals across the region.

“The depth of the tornado and its impact is incredible, devastating. We’re really still at the assessment stage,” Winnebago County Executive Gordon Hintz said at a news conference Tuesday.

## A Matter of Minutes

Officials credit the storm’s timing with preventing loss of life. The National Weather Service in Green Bay issued a tornado warning at approximately 11:59 a.m., just moments before the twister touched down. By that hour, most residents were awake, at work, or otherwise alert — a stark contrast to nighttime tornadoes, which historically carry higher fatality rates.

“Everybody was at work. It was the middle of the day,” Menasha Police Chief Matt Albrecht told reporters. “Most people were not at home and at their place of employment.”

Emergency crews conducted door-to-door searches Monday and Tuesday to ensure no one was trapped in the rubble. A curfew was imposed in Menasha and Fox Crossing, and a boil-water order was issued for parts of those communities due to damage to the water utility infrastructure.

## State of Emergency Declared

Gov. Tony Evers declared a state of emergency on Tuesday, July 28, mobilizing state resources to support the recovery effort. Shelters were opened for displaced residents at the former UW-Oshkosh Fox Cities campus.

“Together, we will recover and recover stronger,” Evers said. “I ask everyone to keep the families and communities affected in your thoughts.”

Utility crews from across the Midwest began arriving to restore power, though officials warned it could take several days to fully restore electricity. Appleton Mayor Jake Woodford noted that downed power lines tangled in debris made restoration a slow and hazardous process.

## An Unusual Tornado Season

The Fox Cities tornado is the fourth EF3 tornado to strike Wisconsin in 2026 — the most recorded in a single year since 1984. Wisconsin has seen 43 tornadoes so far this year, surpassing the 2025 total of 39. Three EF3 tornadoes hit in April 2026 alone.

Amanda Latham, a climate outreach specialist with the Wisconsin State Climatology Office, noted that severe tornadoes are rare in the state. “An EF-2 is still plenty strong, but when we get into EF-3 or greater territory, that’s when we see this unfortunate, really widespread, devastating damage,” she told Wisconsin Public Radio.

AccuWeather senior meteorologist Chad Merrill linked the severe weather to broader climatic patterns. “What we are seeing this summer is significant heat leading to significant severe weather,” Merrill said. “People need to take these warnings seriously. It can happen just about anywhere.”

## Community Rallies in Aftermath

Despite the widespread destruction, stories of community resilience emerged. Manderfield’s Home Bakery, a decades-old Menasha landmark, was destroyed, but owner Jerry Manderfield struck a resolute tone: “This can be replaced. We had people in the building that could possibly have died in there.”

Luigi’s Pizza in Menasha set up a food trailer to distribute free pizza and hygiene packs after donors contributed enough to make at least 125 pizzas. Local businesses including Fleet Farm and Piggly Wiggly distributed bottled water to residents without safe drinking water.

## Looking Ahead

Recovery is expected to take weeks to months as damaged buildings are assessed, power is restored, and displaced residents find temporary housing. Outagamie County Executive Tom Nelson pledged the community would remain committed to the long-term effort.

“We are here for the long haul, and we’ll do all we can to help our neighbors,” Nelson said.

The Wisconsin tornado season continues, with experts warning that the combination of heat, humidity, and favorable wind patterns could produce further severe weather before summer ends. Meteorologists urge residents to remain alert and to heed warnings when issued.
