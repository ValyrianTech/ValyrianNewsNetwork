# Cockpit Alarms Sound During Delta Near Miss in Atlanta

Two Delta Air Lines flights came dangerously close to colliding at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport late Monday, triggering anti-collision alarms in both cockpits and prompting an investigation by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA).

The incident involved Delta Flight 1568 — a Boeing 737-900ER inbound from Pittsburgh — and Delta Flight 2472, an Airbus A321-200 departing for Rochester, New York. According to [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/us/close-call-atlanta-airport-forces-delta-anti-collision-alarms-sound), the FAA confirmed that the departing flight crossed in front of the arriving aircraft, which had initiated a go-around after another plane was slow to exit the runway.

## Background and Disruption

The near miss unfolded after an already disrupted evening for operations at the world's busiest airport. Flight 1568 had originally departed Pittsburgh but was forced to divert to Chattanooga, Tennessee, earlier that evening due to severe weather. It resumed its journey later that night, according to data from Flightradar24.

As the flight approached Atlanta, the pilot asked the tower, "Somebody on the runway?" and was told the aircraft was exiting. Concerned, the pilot initiated a go-around — a standard safety procedure in which an approaching aircraft aborts its landing and climbs to circle for another attempt.

## The Close Call

During the go-around, Flight 2472 departed from a parallel runway and made a right turn, crossing directly in front of the inbound Boeing 737. The Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS) sounded warnings in both cockpits as the aircraft came within approximately 1,800 feet horizontally and 600 feet vertically of each other.

[ATC audio obtained by CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/atlanta/news/delta-flight/) captured a pilot exclaiming "My goodness" as the alarms blared. Air traffic controllers quickly directed the aircraft away from each other. Flight 1568 landed safely after circling north of the airport, while Flight 2472 continued to Rochester and touched down later that night. No injuries were reported.

## Official Responses

Delta Air Lines issued a statement emphasizing that the crew acted in accordance with established procedures. "As safety remains at the core of everything we do, the crew of Delta Flight 1568 performed a go-around during approach into Atlanta due to potential traffic on the runway," the airline said. "During the go-around, the crew maneuvered in accordance with established procedures to maintain separation from other air traffic."

The FAA said the incident is under investigation, and Delta confirmed it is fully cooperating. According to [Simple Flying](https://simpleflying.com/delta-air-lines-atlanta-near-miss/), the TCAS system functioned as designed, and both crews' training proved effective in managing the uncommon scenario.

## Broader Safety Concerns

The incident is the latest in a troubling series of near misses at major U.S. airports. Just three days earlier, another runway incursion occurred at Hartsfield-Jackson involving two commercial airliners, as reported by [AviatorDB](https://aviatordb.com/news/safety/atlanta-runway-incursion-july-2026-raises-safety-alarms-at-katl). The FAA is currently conducting a comprehensive audit of the 45 busiest U.S. airports, with Atlanta flagged as a priority following multiple ground incidents.

Separately, the NTSB is investigating a Delta-American Airlines near miss at Boston Logan International Airport, underscoring a broader pattern of runway incursions at high-volume hubs. Congressional hearings have placed the issue under the national spotlight, with debate intensifying over air traffic control staffing, technological upgrades, and procedural reforms.

Adverse weather on the night of the incident contributed to significant operational stress at Atlanta — FlightAware data shows that nearly 40% of departures were delayed and 2% canceled — potentially increasing traffic density and controller workload.

## What to Watch For

The FAA investigation will examine several critical questions: Why was the departing flight cleared for takeoff while the arriving flight was executing a go-around from the same runway area? Were standard separation minima maintained? And whether adverse weather or controller workload contributed to the conflict.

Safety experts note that while TCAS and runway status lights have been implemented at major hubs, the recurrence of incidents at the same airport days apart suggests systemic issues that may require procedural or infrastructure changes to address.

The incident serves as a stark reminder that as air travel demand continues to grow, the margin for error at the world's busiest airports remains dangerously thin.
