# UK Returns 12 Lost Cultural Relics to China

The United Kingdom has returned 12 lost Chinese cultural relics and artworks to China, with a handover ceremony held on 23 September at the Chinese Embassy in London. The items had been seized by London's Metropolitan Police during a criminal investigation, and their return was arranged under a UNESCO convention framework that both countries have ratified.

The repatriation, [reported by Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20260924/90947d32c17343dd8b19f92aadad0743/c.html), is the latest in a series of UK-to-China cultural returns and comes amid a broader warming of bilateral relations following the appointment of new UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

## The Handover

The ceremony was attended by China's Vice Minister of Culture and Tourism and Director of the National Cultural Heritage Administration Rao Quan, who delivered remarks by video, and Chinese Ambassador to the UK Zheng Zeguang. Representatives of the Metropolitan Police, the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office, the UK Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport, and the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe were also present.

According to Xinhua, the 12 items were seized by the Metropolitan Police in the course of handling a criminal case. Both China and the UK are state parties to the UNESCO Convention on the Means of Prohibiting and Preventing the Illicit Import, Export and Transfer of Ownership of Cultural Property—commonly known as the 1970 Convention. Working within that framework, China's National Cultural Heritage Administration coordinated through the Chinese Embassy in London to secure the return.

The Chinese side characterized the handover as a matter of principle. “Promoting the return of displaced cultural relics to their countries of origin is a just cause that safeguards the cultural security of all countries, respects the cultural rights and national sentiments of all peoples, and protects the achievements of human civilization,” the Chinese representation said, according to Xinhua.

## A Diplomatic Subtext

The return carries a distinctly diplomatic flavor. Chinese officials framed it as “another important outcome of China and the UK jointly implementing relevant international conventions,” and said they hoped to use the cooperation as a basis for implementing “the important consensus” reached in a recent leaders' phone call. A [fuller statement carried by Huanqiu](https://3w.huanqiu.com/a/b65d07/4TKx7x0FMH5), sourced from the National Cultural Heritage Administration, explicitly ties the return to Prime Minister Burnham's recent call with President Xi Jinping.

That call took place on 8 September 2026, according to a [readout published by the Chinese Embassy in Mongolia](https://mn.china-embassy.gov.cn/eng/gnyw/202609/t20260908_12018669.htm). Xi congratulated Burnham on taking office and stressed that the two countries “have more common interests than disagreements,” while Burnham reaffirmed that the UK would maintain consistency in its China policy and build a “long-term and consistent strategic partnership.” The two leaders also discussed cooperation at the UN, G20 and WTO.

For its part, the UK framed the relic return in practical, legal terms. “This return of cultural relics is precisely a positive outcome of practical cooperation between the two countries, and also reflects the firm resolve of both sides to respect each other and jointly combat the illegal trafficking of cultural property,” the UK side said, in the version carried by Huanqiu.

## A Recurring Pattern

The channel used here is well established. In 2020, 68 Chinese cultural relics that had been lost in the UK for 25 years were returned to China, after being seized by the Metropolitan Police, in a process coordinated between the National Cultural Heritage Administration and the Chinese Embassy in London under the same 1970 Convention framework, [according to Beijing News](https://m.bjnews.com.cn/detail/160566916915490.html).

China has intensified its broader repatriation drive in recent years, establishing a dedicated Office for the Recovery of Lost Cultural Relics within the National Cultural Heritage Administration and securing returns from the United States, Argentina and the UK, among others. Cultural repatriation tends to resonate strongly in China, where it is tied to questions of national dignity and cultural rights.

## What's Next

Notably, the identity of the 12 returned items—their types, age and provenance—has not been disclosed in the coverage reviewed, and no UK government statement on the return was located. Both gaps are likely to be filled by follow-up reporting.

For now, the handover serves as a small but symbolically loaded marker of improving China-UK relations under Burnham's premiership, and as the latest demonstration of how the 1970 Convention continues to serve as the practical legal mechanism for cultural property returns between the two countries.
