# Limburg Police Seize 1,300 Cannabis Plants in Record Raids

Belgian police have dismantled two cannabis plantations holding roughly 1,300 plants and arrested 10 people during what the local Limburg Regio Hoofdstad (LRH) force has called the largest operation in its history. The coordinated action unfolded across 18 search locations in Limburg and Flemish Brabant, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/24/tien-arrestaties-na-18-invallen-in-limburg-en-vlaams-brabant/).

## A Coordinated Morning Sweep

Officers moved on 18 locations on the morning of Tuesday, 22 September, targeting an organised drug-cultivation network. Ten suspects were detained — eight men and two women — all of whom authorities say originate from Hasselt, Alken, Herk-De-Stad and Wellen.

An investigating judge subsequently detained and imprisoned nine of the ten.

The two active plantations were found in Wellen and Houthalen, together accounting for approximately 1,300 plants. Beyond the growing operations, police seized 14 kilograms of drugs, 1.5 kilograms of cocaine, 11 kilograms of cannabis and €300,000 in cash.

"For the LRH local police, this is the largest police operation in its history," the force stated, announcing the results at a press conference at its police house in Hasselt. The operation was reported by Leen Paredis for VRT NWS, with contributions from the Belga news agency.

## A Domestic Action Amid a Broader Crackdown

The raids come just days after Belgium led a separate, far larger international operation, underscoring how layered the country's anti-drug enforcement has become. On 21 September, the Belgium-directed operation "Atlas Nexus" produced more than 490 arrests worldwide and the seizure of 157 tonnes of drugs, drawing together police services from Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/21/grote-internationale-politieactie-belgische-leiding/).

That operation involved 34 countries and more than 8,600 police officers and magistrates, who inspected aircraft, vessels, vehicles and containers at ports, airports and major transport routes. It also led to the dismantling of what authorities described as the first cocaine-processing laboratory discovered in Ireland, along with two illegal gold mines in French Guiana.

Crucially, the Atlas Nexus action produced no police operations or arrests on Belgian soil itself. The LRH raids therefore stand as a distinct, domestic enforcement effort — a local force acting on its own intelligence rather than as part of the international dragnet.

The contrast is telling. Belgium has long been a major transit and production hub for illicit drugs in Europe, particularly through the Port of Antwerp for cocaine flows. While high-profile international operations grab headlines by targeting smuggling routes between South America and Europe, the LRH case illustrates the ground-level cultivation networks that supply domestic markets.

## Why It Matters

The scale of the LRH operation — measured both in its 18 locations and in the 1,300 plants seized — signals that cannabis cultivation remains a resilient and organised enterprise in the region. The presence of significant cash reserves and quantities of cocaine alongside the plantations suggests the network was not a small-scale operation but part of a broader criminal supply chain.

Belgian federal police have repeatedly emphasised that intercepting shipments alone is insufficient. As the federal force put it during the Atlas Nexus announcement, the goal is to "map out, dismantle and deprive [criminal networks] of the means with which they finance their activities." The LRH raids appear to follow that same logic: targeting the people and infrastructure behind cultivation, not merely the product.

## What's Next

With nine suspects now imprisoned and an investigating judge leading the case, attention turns to the judicial process and whether the arrests lead to further targets. Police have not indicated whether additional raids are planned.

The operation also raises familiar questions about the durability of such crackdowns. Large cannabis finds in Limburg are not unprecedented — the region has seen substantial plantations dismantled before — and each seizure invites scrutiny of whether enforcement is keeping pace with the networks it seeks to disrupt. For now, LRH can point to its largest-ever action as evidence that local policing remains a central front in Belgium's wider drug fight.
