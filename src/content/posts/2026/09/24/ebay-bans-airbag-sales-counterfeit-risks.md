# eBay Bans Airbag Sales Over Counterfeit Risks

eBay has banned the sale of airbags and key airbag components on its platform, joining a growing list of major online retailers in a crackdown on faulty and counterfeit auto parts that regulators have linked to at least 11 deaths in the United States.

The policy took effect September 24, 2026, and covers airbags, airbag inflators (gas generators), and steering wheel airbag covers across the entire airbag category. Sellers had a 30-day compliance window to remove affected listings; those that remain will be deleted automatically.

## Why eBay Acted

eBay did not publicly state a specific trigger for the decision, saying only that it followed "a comprehensive review of the risks these products may pose to eBay users," according to the company's [policy update](https://community.ebay.com.au/forum/news-updates-44/topic/important-policy-update-airbags-no-longer-permitted-on-ebay-8788/). The company told NPR it would enforce the ban using seller compliance audits, filtering algorithms, and AI-supported monitoring.

The move follows a National Highway Traffic Safety Administration investigation that found certain substandard airbag inflators imported from China were responsible for roughly a dozen deaths or serious injuries since 2023. In July, The Wall Street Journal traced the origins of several faulty — and potentially counterfeit — airbag components and found that one had been purchased as an aftermarket part on eBay, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/24/nx-s1-5978124/ebay-bans-airbags-faulty-counterfeit-auto-parts).

## A Broader Retailer Crackdown

eBay is far from the first company to take this step. Amazon, the nation's largest online retailer, [prohibits airbag sales](https://sellercentral.amazon.com/help/hub/reference/external/GRLKLZ6WQ9R259LC), listing them among banned lifestyle products. Meta, which owns Facebook Marketplace, and the Chinese retail sites Temu and Alibaba have similar bans. Alibaba first prohibited airbags in 2014 over safety concerns and later extended the ban to component parts to halt counterfeits.

Some airbag-related parts that do not contain an airbag or inflator — including control units, crash sensors, diagnostic modules, and passenger sensors — may still be sold. Outside the U.S., all sellers may list these parts; within the U.S., sales are restricted to pre-approved sellers.

## The Human and Regulatory Toll

The regulatory push intensified after the NHTSA investigation tied the defective inflators — marked with the identifier "DTN60DB" and likely illegally imported — to deaths and serious injuries. On April 29–30, 2026, the agency announced a ban on the defective inflators, manufactured by Jilin Province Detiannuo Safety Technology Co., Ltd. of China, and U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean P. Duffy warned that anyone importing or selling them would be held accountable.

The legal consequences have been severe. In June 2026, a Florida jury awarded $603 million to the family of Destiny Byassee, a 22-year-old mother of two killed when a counterfeit DTN airbag detonated during a minor crash, [Repairer Driven News reported](https://www.repairerdrivennews.com/2026/06/15/florida-jury-awards-603-million-to-family-of-mother-killed-by-counterfeit-airbag/). The jury awarded $243 million in compensatory damages and $360 million in punitive damages. An 11th U.S. death linked to the banned inflators was reported in early September.

## Why Airbags Are So Risky

Airbags are not simply cushions. As Becky Mueller, a senior research engineer at the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, explained to NPR, they are "actually like a pyrotechnic device." A crash sensor signals the airbag module, which ignites the inflator; expanding gases fill the bag in a fraction of a second.

When an inflator is defective, its parts can become brittle and break apart, sending shrapnel "flying at your face" as the airbag deploys. Counterfeits compound the danger: they may look like brand-name parts but fail to deploy properly — or at all — and they are nearly impossible to recall or trace.

The stakes echo the largest recall in U.S. history. The Takata airbag recall, which began in 2014, affected an estimated 67 million airbags. Takata later pleaded guilty to wire fraud and was sentenced to pay $1 billion in criminal penalties, according to the [U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/takata-corporation-pleads-guilty-sentenced-pay-1-billion-criminal-penalties-airbag-scheme).

## Enforcement Remains the Open Question

Consumer advocates broadly welcomed eBay's move but cautioned that platform bans alone will not stop dangerous parts from reaching the market. "It's a good thing that eBay is no longer going to be offering airbags or airbag components on their platform. And it's about time," said Rosemary Shahan, president of the nonprofit Consumers for Auto Reliability and Safety.

William Wallace, director of safety advocacy for Consumer Reports, was more skeptical. "It is a game of Whack-A-Mole," he said, adding that "it's one thing to have a policy in place banning the sale of airbags... It's an entirely different thing to actually see the sale of airbags stop."

Because the defective inflators were imported illegally, NHTSA cannot say how many remain in U.S. vehicles. The agency has said it cannot confirm the risk is limited to the Chevrolet Malibu and Hyundai Sonata models where all known ruptures occurred. It recommends that used vehicles involved in a prior crash be inspected by a qualified mechanic.

For consumers, the practical takeaway is vigilance: vehicle history reports from Carfax and other services can reveal past airbag deployments, but experts say only a hands-on inspection by an experienced mechanic can confirm whether an airbag is genuine.

## What to Watch

As more marketplaces tighten their rules, the central question is enforcement. eBay's use of audits and AI monitoring will be tested against a sprawling global supply chain, and regulators may face pressure to extend the ban beyond the two vehicle models currently linked to the deaths.
