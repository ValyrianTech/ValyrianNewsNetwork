# China Busts 6-Billion-Yuan Cross-Border Fraud Ring

Chinese police have dismantled a cross-border fraud network behind a "national asset unfreezing" scam with an amount involved exceeding 6 billion yuan (roughly US$840 million), arresting 257 suspects in an operation spanning China and three Southeast Asian countries.

The Ministry of Public Security (MPS) announced the case on September 23, and the suspects were repatriated to China in the days that followed, according to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34134763), which published the official account via CCTV News.

## A Long-Running Scheme Targeting the Elderly

The case traces back to January 2026, when public security organs in northeastern Jilin province identified a gang running a cross-border "national asset unfreezing" (民族资产解冻) fraud, the [Xinhua](https://www.dzwww.com/news/yw/202609/t20260923_18149298.htm) news agency reported. Investigators found the gang had fabricated an array of fake "national projects"—using names such as "Fuxing Huijin" (Revival Huijin), "Pension Action," "China Strong Agriculture," "Livelihood Security," and "China Dream Realized"—to lure members.

The group hired a technical team to build fraudulent mobile applications, used online platforms to drum up recruits, and ran a sustained campaign of deception against elderly people, authorities said. While officials did not disclose an exact victim count, The Paper described the number as "numerous," with the total amount involved exceeding 6 billion yuan.

## A Coordinated Cross-Border Takedown

Under MPS direction, Jilin police mapped out the network's locations and personnel before moving against it in a series of coordinated strikes.

On July 28, acting through law enforcement cooperation, Lao police arrested 110 suspects in the capital, Vientiane. Simultaneously, the MPS deployed coordinated raids across multiple sites in China, destroying nine fraud dens and arresting 101 suspects domestically.

On August 14, Singapore police arrested the gang's alleged behind-the-scenes "financial backers"—identified only by the surnames Hou and Nie. Then, on September 2, Cambodian police detained another 44 gang suspects.

In total, 257 people were arrested. Authorities said the operation delivered a "full-chain" strike, reaching the network's financial backers, core members, technical operations and maintenance staff, and agents.

## Context: A Distinctly Chinese Fraud Typology

The "national asset unfreezing" scam is a uniquely Chinese fraud category with deep historical roots. According to reporting from [China National Radio](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/sh/2017/04-27/8210264.shtml), the scheme dates back to rumors circulating in the 1970s and 1980s that a vast hoard of assets—variously framed as Nationalist-era treasures or overseas-frozen "national assets"—needed to be "unfrozen" through public fundraising. Victims were promised enormous payouts or lifelong benefits once the assets were released.

Over time, the old deception adapted to the internet, evolving into what police have described as a hybrid of "current-affairs policy manipulation, pyramid scheme recruitment, and outright fraud." Perpetrators have forged government red-header documents, bank seals, and even "Qing dynasty" gold bars, and have produced altered photographs with leaders to lend credibility. Many gangs use pyramid-style recruitment and intense psychological control—including daily flag-raising ceremonies and group recitations of the national anthem—and occasionally instruct members to resist police.

A recurring challenge for investigators is that many victims refuse to come forward. Police have noted that victims often cling to the belief that the payout will eventually materialize, and that they view their individual losses—typically 1,000 to 3,000 yuan—as trivial compared with the promised windfall. China National Radio reported that in one earlier multi-province case involving more than 70,000 victims, not a single one had proactively reported the fraud.

## Broader Enforcement Push and Cross-Border Cooperation

The arrest is part of a wider national crackdown. Since the start of 2026, the MPS has repeatedly deployed public security organs nationwide on a special campaign against national asset unfreezing fraud. To date, the campaign has resulted in 180 cases solved, 75 fraud gangs dismantled, and a batch of suspects arrested, Xinhua reported.

The case also underscores China's expanding cross-border law enforcement footprint in Southeast Asia. The structure of the network—operating dens in Laos, financial backers in Singapore, and suspects in Cambodia—reflects both the globalization of this fraud type and Beijing's willingness to coordinate multilaterally against criminal networks that reach beyond its borders. Chinese fraud operations tied to this scheme have long been documented operating from bases in Laos, Vietnam, and Cambodia.

An MPS official said public security organs would maintain a "high-pressure crackdown" on such fraud, take "multiple measures," continue law enforcement cooperation, and "effectively safeguard the people's property safety and lawful rights and interests."

## Analysis: What the Case Signals

The scale of the operation—over 6 billion yuan involved and more than 250 arrests—makes it one of the most significant anti-fraud actions of the year. But the case also illustrates a persistent structural problem: by the time authorities act, the funds have typically been dissipated through the network's layered structure, and the victims who funded the scheme are rarely made whole.

The cross-border dimension cuts both ways. It demonstrates that China can marshal international cooperation to reach suspects abroad, yet it also confirms that these schemes have become embedded in transnational criminal ecosystems—where financial backers, technical infrastructure, and recruitment operations are deliberately dispersed across jurisdictions to complicate prosecution.

The emphasis on elderly victims is equally notable. The gang's fabricated "projects" leaned heavily on themes of pensions, social security, and national rejuvenation—narratives that exploit trust in state authority and the financial anxieties of China's aging population. That those narratives remain persuasive, even as official warnings multiply, suggests the fraud type may be as much a social phenomenon as a criminal one.

## What to Watch

Authorities have not disclosed the full identities of the suspects, the precise number of victims, or a breakdown of the 6 billion yuan figure, which is described as the "amount involved" rather than confirmed losses.

As the repatriated suspects move through China's judicial system, the next test will be whether investigators can trace the network's financial flows and demonstrate that the operation's cross-border coordination produces not just arrests but accountability. For now, the case stands as a clear signal that Beijing intends to keep pursuing these networks—wherever they operate.
