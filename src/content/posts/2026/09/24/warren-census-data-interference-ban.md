# Warren Presses Trump Officials on Census Data Guardrails

Sen. Elizabeth Warren is demanding that Trump administration officials explain why the Commerce Department quietly stripped language banning political interference from its scientific integrity policy—a change she warns could compromise the reliability of the nation's demographic and economic data. In letters sent Wednesday, the Massachusetts Democrat and top Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee asked [Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/24/nx-s1-5979070/department-commerce-census-bureau-elizabeth-warren), the Office of Management and Budget, and the Federal Reserve for detailed answers by Oct. 7.

The inquiry, first reported by NPR, centers on an unannounced revision to the department's scientific integrity policy dated Aug. 19. The updated version no longer states that "protection from inappropriate influence" is among the "hallmarks of scientific integrity"—a safeguard that the prior policy, updated in January 2025 just days before Trump's second inauguration, had made explicit.

## A Quiet Revision, One Day After an Unusual Report

The timing of the change has drawn scrutiny. The revision was issued one day after the Census Bureau published a highly unusual, unauthored report on non-U.S. citizen voting—produced by a team with ties to the America First Policy Institute (AFPI), a think tank founded by officials from Trump's first administration. [NPR first reported on the team's ties to AFPI](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/19/nx-s1-5938799/trump-census-bureau-noncitizen-voting), and data and election experts have criticized the document's claims as questionable and below the standards normally applied to bureau research.

The revised directive, known as DAO 216-23, applies to all Commerce Department agencies—not only the Census Bureau but also the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and the Bureau of Economic Analysis. The updated policy also establishes a new process for investigating alleged violations with a reduced role for department lawyers, a structure that data experts say leaves more room for political pressure.

"Amid the Trump Administration's broader assault on the integrity of the nation's statistical system, in which you and the Department have played a key role, you owe the public a clear explanation of why the Department removed this language and how it arrived at this decision," Warren wrote to Lutnick, who oversees the Census Bureau as head of the Commerce Department.

## Why the Guardrails Matter

The Census Bureau is the federal government's largest statistical agency and is preparing for the 2030 national head count. That decennial count determines each state's share of congressional seats and Electoral College votes and guides the distribution of billions of dollars in federal funding. The data it produces also feeds into economic indicators that the Federal Reserve weighs when setting monetary policy.

The language removed in August was not merely symbolic. The 2025 version of the policy explicitly banned attempts to shape or interfere in data collection and statistical analysis "against well-accepted scientific methods and theories or without scientific justification," and required that findings not be "suppressed, delayed, or altered for political purposes," [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/31/nx-s1-5948057/department-commerce-census-bureau-scientific-integrity). Those constraints echoed the government's "Trust Regulation," formalized after a 2018 bipartisan law, which requires statistical agencies to produce data that is "impartial and free from undue influence and the appearance of undue influence."

Critics say the revision weakens those protections at a moment when they matter most. "[It's] a major change that eliminates the guardrails against political interference in the statistical and scientific data products of the department," said Nancy Potok, a former deputy director of the Census Bureau and Commerce Department official.

Robert Santos, a former Census Bureau director nominated by President Biden, questioned the sequence of events. "It's entirely possible that this was done to reconcile the Department of Commerce and the administration's own actions by publishing an external partisan document about voting on the Census Bureau website," he said. "The publishing exemplifies inappropriate influence. It was not transparent, and it didn't even include career staff experts."

Beth Jarosz, vice president of the Association of Public Data Users, warned that the new structure itself invites pressure. "There's room for interference because the way that the new rule is structured leaves lots of opportunities to put pressure on staff to act in a certain way, to decide things in a certain way or to come to certain conclusions," she said. "It certainly seems like the guardrails, if they are still there at all, they are much weaker than they used to be."

## A Pattern of Contested Moves

Warren's letters place the August revision within a broader pattern of administration actions touching federal statistics. In June, the administration [banned a data privacy protection technique](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/12/nx-s1-5855734/census-bureau-data-differential-privacy) that the Census Bureau had used for decades, forcing the agency to delay the release of some survey results and potentially cutting future public data about rural communities and other small populations.

This month, the administration [proposed that the census](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/09/nx-s1-5845147/trump-us-census-citizenship-question-race-categories) exclude certain non-U.S. citizen residents from the count that determines each state's share of congressional districts and Electoral College votes—a move that would break with the 14th Amendment's requirement to count the "whole number of persons in each state." The administration has also called for another unprecedented change: ending the census's collection of data about race and ethnicity.

Warren tied those proposals directly to the stakes of the inquiry. "That [census proposal] and other proposed changes would have sweeping implications for the quality of our economic data, the redistricting process that determines congressional representation, the Federal Reserve's monetary policy decisions that rely upon key employment statistics and [Bureau of Economic Analysis] inflation data, and our ability to enforce civil rights and other laws," she wrote.

## Questions About Coordination and Oversight

Warren is probing whether the Commerce Department's decision to remove the interference ban involved the Office of Management and Budget, the White House agency that coordinates federal statistical agencies. She also wrote to OMB officials seeking answers, and separately asked the Federal Reserve how it verifies the accuracy of the Census Bureau data it relies on.

Fed spokesperson Eric Kollig said the central bank plans to respond to Warren's letter. Spokespeople for the Commerce Department and OMB did not immediately respond to NPR's requests for comment.

The administration has also moved to shrink the federal statistical workforce, a drawdown that NPR has [described as fueling a "brain drain"](https://www.npr.org/2025/04/17/nx-s1-5349435/us-census-bureau-data-decennial) of statisticians, economists, and experienced civil servants. Combined with the weakened interference language, critics argue, the changes could erode the institutional capacity and independence that make federal data trustworthy.

## What to Watch

The immediate test is whether the administration meets Warren's Oct. 7 deadline for detailed answers—and whether those answers identify who within the Commerce Department ordered the removal and when the decision was made. Warren is also seeking to learn whether political appointees reviewed the AFPI-linked noncitizen-voting report before it was published.

Beyond the letters, the dispute feeds into larger fights over the 2030 census. If the administration's proposals to exclude certain noncitizens and to end race and ethnicity data collection advance, they are likely to face legal challenges centered on the 14th Amendment and the Trust Regulation's impartiality requirements. For now, the removed language stands as a quiet but consequential shift—one that Democratic lawmakers say the public deserves an explanation for before the next national count begins.
