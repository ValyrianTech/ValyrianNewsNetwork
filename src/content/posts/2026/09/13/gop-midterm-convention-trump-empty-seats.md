# Empty Seats, Socialism and Trump Dominate GOP Convention

DALLAS — The Republican Party staged its first-ever national midterm convention at the American Airlines Center here on Sept. 9-10, an event organizers branded "Trumpapalooza" to energize the base and fill campaign coffers. Instead, the two-day gathering was defined by thousands of empty seats, an unfunded $5,000 giveaway, and a messaging strategy that leaned heavily on President Donald Trump even as his approval ratings sit at record lows.

Trump delivered the Day One keynote and Vice President JD Vance closed the convention on Day Two, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/11/nx-s1-5962848/republican-convention-trump-dividend-vance-midterms), which described the president as simultaneously the GOP's "biggest asset in getting out the vote" and its "greatest obstacle in convincing voters to keep Republicans in power."

## A Convention Designed to Project Strength

The event was unusual in American politics. National party conventions are typically reserved for presidential election years, and the [Texas Tribune](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/09/08/republican-midterm-convention-dallas-texas-trump-paxton-2026/) reported that Republicans branded the Dallas affair "Trumpapalooza," with a clear goal of rallying GOP voters and stocking campaign accounts during a difficult midterm environment.

The stakes are high. Polling averages show Republicans at risk of losing both the House and the Senate, and the party in power historically loses seats in midterm elections. The convention came after Labor Day — traditionally the kickoff of the general-election season — with mail ballots already being returned in states like North Carolina.

## The $5,000 Promise

The headline moment came on Day One, when Trump promised every American adult a $5,000 "dividend" if Republicans retain control of both chambers of Congress.

"If the Republicans win the House of Representatives and the United States Senate, both of them ... because of our tremendous strength and success economically, I will issue a dividend to every adult citizen in the United States of America for $5,000," Trump said, according to [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/trump-500-dividend-republican-midterm-convention/a-79208399).

He added one condition: "The only caveat we have is that the dividend must be spent in the United States of America. We don't want you going to Canada spending the money, we don't want you going to China or Germany."

The president did not explain how the measure would be financed, nor how the government would track where citizens spend the funds. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/09/trump-republican-convention-speech-midterm-elections) noted, the proposal would cost over $1 trillion. The Texas Tribune estimated the figure at roughly $1.3 trillion, amounting to an extraordinary redistribution of wealth. Trump had previously promised $2,000 tariff-rebate checks that never materialized.

Trump also urged supporters to treat the election as a referendum on him personally. "I'm asking you to pretend that I'm on the ballot," he said on Day One. On the second night, he sharpened the appeal: "You know what happens if you don't vote? You go to hell."

## An Enthusiasm Problem

The convention's empty seats became a story in their own right. Both days saw thousands of unfilled chairs, including almost the entire upper level of the arena, even as Trump insisted the venue was "packed" with "thousands" more waiting outside.

The Guardian reported that compared with the four-day traditional convention in Milwaukee two years earlier, the first day felt muted — no long queues to enter, no waits for food or bathrooms, and swathes of empty seats visible throughout Trump's speech. The RNC had announced in mid-August that Americans could apply for tickets through a lottery, yet by the time the convention began, applications were being instantly granted.

NPR reported that the disparity extended to the speaking program. Entertainer Adam Carolla, Rep. Brandon Gill, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent received some of the warmest receptions and more stage time than figures like North Carolina Senate nominee Michael Whatley or Ohio Sen. John Husted.

Behind the optics, the party faces structural headwinds. After defeats in 2024, Democrats have shown more enthusiasm to vote, flipped a number of state and local seats, and improved their margins with nearly every voter demographic. Meanwhile, the Republican base has fractured over the war in Iran, the handling of the release of the Epstein files, surging gas prices, and rising costs driven by tariffs.

## Attacks Over Policy

A frequent feature of the speeches was personal attacks on Democratic opponents rather than detailed policy. Trump described Michigan Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed and other Democrats in stark terms.

"In state after state, the Democrat establishment has been overthrown and replaced by crazed lunatics and radicals," Trump said. "They call themselves Democrat socialists, but actually they're communists. They're not socialists. They're long beyond socialists."

Michigan Republican Senate nominee Mike Rogers targeted El-Sayed directly. "You don't sympathize with terrorists, you don't sanitize extremism, and you sure don't ask the people of Michigan to send you to the United States Senate while surrounding yourself with people who despise America," he said. "He's a con man. I'm a law man."

Al Jazeera reported that Texas officials made banning "Sharia law" a stated priority. Sen. Ted Cruz said "Sharia law has no place in America," while Gov. Greg Abbott said, "We will fully ban Sharia law in Texas." Critics have said lawmakers have misrepresented the Islamic concept and fabricated an issue to stigmatize Islam, noting no meaningful effort has been made to codify Islamic principles into U.S. law.

## The War Nobody Wanted to Discuss

Trump campaigned on a promise of "no new wars," but the conflict with Iran remains ongoing with no resolution in sight. The war has driven up energy costs, with diesel topping $6 a gallon and crude oil reaching near-record highs.

Yet speakers barely acknowledged the war or the military. Vance offered the closest thing to an acknowledgment, pleading with voters who disagree with the administration's policy not to "throw the baby out with the bathwater" by voting for Democrats.

"Don't give the country over to a bunch of crazy people because you happen to disagree with us on a policy issue here or there," he said. "What I am asking you to do is to recognize the contrast with the radicals and recognize the achievements of the last 18 months."

## 2028 Looms

The convention also served as an early stage for potential 2028 presidential contenders. Vance, widely seen as Trump's heir apparent, led the field of possible successors that included Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Cruz, and Donald Trump Jr.

Attendees openly discussed the future. "Trump is kind of a one time thing," convention attendee Meredith Smith told NPR's Tamara Keith. "I really think God picked him personally for such a time as this."

Her friend Sherri Hopson echoed the sentiment. "[Trump's] built this Republican Party and the bench is deep," she said. "I have a lot of faith in JD Vance and Marco Rubio and I really hope they run as a ticket."

## The Road Ahead

In many key races, Democratic candidates have outraised and outspent their Republican opponents and dominated the airwaves. Republican committees and Trump's MAGA, Inc. super PAC are reportedly sitting on hundreds of millions of dollars they could deploy in the closing weeks.

But the polling suggests a disconnect between the issues voters want addressed and what they perceive the GOP to be focused on. With early voting set to begin soon and many ad reservations already booked, last-minute spending may not be enough to change the trajectory.

As NPR put it, the Dallas convention highlighted both the party's greatest asset and its greatest liability — the same man. Whether that proves decisive on Nov. 3 remains the central question of the midterm campaign.
