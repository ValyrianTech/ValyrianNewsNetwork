# Belgian Man, 82, Suspected of Museum Vandalism

A man aged 82 is suspected of a series of vandalism attacks at Bokrijk, one of Flanders' best-known open-air museums, after allegedly slashing several information panels and portraits. Police in Limburg confirmed an investigation is under way, and say the suspect was taken in for questioning on 2 September before being released.

The case has drawn unusual attention in Belgium not because of the damage itself, but because of the reported motive: the man, a long-time annual subscriber to the museum, is said to be unhappy that Bokrijk has become "too modern."

## What happened

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/13/bokrijk-genk-verdachte-vandalisme/), the vandalism targeted a number of advertising and information panels as well as portraits belonging to the museum, which were cut up. The museum's domain wardens filed a complaint with police, and the suspect was reportedly clearly identifiable on camera footage.

"The suspect was taken in for questioning on 2 September," police zone Carma told VRT NWS. "Afterwards he was released. We are investigating the facts further."

The broadcaster stressed that the alleged motive is not an official finding. While the man is reported to have been angered by the museum's modern direction, VRT NWS said the claim comes from its own information rather than police confirmation. Bokrijk itself confirmed the vandalism occurred but declined to comment on the ongoing investigation, adding that the damaged panels are being repaired.

## A museum caught between past and future

Bokrijk, a 550-hectare provincial domain in Genk, is best known for its open-air museum, which opened on 12 April 1958 and holds around 140 historic buildings and some 30,000 objects documenting everyday life in Flanders from the 17th century to 1950. As [Knack](https://www.knack.be/geschiedenis/bokrijk-het-openluchtmuseum-en-de-toekomst/) recounts in its history of the site, the museum was founded to save threatened vernacular architecture, under the impetus of then-Limburg governor Louis Roppe and first curator Jozef Weyns — an explicitly preservationist mission.

In recent years, however, the museum has reframed itself around the idea that the past should serve the present, inviting visitors to experience how the past inspires the future through so-called "inspiration stops" that encourage them to look, do and discover. Recent 2026 programming has leaned heavily into experiential and wellbeing-oriented activities — family expeditions, camp-building, and a "cuddle moment with the sheep" — alongside its traditional heritage displays.

That evolution is the most plausible backdrop to a traditionalist visitor's frustration, though it remains only an alleged motive in this case. It also reflects a wider tension in the museum sector between preservation-focused traditionalism and the push to broaden appeal through modern, experiential programming.

One structural detail is worth noting: under current Flemish heritage law, historic buildings may only be conserved in situ, meaning Bokrijk's building collection no longer grows. As its heritage core freezes, the museum's room to evolve lies increasingly in how it presents its collection — which may be precisely the change that upset the suspect.

## What's next

It remains unclear whether the man has been formally charged or remains only a suspect, and the exact number and nature of the damaged items has been described only in general terms. Local follow-up coverage is likely to clarify those points in the coming days.

For now, the case stands as a small but telling collision between two ideas of what a heritage museum should be: a shrine to the past, or a bridge to the present. Bokrijk's wardens have repaired the panels; what the museum does next with its modern course is likely to draw more scrutiny than ever.
