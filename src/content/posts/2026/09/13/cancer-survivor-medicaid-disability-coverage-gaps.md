# A Cancer Survivor's Medicaid Loss Exposes US Coverage Gaps

Taya Hailstone, a 19-year-old from Roundup, Montana, has been in remission from childhood Hodgkin lymphoma for five years. Yet last year the state decided she was no longer eligible for low-cost disability health coverage through Medicaid — switching her to a children's program just three months before she aged out, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/12/nx-s1-5966884/insurance-coverage-cancer-disability-medicaid), which published the story alongside KFF Health News.

Her case has become a striking illustration of how the US health insurance system can penalize people who try to re-enter the workforce, and it arrives as states prepare to implement sweeping new federal Medicaid work requirements.

## A Life Shaped by Cancer's Aftermath

Hailstone was diagnosed with blood cancer at age 10. The disease tore her intestines, leading to their partial removal, and her body now struggles to process food, putting her at risk of severe dehydration. She said lingering side effects can leave her mind foggy and cause her hands and feet to swell enough that gripping a fork or walking across a room becomes difficult. She typically attends three medical appointments a week — physical and occupational therapy and mental health counseling — and she and her mother make a nearly two-hour round-trip drive to Billings for specialized care.

"Some days you feel fine and then you suddenly crash," Hailstone [said](https://www.mtpr.org/2026-09-12/a-cancer-survivor-hoped-to-work-then-she-lost-her-medicaid-disability-coverage).

The state's decision came after officials learned Hailstone had stopped receiving Social Security disability payments — a step she took because she hoped to get healthy enough to work and save money beyond the strict income caps tethered to those payments. According to letters from her doctors reviewed by KFF Health News, Montana's Department of Public Health and Human Services did not seek records from her treating medical team before ruling. Hailstone has kept Medicaid coverage while her family appeals. "If I lose this, this is life-changing," she said.

## A National Policy Shift

The Hailstone case is separate from the incoming federal work requirements, but advocates say it signals that states are not ready to decide who should be exempt from them. Congress created the national requirements through last year's One Big Beautiful Bill Act, giving states until January 2027 to implement rules requiring many adults to prove they are working, attending school, or volunteering roughly 80 hours a month to keep coverage.

An estimated 18.5 million people will have to meet those requirements, according to the [Congressional Budget Office](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2025-06/Wyden-Pallone-Neal_Letter_6-4-25.pdf), and [more than 40%](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/5-key-facts-about-medicaid-expansion/) of them live with a chronic health condition, according to KFF. The Congressional Budget Office projects that more than 5 million people will lose Medicaid coverage by 2034 because of the work requirements. Many Republican policymakers and the Trump administration have promoted the rules as a way to preserve coverage for the neediest.

Congress allowed states to exempt people with an illness that qualifies them as "medically frail." But federal rules released by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services in June 2026 went further than the law outlined: enrollees must not only have a qualifying diagnosis but also prove the illness makes it too hard to work, potentially requiring documentation from a medical professional. Many states had built plans around those judgment calls, only to be surprised by the stricter federal standard.

In late June, 25 mostly Democratic-led states [sued the Trump administration](https://www.kff.org/quick-insights/states-sue-cms-over-medicaid-work-requirements-rule-citing-departure-from-earlier-guidance-on-medical-frailty/) over the medical frailty rules, arguing they are too hard for patients to meet and for states to assess. That case is ongoing. CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz has said the documentation "should be relatively easy to provide" and that states should draw on existing data before demanding additional records from patients.

## Montana Moves First, and Fast

Montana began enforcing work requirements in July 2026, becoming the second state after Nebraska to do so and moving six months ahead of the federal deadline, as [KFF Health News reported](https://kffhealthnews.org/medicaid/medicaid-work-requirements-trump-montana-budget-shortfalls/). The state instituted a three-month grace period, with officials saying they would not begin disenrolling people for noncompliance until October 2026.

The early rollout has unfolded amid significant strain. Montana's health department faced a $183 million shortfall and proposed withholding a 3% Medicaid provider rate increase to save money. Health organizations warned that low reimbursement rates already make it hard to recruit and retain staff. The state was also among the slowest in the country to process income-based applications during the post-pandemic unwinding, and as of early March it had filled only 39 of 59 new positions meant to handle intensified eligibility checks.

Jean Branscum, CEO of the Montana Medical Association, captured the concern: "Is the department ready? Does the capacity exist for all this to be done right and ensure that patients don't pay the price?"

Already, doctors worry they will bear the burden of judging whether someone's illness qualifies them for a work exemption. As [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/medicaid/medicaid-work-requirements-medical-frailty-documentation-doctors/) reported, clinicians "cringe" at the prospect of documenting which enrollees are too sick to work — a gatekeeping role many feel unprepared for.

## The Human Cost of Administrative Complexity

Advocates argue the patchwork system states are being asked to build would be larger and more complicated than the Social Security Administration's own disability review process. Last year that federal program cost more than $5 billion to administer to roughly 7 million people. By comparison, the federal law provided only $200 million for states to share as they implement the work requirements, while states pay contractors millions to prepare often-flawed public aid systems.

Montana Legal Services Association deputy director Megan Dishong said state disability determinations are "always a bit of a crapshoot just based on state capacity." She warned the state could end up with a "slow-roll mess," adding: "This is a problem that's just starting."

Pamela Herd, a University of Michigan social policy professor who studies bureaucratic obstacles to public benefits, framed the stakes bluntly. "When we've designed public programs in ways that people can't figure out whether they're eligible without consulting lawyers, we've done something wrong," she said. "That has huge, huge implications for what's to come."

Anthony Wright, executive director of the advocacy group Families USA, predicted the Hailstone case will be replicated at scale: "This will be the story of millions of people."

Hailstone's own path illustrates the bind. She would qualify for Medicaid based on her income if she cannot prove disability eligibility — but that would require proving she is too sick to meet the work requirement, returning her to the same reliance on a state review of her illness. She is now reapplying for Social Security disability payments, which would limit how much she can work but guarantee access to Medicaid.

Montana's Department of Public Health and Human Services declined to comment on individual cases. Spokesperson Jon Ebelt said the agency is "committed to treating every client with respect and helping those who are eligible receive appropriate Medicaid coverage," and noted the state accepts Social Security disability decisions.

## What to Watch

Montana's grace period ends in October, when the state is expected to begin disenrolling people for noncompliance — the first real test of how the rules work in practice. Work requirements take effect in most of the country on January 1, 2027, and the multistate lawsuit over the medical frailty standard remains unresolved. As [Montana Budget and Policy Center](https://www.montanabudget.org/post/montana-dphhs-moving-forward-with-july-1-work-requirement-start-date-despite-questions) analysts have noted, DPHHS has pushed forward despite unanswered questions about exactly how exemptions will be granted.

For Hailstone, the outcome hinges on decisions made far from her small Montana town. "It feels like this process was made to make you give up," she said — a sentiment that, if advocates are right, millions of other Americans may soon share.
