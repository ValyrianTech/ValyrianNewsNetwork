# A Year After Charlie Kirk's Assassination, Turning Point USA Faces Its Test

One year to the day since founder Charlie Kirk was fatally shot at Utah Valley University, his organization Turning Point USA is simultaneously claiming record growth and confronting deepening internal fractures. The central question the anniversary poses is stark: can a movement built around one charismatic figure survive — and expand — without him?

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/10/nx-s1-5928517/charlie-kirk-death-anniversary-turning-point), Kirk was 31 when he was killed on Sept. 10, 2025, during a "Prove Me Wrong" campus event in Orem, Utah, on his "American Comeback" tour. In the days that followed, his widow, Erika Kirk, took the helm of the Phoenix-based conservative youth network, elected unanimously as CEO and board chair.

## A movement that looks both bigger and weaker

Turning Point says the 2025-2026 school year brought "unprecedented expansion." A fact sheet cited by NPR reports more than 1 million involved students, roughly 3,500 high school "Club America" chapters — nearly triple the pre-death number — and 1,500 college chapters, a 64% increase. The organization now employs more than 1,200 people.

An exclusive review by [The Arizona Republic and USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/arizona/2026/09/09/turning-point-still-growing-post-charlie-kirk/91615587007/) found that Turning Point received cash from more than 1 million donors over the past year, with more than 95% giving under $1,000. Its "Turning Point Faith" program, which trained nearly 10,000 pastors as part of a $10.6 million effort in fiscal 2024, now claims a network of more than 12,000 churches and pastors. Attendance at its Chapter Leadership Summit doubled compared to 2024.

"Everything that we can control from the organization standpoint ... Everything's growing. It's still growing," spokesman Andrew Kolvet told the Arizona Republic. In a separate statement to NPR, Kolvet acknowledged "a confluence of challenges" but insisted the group is "still growing in nearly every conceivable way."

Yet the view from campus tells a more complicated story. Student organizers describe a movement that has "fizzled," lacking a unifying leader and riven by the same divides fracturing the broader Republican Party.

## A leadership vacuum no one can fill

At the time of his death, Kirk was one of the most influential voices on the American right and a key conduit between young conservatives and the White House. Supporters credit him with helping then-candidate Donald Trump make critical gains with young voters in 2024.

That direct connection has proven impossible to replace. Turning Point leaders vowed to continue campus visits in Kirk's stead, but according to [NPR's reporting on the spring tour](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/30/nx-s1-5803865/turning-point-idaho-college-campus-tour-kirk), the events drew "mixed and, at times, lackluster turnout." A University of Georgia stop on April 14 saw sparse crowds, and an April 30 Iowa State event featuring Erika Kirk and Vice President JD Vance was canceled. At the tour's final stop in Idaho, organizers restricted arena seating to 869 — down from an initial request of 3,000 — turning away more than 1,000 people.

"Charlie Kirk got it. He spent all of his time on college campuses talking to people my age, whether he agreed or disagreed with them," Dino Fantegrossi, 23, a former University of Arkansas chapter leader who broke away to form a new group, Young American Revival, told NPR. "There's no one else that was quite at that level of plugged in."

## A youth coalition souring on Trump

Compounding the leadership gap is a political environment that has turned sharply against the broader movement. According to Pew Research Center data cited by NPR, approval of Trump's job performance among his voters under 35 fell from 92% at the start of his second term to 57% by late April 2026. Student organizers point to the U.S.-Iran war and the administration's relationship with Israel as major tension points, with Gen Z and millennial Republicans far less likely than older generations to hold favorable views on both.

"I would say the interest in conservative organizations is low right now, even if it did rebound when Charlie Kirk got shot," Max Williams, 20, who leads the University of Richmond's Turning Point chapter, told NPR. "Some of this stuff is just infuriating, and you can't defend any of it. And I don't even try anymore."

## Conspiracy theories threaten hard-won credibility

The internal divides extend beyond policy. A robust ecosystem of unfounded conspiracy theories about Kirk's assassination — amplified by figures such as podcaster Candace Owens — has become a source of reputational harm.

"The conspiracies revolving around Charlie Kirk's assassination [have] really tanked the credibility of Turning Point," Alex BirdSong, 19, president of the Georgia State University chapter, told NPR. "We've had people come up to us who are Candace Owens fans and get mad at us for being Turning Point, and so that's what the climate is."

Hazel Roberts, 18, a first-year student at Grand Canyon University, put it more simply: "Charlie was the glue that held us together."

## Trial and succession loom over the year ahead

Two unresolved threads will shape Turning Point's next chapter. The first is legal: on Sept. 1, 2026, Utah District Court Judge Tony Graf ruled there was sufficient evidence for Tyler Robinson, 23, to stand trial on all seven charges, including aggravated murder, in Kirk's killing. Prosecutors are likely to seek the death penalty, and Robinson could face capital punishment if convicted, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/01/nx-s1-5950452/tyler-robinson-charlie-kirk-murder-hearing).

The second is leadership. On Sept. 8, 2026, radio host Baron Coleman claimed on X that Erika Kirk would step down as CEO and remain as board chair, a claim the Hindustan Times [reported was amplified by others](https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/us-news/erika-kirk-to-step-down-as-tpusa-ceo-explosive-claims-surface-a-year-after-charlie-kirks-death-101788826103163.html) before Kolvet reposted it with the words "News to me!" The report noted that neither Erika Kirk nor Turning Point has made any statement about her stepping down. The claim remains unconfirmed.

## Can a personality movement outlive its personality?

Observers on both sides of the aisle agree the organization is not what it was a year ago. "Turning Point is, in the fall of 2026, not the same organization it was in August 2025," Doug Cole, chief operating officer of the public affairs firm HighGround, told the Arizona Republic. Arizona voters showed a willingness to reject Turning Point-backed candidates in the state's July 21 primary, leading some to argue its political influence is waning.

Supporters counter that the mission, not the man, is the point. "The support is there financially," Brandon Tatum, a Scottsdale influencer and Turning Point contributor, told the Arizona Republic. "I don't anticipate that they would go down because the mission is still out there."

Whether that holds will be tested this fall, as Erika Kirk leads the organization into a school year without the founder who defined it. For a movement born of one voice, the coming months will answer a question that every personality-driven institution eventually faces: what remains when the personality is gone?
