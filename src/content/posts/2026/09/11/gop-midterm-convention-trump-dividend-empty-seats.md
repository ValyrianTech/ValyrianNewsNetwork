# Empty Seats and Trump's 'Dividend' at GOP Convention

DALLAS — President Donald Trump headlined the Republican Party's first-ever midterm convention this week, closing a two-day event that party leaders hoped would energize the base ahead of November's elections. Instead, the gathering at the American Airlines Center was defined by a familiar paradox: Trump as both the GOP's greatest turnout engine and its biggest general-election liability. As [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/11/nx-s1-5962848/republican-convention-trump-dividend-vance-midterms) reported, the convention drew attention for thousands of empty seats and an audacious, and possibly unfulfillable, spending promise.

## A Party Rallies Around Its President

Held Sept. 9-10, the convention was the first of its kind for Republicans. National Republicans branded the gathering "Trumpapalooza," the [Texas Tribune](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/09/08/republican-midterm-convention-dallas-texas-trump-paxton-2026/) reported, while [The New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/09/08/us-news/gop-to-rail-against-socialist-dems-at-dallas-midterm-convention-as-some-fear-trump-a-palooza-backlash/) noted that the party styled it a "Trump-a-Palooza" and that Democrats are not hosting a midterm convention of their own.

The event's production left little doubt about its central figure. Credentials featured Trump's likeness, images of the president were displayed throughout the arena, and speech after speech praised him. Trump headlined both nights, while Vice President JD Vance delivered the closing keynote — a lineup that underscored how thoroughly the party has organized itself around one man.

## The $5,000 'Trump Dividend'

The convention's headline policy promise came Wednesday when Trump pledged a $5,000 payment to every adult American if Republicans retain control of the House and Senate. "If the Republicans win, you win with us and you get 5,000 dollars," Trump said. "It will be called the Trump dividend."

The proposal immediately ran into mathematical and logistical obstacles. As [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/10/trump-dividend-midterms-gop.html) reported, giving roughly 245 million U.S. adult citizens $5,000 apiece would cost north of $1.2 trillion and would require congressional approval, with the House scheduled to be in session for only one week before the November elections. Within an hour of Trump's remarks, Vance scaled the promise back, suggesting the wealthy would be excluded and that the funds would come from tariff revenue. But the Congressional Budget Office estimates annual tariff revenue at roughly $167 billion, far short of what the plan would require.

The promise drew criticism from an unusual coalition. Democratic Rep. Dan Goldman of New York called it corrupt and "blatantly illegal," while Rep. Jamie Raskin of Maryland described it as a political bribe that would add $1.3 trillion to the deficit. Republicans joined in: Palantir co-founder and GOP donor Joe Lonsdale called the plan "bread and circus bribes," Rep. Chip Roy of Texas asked how it would be paid for, and former Freedom Caucus Chair Bob Good described it as a "socialist vote-buying scheme." Republican Gov. Ron DeSantis of Florida also denounced the pledge, saying at a press conference in Miami that the money would only cause more debt and inflation, [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/10/nx-s1-5963880/trump-republican-midterm-convention-vance) reported.

Legal analysts cited by [USA TODAY](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/09/10/trump-dividend-5000-checks-legal/91688688007/) noted that federal law bars paying or offering money to influence voting, though most experts concluded a universal promise is likely protected political speech, because a payout offered to all adults is not a targeted inducement to vote.

It was also not the first time such a pledge has failed to materialize. As [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/trump-5000-dollar-dividend/) noted, Trump previously floated $2,000 tariff dividends and $5,000 DOGE-funded stimulus checks, neither of which came to pass.

## An Enthusiasm Problem, in Plain Sight

If the convention was designed to project strength, the optics told a different story. Both days saw thousands of empty seats, including almost all of the arena's upper level, even as Trump insisted the venue was "packed" with thousands more waiting outside. [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/were-seats-empty-at-rnc-midterm-convention-what-photos-videos-show-12423679) documented the sparse crowds in photos and video from the event, estimating the arena at roughly 65 to 70 percent full as Trump took the stage on the first night.

Trump's own remarks betrayed the stakes. "I'm asking you to pretend that I'm on the ballot," he told attendees on the first night. "You know what happens if you don't vote? You go to hell," he said on the second.

The plea reflected a structural challenge: Trump's coalition does not reliably turn out in non-presidential years. Democrats, by contrast, have shown heightened enthusiasm since 2024, flipping state and local seats and improving their margins across nearly every demographic group. Meanwhile, the Republican base has fractured over the war in Iran, the release of the Epstein files, rising gas prices and tariff-driven costs.

## Attacks Over Policy

As candidates honed their general-election messaging, policy often took a back seat to personal attacks and ideological labeling. Speakers repeatedly characterized Democrats as socialists, communists and Marxists, with particular focus on Michigan Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed. "Abdul El-Sayed may be comfortable with that crowd. I certainly am not," former Rep. Mike Rogers said. "He's a con man. I'm a law man."

Trump himself declared that "the Democrat establishment has been overthrown and replaced by crazed lunatics and radicals," adding, "They call themselves Democrat socialists, but actually they're communists." Texas Sen. Ted Cruz went further, warning of "communists and Islamists coming together and sweeping over the Democrat Party" and saying that "Shariah law has no place in America," according to NPR.

Notably absent from the messaging was the ongoing war in Iran, which has driven diesel prices above $6 a gallon and crude oil to near-record highs. Vance addressed the conflict only indirectly, urging voters not to "throw the baby out with the bathwater" by backing Democrats.

## 2028 Looms

The convention also served as an early stage for the next presidential cycle. Vance, Cruz and Donald Trump Jr. — all seen as potential 2028 contenders — were among the speakers, though there was no clear passing of the torch. Attendees interviewed by NPR's Tamara Keith already looked past Trump's presidency. "Trump is kind of a one time thing," said convention-goer Meredith Smith. Her friend Sherri Hopson added: "[Trump's] built this Republican Party and the bench is deep. I have a lot of faith in JD Vance and Marco Rubio and I really hope they run as a ticket."

## What's Next

The convention arrived after Labor Day, the traditional kickoff of the general-election season, with polling averages showing Republicans at risk of losing both chambers. Democratic candidates have outraised and outspent their opponents in many key races, though GOP committees and Trump's MAGA, Inc. super PAC retain hundreds of millions of dollars to deploy in the closing weeks.

Whether that spending can overcome the enthusiasm gap the convention inadvertently displayed is the central question of the campaign's final stretch.
