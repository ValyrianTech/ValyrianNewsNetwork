# Flanders Approves Sweeping Land-Use Reform Plan

After months of delay and internal coalition friction, the Flemish government reached agreement on Friday on the Beleidsplan Ruimte Vlaanderen (BRV), a spatial planning blueprint that will shape how Flanders uses its land for decades. The plan, driven by Environment and Agriculture Minister Jo Brouns of the CD&V party, earmarks 30,000 additional hectares of protected open space, plans 450,000 new homes by 2050 through densification, and commits the region to net-zero additional land take by 2040, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/11/beleidsplan-ruimte-vlaanderen/).

The BRV replaces the Ruimtelijk Structuurplan Vlaanderen (RSV) drawn up in 1997, a document nearly three decades old. It sets the framework for where building is permitted in Flanders — and where it is not — as the region grapples with urban sprawl, a growing housing crunch and mounting climate pressures.

## Context: A Region Running Out of Open Space

About one third of Flanders' surface is already occupied by human activity — housing, gardens, industry, transport, sports fields and parks. Every day, an additional 3.8 hectares of open land is still consumed, placing pressure on space needed for biodiversity, food production and water retention, according to the [Flemish Department of Environment](https://omgeving.vlaanderen.be/beleidsplan-ruimte-vlaanderen).

The centerpiece of the plan is the so-called "bouwshift" or "betonstop" — a commitment to reach net-zero additional land take by 2040. Without it, experts have warned that Flanders risks losing tens of thousands of hectares of open space.

## A Balancing Act Between Farmers and Nature

Under the plan, 30,000 hectares of currently unbuilt land with hard development designations will be re-designated as protected open space. Half — 15,000 hectares, roughly the size of the city of Ghent — will become irrevocably agricultural land, while the other 15,000 hectares will be designated for nature and forest.

The agricultural provision was politically delicate. The plan starts from the 750,000 hectares of agrarian area designated under the old RSV, of which only around 645,000 hectares is actually in agricultural use. An additional 15,000 hectares will now receive an agricultural designation — a figure farm organizations frame as 765,000 hectares recognized as farmland.

"I think this is a very important turnaround in which we have chosen to give part of it to agriculture," Brouns said. "We also opt for sustainable agriculture, for agriculture that must be brought within ecological boundaries."

Farm groups welcomed the move. "We are satisfied that the Flemish government recognizes 765,000 hectares of agricultural land as farmland and thus confirms the strategic importance of our agricultural and horticultural sector," said Lode Ceyssens, chairman of Boerenbond, as [VILT](https://vilt.be/nl/nieuws/vlaanderen-heeft-akkoord-over-nieuw-ruimtelijk-beleid-meer-ontharding-verdichting-en-open-ruimte) reported. Justine Arkens, chairwoman of the young farmers' group Groene Kring, added: "Every square meter we lose puts the future of our young farmers further at risk. What disappears now never comes back."

Nature organizations were far less enthusiastic. "Instead of an inspiring vision for a prosperous and healthy Flanders with room for 30 percent nature, the Beleidsplan Ruimte Vlaanderen remains stuck in the zoning logic of the 1990s," said Frederik Mollen of Natuurpunt. "Here and there, paper hectares are shuffled around to keep everyone happy, but no fundamental choices are made to arm Flanders for the climate challenge."

## Housing: Splitting Large Homes, Living Smaller

To accommodate an estimated 450,000 additional homes by 2050, the government is betting on densification rather than expansion. The plan proposes making it easier to split large homes into smaller units, reusing vacant buildings, adding extra storeys where neighborhoods can handle it, and promoting "zorgwonen" — care living arrangements.

The shift reflects changing demographics: more singles, smaller families and elderly residents aging in place. "People simply live differently than years ago," said Housing Minister Melissa Depraetere of Vooruit. "There are many more singles, smaller families, more elderly. Either we organize a dating agency from Flanders… or we of course have to divide the space better. And that is what we are doing here today."

## Economy, Water and Energy

For businesses, the plan promises more flexibility for expansion on existing sites — including higher buildings and shared infrastructure — while guaranteeing no net loss of business parks compared with 2024. Roughly 62,600 hectares are currently reserved for economic activity, equivalent to 88,000 football fields. "We want to keep that at that level. Net, no business park may be lost compared with 2024," said Minister-President Matthias Diependaele of N-VA.

On water, the plan targets the de-paving of 13,000 hectares by 2040 compared with 2025, aimed at improving drought and flood resilience. In predefined flood zones, the government is preparing a right of first refusal and purchase obligation — though officials stressed this is not a general obligation for residents to sell.

On energy, the plan seeks better use of rooftops, car parks and existing infrastructure for solar power, easier siting of wind turbines, and co-location of energy-intensive installations that produce residual heat — such as data centers — near matching heat demand.

## Local Deals and Unresolved Questions

A distinctive feature is the "local bouwshift deal": each municipality can strike a tailored agreement with the Flemish government. "Every local authority must be able to close a tailor-made deal with the Flemish government," said Home Affairs Minister Hilde Crevits of CD&V. "The city of Antwerp, for example, has completely different needs than the city of Torhout."

Yet the plan is far from final. It still requires consultation with local authorities, advice from advisory councils and a public inquiry before it can be finalized — a process expected to conclude in 2027. Additional financing also remains to be found. "The available resources today cover only part of the full task. The financing requires further elaboration," the government said.

Criticism has come from within the governing coalition itself. N-VA MP Sanne Van Looy has questioned the plan's length and its agricultural-land claim, arguing that retaining 782,000 hectares of land juridically designated for agriculture is an unrealistic demand. As she [told VILT](https://vilt.be/nl/nieuws/is-landbouw-struikelblok-voor-beleidsplan-ruimte-vlaanderen), citing the planological figure rather than actual use: "Today, about 782,000 hectares in Flanders are juridically designated for agriculture. The previous policy plan set a shrinkage to 750,000 hectares, so that there would also be room for industry, nature, housing and recreation. Actual agricultural use today is only 650,000 hectares. But the new plan still insists on holding onto those 782,000 hectares. That is an unrealistic claim that nobody will digest well."

Van Looy later softened her remarks in a response to VILT, saying a new spatial plan remains "super necessary" and that she wanted to give the government "every opportunity to reach a broadly supported compromise."

## What to Watch

The approval marks a significant political milestone, but Brouns was quick to frame it as a beginning rather than an end. "The approval is an important step, but really this is only the start," he said. "In the coming months and years we must reshape Flanders based on this plan. That will still be an enormous task."

With a consultation round, a public inquiry and a still-unresolved financing question ahead, the coming months will test whether the coalition can hold its fragile compromise together — and whether a plan designed to define Flanders' landscape for a generation can survive contact with the communities it will reshape.
