# GOP Midterm Convention: Empty Seats, $5,000 Dividend

DALLAS — The Republican Party's first-ever midterm convention, held September 9-10 at the American Airlines Center, was designed to fire up the base ahead of the November 3 elections. Instead, it showcased the paradox at the heart of the GOP's midterm strategy: the party's greatest motivator and its greatest liability are the same man, President Donald Trump.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/11/nx-s1-5962848/republican-convention-trump-dividend-vance-midterms), the event — billed internally as a "Trumpapalooza," with credentials emblazoned with the president's likeness — was dominated by three themes: thousands of empty seats, sustained attacks branding Democrats as socialists and communists, and an overwhelming focus on Trump personally. The convention's signature moment came on its opening night, when Trump promised a $5,000 "Trump dividend" to every adult American citizen — but only if Republicans retain control of both the House and the Senate.

## A Staggering, Undefined Price Tag

The proposal drew immediate, bipartisan pushback. There are roughly 245.3 million U.S. adult citizens, and at $5,000 each the payout would cost more than $1.2 trillion, as [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/10/trump-dividend-midterms-gop.html) reported. That is more than all three rounds of COVID-era stimulus checks combined — with no recession to justify it.

"This would cost about $1.2 TRILLION in just one year," Marc Goldwein of the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget said, per NPR. "If this works, the lesson for every future politician is going to be: if you want to win an election, promise a trillion dollars."

Trump suggested tariff revenue could fund the payments, citing "$21 trillion in U.S. investment" — a figure repeatedly fact-checked as false and contradicted by the White House's own website, which lists nearly $10 trillion. Rep. Chip Roy, a leader of the fiscally conservative Freedom Caucus, told Politico he wanted to know how the administration planned to pay for it.

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick offered a different answer. "It's not tax money," he [told NBC News](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/12/trump-midterm-dividend-funding), floating a plan to charge wealthy visitors up to $5 million to extend their visas — which he claimed could raise $500 billion — alongside the government's appreciating Intel stake. National Economic Council Director Kevin Hassett told Bloomberg TV the White House was weighing a congressional "reconciliation process."

Fiscal analysts were unpersuaded. Tariffs are taxes on imports paid by importers, who generally pass costs to consumers. The Penn Wharton Budget Model estimates Trump's import duties brought in roughly $300 billion between January 2025 and July 2026 — and more than $100 billion of that was refunded after the Supreme Court struck down a swath of the tariffs as illegal. A past proposal to send $2,000 tariff rebates exceeded the revenue actually raised, according to the nonpartisan Tax Foundation.

## Legal Questions Loom

Beyond the math, the pledge raised legal concerns. Under U.S. federal law, offering or making a payment to induce someone to vote, withhold their vote, or vote for or against a candidate is a criminal offense, punishable by a fine, up to one year in prison, or both. Critics — including some conservatives — branded the offer "bribery" and "vote-buying."

"I am hugely in favor of winning the midterms, and love much about this admin, but am strongly against bread and circus bribes," Palantir co-founder and GOP donor Joe Lonsdale said on X, as CNBC reported. Former House Freedom Caucus Chair Bob Good questioned whether "post-conservative Republicans" would defend what he called a "$5K 'dividend' socialist vote-buying scheme."

Democrats dismissed the promise outright. "The dividend is not happening," Rep. Robert Garcia (D-Calif.) said, noting that earlier pledges — a DOGE dividend and a tariff rebate — had both evaporated. California Gov. Gavin Newsom called it "$5,000 in taxpayer-funded blood money."

The proposal unfolded against a bleak fiscal backdrop. U.S. national debt surpassed $40 trillion for the first time in August 2026, up from $10 trillion in 2008. The federal deficit is nearing $1.8 trillion for the fiscal year to date, while the government already spends roughly $1.3 trillion on interest payments — more than on defense or Medicare.

## Empty Seats and an Enthusiasm Gap

The optics inside the arena matched the party's polling troubles. Both nights saw thousands of empty seats, including almost all of the upper level, even as Trump insisted the arena was "packed" with thousands more waiting outside.

"I'm asking you to pretend that I'm on the ballot," Trump implored on the first day. "You know what happens if you don't vote? You go to hell," he said on the second night.

That plea reflected a strategic bet: that Trump's coattails can carry Republicans even when his name isn't on the ticket. Yet the convention ran against the NFL season opener and a second primetime football game, and fewer than half of Republican candidates in competitive House and Senate races were listed as speakers, according to an NPR analysis.

Vice President JD Vance, seen as the party's likely 2028 heir apparent, closed the convention with a plea for voters not to "throw the baby out with the bathwater." "Don't give the country over to a bunch of crazy people because you happen to disagree with us on a policy issue here or there," he said. His speech was briefly interrupted by a protester carrying a Mexican flag.

The party's message leaned heavily on personal attacks and the "-isms." Michigan GOP Senate nominee Mike Rogers called his Democratic opponent Abdul El-Sayed a "con man," while Texas Sen. Ted Cruz warned of "communists and Islamists coming together and sweeping over the Democrat Party."

Notably absent from the stage was sustained discussion of the ongoing war in Iran — a conflict Trump campaigned against, but which has driven diesel above $6 a gallon and crude oil to near-record highs, weighing on the affordability concerns dominating the race.

## What's Next

The stakes are steep. Polling averages show Republicans at risk of losing the majority in both chambers, and the historic pattern is that the party in power loses midterms. An AP poll found only about 32% of U.S. adults approve of Trump's handling of the economy, down from 40% at the start of his second term.

Trump's own framing may be the sharpest acknowledgment of the challenge. "We might not win," he told the crowd. Still, Republicans are banking on hundreds of millions of dollars in late ad spending from party committees and Trump's MAGA, Inc. super PAC to close the gap.

With mail ballots already being returned in states like North Carolina, the window is narrowing. Whether the Trump dividend becomes a rallying cry or a cautionary footnote may depend on whether voters see it as a promise kept — or the third in a series of payouts that never arrived.
