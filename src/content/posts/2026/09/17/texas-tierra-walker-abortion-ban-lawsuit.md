# Family Sues Texas Officials Over Abortion-Ban Death

The family of a San Antonio woman who died at 20 weeks pregnant has filed a wrongful death lawsuit against Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, state medical regulators and several doctors, alleging that the state's near-total abortion ban created a climate of fear that cost Tierra Walker her life ([Texas Public Radio](https://www.tpr.org/news/2026-09-16/tierra-walkers-family-sues-texas-officials-doctors-alleging-abortion-ban-contributed-to-her-death)).

The suit, filed Tuesday, Sept. 15, in Bexar County District Court, contends that Walker, a 37-year-old dental assistant, repeatedly sought to end a wanted pregnancy as her health deteriorated, but that more than 90 doctors involved in her care never offered her the procedure. She died in late December 2024 of preeclampsia and hypertensive cardiovascular disease, just days after being discharged with dangerously high blood pressure.

"At every turn, Texas's political and medical establishments not only failed but actively harmed Ms. Walker," the lawsuit alleges, according to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/texas-abortion-death-lawsuit-tierra-walker-ken-paxton/). "Tierra Walker is dead because not one of her healthcare providers would give her the one medical procedure that would have saved her life."

## A Wanted Pregnancy, a Spiraling Health Crisis

Walker learned she was about five to eight weeks pregnant on Sept. 19, 2024, during an emergency room visit after her 14-year-old son, JJ, called 911 when she began shaking uncontrollably. She had spent years managing chronic conditions — obesity, high blood pressure, asthma and Type 2 diabetes — and had previously suffered a stillbirth of twins after developing severe preeclampsia three years earlier.

By mid-2024 her health had been improving, with lower blood pressure and weight loss aided by a GLP-1 drug. The pregnancy reversed that progress. Over the following months she was hospitalized for weeks at a time, suffering seizures, blood clots and blood pressure that at one point reached 174/115.

Her family says she raised the possibility of terminating the pregnancy many times. "I asked them time and time again to give her an abortion," her aunt, LaTanya Walker, told CBS News. "And they would tell me … 'It's her, but it's not the baby.'"

According to her attorneys, Walker first asked for an abortion on Oct. 12, 2024, and again on Oct. 30, when she was about 11 weeks pregnant. In late October and early November, two doctors documented that she was at "high" risk of death. Yet she was discharged and readmitted repeatedly. On Dec. 27, at about 20 weeks, she was diagnosed with preeclampsia — but sent home again that night, even though her discharge paperwork noted that delivery may be the best treatment for the condition.

Three days later, JJ found his mother unresponsive in her bed. It was his 15th birthday. An autopsy found she had died of hypertensive cardiovascular disease and preeclampsia, with an enlarged, fluid-filled heart and kidney damage. The fetus was healthy, weighing just under a pound.

## The Legal Claims

The lawsuit names Paxton, Texas Medical Board Executive Director Stephen Brint Carlton, UT Health San Antonio, multiple Bexar County entities and officials, Bexar County District Attorney Joe Gonzales and several individual doctors. It accuses Paxton and Carlton of depriving Walker of her right to life by enforcing "a merciless blanket prohibition" on abortion, and argues the ban itself is unconstitutional.

"The basic principle here is that you cannot take away someone's life as a state and not be held liable for doing so," attorney Molly Duane said at a Wednesday news conference, per Texas Public Radio.

The family is represented by Amplify Legal, the litigation arm of Abortion in America, and the Marynell Maloney Law Firm. Attorneys said the two-year statute of limitations — not politics — drove the timing of the filing, which came as Paxton campaigns in a competitive U.S. Senate race. Paxton's office did not respond to requests for comment; UT Health San Antonio said it had no information to offer.

A central factual dispute concerns the medical record. LaTanya Walker says her niece used the word "abortion" when asking doctors to end the pregnancy. But CBS News, after reviewing 6,500 pages of records, found that doctors never documented such a request — nor did they offer one. Duane said that omission is itself evidence of the ban's chilling effect. "Doctors are so scared to even write the word in their medical chart that it does not appear there," she said.

The Texas Medical Board said in a statement that physicians are "not required to wait until a mother's life is in immediate danger" before providing necessary care, which may include terminating a pregnancy. University Health said its priority is "to provide medically appropriate and timely care, while safeguarding patient privacy and complying with all applicable laws." Gonzales's office said he has not prosecuted any abortion cases.

## A Pattern Across Texas

Walker's death was first documented in a November 2025 [ProPublica investigation](https://www.propublica.org/article/texas-abortion-ban-tierra-walker-preeclampsia), which condensed more than 6,500 pages of her records with two high-risk pregnancy specialists. More than a dozen OB-GYNs reviewed the case and described her condition as a "ticking time bomb"; every expert believed an abortion at any point would have saved her life. ProPublica has identified Walker as at least the fourth documented Texas woman — and the 12th nationally — to die under such circumstances.

The case is the latest in a series of legal and political aftershocks from Texas' restrictions. Texas banned abortion in 2021 via SB 8, before Roe v. Wade was overturned, and its trigger ban took effect in August 2022. The law prohibits abortion from the moment of fertilization, with an exception only when a pregnancy poses a risk of death or substantial impairment of a major bodily function. Providers face up to 99 years in prison, fines and loss of their licenses.

In 2025, lawmakers passed SB 31, the "Life of the Mother Act," to clarify those medical exceptions, stating an emergency no longer needs to be "imminent" — though it must still be "life-threatening." Doctors say uncertainty has persisted anyway. Dr. Emily Briggs, an OB-GYN in the greater San Antonio area, told CBS News that the law "made the situation a little bit clarified," but added: "We still have a hesitation. And that hesitation is not just with me. That's with, I would say, a bulk of my colleagues who provide the same care that I do."

Dr. Damla Karsan, a Houston OB-GYN, told reporters that "Tierra's pregnancy would have ended with the same result today."

Not everyone attributes the death to the law. Dr. Christina Francis, CEO of the American Association of Pro-Life Obstetricians and Gynecologists, said Walker's death "was likely preventable and the result of inadequate medical care; it was certainly not the fault of Texas' abortion law." She argued that aggressive blood pressure control and continued hospitalization would likely have saved her.

Other physicians disagreed sharply. "She didn't have to die. I don't think she would have died if she was in a different state," said Dr. Kameelah Phillips, a New York City OB-GYN who reviewed part of the records. Dr. Céline Gounder, a CBS News medical correspondent, said guidelines called for Walker to be admitted, stabilized and given anti-seizure medication — with her pregnancy terminated.

## What to Watch

The suit seeks to hold individual officials personally liable and to challenge the constitutionality of Texas' ban — a legal theory that faces steep odds, as similar challenges have generally failed. The immediate questions are whether Paxton, Carlton and the named hospitals and doctors will seek dismissal, and what defenses they will raise.

As the [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/16/pregnant-woman-denied-abortion-ken-paxton-lawsuit) noted, reports have surfaced since Texas' 2021 ban of doctors denying or delaying abortion care even in emergencies, with some patients reportedly reaching the brink of death before staff intervened. Whether this case changes that practice — or the law behind it — may depend less on the courtroom than on the campaign trail and the next legislative session.

For LaTanya Walker, the stakes are simpler. "Her life mattered," she said. "And I wouldn't want this to happen to anyone else."
