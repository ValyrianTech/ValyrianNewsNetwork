# Sikh Truck Driver Stabbed in Wyoming Amid Hate Surge

A Punjabi Sikh truck driver was followed into a rest-area bathroom on a Wyoming interstate and stabbed repeatedly early Sunday morning, in an unprovoked attack that has intensified fears across the South Asian community at a moment of rising anti-immigrant rhetoric.

The victim, identified only by his surname, Singh, told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/17/nx-s1-5971866/truck-driver-attack-wyoming-sikh-south-asian) that he was attacked from behind without warning while using the restroom at the Bitter Creek Rest Area on eastbound Interstate 80, about 41 miles east of Rock Springs. He sustained 17 lacerations, including two stab wounds to his neck and two to his chest, and was airlifted to Wyoming Medical Center in Casper, where he remained hospitalized as of Wednesday night.

A suspect, 45-year-old Andrew Kris Bzdak, was arrested roughly 90 minutes after the first 911 call and charged Monday with attempted first-degree murder. According to a detailed account of the charging documents first reported by [Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2026/09/15/truck-driver-accused-of-stabbing-man-17-times-in-i-80-restroom-before-fleeing/), investigators have not established a motive.

## A Trail of Blood and a Family's Quick Thinking

The attack unfolded in less than a minute, according to the charging documents. Sheriff's deputies were dispatched at about 3:27 a.m. Sept. 13. A camera on a semi parked nearby recorded Singh walking casually toward the restroom; a short time later, a man in a black sweater and blue jeans approached quickly and followed him inside. Less than a minute after he entered, the attacker allegedly bolted out in tennis shoes and fled in his semi-truck.

Investigators documented large amounts of blood inside the restroom, in the lobby, outside the building and beside a parked truck. A family at the rest area heard "grunting and screaming" from the men's room, saw a man run from the building and followed his semi-truck east toward Rawlins long enough to photograph its identifying details. A Wyoming Highway Patrol trooper spotted the truck near Rawlins at about 5 a.m.; Bzdak was taken into custody without chase or confrontation.

Singh said a Hispanic family at the rest area tended to his wounds and sought medical help, actions he credited with saving his life.

"They [gave] me the second life," he said.

## A Flashpoint for a National Conversation

While investigators have not called the stabbing a hate crime, advocates argue it cannot be separated from a broader climate of hostility toward South Asian and Sikh Americans.

"The Sheriff's Office has said there is no known motive at this time, but we cannot separate this incident from the climate in which it happened," said Jasjit Singh, an attorney and director of legal services at the Jakara Movement, a California-based organization supporting the victim.

The attack came days after the Department of Homeland Security posted an image on social media depicting an Indian man with the words "Get off our roads, you don't know how to drive Mr. Singh." The post was removed the next day following backlash from political leaders and advocacy groups, who criticized it as racist and dehumanizing to an entire community. The [Sikh Coalition](https://www.sikhcoalition.org/blog/2026/take-action-against-new-dhs-profiling-program-social-media-posts-that-target-sikh-truckers/) had warned the post reflected a broader pattern of profiling Sikh truck drivers.

DHS told NPR it "will not speculate on motives" regarding the Wyoming attack, adding that "any attempt to blame DHS for an unrelated attack is RECKLESS and FALSE."

## Data Shows a Surging Tide of Hate

The stabbing coincides with documented increases in anti-South Asian hate. A report [published](https://stopaapihate.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Stop-AAPI-Hate-TheStateofAntiSouthAsianHate-September-2026.pdf) this month by Stop AAPI Hate found that slurs used online against South Asians rose 109% between January 2023 and December 2025. Anti-South Asian slurs in online spaces associated with targeted violence climbed from 22,996 in January 2023 to 48,033 by December 2025, accounting for 66% of all anti-Asian slurs in those spaces.

The report, detailed by [India-West](https://indiawest.com/anti-south-asian-slurs-surge-109-in-us/), also found that 48% of South Asian adults reported experiencing a hate act in 2025 because of their race, ethnicity or nationality. South Asians accounted for 71% of all anti-Asian threats of violence recorded between August 2024 and December 2025. The report attributes the rise to a combination of factors, including the Florida truck crash, President Trump's re-election, debates over H-1B visas, and the New York City mayoral campaign of Zohran Mamdani.

Separately, an analysis of preliminary 2025 FBI data by Asian Americans Advancing Justice, reported by [American Community Media](https://americancommunitymedia.org/spotlight-ethnic-media/anti-south-asian-hate-surges-online/), found that anti-Asian hate crimes have more than doubled compared with pre-pandemic levels—about 133 incidents per year from 2013 to 2018, compared with 318 in 2025. Hate crimes against Sikhs, Hindus and Buddhists rose to their highest recorded levels, with Sikhs remaining the third most targeted religious group.

## The Trucking Crackdown Context

The attack lands in the middle of an escalating federal crackdown on immigrant truck drivers that advocates say has put Sikh drivers at greater risk. Since 2024, the administration has issued rules barring non-domiciled individuals without citizenship or permanent residency from holding commercial driver's licenses—regulations that took effect in March 2026 and target thousands of immigrant Sikh truck drivers.

Mannirmal Kaur, senior federal policy manager at the Sikh Coalition, said the surge in bias is layered.

"Racial hatred against the Sikh community is now compounded with a new wave of bias-based, anti-immigrant sentiment, both in public discourse and from the highest levels of our government," Kaur said. "This absolutely translates to a spike in hate violence that our community experiences."

Raman Dhillon, head of the North American Punjabi Trucking Association, said drivers have reached out to him since the attack with concerns about their own safety.

"It's on everybody's mind," he said. "We never thought in this country that we will ever gonna think about things like this."

## A 25-Year Echo

The attack and the official rhetoric surrounding it come almost exactly 25 years after the post-9/11 surge of hate and discrimination against Sikhs—a period that included the 2001 murder of Balbir Singh Sodhi in Mesa, Arizona, the first post-9/11 hate-crime fatality. The Sikh Coalition held a memorial for Sodhi on Sept. 15.

Singh, who lives in Sacramento and has driven trucks for three years, said he was aware of the rhetoric targeting his community. Although it worried him, he said it did not stop him from working because he needed the income. As he recovers, he said he is stressed about medical bills and missed work.

"I am scared and also upset because I have [to] work regularly," he said. He added that he worries for other Sikh drivers: "I am just worried … it can happen again."

## What's Next

Bzdak is being held in the Sweetwater County Detention Center; attempted first-degree murder carries the possibility of life imprisonment under Wyoming law. Prosecutors have alleged he acted with premeditated malice, though they have not offered a motive.

Key questions remain: whether authorities will add a hate-crime enhancement, how the victim's experience reshapes the national debate over the trucking crackdown, and whether the federal government adjusts a profiling program that advocacy groups say has made drivers less safe. For now, a community already on edge is left waiting—and watching the road.
