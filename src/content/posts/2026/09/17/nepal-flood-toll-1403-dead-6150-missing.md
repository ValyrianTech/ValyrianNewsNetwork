# Nepal Flood Toll Hits 1,403 Dead, 6,150 Missing

Nepali authorities say at least 1,403 bodies have been recovered and 6,150 people remain missing after catastrophic flash floods tore through the country's Himalayan valleys on 26 August, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20260917/f2b288e2e08c4f12afcfbaa85713d280/c.html). The revised figures, issued by the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority (NDRRMA) on 16 September, place the disaster among the deadliest in the region in recent memory—and expose deep gaps in the systems meant to warn people in harm's way.

## A Glacier Collapse, Then a Wall of Water

The disaster began high on the north face of Langtang Lirung in Nepal's Langtang National Park, where a section of glacier broke away at roughly 5,200 metres and plunged some 1,200 metres into the valley below, according to an [Al Jazeera explainer](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/27/nepal-tibet-floods-what-happened-what-caused-them-and-who-is-missing). The resulting ice-rock avalanche struck the Lhende River, which feeds the Bhote Koshi and then the Trishuli, temporarily damming the flow before the natural barrier gave way and released a surge of water, mud and boulders downstream.

Early reports suggested a magnitude 4.4 earthquake had triggered the collapse. The United States Geological Survey later revised that assessment, concluding the seismic signal was generated by the landslide itself—which ultimately registered as a magnitude 5.2 event.

Water levels in the Trishuli reportedly rose by as much as nine metres in just 30 minutes. Swept southward through Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha and Chitwan districts, the flood destroyed at least 19 bridges and about 40 kilometres of roads, and carried debris up to 100 kilometres downstream. Some bodies were later recovered as far away as India's Bihar state.

## A Toll Still Climbing, and Mostly Unidentified

The human cost has been staggering and remains deeply uncertain. The NDRRMA's updated count of 6,150 missing—up from 5,179 a day earlier—came after district-level data verification. "We have been collecting details on the missing people from the district offices, and revised the data after verification," NDRRMA spokeswoman Shanti Mahat told AFP, as reported by the [Jamaica Observer](https://www.jamaicaobserver.com/2026/09/16/nepal-raises-flood-missing-toll-nearly-1000-people-6150/).

Of the 1,403 bodies recovered, only 105 had been identified. Across the border, Chinese state media reported 43 dead and 519 missing in the Tibet Autonomous Region, putting the combined toll at 1,446 dead and at least 6,669 missing.

Those unaccounted for include hundreds of foreign nationals. Nepal's Department of Tourism said 668 tourists from 34 countries had been out of contact, and Beijing's foreign ministry said 100 Chinese nationals were among the missing in Nepal. Among the reported missing were 177 Indian nationals, 90 from the United States, 34 from Australia, 33 from the United Kingdom and 25 from Canada. Many were pilgrims travelling toward Mount Kailash and Lake Mansarovar in Tibet.

## An Early Warning That Came Too Late

The scale of the loss has focused attention on whether more lives could have been saved. Nepal's Department of Mines and Geology recorded seismic activity at magnitude 4.4 but did not issue a public alert, because the signal's frequency did not match that of an earthquake, which is the department's monitoring mandate.

"Our records showed a 4.4 magnitude. But the frequency of the seismic activity did not match that of an earthquake, which is the department's monitoring mandate, so we did not alert the public like we usually do," Lok Vijay Adhikari, chief of the National Earthquake Monitoring and Research Center, told the [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2026/09/02/nepal-sent-its-disaster-alert-38-minutes-after-the-glacier-collapse-it-wasn-t-enough).

A hydrological gauge on the Bhote Koshi at Syabrubesi was swept away within minutes—designed to detect gradual rises, it was ill-equipped for a high-speed flood. The first text alerts reached residents of Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading and Chitwan roughly 38 minutes after the collapse, with survivor Nima Dorje Tamang telling the Post he received his message only some minutes after that.

"There is no such thing as an early warning system in Nepal. Simply having sensors does not mean it's a system; it has to be much more comprehensive," said Anil Pokhrel, former chief executive of the NDRRMA, describing a fragmented arrangement with no mandatory protocol for cross-border alerts—despite a 2019 agreement between the Chinese and Nepali presidents to deepen cooperation.

## A Warming Mountain Region

Scientists have not directly attributed this specific collapse to climate change, but they place it in a rapidly changing landscape. Glaciers across the Hindu Kush Himalaya lost ice 65 percent faster between 2011 and 2020 than in the previous decade, and Nepal's glaciers have shed close to one-third of their volume in roughly three decades. As permafrost weakens and meltwater accumulates, mountain slopes grow more unstable.

Himanshu Thakkar, coordinator of the South Asia Network on Dams, Rivers & People, argued that hydropower development in the Trishuli basin—home to 36 projects at various stages—compounds the danger. "Our dishonest EIAs, lack of accountability in governance and non-implementation of basic monitoring, forecasting and implementable disaster management plans is worsening the disaster impacts in river valleys with multiple hydropower projects," he told the Kathmandu Post. The floods damaged 13 hydropower plants in Rasuwa and Nuwakot, disconnecting roughly 431 megawatts—about a tenth of Nepal's power—from the national grid.

## Food, Farms and a Costly Rebuild

The economic and food-security consequences are still mounting. Preliminary assessments found that 1,806 hectares of farmland were damaged, with food production in affected areas expected to fall by 7,686 tonnes, according to the [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2026/09/07/floods-wash-away-1-806-hectares-of-farmland-threaten-food-supplies). Experts warn the losses could raise Nepal's reliance on imported rice and push consumer prices higher.

Nepal now estimates the reconstruction bill will reach US$4.78 billion—a heavy burden for a largely rural nation of 30 million that contributes a fraction of global greenhouse gas emissions.

## What to Watch

Recovery teams are still searching for the missing, and officials have warned the death toll is likely to rise as rescuers reach areas that remain inaccessible. A lake that formed at the site of the collapse has raised fears of further flooding downstream.

In the longer term, the disaster has sharpened a debate about accountability—over early warning systems, dam-building in fragile valleys, and who bears responsibility when hazards cross borders. Prime Minister Balendra Shah is expected to take Nepal's case to the UN General Assembly, where he will press the climate impacts on nations that have done least to cause them. For the families still waiting for answers, those arguments arrive long after the water did.
