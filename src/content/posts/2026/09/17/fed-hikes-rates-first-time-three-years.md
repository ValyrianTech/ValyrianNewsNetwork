# Fed Hikes Rates for First Time in Three Years

The US Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by 25 basis points on Wednesday, lifting the federal funds target range to 3.75%–4.00% — its first increase in more than three years, [Xinhua reported](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20260917/c6d771bba3134eea842d400459558d85/c.html). The unanimous decision reverses the central bank's prior easing cycle and signals that officials expect at least one more hike before year-end, with consequences expected to ripple through global markets, exchange rates and capital flows.

The move, approved 12-0 by the Federal Open Market Committee, is the first rate increase since July 2023 and ends a stretch in which the Fed cut rates six times and then held steady through five consecutive meetings this year.

## A Reversal After Years of Easing

The hike closes a chapter that began in 2022, when the Fed raised rates 11 times to combat post-pandemic inflation. As the economy cooled, it pivoted to six cuts between September 2024 and December 2025, then paused from January through July 2026. Market expectations shifted sharply in the weeks before Wednesday's meeting: the CME FedWatch tool put the probability of a quarter-point hike at 92.3%, up from a 40% chance a week earlier, [according to Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/9/16/us-fed-raises-interest-rates-as-inflation-weighs-on-economy).

Announcing the decision, Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh said inflation had been "too high ... for too long."

"We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed," Warsh said. "Today, the FOMC decided that this standard has not been satisfied."

He cited three factors behind the unanimous vote: a resilient economy and labor market, inflation still above the Fed's 2% target, and tension in the Middle East. "All three of those things lend themselves to a firm unanimous decision today," he said, [according to CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/16/fed-rate-decision-september-2026.html).

## An Unusual Rationale

The Fed typically "looks through" supply-driven inflation such as higher fuel costs from the Iran war and the lingering effects of tariffs, since raising rates cannot lower global oil prices. This time, officials weighed the risk that a sustained energy shock could entrench inflation expectations — a concern sharpened by memories of the "transitory" episode after the pandemic, when officials waited too long and inflation hit 40-year highs.

Warsh acknowledged the limits of monetary policy on commodity prices. The Fed cannot affect oil and food prices, he said, "but what we can do and will do is ensure price increases do not pass through" to the broader economy.

Updated projections underscored the hawkish shift. The Fed's so-called dot plot showed 16 of 18 participants expected another increase this year, with four of those seeing two more, while two expected no further hikes. Warsh himself has chosen not to submit a dot since taking the chair. Officials also nudged their 2026 inflation forecasts higher — headline PCE to 3.7% and core PCE to 3.4%, both up 0.1 percentage point from June — and now do not expect to reach the 2% target until 2029, though they see inflation falling sharply in 2027. The committee lowered its 2026 unemployment outlook to 4.1%, down from 4.3% in June.

## Market Reaction and Global Spillover

Investors initially welcomed the anti-inflation stance. The S&P 500 rose after the announcement and Treasury yields fell — a signal that markets were encouraged by the central bank's effort to tamp down inflation. But sentiment reversed during Warsh's press conference, and US stocks closed lower: the Dow fell 1.21% to its lowest close since mid-June, the S&P 500 slipped 0.44% and the Nasdaq edged down 0.01%. Gold and silver prices dropped, and international oil prices declined sharply, [according to Futures Daily](https://goodsfu.10jqka.com.cn/20260917/c679986989.shtml).

The decision quickly reverberated overseas. Because several Gulf currencies are pegged to the dollar, the central banks of Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Bahrain and Oman each raised rates by 25 basis points. Brazil moved in the opposite direction, cutting its benchmark lending rate by 25 basis points to 13.75%.

Turkey's Borsa Istanbul 100 fell as much as 6% intraday, triggering a market-wide circuit breaker in the worst two-day decline since February 2023. The drop was sparked by a reported default at asset manager Pusula Portfolio, whose funds failed to meet redemption requests — raising fears of broader liquidity stress in Turkey's fund industry after regulators tightened hedge-fund investment limits in late August.

## Bond Market Stress and Political Pressure

The backdrop to the decision has been a sharp rise in borrowing costs. The 10-year Treasury yield broke above the psychologically important 5% threshold on September 14 — its first time since October 2023 — and climbed as high as 5.02% intraday on September 15, the highest in 19 years, [Xinhua noted in a separate analysis](https://www.news.cn/20260915/2d059ec1a37b47bbb96643e992a144e1/c.html). The 30-year yield also exceeded 5%, a roughly 20-year high.

The surge has coincided with a run-up in oil prices, with benchmark crude rising from just above $80 a barrel in late August to more than $100, and with heavy corporate borrowing tied to the artificial intelligence boom, which competes with Treasuries for capital. In August, US federal debt passed $40 trillion for the first time — nearly $10 trillion more than the country's 2025 GDP, and roughly $116,000 per American. Norway's sovereign wealth fund has reportedly advised its finance ministry to reduce holdings of US government bonds to diversify risk.

The hike also placed the Fed on a collision course with the White House. President Donald Trump has repeatedly demanded lower rates, posting on Truth Social that US rates "should be 1%, or less, because we are the Best Credit in the World" and urging the Fed to "LOWER THE INTEREST RATES FOR THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA, AND FAST!" Asked later whether he still had confidence in Warsh, Trump said he did.

White House spokesperson Kush Desai told Fox News that the hike had no "particularly convincing economic basis," arguing that current inflation is entirely a supply shock from the Middle East war's energy impact and "has nothing to do with interest rates." Wall Street Journal reporter Nick Timiraos, widely dubbed the "Fed whisperer," wrote that the unanimous vote implicitly undercut the White House's argument that inflation "need not be worried about."

## A One-Off — or the Start of a Cycle?

Analysts were divided on whether the hike marks a turning point or a single precautionary move. Brad Conger, chief investment officer at Hirtle & Co., told CNBC that the decision "could mark the moment when the FOMC regained a measure of spine," adding that "we might have just caught a glimpse of Volckerian decisiveness." Others were more skeptical. Citi global chief economist Nathan Sheets argued that the market was casting "a vote of no confidence, questioning the Fed's willingness and policy ability to push down inflation," and that Warsh "points to the inflation problem but offers no solution beyond 'trust me, I'm a hawk.'"

Chinese brokerage CICC cautioned that the hike may not equal the start of a full tightening cycle, suggesting the sustained impact on A-shares would be relatively limited if it proves short-term or a one-off. Traders, however, increased bets on two more hikes by year-end, with CME FedWatch putting October hike odds at roughly 50%.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is whether the Fed follows through. Its own projections point to at least one more hike this year, and the Fed "rarely only moves once" when it judges inflation too high. Much now depends on two variables largely outside the central bank's control: the trajectory of the Middle East conflict and global oil prices, and whether the energy shock bleeds into broader prices.

The stakes extend beyond the United States. With Treasury yields near multi-decade highs, US debt above $40 trillion and midterm elections weeks away, the Fed's renewed tightening risks sharpening volatility across currency, bond and equity markets — and testing the central bank's independence at a moment of intense political pressure. For now, the message from the Warsh Fed is unambiguous: price stability comes first.
