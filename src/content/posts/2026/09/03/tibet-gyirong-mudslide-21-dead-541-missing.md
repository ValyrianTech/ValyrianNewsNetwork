# Tibet Gyirong Mudslide: 21 Dead, 541 Missing as Rescue Operations Intensify

A catastrophic mudslide triggered by a glacier collapse in Nepal has killed at least 21 people and left 541 others missing in Gyirong County, Tibet, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/20260902/b40b786c7d52460784b7252ee48f197e/c.html). The disaster struck Gyirong Port, a key border crossing on the China-Nepal frontier, on August 26, flattening approximately 0.7 square kilometers and destroying 27 buildings in a matter of minutes.

## A Disaster Unfolds in Minutes

On the morning of August 26, a massive ice-rock avalanche broke loose from the north face of Langtang Lirung peak in Nepal's territory at roughly 5,200 meters elevation. The collapse sent debris cascading approximately 22 kilometers down a steep Himalayan gorge, evolving into a high-velocity mudslide that struck Gyirong Port just seven minutes later.

Experts at the China Aero Geophysical Survey and Remote Sensing Center confirmed the debris flow traveled at an estimated speed of 40-50 meters per second. The U.S. Geological Survey initially recorded the event as a magnitude 4.4 earthquake but later revised its analysis, determining that the seismic signals were consistent with a glacier collapse rather than a tectonic event, as [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/climate-change-nepal-tibet-glacier-collapse-floods-61b592d428e4631add31ce052d0daf1f) reported.

The mudslide destroyed the Resuo Bridge on the China-Nepal border, the Gyirong Port joint inspection building, and buried commercial warehouses and shops around the port. Surveillance footage showed buildings being swallowed in seconds by the torrent of mud, rock, and debris.

## Rescue Operations: A Multi-Front Effort

Rescue operations have deployed over 2,300 personnel, 400 search vehicles, 8,000 pieces of rescue equipment, and 800 drone flights, according to officials at a [press conference](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260902/f7e97ce1422246b6981e61c86f7dfa0d/c.html) held by the Tibet Autonomous Region government on September 2.

Before road access was restored, rescue teams advanced on three fronts—water, land, and air—to reach the core disaster area. Helicopters conducted 24 sorties to deliver personnel and supplies, while teams used rubber boats to navigate treacherous river conditions and trekked over 12 hours through mountainous terrain to reach the site.

Gama Zedeng, Chairman of the Tibet Autonomous Region and Commander of the Emergency Rescue Command, said at the press conference that search teams have conducted multiple rounds of grid-based searches covering 1.591 million square meters. The G216 road to Gyirong Port was successfully reopened on the morning of September 2 after intensive efforts involving 1,333 personnel and 658 units of specialized equipment.

Ren Wei, Executive Vice Chairman of the Tibet Autonomous Region, noted that mudslide deposits in the core area average 4 meters thick, reaching up to 16 meters in some locations, with large boulders and mud on the surface complicating search efforts. Rescue personnel are conducting zoned searches, using professional instruments for deep searches in addition to surface-level operations.

## International Dimensions of the Tragedy

Of the 541 missing persons, approximately 261 are foreign nationals from 23 countries, according to Chinese authorities. The international scope of the disaster has drawn consular responses from multiple nations, including the United States, Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada, South Korea, and Japan.

On the Nepalese side of the border, the disaster has proven even more devastating. As of September 2, Nepal reported 1,243 deaths and 3,916 missing persons, with over 850 of the combined dead and missing being non-Chinese or non-Nepali citizens. Many of those affected were pilgrims traveling to the sacred Mount Kailash, a site of profound religious significance for Hindus, Buddhists, and other faiths.

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi spoke with Nepal's Foreign Minister Shisir Khanal on August 29, offering comprehensive assistance including emergency cash support, humanitarian aid, rescue experts, and hydrological data sharing. "In the face of disaster, the two countries must stand firmly together," Wang Yi said.

## The Scientific Cause: Climate Change and Glacier Instability

Chinese authorities confirmed on August 30 that the disaster was caused by a glacier collapse on Langtang Lirung's north slope, triggered by glacier destabilization under the long-term effects of climate warming. Su Pengcheng, a researcher at the CAS Chengdu Institute of Mountain Hazards and Environment, stated that the ice-rock avalanche fell from approximately 5,200 meters to 4,000 meters before evolving into a massive debris flow.

Scientists at the [CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-08-28/3D-imagery-reveals-path-and-causes-of-Xizang-s-Gyirong-mudslide-1PYNEvSLMUE/p.html)-reported analysis noted that the glacier on Langtang Lirung had been moving at about 10mm per month from January to August 2026, with the rate accelerating in the weeks before the collapse. The affected region has been described as a monitoring blind spot by experts.

Liu Qiao, a researcher at the CAS Chengdu Institute, acknowledged the difficulty of predicting such events: "There were no obvious abnormal signals on the ice surface or terrain in the early stage; without special observation it is very difficult to predict in advance."

Ge Daqing, Director of the Remote Sensing Application Technology Institute, explained that not all glaciers are susceptible to ice-rock avalanches. "Large elevation differences, large glacier mass, steep terrain, and high potential energy are the basic conditions for ice-rock avalanches," he said in an [expert analysis](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260830/c203f16b08074b46899be71b2c159f30/c.html) published by Xinhua.

The disaster highlights the growing risks posed by climate change to Himalayan communities. The region experienced similar flash floods in August 2025, when a glacial lake in Tibet overflowed due to high temperatures, killing nine people and damaging critical infrastructure including a bridge connecting Nepal and China. As [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/nepal-tibet-missing-flash-flooding-mud-flow-glacier-collapse/) reported, researchers in Kathmandu found earlier this year that glaciers across the Hindu Kush Himalaya region are melting faster, with ice loss rates doubling since 2000.

## Warning System Gaps Exposed

China and Nepal had maintained a joint flash flood early warning system for years, credited with preventing casualties in previous events. However, the system failed to detect this disaster because water level monitors were designed for monsoon floods, not glacier collapse-triggered flash floods. Upstream monitoring stations in Nepal were swept away before alerts could be sent.

After the 2025 floods, Gyirong County had updated emergency plans, built flood walls and channels, and conducted evacuation drills—but the August 2026 mudslide overwhelmed these defenses within minutes.

## Human Toll and Recovery Efforts

Beyond the search and rescue operations, authorities have focused on supporting the families of victims and the displaced. Wang Fanghong, Secretary of the Shigatse Municipal Party Committee, reported that hotlines for families of missing persons have received 876 calls—448 domestic and 428 international. A total of 587 family members of 253 missing persons have been accommodated.

Tan Xiangdong, Party Secretary of the Tibet Health Commission, said over 210 psychological experts have been deployed in three batches, with psychological first aid provided to 966 displaced persons and 1,107 family members. A psychological hotline (12356) has been established for ongoing support.

A barrier lake that formed upstream of Gyirong Port with an estimated volume of 2-2.5 million cubic meters naturally drained by August 30, eliminating the immediate flood risk. However, authorities continue to monitor the region for secondary landslides and debris flows, deploying 24-hour slope monitoring using radar and video systems.

## Looking Ahead

As rescue operations continue, authorities face the grim reality that the window for finding survivors is closing. The mudslide deposits in the core area remain thick and treacherous, and secondary disaster risks persist in the unstable Himalayan terrain.

The disaster has underscored the urgent need for enhanced glacier monitoring and early warning systems in high-mountain regions. Chen Fuqi, Chief Engineer of the Tibet Natural Resources Department, emphasized that the region will strengthen disaster prevention measures, including building an integrated "air-space-ground" monitoring system and enhancing cross-border cooperation on disaster early warning.

For the families of the 541 missing, the wait continues. For the broader Himalayan region, this disaster serves as a stark reminder of the accelerating impacts of climate change on the world's highest mountain range—and the growing vulnerability of communities living in its shadow.
