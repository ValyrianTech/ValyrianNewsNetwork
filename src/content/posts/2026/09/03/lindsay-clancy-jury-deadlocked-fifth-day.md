# Clancy Jury Deadlocked After Fifth Day Without Verdict

The jury in the Lindsay Clancy murder trial was sent home Wednesday after a fifth day of deliberations without reaching a verdict, having twice reported to the judge that they were at an impasse over whether the Massachusetts mother is criminally responsible for killing her three young children. Jurors returned Thursday morning for a sixth day of trying to reach a unanimous decision, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/lindsay-clancy-jury-deliberations-6f3a9556fb5d7a2b159b33ac6f2edc2c).

Clancy, a 36-year-old former labor and delivery nurse, does not deny strangling her children — Cora, 5; Dawson, 3; and Callan, 8 months — in the basement of the family home in Duxbury, Massachusetts, on January 24, 2023. The central question before the nine women and three men on the jury is whether she was suffering from postpartum psychosis at the time or acted with criminal intent.

## The Deadlock

After roughly 33 hours of deliberations spread across six days, the jury has twice notified Judge William Sullivan that they could not reach a unanimous decision. The first note came Tuesday morning, and a second followed Wednesday afternoon. Neither note revealed how the jury might be split or offered insight into the tone of the deliberations.

Following the second deadlock notification, Sullivan delivered what is known as a "Tuey-Rodriguez" charge — sometimes called a "dynamite charge" — a special instruction urging jurors to seriously consider opposing views and reassess their positions. As [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/lindsay-clancy-jury-deadlocked-entering-5th-day-deliberations-person-a-rcna595693) reported, the instruction tells jurors that "it is desirable that the case be decided" and that there is no reason to suppose another jury would be more capable of reaching a verdict.

"You have been selected in the same manner, and from the same source, as any future jury would be," Sullivan told the panel, according to [USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/crime/2026/09/02/lindsay-clancy-trial-update/91575757007/). "There is no reason to suppose that the case will ever be submitted to twelve persons who are more intelligent, more impartial, or more competent to decide it."

Such instructions are common in many states and have been upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court, though they are barred in some jurisdictions over concerns about coercion.

## A Trial Divided Over Mental Health

The trial, which began in late July at Plymouth Superior Court, has featured testimony from more than 80 witnesses over 21 days of proceedings. At its heart lies a fundamental disagreement about Clancy's mental state on the day of the killings.

Clancy's defense attorney, Kevin Reddington, argues that his client suffered from postpartum psychosis — a rare mental illness estimated to affect 1 to 2 per 1,000 women after delivery — and that she heard a voice commanding her to kill her children. In the months before the killings, Clancy was prescribed 13 different psychiatric medications, called a suicide hotline twice, and briefly checked herself into a psychiatric hospital as her mental health deteriorated.

Prosecutors, however, contend that Clancy planned the killings. They point to evidence that she deliberately sent her then-husband Patrick on errands to get him out of the house and that she was not in a psychotic state when she strangled the children with exercise bands before attempting suicide by jumping from a second-floor window — an act that left her paralyzed from the waist down.

As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/09/lindsay-clancy-murder-trial) noted, the case has been called "America's saddest murder trial," drawing intense public interest and sparking deep divisions over maternal mental health care and criminal responsibility. The livestreamed proceedings have drawn supporters wearing pink to the courthouse, many of whom believe Clancy was failed by the healthcare system.

## What Happens Next

If the jury ultimately cannot reach a verdict, Sullivan would declare a mistrial. Prosecutors would then decide whether to retry Clancy, drop the charges, or negotiate a plea deal.

Reddington has signaled he would be prepared for a second trial, telling reporters, "I want to try it next week." He also predicted that Plymouth County District Attorney Timothy Cruz would pursue a retrial, saying, "Tim Cruz will never drop it, that's for sure."

Boston criminal defense attorney Elyse Hershon, who has been following the case, noted that the next deadlock notification would likely trigger a mistrial declaration. "The next time they say they are deadlocked or unagreed — that is usually the mistrial call," she said.

The jury has several options on the verdict slip: not guilty, not guilty by lack of criminal responsibility, guilty of first-degree murder, guilty of second-degree murder, or guilty of manslaughter. A first-degree murder conviction carries a mandatory life sentence in Massachusetts. If Clancy is acquitted, a judge could still order her confined to a mental health facility if an evaluation determines she poses a danger to the public.

## Jury Intimidation Incident

Deliberations have been complicated by an incident outside the courthouse. Dawn Light, a 56-year-old retired nurse and Clancy supporter, was arrested Tuesday and charged with intimidation after authorities said she filmed jurors leaving the courthouse. Light pleaded not guilty at her arraignment, with her attorney calling the incident a "misunderstanding." The arrest prompted Sullivan to question each juror about whether anything had affected their impartiality.

## A Case That Resonates

The Clancy case has become a flashpoint in the national conversation about postpartum mental health. Experts and advocates have noted that postpartum psychosis is treatable but often goes unrecognized, and the trial has highlighted gaps in maternal healthcare. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/01/lindsay-clancy-jury-deliberating) reported, Patrick Clancy, Lindsay's former husband, was among those who testified during the trial, and the couple has since pursued separate civil litigation against her medical and mental healthcare providers, accusing them of medical malpractice for failing to properly diagnose, treat, and monitor her severe postpartum psychiatric condition.

For now, the nation watches and waits as a jury struggles to find consensus in one of the most emotionally charged murder trials in recent Massachusetts history. The coming days will determine whether the case ends with a verdict, a mistrial, or yet another round of deliberations.
