# Belgium's GP Shortage Fuels Rise of Emergency Medical Posts

A worsening shortage of family doctors is pushing Belgian municipalities to open a growing number of emergency primary-care posts, staffed largely by recently retired doctors and volunteers. The doctors' association Domus Medica confirmed the trend, first reported by De Standaard, warning that the healthcare system is being stretched to its limits.

"We are really hitting the limits of our system," Maaike Van Overloop of Domus Medica told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/16/noodposten-artsen/). "Various factors are causing an acute shortage of doctors across the whole of Belgium."

## A System Under Pressure

The emergency posts — known in Dutch as *noodposten* — are temporary, pop-up clinics designed to give patients without a regular doctor access to basic care while also relieving pressure on overburdened practices and hospital emergency departments.

According to Van Overloop, the rule of thumb is that a single GP can handle between 1,000 and 1,200 patients. On paper, that makes it look as though Belgium has just enough doctors — but the picture is deceptive, because many GPs also take on additional roles at organisations such as Kind en Gezin, the Flemish child welfare agency.

Three factors, in Van Overloop's account, are driving the crisis. First, an ageing population requires more complex care, with patients increasingly managing several chronic conditions at once, such as diabetes, heart failure or kidney disease. Second, administrative burdens consume time that could otherwise go to patients. "Especially now that employees must go to the doctor more quickly for a sickness certificate, precious time is lost that cannot go to real care," she said. Third, younger doctors are working differently, legitimately seeking a better work-life balance to sustain a demanding job.

## Vilvoorde: A City Stretched to 2,000 Patients per Doctor

The most detailed example comes from Vilvoorde, north of Brussels. The city's doctors' network, HARNO — active across 10 municipalities — opened a temporary post called "Dokter1800" in two vacant consultation rooms at AZ Jan Portaels hospital.

According to the city, HARNO data shows 33 GPs currently active in Vilvoorde against a population of 48,150 — an average of 1,459 patients per doctor, which on its face falls within the recommended guideline of 1,200 to 1,500 patients per full-time GP. But the reality is more nuanced, as [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/14/tijdelijke-noodpost-huisartsen-vilvoorde-huisarts/): the number of registered doctors does not always match the number actively working, since some have retired or work part-time. As a result, the real caseload rises to roughly 2,000 patients per GP — well above the recommended guideline.

"Residents go to the emergency department simply because they cannot get in with a GP," said Christel Herbosch, Vilvoorde's alderman for welfare, adding that the growing workload further undermines the profession's attractiveness.

At the post's opening hours, a recently retired doctor handles consultations and refers patients to practices with available capacity, while volunteers staff the reception. "With Dokter1800 we want to give residents without a fixed GP access to necessary care while relieving both GP practices and emergency departments," said HARNO coordinator Lindsay Gysen. "It is not a solution to the GP shortage, but it is a necessary step."

## Halle and the Zennevallei: 93 Doctors Where 125 Are Needed

A similar effort took shape in the Zennevallei region, where the first such post opened on 6 July 2026 in the Wit-Gele Kruis nursing facility in Halle. Five retired GPs and three reception staff receive patients by appointment only — Monday afternoon and Thursday morning, [according to the hospital AZ Sint-Maria Halle](https://www.sintmaria.be/nl/nieuws/nieuwe-noodpost-moet-huisartsentekort-in-de-zennevallei-helpen-opvangen). A modest administrative fee of €5 applies, waived for patients with a reduced contribution.

The region's doctors' network, Artsenkring Zennevallei, says 93 GPs are active in the area against roughly 125 needed to meet demand. Nearly all local practices have closed their patient lists, with more retirements expected. [Ring TV reported](https://www.ringtv.be/samenleving/artsenkring-zennevallei-opent-allereerste-huisartsennoodpost-verpleegpost-wit-gele) that the network had already set up a registration point, "Ik zoek een huisarts" (I'm looking for a GP), for residents without a regular doctor.

Co-locating the post with a nursing facility allows nurses to handle blood draws, injections and wound care on the spot. "We are happy to make our nursing post available to support the GPs," said Ellen De Camps, care director for Wit-Gele Kruis Vlaams-Brabant. "Our nurses have the expertise to take over various tasks, giving GPs more time for other medical work."

One of the retired doctors volunteering in Halle, 82-year-old André Lerminiaux, spent 50 years in practice before retiring six years ago. "When we chose the profession of GP, we wanted to help people," he said. "Nobody could have predicted that we would return after our retirement to help absorb the GP shortage."

## A National Pattern

The shortage is national in scope. According to [Data Magazine](https://data-magazine.be/huisartsentekort-regio), Belgium has roughly 14,200 active GPs, but about one in five municipalities is classified as "GP-poor" — with fewer than 0.9 doctors per 1,000 residents. The worst-affected provinces are Luxembourg (38% of municipalities short), Limburg (32%) and Hainaut (30%). Some 35% of active GPs are over 55, rising to 42% in Luxembourg province.

Inflow is barely keeping pace with outflow: roughly 1,100 new GPs enter practice each year against about 900 leaving — a net surplus of just 200, too small to match rising demand. Data Magazine cites three structural causes: a federal quota system that caps the number of graduating doctors; heavy workloads, with an average GP seeing 25 to 30 patients a day; and the pull of specialist medicine, which pays 50% to 100% more. About 60% of graduates are now women, who are more likely to work part-time.

The consequences reach beyond convenience. [Apache.be reported](https://apache.be/2026/02/16/huisartsentekort-vergroot-kloof-zorg) that one in 10 Belgians has no regular GP, and that the gap falls hardest on people living in poverty, those with a migration background or those with complex health needs — deepening inequality in access to care.

## Short-Term Relief, Long-Term Questions

Domus Medica frames the emergency posts as a stopgap that buys the system breathing room. "These posts give GPs room to breathe," Van Overloop said. "Patients without a fixed GP are helped there, while practices get time to reorganise and expand their capacity." Local authorities, she added, are increasingly consulting doctors' networks to make their municipalities attractive to young doctors — deploying nurses, encouraging collaboration between practices, and improving support such as secretarial services and childcare.

The association expects relief in three to four years, when enough students graduate to fill the gaps — though it acknowledges that more doctors than expected left practice after the COVID-19 pandemic. In the meantime, it is pressing politicians to cut red tape. "The pressure and the administrative burden the government places on doctors is too great," Van Overloop said. "Doctors must be able to focus again on providing care, not on filling in paperwork."

The warning signs are already visible. The Data Magazine analysis notes that Belgium's healthcare knowledge centre, the KCE, has cautioned that without additional measures the number of GP-poor municipalities could double by 2035, especially in Wallonia and the Flemish periphery — bringing longer waits, more non-urgent use of emergency services, and more preventable illness.

In early September, [Domus Medica published new guidance on cooperation between hospital emergency departments and GP out-of-hours posts](https://www.domusmedica.be/actueel/nieuw-ondersteunende-kader-samenwerking-spoeddienst-en-huisartsenwachtpost), together with Wachtposten Vlaanderen and Zorgnet-Icuro — supporting frameworks on shared triage, referrals and physical co-location — signalling that the reorganisation of primary care is far from complete.

For now, the emergency posts are holding the line. But as one retired doctor in Halle put it, the fact that pensioners are being called back to fill the gaps was something no one could have foreseen. Whether the system can bridge the next three to four years — and whether young doctors can be persuaded to take up a profession under strain — remains the central question.

## What to Watch

- Whether the federal government moves to reduce the administrative burden on GPs, as Domus Medica is demanding.
- Whether the projected recovery in three to four years materialises as more medical students graduate.
- How many more municipalities open emergency posts — and whether they remain temporary fixes or become a permanent feature of Belgian primary care.
