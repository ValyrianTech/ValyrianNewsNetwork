# Trump-Backed Crypto Bill Suffers Bruising Defeat in the Senate

The Senate on Tuesday blocked the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act, a roughly 600-page bill that would have established the first comprehensive regulatory framework for the U.S. cryptocurrency industry, delivering a decisive setback to the crypto sector and to President Donald Trump's digital asset agenda. The procedural motion to advance the bill, known as the CLARITY Act, fell far short of the 60 votes needed after all Senate Democrats and several Republicans opposed it.

## A Procedural Vote That Stalls the Bill

The vote was a cloture motion—a step that would have ended debate and moved the legislation toward floor consideration. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5968711/clarity-act-crypto-senate-vote), the tally was 49 senators in favor and 50 against, with all Democrats voting no alongside four Republicans: Sen. Susan Collins of Maine, Josh Hawley of Missouri, Jerry Moran of Kansas and Thom Tillis of North Carolina. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/15/senate-cloture-vote-on-clarity-act-fails-dealing-regulatory-setback-to-crypto-industry.html) reported the count as 50 for and 49 against—a discrepancy that reflects the narrow, chaotic nature of the vote, but which leaves the outcome unchanged: the bill did not clear the 60-vote threshold.

Tillis filed a motion that would allow the bill to be reconsidered and brought up for another vote, though the path forward remains uncertain.

The CLARITY Act—officially the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act (H.R. 3633)—passed the House last year and had been at the top of the crypto industry's wish list for years. It would have formally split oversight of digital assets between the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, set registration requirements, and strengthened anti-money-laundering rules. Controversially, it would have given the smaller CFTC the majority of regulatory control, which critics said was designed to shield the industry from heavier SEC scrutiny.

## Why Democrats Objected

The central sticking point was an ethics clause intended to prevent presidents and other public officials from profiting from the crypto industry while in office. Democrats, including Massachusetts Sen. Elizabeth Warren, argued the provision was too weak to meaningfully check conflicts of interest.

Officials would have had to divest "significant" financial stakes in the crypto sector, and state attorneys general would have gained more authority to sue over suspected ethics breaches. Republicans released a revised version on Sunday that folded in more than 120 Democratic-requested changes and introduced a "historic ethics agreement" covering the president, vice president, members of Congress and federal judges. Sen. Cynthia Lummis, the Wyoming Republican who has championed the bill, argued that Democrats had run out of reasons to object.

"It's now or never for the Clarity Act," Lummis posted on X ahead of the vote, [according to Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/378268/now-or-never-clarity-act-bitcoin-cynthia-lummis). "Dems can lock in 120+ negotiated wins." Speaking to reporters before the vote, she was more blunt: if the procedural motion failed, "I think we're done. It's over."

Sen. Ruben Gallego of Arizona, a key Democratic negotiator, said the compromise had been genuine but charged Republicans with caring more about ensuring the president could keep making money than about enacting real rules—an argument sharpened by Trump's disclosure that he and his family earned $1.4 billion from crypto ventures in 2025.

## Beyond Politics: A Battle With Banks

Opposition was not confined to Democrats. The banking sector—especially community banks—fought the bill fiercely, centering on a dispute over stablecoins. The legislation would have allowed crypto companies to pay interest to customers, which banks argued would let crypto firms compete for deposits without facing equivalent regulation.

"If community banks aren't there, and those local deposits aren't there to fund it, who's going to fund those small businesses and ranchers and farmers?" Rebeca Romero Rainey, president and CEO of the Independent Community Bankers of America, said before the vote. "I don't think it's going to be the crypto industry."

## Markets React—and the Industry Regroups

The failure rippled through crypto markets. Bitcoin fell about 3% to roughly $75,700, while Coinbase shares slid 8% and Circle dropped 10%, CNBC reported. Before the vote, Ryan VanGrack, vice chair of Coinbase, had framed the stakes in stark terms: "Tens of millions of Americans are investing in products that don't have clear regulatory oversight. That's an abomination."

Analysts were divided on the consequences. Nic Puckrin, founder of Coin Bureau, told the [New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/09/15/business/senate-fails-to-advance-clarity-act-in-blow-to-crypto-industry-ahead-of-2026-midterms/) that the bill had become "too much of a political hot potato," warning that many altcoins now sit in "a sort of legal purgatory" with no clear regime determining whether they are securities or commodities.

Jessica Martinez, U.S. policy director at Fireblocks, said large institutions are already building under the existing framework, while more cautious banks and asset managers are holding back: "Without it, adoption continues, just more unevenly and with fewer institutions willing to move at scale."

## What's Next

The defeat effectively ends market-structure legislating in the Senate for 2026. Senators are scheduled to leave Washington in early October and will not return until after November's midterms, and a Democratic takeover of either chamber would dim the bill's prospects further. With the midterm elections just seven weeks away, the crypto industry's tens of millions of dollars in political spending may shift toward other vehicles—including a crypto tax bill that a House committee advanced on Wednesday, signaling where the industry's policy agenda may head next.

For now, the CLARITY Act is not dead, but it is stalled. Whether it moves again may depend less on the merits of its 600 pages than on the outcome of an election that has little to do with crypto policy and everything to do with who controls Congress.
