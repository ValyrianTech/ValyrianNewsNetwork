# A Headless Buddha Comes Home: US Returns 64 Relics to China

China's National Cultural Heritage Administration (NCHA) has received 64 pieces of cultural relics, artworks, and paleontological fossils from the United States, in two ceremonies held in Washington on September 16 and New York on September 17. The single most striking item is not a complete treasure but a fragment: the Tang-dynasty body of a Bodhisattva statue from the north side of the west wall of Cave 17 at the Tianlongshan Grottoes in Shanxi Province — one whose original head remains missing.

According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20260918/ba9cd096c5264f9788f585c253d4bc5a/c.html), the returns were made by two U.S. agencies: Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) and the Manhattan District Attorney's Office. The HSI operation drew on nine of its branch offices, while the Manhattan DA's Office completed its fifth such return to China. All the items were seized by U.S. law enforcement in the course of their work.

China's NCHA Director Rao Quan signed the handover certificate with HSI Assistant Director Mayoral. China's Ambassador to the U.S. Xie Feng, Consul General in New York Chen Li, and representatives of the U.S. State Department also attended.

## The Body Without a Head

The Tianlongshan Grottoes, carved between 534 and 720 AD, comprise 25 caves and more than 500 statues, celebrated for a sculptural idiom now known as the "Tianlongshan style." The returned Cave 17 Bodhisattva body — standing with hands pressed together and full Tang features — carries a later-fitted replacement head. Its original head has never been located.

As [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34094924) explains, that separation was no accident of erosion. In the early 20th century, the Japanese architectural historian Sekino Tadashi surveyed the site around 1918, and his 1921 findings in the magazine *Kokka* exposed the grottoes to overseas collectors. The antiquities dealer Yamanaka Sadajiro, through Yamanaka & Co., then acquired and shipped large numbers of carvings abroad. Between roughly 1923 and 1925, more than 240 statues were damaged — almost no Buddha head escaped. A 1928 Osaka auction featured dozens of Tianlongshan stone works, including 45 Buddha heads.

Crucially, Yamanaka's commercial strategy was to deliberately sever the heads of complete statues and sell them piecemeal. The body-head separation seen today is therefore not natural decay but a deliberate commercial act — a historical wound.

## A Legal Landscape Built on Seizures, Not Judgments

The return was carried out under the bilateral Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) restricting the illegal import of Chinese cultural property into the United States, first signed on January 14, 2009, and renewed three times since. [Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20260917-9691750) reported that the Washington ceremony covered 58 pieces, which combined with the New York batch brought the total to 64.

Beijing framed the handover as a demonstration of the consensus reached at the May 2026 U.S.-China summit in Beijing and as a concrete expression of the two sides' "constructive strategic stability" relationship. "The return of cultural relics is a lamp of justice, guarding humanity's shared civilizational treasures; a ladder of progress, witnessing the renewal of the Chinese cultural lineage; a bridge of cooperation, broadening the bright avenue of mutual benefit and win-win," Xie said at the Washington ceremony, according to the Lianhe Zaobao account. The U.S. side said relics and fossils carry deep historical and cultural information and that repatriation cooperation reflects friendship and mutual respect.

Yet the mechanics reveal the limits of the framework. Repatriation under the MOU depends on U.S. law enforcement happening to intercept objects, rather than on binding legal judgments. UNESCO's 1970 Convention lacks retroactivity, its core clauses are ambiguous, and differences in national laws — including good-faith acquisition and statutes of limitations — complicate claims. As [People's Daily](http://ent.people.com.cn/n1/2026/0918/c1012-40801222.html) noted, the two governments have used the MOU to bring home multiple batches of relics. In short, returns are opportunistic rather than systematic.

## A Precedent for What Is Possible

There is nonetheless a model for on-site physical return. In 2020, the NCHA detected a "Tang Tianlongshan stone Buddha head" listed by a Tokyo auction house, the stolen head of the main Buddha of Cave 8's north-wall niche, looted around 1924. After the NCHA intervened, the lot was withdrawn, and the head returned in February 2021. As [Guangming Daily](https://epaper.gmw.cn/gmrb/html/2021-07/31/nw.D110000gmrb_20210731_2-04.htm) reported, it was the first looted relic to return to the Tianlongshan original site — and the first to come back to Tianlongshan from Japan. The donor, a Chinese resident of Japan named Zhang Rong, was named an honorary citizen of Taiyuan.

That case proved that when conditions align, a severed head can come home. It offers a path that might one day be followed by the Cave 17 head.

## Digital Restoration as a Bridge, Not a Substitute

For heads still missing, technology offers a different kind of return. In 2014, the University of Chicago, the Tianlongshan Grottoes Museum, and Taiyuan University of Technology launched a digital restoration project, combining data on statues held overseas with on-site data to digitally reunite heads and bodies. According to Guangming Daily, the effort gathered three-dimensional scans of 108 dispersed Tianlongshan statues from 27 museums in nine countries, completing digital reconstructions of 11 major caves.

The project's architects are clear about its limits. The NCHA has said it must "properly handle the relationship between physical return and digital restoration" — digital tools help locate, match, and document relics and can provide evidence for legal claims, but physical return remains the ultimate goal.

## What's Next

The return of the Cave 17 body is a milestone, not an endpoint. China reports that 537 pieces and sets of lost cultural relics were returned in 35 batches during the 14th Five-Year Plan period, part of a broader repatriation momentum that has brought back treasures ranging from Yuanmingyuan stone columns to the Changsha Zidanku Warring States silk texts.

For the Cave 17 Bodhisattva, the journey is incomplete. Its head is still out there — perhaps in a private collection, an auction catalogue, or a museum storeroom — waiting for the kind of breakthrough that freed the Cave 8 head.

As The Paper put it, the statue's incompleteness is not the work of time but of human cutting and plunder. The search for the missing head is thus not only a quest for a single object's wholeness, but a pursuit of historical justice — and, as the Cave 8 return showed, each effort shortens the distance.
