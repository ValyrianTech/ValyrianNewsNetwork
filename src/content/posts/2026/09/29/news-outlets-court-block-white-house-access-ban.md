# News Outlets Ask Court to Block White House Access Ban

Three news organizations that President Donald Trump barred from the White House this month asked a federal court late Monday to block the ban until their lawsuit is resolved, arguing the administration has "persisted in unpredictably and inconsistently implementing its ban" despite an existing judge's order. The filing by [CNN, MS NOW and Politico](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/29/nx-s1-5984349/news-organizations-court-white-house-ban) marks the latest escalation in a widening legal battle over media credentials and the limits of presidential power over the press.

The 42-page motion seeks a preliminary injunction that would extend a two-week temporary restraining order granted by U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly, which is set to expire Oct. 8.

## How the Standoff Began

On Sept. 18, Trump announced on Truth Social that the three outlets were banned from the White House "effective immediately," citing what he called their "constant reporting FAKE NEWS." Reporters for the outlets were denied entry to the grounds the following day, with some saying Secret Service officers confiscated and disabled their credentials on the spot. As [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/19/nx-s1-5974854/trump-cnn-msnow-politico-ban), MS NOW White House reporter Akayla Gardner said an officer asked her to hand over her badge, telling her the decision was "above him."

The outlets filed a joint First Amendment lawsuit two days after the ban, arguing viewpoint discrimination. Judge Kelly, a Trump appointee, granted a temporary restraining order on Sept. 24 requiring the White House to restore the outlets' hard passes, writing that the news organizations were "likely to succeed in showing that their hard passes were revoked without constitutionally adequate due process." As [NPR detailed](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/24/nx-s1-5979582/judge-order-trump-restore-access-white-house), the ruling found that "the court is skeptical — at least on this record — that Defendants' interest in safeguarding national security is the actual motivation for, or is even advanced by, the revocation of Plaintiffs' hard passes."

## A Pattern of Non-Compliance

Monday's filing argues the White House has not fully complied with the restraining order. On the day the order took effect, reporters for all three outlets were initially denied entry, with credentials restored only about 30 minutes before a court deadline. The outlets were still barred from a state dinner for Chinese President Xi Jinping that evening.

The dispute has since expanded to the White House broadcast pool — the rotating group of five networks (CNN, ABC, CBS, NBC and Fox News) that travel with and cover the president, sharing footage with outlets worldwide. When the ban took effect, CNN was removed from its pool duties. In solidarity, the other four networks suspended pool coverage.

On Sept. 26, the White House barred CNN from fulfilling its designated role as television pooler. A day later, CNN was banned from Air Force One for Trump's trip to a Tennessee college football game, and the rest of the pool declined to provide a replacement — leaving no primary television network pooler aboard the presidential aircraft. The filing said CNN "received no official communication from the White House explaining its exclusion."

"The White House's words and actions leave little doubt that it will immediately reinstitute a complete ban of CNN, MS NOW, and POLITICO if allowed the opportunity," the outlets' lawyers wrote.

## The Legal Argument

Theodore J. Boutrous Jr., lead attorney for the three outlets, framed the case in stark constitutional terms. "Because the ban is an unlawful assault on the most fundamental First Amendment freedoms and will inflict irreparable harm on Plaintiffs and the public if not restrained, this Court should preliminarily enjoin its enforcement pending the resolution of this case," he said in a statement.

"The president and his administration have continued to make clear that this ban has only one purpose: to punish reporting he doesn't like," Boutrous added. "That's retaliation and viewpoint discrimination, pure and simple."

The administration has countered that White House access is a discretionary privilege rather than a right. "When it comes to the White House, it is the people's house," Attorney General Todd Blanche said on ABC on Sunday. "It is a privilege, and not a right, to be there." Blanche also defended excluding CNN from Air Force One, saying, "The president has every right to decide who gets to fly on his plane."

Judge Kelly rejected the national security rationale in his earlier ruling, noting that "nothing in the record that predates this suit suggests that the revocation of Plaintiffs' hard passes was motivated by national security concerns" and that Trump had instead "focused on the alleged lack of truthfulness and negativity of Plaintiffs' reporting." More than 50 media outlets have joined an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

## A Parallel Media Strategy

The legal fight has unfolded alongside the administration's launch of its own 24-hour streaming channel, "TRUMP TV," described as "The Essentials Station," days after the ban took effect. As [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/22/nx-s1-5977768/trump-tv-media-ban-white-house), critics — including Democratic Sen. Mark Kelly and California Gov. Gavin Newsom — have labeled the channel "state-run media," while media ethics scholars questioned the timing.

"I cannot think that the timing is coincidental, that the decision to ban these media organizations from the White House on the eve of the launch of Trump TV just happened by coincidence," said Jane Kirtley, an emerita professor of media ethics and law at the University of Minnesota.

The White House currently has no press secretary following the departure of Karoline Leavitt, adding to the unusual posture of an administration simultaneously restricting traditional press access and building its own communications outlet.

## Tough Choices for the Networks

The pool standoff has forced the remaining networks into a difficult position. Media law experts say the options are limited. "There are not a lot of good options," said David A. Schulz, director of the Media Freedom and Information Access Clinic at Yale Law School, who framed the dynamic as a test of wills: "Who's going to back down first?"

RonNell Andersen Jones, a law professor at the University of Utah, noted the competing pressures. "Capitulation might be perceived as abandonment of core components of press freedom," she said. "But the practicalities of lengthy White House news blackouts are also complicated, and refusing to cover major news at the highest levels of government might itself feel like a failure to serve the goals of democracy."

For now, the networks are handling the standoff case by case, deciding together whether to supply replacements based on the newsworthiness of each event.

## What's Next

Judge Kelly's temporary restraining order remains in effect until Oct. 8, giving the outlets continued access while the case proceeds. A ruling on the preliminary injunction would extend that protection until the lawsuit is fully resolved — a process that could take months. Trump has vowed to appeal the restraining order, posting that he would "go for appeal" against outlets that "only write negatively."

The case carries implications well beyond the three plaintiffs. It tests how far a president may go in deciding who covers him, and whether the courts will treat press credentials as a protected interest or a revocable courtesy. With the restraining order clock ticking and the pool dispute unresolved, the next several weeks are likely to determine the contours of White House press access for the remainder of Trump's term.
