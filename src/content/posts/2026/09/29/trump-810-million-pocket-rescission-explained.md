# Trump's $810M Pocket Rescission: What to Know

President Donald Trump is withholding $810 million in congressionally appropriated federal funds through a maneuver known as a "pocket rescission," setting off a fresh constitutional clash over who controls the nation's purse strings. The White House [announced the move](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2026/09/president-trump-takes-historic-action-to-eliminate-wasteful-and-harmful-spending/) on the evening of Friday, September 25, 2026 — less than a week before the fiscal year ends on September 30, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/28/nx-s1-5982739/what-to-know-about-the-810-million-in-spending-canceled-by-president-trump).

Because the funds expire before Congress receives the full 45-day window to consider the request under the Impoundment Control Act (ICA), the money will go unspent, effectively canceling it without congressional approval. Critics, including the independent Government Accountability Office, say that is illegal. The White House did not respond to a request for comment.

## What Is a Pocket Rescission?

Under the ICA of 1974, a president who wants to cancel appropriated funds must send a special message to Congress, which then has 45 days to agree or disagree. A pocket rescission short-circuits that process: the request is submitted so close to the end of the fiscal year that the funds expire before lawmakers can respond.

According to the [Government Accountability Office](https://www.gao.gov/blog/what-pocket-rescission-and-it-legal), a pocket rescission is simply not permitted. "A pocket rescission is illegal," the GAO states, explaining that the ICA does not authorize a president to shorten the period for which funds may be used. Allowing it, the agency argues, would cede Congress's constitutional power of the purse.

The White House disputes that reading. In its formal announcement, it said Trump is "continuing his commitment to the American taxpayer by utilizing a pocket rescission for another nearly $1 billion of the most harmful government spending."

## Where the Money Comes From

The largest single tranche — $567 million — comes from Health and Human Services programs that, in the White House's words, "provided services to refugees, asylees, and other non-citizens." Other targeted amounts include $70 million for Department of Education programs bringing foreign students and faculty to the U.S., $56 million for Department of Housing and Urban Development counseling grants, $28 million for HHS research, and $25 million for Education Department programs for migrant students.

Smaller cuts target the Department of Justice's Community Relations Service ($15 million), Biden-era Department of Homeland Security programs providing services to undocumented immigrants ($25 million combined), the Minority Business Development Agency ($10 million), a Treasury debt-relief program tied to tropical forest and coral reef conservation ($9 million), and the HHS Office of Minority Health ($5 million).

The targets, detailed in the White House announcement, align with the administration's stated "America First" priorities, hitting immigration and refugee services, diversity and minority-focused programs, and LGBTQ- and climate-related research.

## A Bipartisan Fault Line

The move drew sharp criticism from an unusual source: Sen. Susan Collins, R-Maine, who chairs the Senate Appropriations Committee. In a statement posted to X, [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/26/white-house-810-million-pocket-rescission.html), Collins called it "the most recent attempt by this Office of Management and Budget to undermine Congress's Constitutional power of the purse."

"OMB is an agency of the executive branch," she added. "It does not get to decide which programs are worth funding."

Democrats were blunter. Sen. Patty Murray of Washington, the top Democrat on the Senate Appropriations Committee, called it "theft from the American people, plain and simple," while Rep. Brendan Boyle of Pennsylvania, the top Democrat on the House Budget Committee, said Trump was "illegally making them through the back door."

OMB General Counsel Mark Paoletta defended the maneuver, arguing that the ICA's text "specifically allows for pocket rescissions" and that the GAO had agreed with that view for 43 years — until it reversed course in 2018.

Conservative lawmakers, including House Freedom Caucus members Josh Brecheen, Eric Burlison, and Lauren Boebert, praised the move. "With a $40 trillion national debt, we cannot afford to waste almost a billion dollars on illegal aliens and DEI programs," Brecheen told [The Daily Signal](https://www.dailysignal.com/2026/09/28/trump-returning-funds-illegal-immigrants/).

## Second Year, Same Playbook

This is the second consecutive year the administration has used a pocket rescission. Last year, it deployed the maneuver for the first time in roughly 50 years to block about $5 billion in foreign aid. That dispute reached the Supreme Court, which in September 2025 allowed the administration to withhold roughly $4 billion of the funds on a similar timeline while litigation continued.

The Daily Signal noted the current dispute "could be taken to the Supreme Court" as well. The high court's earlier ruling suggests the administration may again find a sympathetic audience, though it left the underlying legal questions unresolved.

## What to Watch

The withheld funds expire on September 30, making the rescission effectively final regardless of any congressional objection. The central question — whether pocket rescissions are lawful — remains unsettled. Congress could pursue legislation to clarify the ICA, affected agencies could challenge the action in court, and further rescissions remain possible: OMB Director Russell Vought has said "all options are on the table."

For now, the episode crystallizes a broader fight over executive power and the limits of the president's authority to refuse to spend money Congress has appropriated.
