# China-US Summit Injects 'Stability' into a Turbulent World

Xi Jinping's state visit to the United States last week has been described by a prominent Singapore-based scholar as a diplomatic achievement that "injected expectations of stability into the world" — even as independent analysts in Washington characterized the same summit as heavy on symbolism and light on concrete results.

The visit, which ran from September 23 to 25, marked the first state visit by a Chinese leader to the United States in more than a decade. During the trip, Xi and US President Donald Trump agreed to jointly build what both sides call a "constructive strategic stability relationship based on respect, fairness, and reciprocity."

## The Interview That Framed the Summit

In an exclusive interview with [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/20260929/dda1075aa1724b38a71903fe80674456/c.html), Gu Qingyang (顾清扬), an associate professor at the Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy at the National University of Singapore, argued that Xi's visit "not only enriched the substance of China-US constructive strategic stability, but also injected expectations of stability into the world."

Gu described the outcome as a "reassurance pill" (定心丸) for a turbulent world, and as a model for how countries — regardless of size or strength — should manage their relations through equal consultation. Quoting Xi's own framing, he noted that "building China-US constructive strategic stability is neither an expedient nor an overnight task."

The scholar emphasized what he called China's "long-term vision" (长期视野), which he said operates on two axes: horizontally, requiring action across every sector and level of government; and vertically, oriented toward ties between generations. He pointed to Xi's announcement that China will invite 100,000 American youth to China for exchange and study over the next five years as evidence of that generational approach.

"The two major countries must act across all dimensions to understand each other's concerns and interests, forming a stable relationship of interwoven interests," Gu said.

## What the Two Sides Actually Agreed

The official readouts issued after the summit point to a modest but concrete set of deliverables. According to a [Chinese Foreign Ministry](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/fyrbt_673021/202609/t20260928_12032500.shtml) press conference held on September 28, the two leaders reached "a series of important consensuses" on building the new relationship framework, and agreed to support each other's hosting of the APEC and G20 summits.

A [White House fact sheet](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/09/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-advances-a-fair-and-reciprocal-relationship-with-china-while-hosting-historic-state-visit/) added further detail. The two countries operationalized a US-China Board of Trade and Board of Investment, reached consensus on more favorable tariff treatment for $30 billion in non-sensitive goods in each direction, established a dialogue on artificial intelligence — which the US side termed "Super Intelligence" — and agreed to create a bilateral communication channel for AI-related incidents. Both leaders signaled their intention to attend each other's summits: APEC in China in November and the G20 in Miami in December.

Other agreed measures include expanded counternarcotics cooperation, with China making 21 arrests based on US information and adding export controls on two additional fentanyl precursors, and cultural and people-to-people steps including the arrival of two giant pandas at Zoo Atlanta and plans to increase direct flights, as reported by [Guangming](https://world.gmw.cn/2026-09/28/content_39025721.htm).

## A Fragile Truce, Seen from Washington

If the Chinese framing emphasized stability and mutual respect, the Western assessment was notably cooler. In a multi-author analysis published by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), several analysts argued that the summit produced far fewer substantive outcomes than its ceremonial weight suggested.

In the [CSIS analysis](https://www.csis.org/analysis/takeaways-trump-xi-white-house-summit), Edgard D. Kagan described the visit as a "nothingburger" — heavy on symbolism but light on substance. Bonny Lin wrote that Xi sought to recast US-China relations "more rosy than reality," noting that China did not publicly address US concerns about rare earths and pressed Washington on Taiwan. Thomas J. Christensen called the underlying truce "fragile," citing unresolved disputes over semiconductors, rare earths, Taiwan arms sales, and AI.

CNBC reported that the summit ended with few breakthroughs, including only a short two-month extension of a trade truce. "I'm concerned that this kind of diplomacy and this fragile detente is really unsustainable — if it doesn't result in more tangible outcomes," Daniel Kritenbrink, a partner at the consultancy The Asia Group, told [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/28/trump-xi-summit-tangible-outcomes-us-china-truce.html). Scott Kennedy, a China specialist at CSIS, observed that the deliverables on trade, rare earths, refined oil, investment, and AI were "all quite limited and tentative."

The divergences extended beyond economics. CSIS analysts noted that China did not restate the US position that Iran "can never have a nuclear weapon," and that human rights did not appear on the summit agenda — a departure, they argued, from past US presidential practice.

## The View from Southeast Asia

Gu's interview also foregrounded the perspective of ASEAN, the regional bloc that sits economically between the two powers. He said ASEAN countries view the coming decade as critical for development, and said they have felt keenly the disruption caused in recent years by unilateral tariffs and "decoupling and breaking supply chains."

"ASEAN countries welcome frank China-US dialogue and hope to use it to push regional and global situations toward greater stability and predictability," Gu said. In his assessment, building a constructive strategic stability relationship "will, in the medium-to-long term, create a relatively stable development environment for China, the US, and the world."

That regional interest in predictability explains why the summit, for all its limited deliverables, carried outsized significance. A more stable framework between the world's two largest economies — even an imperfect one — reduces the risk of abrupt escalation that could ripple through global supply chains and emerging markets.

## What to Watch Next

The immediate test will be implementation. With both presidents expected to meet again at APEC in Shenzhen and the G20 in Miami, the coming months will reveal whether the agreed mechanisms — the boards of trade and investment, the AI dialogue, and the counternarcotics channel — produce results or remain largely symbolic.

Several open questions remain. Whether Washington and Beijing can resolve their disputes over rare earth exports, semiconductor restrictions, and Taiwan will determine whether the truce hardens into something durable or, as Christensen warned, stays fragile. And the question of which power, if any, will champion human rights in the bilateral relationship remains unanswered.

What is clear is that both capitals have chosen to frame the summit as a stabilizing moment. For a world unsettled by economic uncertainty and geopolitical friction, that framing — as much as any signed document — was itself a deliverable of sorts.
