# DHS Email Fuels Fears Over Election Enforcement Plans

An internal Department of Homeland Security email — sent last year but newly obtained by NPR — is raising fresh questions about whether the Trump administration intends to target jurisdictions that offer election materials in languages other than English, as federal agents surge resources to hunt for noncitizen voters ahead of the 2026 midterms.

The email, dated Dec. 19, shared a 78-page document listing counties and states that provide ballots and election materials in non-English languages, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/18/nx-s1-5973278/dhs-noncitizen-voting-english-language). The list was attached alongside a Department of Justice criminal checklist outlining the requirements for prosecuting voter-related charges — a checklist that itself is heavily redacted.

Nothing in the document alludes to enforcement activity at the locations named, and DHS has denied it is planning operations targeting polling places. But the disclosure has alarmed voting rights advocates, who say the pairing of a multilingual-jurisdiction list with a prosecution checklist suggests an intent to scrutinize those areas for voter fraud enforcement.

## What the Email Contains

The email was released as part of a tranche of materials after the Democratic National Committee filed suit over a public records request and then shared the documents with NPR. A court filing by DHS defending its redactions described the email as a "subordinate sharing initial thoughts and opinions with leadership."

It was sent by a DHS border policy acting director, whose name is redacted, to Rob Law, the undersecretary for strategy, policy and plans. Other officials from that office were included, as well as [Heather Honey](https://apnews.com/article/heather-honey-trump-election-integrity-homeland-security-e00b0dd4df548d317ce2186a3249f6f0), the DHS official leading the administration's efforts to root out noncitizen voting.

In Georgia, the document references Gwinnett, Cobb and DeKalb counties — jurisdictions that offer ballots in Spanish and other languages, according to the [Atlanta Journal-Constitution](https://www.ajc.com/politics/2026/09/trump-administration-eyed-non-english-ballots-in-georgia-email-shows/). Denver, where roughly one-third of the population is Hispanic or Latino, is also named.

## A Legal Requirement, Not a Red Flag

The jurisdictions on the list share a common feature: Under the Voting Rights Act, areas where more than 5% of voting-age citizens primarily speak a language other than English are required to provide election materials in that language. The [Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/crt/about-language-minority-voting-rights) describes the requirement as straightforward — all election information available in English must also be available in the minority language so that citizens have an effective opportunity to register and vote.

That the list corresponds to public information and a long-established legal mandate is central to the dispute. DHS has not said why the list was compiled or attached.

When asked about the document, DHS said anyone who votes illegally should not be able to use a "purported lack of knowledge" of English to avoid prosecution. The agency also said it "is not planning operations targeting polling locations."

## The Broader Enforcement Push

The email emerges amid a widening federal effort to investigate noncitizen voting, an initiative DHS calls the Unlawful Voter Initiative. The agency has [surged resources](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/14/nx-s1-5968892/dhs-whistleblower-voter-fraud) to that effort, even as prior audits have found such fraud to be [extremely rare](https://www.npr.org/2025/07/30/nx-s1-5462836/noncitizen-voting-trump-ceir-review).

A whistleblower report released this month by Democratic Sen. Alex Padilla and Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer alleged that federal agents may have broken state laws while scouring voter rolls, using personally identifiable information to make false attestations in order to access individual voter records. The report also claimed officers were given a quota of at least five subjects per hour, leaving roughly 12 minutes to determine citizenship status. DHS has denied there is a quota on criminality and said its investigative efforts are conducted "diligently and professionally."

In recent weeks, federal authorities have [charged](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndtx/pr/federal-prosecutors-crack-down-illegal-voting-seven-non-citizens-charged-sweeping) a number of individuals with alleged illegal voting. The Department of Homeland Security has also quietly walked back earlier claims about the scale of the problem; in Nevada, it scaled an estimate of 16,000 noncitizens on voter rolls down to 185.

## Fears of Intimidation at the Polls

The uncertainty has spilled into concerns about whether federal agents might appear at polling places during the midterms. Civil rights coalitions have filed lawsuits seeking to block DHS from deploying federal officers to the polls, and administration officials have at times suggested such a scenario is possible.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said ICE agents could serve warrants at polling places. When CNN's Dana Bash pressed him to promise federal agents would not be sent to polling places absent a legitimate emergency, Mullin responded that the question was "so ridiculous" and said agents would only be present if required by "some type of threat."

DHS has said ICE "is not planning operations targeting polling locations," while adding that the agency conducts "intelligence-driven targeted enforcement" and could act if an active public safety threat endangered a site.

Federal interference at the polls is [against the law](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/preventing-voter-intimidation-federal-agents-polls), according to the Brennan Center for Justice, which notes that three federal laws criminalize federal interference in elections, including an 1865 statute barring federal officials from stationing armed men where elections are held.

"The very simple connection that I logically drew is that they're targeting [these jurisdictions]," Dan Freeman, the DNC's litigation director and a former DOJ Voting Section attorney, told NPR. "The logical inference that I think anyone would make is that they are thinking about prosecution of federal election crimes in jurisdictions where elections are conducted in a language other than English."

In a [statement](https://democrats.org/new-dnc-sheds-light-on-trump-admin-documents-as-dhs-sec-refuses-to-rule-out-sending-ice-agents-to-the-polls/), Freeman called it "extremely concerning that the Trump administration may be targeting jurisdictions where voting materials are available in multiple languages," adding that American citizens have the right to vote "regardless of the language they speak" and "without fear or intimidation."

## A Chilling Effect on Voters

The rhetoric is already affecting public perception. Roughly 40% of voters now think it's likely ICE agents will be present at voting locations in their area, according to [polling](https://elections.ucsd.edu/_files/surveys/Trust-in-Elections-Sept-2026.pdf) released this month by the University of California San Diego. That includes roughly a third or more of Democrats, Republicans and independents. The survey also found voters of color were significantly more likely than white voters to say they feared voting could put them at risk of being questioned by immigration authorities despite being U.S. citizens.

Paul López, Denver's top voting official and a plaintiff in one of the lawsuits, said the goal appears to be deterrence. "What they're trying to do is put it out there to discourage people from voting, to create an atmosphere of fear, and it creates a chilling effect on the democratic process," he told NPR.

López added a message of defiance to voters: "Don't let these thugs scare you. Don't let them intimidate you. You're not going to be bullied and we're not going to be intimidated. Democracy will happen."

## What to Watch

The DNC is still litigating to obtain more records, and the full scope of the administration's election enforcement plans remains unclear. The lawsuits now moving through federal court — including one filed in Washington, D.C., that invokes the 1965 Voting Rights Act — could determine whether federal agents can lawfully approach polling sites in November. With roughly two months until Election Day, the intersection of an expanding fraud investigation, a disputed document trail, and mounting voter anxiety has made the mechanics of running a free and fair election the central political question of the midterm season.
