# How the Fed's Interest Rate Actually Works

In a single unanimous vote, the Federal Reserve this week did something it had not done in more than three years: it raised interest rates. The move lifted the central bank's benchmark rate by a quarter percentage point, to a target range of 3.75%–4%, and put the Fed's most important tool squarely in the spotlight.

So what does that tool actually do — and why does it matter for mortgages, credit cards, and the price of groceries? [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/19/nx-s1-5969816/federal-reserve-interest-rate-inflation-economy) walked readers through the mechanics of rate decisions in an explainer published Saturday, and the answers offer a clear window into how the central bank steers the economy.

## Two Mandates, One Tool

The Fed has two main jobs: keeping prices stable — inflation that is "neither too hot, nor too cold" — and promoting maximum employment. Often, those two goals coexist comfortably. But frequently they conflict, forcing policymakers to choose which mandate to prioritize.

The Fed's principal tool for striking that balance is the interest rate. When inflation runs hot, as it is now, the Fed raises rates. It doesn't set mortgage or credit card rates directly, but by lifting its own rate it triggers a domino effect that pushes borrowing costs higher across the economy. The aim is to cool spending — to make households and businesses think twice before buying a home or taking out a loan. When the labor market needs a boost, the Fed does the opposite: it cuts rates to make borrowing cheaper and spur activity.

## Why the Fed Acted Now

The rate hike, approved Wednesday, was the first since July 2023 and brought the federal funds rate to its current 3.75%–4% range. The vote was 12-0, a notable display of unanimity. According to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/16/fed-rate-decision-september-2026.html), the "highly anticipated" decision followed persistently elevated inflation readings that had convinced Wall Street the Fed would act.

The rationale, however, was unusual. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh cited three converging factors: a strong economy and labor market, inflation that remains above the Fed's 2% target, and tensions in the Middle East driving up energy costs. "All three of those things lend themselves to a firm unanimous decision today," he said.

The plain facts have grown difficult to ignore. Inflation has run above the Fed's 2% target for five years. "The plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long," Warsh said at his post-meeting news conference. "The least well-off are the ones who have the most to gain from stable prices."

## A Deliberate Shift Away From 'Looking Through'

Ordinarily, the Fed "looks through" inflation caused by supply shocks — such as war-driven fuel costs and tariffs — on the theory that such pressures will fade on their own. This time, officials weighed the cost of continuing that approach against a stabilizing labor market.

As [KPMG](https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2026/september-2026-fomc-meeting.html) chief economist Diane Swonk observed, the September statement marked a hawkish turn, sharpening the Fed's assessment of the supply side and stating bluntly that "inflation remains elevated." The committee said the hike would "support a timelier return" to its 2% goal and pledged simply: "The Committee will deliver price stability."

The episode echoes a painful recent memory. During the pandemic, the Fed — then led by Jerome Powell — cut rates to near zero to prevent mass layoffs. It worked, until inflation climbed and the Fed had to reverse course and raise rates. Officials later conceded that the supply-and-demand shocks they expected to fade instead produced the highest inflation in four decades.

## What It Means for Your Wallet

The hike is modest enough that it won't transform household finances overnight — but it arrives when many borrowing costs are already climbing. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate jumped to 6.95% this week, nearly two-tenths of a percent higher than a week earlier, according to Freddie Mac data. That's enough to add hundreds of dollars a month to a typical home loan and to further stall a stagnant housing market.

"It's like that Godfather movie," said Kara Ng, a senior economist at Zillow. "Every time it tries to break out, something pulls it back in."

The effect on credit cards is far smaller. Analysts at LendingTree estimate that for someone carrying $7,000 in card debt, the hike will cost just a few extra dollars a month.

## The Road Ahead

The Fed's rate-setting committee signaled it expects to raise rates once more this year before holding steady in 2027. Per [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/nx-s1-5971122/fed-raises-interest-rates-for-first-time-in-more-than-three-years), diesel prices have already hit an all-time high of $6.31 a gallon — a reminder that higher borrowing costs can't directly address the energy prices driving much of today's inflation. Warsh was candid that no single policy lever can lower the price of oil or groceries. What the Fed can do, he said, is keep one category of prices from spilling into the rest of the economy.

That leaves households waiting. Committee members don't expect inflation to return to 2% until 2029, and any relief on the cost of living is unlikely to arrive quickly. There are, as the Fed's own explainers put it, no quick fixes to inflation — only a long, deliberate effort to bring it back down.
