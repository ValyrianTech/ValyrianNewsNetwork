# Tung Chee-hwa, Hong Kong's First SAR Chief Executive, Dies at 89

Tung Chee-hwa, the first Chief Executive of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region who steered the territory through its historic 1997 handover from British to Chinese rule, has died at the age of 89. His office announced in the early hours of September 9 that Tung passed away peacefully at approximately 10 p.m. the previous evening at Hong Kong Sanatorium and Hospital, surrounded by his loved ones, according to [Xinhua News Agency](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260909/81bb8a95544a4b8593b531d6128d2fb9/c.html).

Tung's office described him as "upright and principled, devoted to his country, and working with utmost dedication," adding that "his noble spirit will be immortal and remain in our hearts forever." Xinhua, China's official news agency, designated him an "outstanding contributor to One Country Two Systems," a "famous patriot," and a "close friend of the Chinese Communist Party."

## A Life Bridging Business and Politics

Born in Shanghai in 1937 to C.Y. Tung, the shipping magnate known as the "King of the Seas," Tung Chee-hwa was the eldest of five siblings. His family relocated to Hong Kong in 1948 during the Chinese Civil War. After studying marine engineering at the University of Liverpool and working in the United States, he returned to Hong Kong in 1969 to join the family's Orient Overseas Container Line (OOIL) business.

Following his father's sudden death in 1982, Tung inherited a heavily indebted shipping empire. The company was saved in 1986 when Hong Kong tycoon Henry Fok Ying-tung injected US$120 million. In 2017, the family sold OOIL to Chinese state-owned COSCO Shipping for HK$49.2 billion (US$4.34 billion).

Tung's path to politics began in 1992 when the last British governor, Chris Patten, appointed him to the Executive Council of British Hong Kong. In December 1996, he won the first Hong Kong SAR Chief Executive election with 320 of 398 votes from the Selection Committee, as [BBC Chinese](https://www.bbc.com/zhongwen/articles/cx2yn54wn4eo/simp) detailed in its comprehensive obituary.

## The 1997 Handover and Early Years in Office

Tung was sworn in as the first Chief Executive of the Hong Kong SAR at the midnight handover ceremony on July 1, 1997, before Chinese Premier Li Peng. In a BBC special program marking the handover, Tung articulated his vision: "I hope that in five years, we will see a stable, passionate, democratic Hong Kong, a Hong Kong governed by the rule of law."

His tenure was immediately tested by the Asian Financial Crisis. In August 1998, the government made an unprecedented intervention in the stock market, spending HK$118 billion to fight off international speculators. The move was controversial but widely credited with saving Hong Kong's financial markets.

Tung's policy agenda included an ambitious plan to build 85,000 housing units per year, which was later quietly shelved after destabilizing the property market. He also announced plans for Hong Kong Disneyland in 1999 and pursued various infrastructure megaprojects.

## Crisis and Resignation

The defining challenges of Tung's leadership came in 2003. The SARS epidemic killed 299 people in Hong Kong, exposing government shortcomings. Simultaneously, his administration's attempt to enact Article 23 national security legislation under the Basic Law triggered massive public opposition. On July 1, 2003, approximately 500,000 people marched in protest - the largest demonstration in Hong Kong's history at that time. Tung withdrew the legislation in September.

Tung was re-elected unopposed for a second term in July 2002 but did not complete it. In December 2004, President Hu Jintao publicly admonished Tung's administration at the Macau handover anniversary event - a signal widely interpreted as Beijing's loss of confidence in his leadership. Tung resigned on March 10, 2005, citing health reasons, noting: "I am nearly 68 years old, and time waits for no one. My health has been affected by long-term work of 16 to 18 hours a day."

## Later Career and Legacy

Two days after his resignation, Tung was elected Vice Chairman of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference (CPPCC) National Committee, a vice-national-level position he held until 2023. He continued to play an active role in public life, founding the China-US Exchange Foundation in 2008 and Our Hong Kong Foundation in 2014.

In 2019, President Xi Jinping awarded Tung the "One Country Two Systems Outstanding Contributor" national honor medal, recognizing his lifelong service. Current Hong Kong Chief Executive John Lee Ka-chiu expressed deep sorrow at Tung's passing, stating that under Tung's "outstanding leadership, he made remarkable contributions to Hong Kong's smooth return to the motherland and the implementation of the One Country Two Systems policy and the Basic Law," as reported by [Sing Tao Headline](https://www.stheadline.com/zh-hans/politics/3613253/).

Leung Chun-ying, the third Chief Executive of Hong Kong and current CPPCC Vice Chairman, paid tribute on social media, saying Tung "led Hong Kong in establishing the Special Administrative Region and its government, earning the nation's high trust," and praised him for planning Hong Kong's long-term development with foresight. Leung described Tung as "kind-hearted and reasoned with people, setting an example for our generation," according to [Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20260909-9648050).

## A Complex Historical Figure

Tung's legacy remains complex and contested. As [South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/news/hong-kong/society/article/3366843/tributes-pour-hong-kong-mourns-death-tung-chee-hwa-first-chief-executive) noted, tributes poured in from across Hong Kong society following the announcement of his death. Supporters remember a dedicated public servant who guided Hong Kong through its most turbulent transition, while critics recall the Article 23 crisis, the 2003 protests, and what some viewed as excessive deference to Beijing.

Chris Patten, the last British governor who appointed Tung to the Executive Council, once observed that Tung's challenge was "to prove to the people of Hong Kong that he represents them in dealing with Beijing, rather than representing Beijing in dealing with Hong Kong." Tung himself, reflecting years later on the Article 23 episode, acknowledged in an interview that the central government "began to have doubts about Hong Kong" when the legislation stalled.

In his later years, Tung remained a steadfast advocate for closer Hong Kong-mainland ties and publicly supported Beijing's national security legislation for Hong Kong in 2020. His health declined notably after a fall at a CPPCC session in March 2021 and subsequent heart surgery.

Tung is survived by his wife, Betty Tung Chiu Hung-ping, and their family. He was the first former Hong Kong Chief Executive to pass away, closing a chapter on the generation that navigated Hong Kong's transition from British colony to Chinese Special Administrative Region.

As [Voice of America](https://www.voachinese.com/a/first-hong-kong-chief-executive-tung-chee-hwa-dies-20260908/8195678.html) and other international outlets reported, his death marks the end of an era for Hong Kong - one defined by the promise and challenges of One Country Two Systems. The territory he helped guide through its first years under Chinese sovereignty now remembers the man who, as he once said, devoted his life to the belief that "Hong Kong good, country good; country good, Hong Kong better."
