# CLARITY Act Faces Pivotal Senate Procedural Vote

The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act — a sweeping, 600-plus-page cryptocurrency market-structure bill — faces a critical Senate cloture vote on Tuesday at 2:15 p.m. ET, a procedural threshold that will determine whether the most ambitious crypto regulation in U.S. history can advance or stall for the remainder of the year. The measure needs 60 votes to proceed, and with Republicans holding 53 seats, its fate rests on finding Democratic crossover support amid unresolved fights over ethics, banking, and law enforcement.

If the bill clears the hurdle, the Senate can begin formal debate and eventually hold a final vote. If it fails, the legislation is effectively dead for 2026.

## What the Bill Would Do

Officially designated H.R. 3633, the CLARITY Act would establish the first comprehensive federal regulatory framework for digital assets, resolving the long-contested question of when a crypto asset falls under the Securities and Exchange Commission versus the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. As [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5968711/clarity-act-crypto-senate-vote) reported, the bill would formally split oversight between the two agencies — but controversially would give the CFTC, a far smaller regulator, the majority of control.

Critics argue that handing the CFTC the bulk of the reins is the crypto industry's way to avoid heavy regulatory scrutiny. Industry executives deny that, and Trump administration regulators have defended their oversight.

The bill follows the GENIUS Act, the stablecoin framework signed into law in July 2025 — the country's first major standalone cryptocurrency legislation. A version of the CLARITY Act passed the House 294–134 in July 2025 with 78 Democrats voting yes, and the Senate Banking Committee advanced its portion 15–9 on May 14, 2026, with Democratic Sens. Ruben Gallego and Angela Alsobrooks crossing over.

"Tens of millions of Americans are investing in products that don't have clear regulatory oversight," said Ryan VanGrack, vice chair of Coinbase, one of the largest crypto firms. "That's an abomination." He added, "Whether you love crypto or you hate crypto, you should want it regulated. And this is the best opportunity we have ever had to do just that."

## The Ethics Standoff

The central Democratic objection concerns an ethics clause meant to prevent presidents and other elected officials from profiting from the crypto industry while in office. Democrats, led by Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-MA), argue the provision does too little to prevent conflicts of interest, especially because the Department of Justice — currently led by Todd Blanche, a former Trump attorney — would enforce it.

Those concerns intensified after President Trump disclosed that he and his family earned $1.4 billion in 2025 from crypto ventures, an unprecedented sum for a sitting president.

Republicans sought to break the impasse with new language released days before the vote. Under the revised text, federally elected officials and their spouses would be barred from issuing their own cryptocurrencies — a provision that could effectively end Trump's $TRUMP meme coin, which he agreed to abide by. Officials would also have to divest "significant" crypto stakes, and state attorneys general would gain new authority to sue over ethics breaches.

According to [Political.org](https://political.org/2026/09/13/white-house-agrees-to-new-ethics-language-in-clarity-act-ahead-of-critical-senate-cloture-vote-on-september-15-2026/), the White House agreed to roughly 80 percent of what Democrats requested in the ethics negotiation, with a senior administration official calling it "the most comprehensive and wide-ranging ethics provision in history." Whether that satisfies the caucus remains unclear. Critics note the divestiture language is loose enough for the president to sidestep, and a sunset clause expiring in January 2029 would lapse the ethics provisions before the next inauguration.

Seven Democratic senators — Alsobrooks, Cory Booker, Catherine Cortez Masto, Gallego, John Hickenlooper, Mark Warner, and Raphael Warnock — have said the bill falls short on ethics, consumer protection, illicit finance, and market integrity.

## Banking and Law Enforcement Objections

Beyond ethics, the bill faces opposition from community banks and prosecutors. Banking groups argue that stablecoin reward provisions — financial incentives such as interest for customers who park money with crypto firms — would let crypto companies compete for deposits without bank-level regulation.

"If community banks aren't there, and those local deposits aren't there to fund it, who's going to fund those small businesses and ranchers and farmers?" said Rebeca Romero Rainey, president and CEO of the Independent Community Bankers of America. "I don't think it's going to be the crypto industry."

The National District Attorneys' Association has argued that Section 604 would impair criminal crypto investigations, a concern echoed by Sens. Cortez Masto and Warner. A compromise would give the Treasury Secretary temporary authority to restrict stablecoin rewards if they cause substantial deposit outflows from community banks.

On the Republican side, Sens. Rand Paul (R-KY) and Josh Hawley (R-MO) have signaled reservations. Hawley has said he will not vote for the bill until it addresses deposit-flight concerns.

## A Narrow Window and Long Odds

The vote arrives during a compressed pre-midterm legislative sprint. As [crypto.news](https://crypto.news/clarity-act-delay-draws-backlash-before-september-vote/) noted, the Senate has roughly seven weeks between returning from recess and the November midterms, narrowing available floor time for a complex bill. The delay before the August recess drew frustration from key architects; Sen. Cynthia Lummis (R-WY), the bill's chief sponsor, said she would keep working and described the effort as "far from over."

Prediction markets reflect deep uncertainty. A Kalshi contract placed the probability of a Senate vote before October 1 at 88 percent, aligning with the scheduled procedural test. But a separate Polymarket contract assigned only a 25 percent probability that the bill would be signed into law during 2026, and Galaxy Digital cut its own estimate to about 10 percent. Kalshi traders put the odds of the legislation taking effect before July 1, 2027, at 41 percent.

Even if the Senate advances the bill, the House and Senate versions would still need to be reconciled, since senators have changed the text since House passage.

## What to Watch

Tuesday's vote is the most critical test to date for the CLARITY Act, but it is only a procedural threshold — not final passage. Clearing cloture would allow debate to begin; failing it would push the next realistic opportunity out by years. Because congressional sessions run on a two-year cycle, a failed vote would require the bill to be reintroduced from scratch, and Lummis has warned that the next viable window may not arrive until 2030.

For the crypto industry, which has amassed tens of millions of dollars for the upcoming elections, the vote will signal whether years of lobbying can translate into durable federal rules. For Democrats, it is a test of whether a White House ethics concession is sufficient to earn their votes. And for the broader financial system, the outcome will shape how digital assets are overseen in the United States for years to come.

Lawmakers, industry executives, and investors will be watching the Senate floor Tuesday afternoon for an answer.
