# Homebuyers Face Yet Another Cost: Junk Fees

Buying a home in the United States already carries a hefty price tag. Now, a growing number of buyers and sellers are discovering an extra line on their closing statements: a so-called "junk fee" that is often tacked on top of commissions and can run thousands of dollars. According to [new research](https://consumerpolicy.org/most-home-buyers-and-sellers-are-routinely-charged-junk-fees-that-sometimes-exceed-1000/) from the Consumer Policy Center (CPC), these administrative fees cost homebuyers and sellers collectively nearly $2 billion a year — an estimate the think tank calls conservative. The fees typically range from $400 to $600, but sometimes exceed $1,000 and have been reported as high as $2,500.

The charges arrive at a moment of acute strain in the U.S. housing market. The median existing home sold for $440,600 in June 2026, and 30-year fixed mortgage rates hover around 6.5%, making every additional cost at closing harder to absorb.

## What the Fees Are

In the contracts between consumers and their agents, these charges go by many names: administrative fee, admin fee, transaction fee, regulatory compliance fee, document storage fee, broker fee, and technology fee. Ostensibly, they cover overhead — processing paperwork, meeting regulatory requirements, insurance, legal reviews, and document storage. In practice, the proceeds generally flow straight to the brokerage rather than to the agent.

"It's been growing for so long," said Wendy Gilch, a fellow at the Consumer Policy Center who co-authored the report. "Consumers didn't really ask what it was for, or were told it was their cost. And we're finding out that's not really the case."

The fees are due at closing, meaning they are an immediate out-of-pocket expense rather than something spread over a 30-year mortgage. That timing, consumer advocates argue, makes them particularly punishing for buyers who are already stretching to cover a down payment and inspection costs.

"For some buyers that's doable," Gilch said. "But for other buyers who are scraping together as much as possible to even get their down payment and to pay for their inspection, that can be a lot of money."

## Agents Are Among the Loudest Critics

The real estate industry itself is divided over the practice. Many agents openly criticize the fees as unjustifiable given that commissions already exceed $10,000 on a typical sale.

"I've experienced it and I think they're crap," said April Green, a real estate agent in South Florida with 15 years of experience, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5968763/housing-home-buying-junk-fees). "They just put $495 or whatever and it was just because they could. I've had a bad taste in my mouth with the admin fees since then, because what's it really for?"

The CPC report cites dozens of agent complaints, with critics describing the fees as "unethical," "greedy," "bottom-feeding," and "money grab." Some agents have refused to pass the charges to clients, a few have covered them personally, and some have left their brokerages in protest.

"It is difficult for brokers to justify charging a buyer or seller an admin fee when they are also charging them a three percent commission," said Stephen Brobeck, a senior fellow at the CPC. "Because the fees are regressive, sometimes effectively increasing the commission rate by over half a percentage point, they hit first-time homebuyers especially hard."

## Why the Fees Have Spread

One driver, researchers suggest, is competition among brokerages to recruit agents by offering more generous commission splits. To offset that lost revenue, some brokerages have shifted their income stream toward client-facing fees. The CPC found that fees are usually imposed by brokerages on their agents, who then pass them to clients — though the report also noted that some agents have increased the charge and pocketed the difference, or levied a fee where none was required.
There is little public data on exactly how far the fees have spread, because settlement statements are not public records. But the CPC reports that the fees now appear in most home sales, and that their frequency and size have grown.

"Had to work hard for it." — Kelly MacDonald, homebuyer

Kelly MacDonald, a homebuyer in a Cincinnati suburb, encountered the practice directly. After finding an unfamiliar $425 fee in her contract, she turned to Reddit for answers and learned it was a junk fee. She ultimately negotiated for the seller to cover it.

"I kind of feel guilty they paid for it," MacDonald said. "I think the realtor should have paid for it."

## A Disclosure Problem — and a Lawsuit

Beyond the cost itself, the CPC flags a disclosure problem. Gilch found that the fees are sometimes added without proper notice — sometimes just days or even hours before closing, when consumers are under intense pressure to sign off on the sale.

"It appears that in the early stages of the sale, a number of agents are not informing, either verbally or in writing, the imposition of these junk fees," Gilch said. "When a consumer learns about the junk fee at a closing, they are under great pressure to approve it."

That lack of disclosure is at the heart of litigation. Florida homebuyers Jeff and Milissa Efron filed a class-action complaint against the brokerage Compass over a $475 fee they said was added to what had been a Florida Bar-approved purchase contract, according to [Real Estate News](https://www.realestatenews.com/2026/06/26/compass-transaction-fees-under-fire-in-new-class-action-suit). The complaint alleged that the modification amounted to "the illegal practice of law" and violated the Florida Consumer Collections Practices Act and the Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act.

Compass, which began rolling out nationwide transaction fees in February 2026 after acquiring Anywhere Real Estate, has said charging such a fee "has been standard practice in major markets for years, and is done by many other brands in the industry." The Efron case was later voluntarily dismissed and closed, per NPR. Compass did not respond to NPR's request for comment.

## A Broader Backdrop

The dispute sits within a wider national focus on hidden fees. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has pursued a junk-fee initiative targeting overdraft and credit card late fees, and states have begun moving on their own. Real estate's version is distinct, however: it is layered on top of commissions rather than a ticket or banking charge.

Housing affordability has become a potent political issue ahead of the midterms. In June 2026, Congress passed the 21st Century Road to Housing Act — which NPR described as the largest housing affordability bill in decades — with bipartisan support. President Trump refused to sign it, calling it "a big yawn," so it became law automatically at midnight on July 10–11, 2026, when the 10-day clock expired. The law contains more than 40 provisions aimed at boosting homebuilding. As [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/10/nx-s1-5885027/housing-bill-without-trump-signature), it caps how many homes large corporate landlords can buy, reforms manufactured-home rules, and streamlines certain environmental reviews.

Those measures address supply and construction — but they do nothing directly about fees added at the closing table.

## What to Watch

The CPC has urged federal and state regulators to investigate the fees, especially those that are not properly disclosed, and has advised buyers and sellers to question any admin charge in their contracts. As [Business Insider noted](https://www.businessinsider.com/real-estate-junk-fees-home-buying-house-prices-more-expensive-2023-5) in an earlier examination of the practice, the issue raises a broader question of how many additional costs buyers and sellers should bear in an already expensive market.

"Any homebuyer or seller that is asked to pay this fee, they should ask their agent, 'Why isn't the 5% or 6% commission sufficient to cover all your costs?'" Brobeck said.

For now, the burden falls on consumers to read the fine print, push back on unfamiliar charges, and ask exactly what a fee is paying for. The National Association of Realtors, which represents agents and brokers, told NPR that its members must "act in the best interests of their clients," including explaining and disclosing all contracts in clear language and avoiding misrepresentation. Whether that standard is met — and whether regulators act — will determine how much longer the junk fee remains a quiet addition to the cost of buying a home.
