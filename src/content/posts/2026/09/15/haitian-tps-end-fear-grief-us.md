# End of Haitian TPS Brings Fear and Grief Across US

Nearly 350,000 Haitians in the United States lost their legal status on August 5, when Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for Haiti formally expired following a Supreme Court ruling that cleared the way for the Trump administration to end the designation. Sixteen years after a devastating earthquake first brought Haitians under American protection, families are now weighing impossible choices, and communities from Miami to Springfield, Ohio, are reeling.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5961154/for-thousands-of-haitians-in-the-u-s-end-of-protected-status-brings-fear-grief-and-defiance), the change has left Haitian nationals facing the prospect of deportation to a country the U.S. State Department itself warns Americans not to visit.

## A Protection Built on Disaster, Dismantled by Court Ruling

Congress created TPS in 1990 to shield immigrants from deportation to countries suffering natural disasters, armed conflict, or other dangerous instability. Haiti received its designation after the January 2010 earthquake, and over 16 years the status was repeatedly extended and redesignated as the country's crises compounded.

The termination accelerated in June, when the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in Mullin v. Doe that the TPS statute bars judicial review of most non-constitutional challenges to a termination decision. Justice Samuel Alito wrote the majority opinion, joined by the court's other conservative justices, holding that the statutory language is "clear" and "very broad." Justice Elena Kagan dissented, joined by Justices Sonia Sotomayor and Ketanji Brown Jackson.

As [Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/144302/supreme-court-mullin-doe-tps/) analyzed, the ruling "effectively bars federal courts from exercising jurisdiction over all statutory claims" tied to the TPS decision-making process, meaning the Secretary of Homeland Security's failure to consult with other agencies, or her reliance on factors not authorized by the statute, could go unreviewed. The decision placed protections for more than a million TPS holders from 17 countries at risk, most of whose designations have now been terminated.

The court's majority concluded that "none of the cited statements by either the President or the Secretary was overtly racial." Kagan's dissent countered that the evidence, including presidential remarks she said were "so repellent and racially inflected that the majority declines to put them in print," "fairly shout[s], in their racial undertones and overtones alike, that race entered into the President's resolve to remove Haitians from this country."

The Department of Homeland Security defends the move on policy grounds, telling NPR that "Temporary Protected Status is exactly that" — temporary. "For too long, TPS has been allowed to function as a de facto amnesty program despite Congress never intending it to be permanent."

## A Neighborhood Gone Quiet

Nowhere is the human cost more visible than in Miami's Little Haiti, the historic heart of America's largest Haitian diaspora. Florida is home to nearly half of all Haitian TPS recipients in the country, and the neighborhood's main business corridor has grown eerily still.

"Our businesses are empty. Churches are empty. The streets are empty. People have completely gone underground," Santra Denis of the Haitian American Professionals Coalition told [USA TODAY](https://www.detroitnews.com/story/news/nation/2026/08/20/trump-ended-haitian-tps-how-miamis-little-haiti-affected/91383164007/). "It's quite devastating."

Jan Mapou, owner of Libreri Mapou, a Haitian Creole bookstore that has anchored the neighborhood for decades, described a community in hiding. "Everybody is afraid, traumatized," he said. "They just don't want to expose themselves. They don't go out."

Father Reginald Jean-Mary, who has led Notre Dame d'Haiti Catholic Church for 25 years, said attendance at his adult learning school has collapsed from 231 students to 17, and that he knows of 14 parishioners detained by immigration officers. "This is a community being deconstructed completely," he told NPR.

## Economic Ripples Across South Florida

The loss of legal work authorization has rippled through an economy that depended on Haitian labor. An estimated 52,000 Haitian TPS holders worked in the greater Miami metro area in agriculture, food service, nursing assistance, landscaping, housekeeping, and security, according to research by Princeton's Phillip Connor cited by USA TODAY.

About 60 airport workers, members of the Service Employees International Union, lost jobs at Fort Lauderdale's airport. Two home-care agencies closed after losing 85 of their 86 workers, and Jewish Community Services of South Florida lost 18 Haitian employees who had built long-term bonds with aging Holocaust survivors.

Chef Wilkinson Sejour, owner of the Chef Creole restaurant chain, lost 14 staffers and has struggled to keep seven locations open. A Trump voter in 2024, he now says he regrets it. "I'm not saying to break the law," he said. "But at least say, let's take our time and really figure out a pathway. They've been rooted here. They've been here for a while. It's not fair just to uproot them overnight."

Advocacy groups report that some former TPS holders are being picked up in traffic stops or receiving notices to appear in immigration court, steps that often lead to deportation orders. U.S. officials have warned the Haitian government to expect at least two weekly deportation flights carrying about 200 people each, landing at Cap-Haitien because gang violence has shut down Port-au-Prince's airport.

## A Country Too Dangerous to Return To

The administration's push to deport Haitians sits awkwardly alongside its own travel guidance. The State Department has issued a "Do not travel" advisory for Haiti, citing crime, terrorism, kidnapping, and unrest.

Conditions on the ground have only worsened. At a Security Council meeting in late August, United Nations officials [reported](https://press.un.org/en/2026/sc16444.doc.htm) that at least 47 people were massacred in Kenscoff, including children and civilians sheltering in a church. The UN documented 1,408 people killed and 656 injured between April and June 2026 alone, with nearly 1.5 million people, 12 percent of the population, internally displaced, half of them children.

Harlaine Dominique, a 38-year-old travel nurse from Coral Springs, captured the dilemma facing families with U.S.-born children. "If I was to be picked up by ICE today, I would be separated from my son," she said. "I would be forced to leave without him."

Some former TPS holders have applied for asylum, but approval rates have fallen below 5 percent, according to the American Immigration Council. Others are considering Canada, Chile, or Brazil, or returning to smaller Haitian cities such as Cap-Haitien despite the dangers.

## A Political Fight Without a Fix

Congress has not been silent. In April 2026, the House passed a bill 224-204 to extend Haiti's TPS through April 2029, following a discharge petition led by Rep. Ayanna Pressley (MA-07), co-chair of the House Haiti Caucus, that secured the 218 signatures needed to force a floor vote.

"Throughout the nation, Haitians are parents, workers, caregivers, faith leaders, business owners, and children who are deeply rooted in our communities, essential to our economy, and are shamefully at risk of being deported to an island grappling with a devastating humanitarian crisis," Pressley [said](https://pressley.house.gov/2026/03/28/breaking-pressley-led-effort-to-extend-haiti-tps-secures-majority-support-moves-to-floor-vote/) at the time.

The measure has not become law, and the administration has continued its broader campaign to end TPS designations. Advocacy groups now say the only realistic path forward runs through Congress.

For those directly affected, the wait is measured in fear and faith. Father Jean-Mary said he reminds his parishioners to hold on to one another. "We will not give up," he said. "I believe Satan has his hour. But God has his day."

Herlain, a registered nurse who asked NPR to withhold her last name, rarely leaves her home anymore, and has instructed family members to record on their phones in case she is detained. "You would think I was on the run," she said. "Who am I on the run from? This is crazy!"

## What to Watch

The immediate questions are legal and legislative. Whether the Senate takes up the House's TPS extension bill, and whether the broader Mullin ruling invites further challenges as other TPS terminations work through lower courts, will shape the fate of hundreds of thousands. Meanwhile, the nearly 350,000 Haitians who lost protection face a narrower present: applying for any remaining legal pathway, preparing their American-born children for an uncertain future, and deciding, each day, whether it is safe to leave the house.
