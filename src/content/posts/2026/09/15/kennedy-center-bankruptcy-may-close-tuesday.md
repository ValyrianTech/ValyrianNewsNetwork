# Kennedy Center on Brink of Bankruptcy, May Close Tuesday

The John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts says it is on the brink of bankruptcy and could close its main building as early as Tuesday, according to the [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/14/nx-s1-5968690/kennedy-center-financial-physical-state). The iconic Washington, D.C. cultural institution faces a severe financial and physical crisis that has raised alarm about the future of one of the nation's premier performing arts venues — a crisis entangled with a legal battle over whether President Donald Trump's name may be added to the building's facade.

According to NPR, the center's administration told its board that the institution is on the brink of bankruptcy. Both the majority of the center's administration and its board were selected by Trump, who now serves as the center's chairman.

## How the Crisis Unfolded

A 57-page internal document, obtained by NPR and first reported by the Washington Post on Sunday evening, was issued ahead of a board meeting slated for Tuesday. The document claims that within a few weeks the institution will not be able to pay employees or meet maintenance costs, and it recommended that the main building be closed immediately on safety grounds.

The board's Tuesday vote, scheduled for 12:30 p.m., is now focused "exclusively on the necessary closure of the Center," citing "recently exacerbated structural emergencies," according to Joe LaFauci, the center's vice-president of board relations.

The crisis reached a visible tipping point on September 5, when the center said a portion of the ceiling in the main building's grand foyer collapsed due to rain damage. In a statement after the collapse, Roma Daravi, the center's vice-president of public relations, called it "another example of the urgent need to close for renovation and revitalization, as our Chairman President Trump has championed."

"This structural failure stems from decades of neglect and deferred maintenance by the previous leadership, and there's no justification for further delays in restoring America's cultural center," Daravi said.

## A Fight Over Trump's Name

At the center of the dispute is a proposal to inscribe Trump's name on the building. The center's administration argues that Trump is the only person who can fundraise at scale or oversee such an extensive renovation, and that he therefore deserves his name on the building — comparing the arrangement to big donors at hospitals and universities who see their names on prominent display.

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick made that argument last month outside a court hearing in a lawsuit brought by Rep. Joyce Beatty, a Democrat from Ohio and an ex-officio Kennedy Center board member. "Basically, it would cost four or $500 million if you do it over time or it'll cost $257 million if President Trump is the construction manager," Lutnick said. "He has the power and he has the expertise to get this done. He is the greatest builder in the world. And he will get it done on time and on budget."

The board's weekend documents drew sharp criticism. Norm Eisen, one of Beatty's attorneys, called the board's document release "an outrageous act of hostage taking," writing on X: "Pay up or the Kennedy Center closes, & the price is Trump's name on the building."

The proposal to close and rename has also faced legal setbacks. A judge ruled in May that adding Trump's name to the facade was illegal because only Congress has authority to rename the building, and the lettering was removed in June after a judge rejected an emergency appeal. In August, the board voted again to reinstate Trump's name and to close the venue for a multiyear renovation, according to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/14/kennedy-center-bankruptcy-trump).

On Tuesday, Judge Christopher Cooper reaffirmed his earlier position, ruling that Trump's name must stay off the center's exterior. "Simply put, defendants cannot install memorials for President Trump or anyone or anything else at the Kennedy Center without Congress's blessing," the judge wrote. "The board resolution bucks a federal court order and a statute Congress enacted."

## A Steep Slide Since 2025

The turmoil traces back to February 2025, when Trump dismissed 18 board members appointed under President Joe Biden. Days later, the remaining trustees voted him in as chair, breaking with decades of precedent under which sitting presidents kept the institution at arm's length. The board also fired Deborah Rutter, who had served as the center's president since 2014, and replaced her with Trump ally Richard Grenell.

The shake-up prompted a wave of departures and cancellations. Board treasurer Shonda Rhimes, National Symphony Orchestra artistic adviser Ben Folds and opera singer Renée Fleming resigned their positions, while actor Issa Rae pulled her sold-out show from the venue in protest. The Washington Post cited a 36% drop in subscription sales by June 2025, and ticket sales continued to soften into the autumn. Both artists and audiences have fled the center over the past year and a half, saying it has become politicized under Trump; its live event calendar is now a small fraction of its former size, and donations have dried up.

Grenell, who tried to insist that every performance be revenue-generating or revenue-neutral, was himself replaced as president in March 2026 by Matt Floca, formerly the center's vice-president of facilities operations. Floca has no experience in artistic direction, fundraising or arts administration, unlike his peers at similar centers across the country, and no one else on the board has any known experience with comparable performing arts institutions.

## The Financial Picture

A spokesperson said the center had historically run an operating deficit of about $100 million a year, and that prior leadership left a bottom-line deficit of $26 million, describing the previous budget as "falsified." That echoes an allegation Grenell made in 2025, when he said the center's new chief financial officer had found "$26 million in phantom revenue" and called it "criminal" — a claim Rutter and former board chair David Rubenstein rejected at the time. Rubenstein said the center's financial reports "were reviewed and approved by the Kennedy Center's audit committee and full board as well as a major accounting firm."

Some claims surrounding the closure are also disputed. The board's draft resolution describes the main building as "unsafe for continued occupancy," but Beatty's lawyers argue that the safety consultants cited — Delta Consulting Group — "expressly disclaimed" reaching any such opinion.

## What to Watch

Tuesday's board meeting marks the latest turn in a financial and legal fight that has dogged the center since Trump installed himself as chair. According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/15/trump-kennedy-center-renovations), the board quietly shelved a planned vote to restore Trump's name to the facade, though it planned to press ahead with the closure vote.

Separately, lawyers for the center have argued in litigation that hundreds of millions of dollars in private donations already pledged would have to be returned if Trump's name were not restored, citing the institution's bylaws. The board later told a federal judge it would hold off on adding the name until at least October 8 — a month's delay on its earlier timetable.

Whether the board formally votes to close the building, how the Beatty lawsuit proceeds after Cooper's ruling, and whether the institution can survive without the bankruptcy it now warns of all remain open questions. What is clear is that a venue conceived as a living memorial to President John F. Kennedy — and long kept at arm's length from the presidency — now finds its survival bound up with the very politics it was designed to transcend.
