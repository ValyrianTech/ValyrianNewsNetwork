# Times Square Probe Sparks GOP Backlash Over New York's Mental Health Crisis

New York Attorney General Letitia James is facing sharp criticism from Republican lawmakers and her November election challenger after her office opened a legally required investigation into the police shooting of a woman who fatally stabbed a Bank of America executive in Times Square. The probe has become a flashpoint in the ongoing debate over how New York handles its most severely mentally ill residents.

The controversy centers on Pamela Cisneros, 49, of Queens, who went on a random stabbing spree in Times Square on August 31, killing Erin Piacenti, 32, a Bank of America vice president and new mother who was in her first week back from maternity leave. Cisneros also wounded Tak Kam, 68, of Rhode Island, before NYPD officers confronted her in a tense standoff that ended in deadly gunfire, according to [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/letitia-james-times-square-shooting-probe-draws-gop-criticism-new-yorks-mental-health-crisis).

## The Investigation and the Backlash

On September 2, James' Office of Special Investigation announced it would investigate Cisneros' death. Under New York's Executive Law, the office is mandated to investigate any death caused by police officers — a statutory requirement, not a discretionary choice. But Republican Attorney General candidate Saritha Komatireddy has seized on the probe, arguing that it fails to address the systemic failures that allowed a known mentally ill individual to commit a deadly attack.

"The problem is that we have people who are known dangerous individuals, people with documented mental illness," Komatireddy told Fox News Digital. "They've been in the courts before, or they've been to the hospitals before. The police know exactly who they are, but we have no ability to take care of them."

A spokesperson for James' campaign pushed back sharply, accusing Komatireddy of seeking to "score cheap political points off of a tragedy."

"Saritha Komatireddy wants to be Attorney General but doesn't understand the law," the spokesperson said. "Under New York's Executive Law, the Office of the Attorney General's Office of Special Investigation is required to investigate any incident where a police officer's actions may have caused a death, including this one."

## A Documented History of Mental Health Struggles

NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch confirmed that Cisneros had no arrest record in New York City but had two prior police encounters linked to mental health crises. In March 2019, she attempted suicide by jumping onto E train tracks. In March 2018, officers found her in Midtown, screaming, belligerent, and intoxicated, as [reported by Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/us/knife-wielding-woman-accused-stabbing-2-times-square-before-being-shot-police).

Cisneros' brother, Patrick Peralta, told the [New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/09/01/us-news/times-square-stabbers-brother-reveals-long-struggle-with-mental-health-possible-bipolar-diagnosis/) that his sister battled what he believed was bipolar disorder and had been receiving monthly injections for treatment. A mother of three daughters, she had been getting treatment in recent years and reported feeling "fine" as recently as the Saturday before the attack.

"She never showed any aggressiveness or being violent," Peralta said. "I don't know what happened."

## Komatireddy's Proposed Solution

Standing at the Times Square crime scene on September 2, Komatireddy announced that if elected, she would create an Office of Civil Commitment within the Attorney General's Office — staffed jointly by law enforcement and physicians — to identify and commit severely mentally ill dangerous individuals before they strike again.

"The Office of Civil Commitment that I'm going to launch will be staffed with both law enforcement personnel and also physicians," Komatireddy said. "You need both because, under state law, the law requires that physicians certify that someone has the mental state required for involuntary civil commitment. There's nothing like that right now in New York state. There's no office that has both law enforcement and physicians who can work together to get these people off the streets."

Komatireddy cited a disturbing pattern of recent attacks by individuals with known mental health issues. In May 2026, Ross Falzone, 76, a retired schoolteacher, was shoved to his death down a Chelsea subway staircase by a man who was still wearing the wristband from the Bellevue psychiatric unit that had discharged him one hour after admission. In March 2026, Richard Williams, 83, an Air Force veteran, was shoved onto the tracks at Lexington Avenue-63rd Street. And in December 2024, Debrina Kawam, 57, was burned alive on an F train by a man living in a Brooklyn shelter, as [reported by the Yonkers Times](https://yonkerstimes.com/from-times-square-crime-scene-saritha-for-nyag-says-just-fight-crime/).

"Erin Piacenti walked through the busiest intersection in America and never walked out," Komatireddy said. "The woman who killed her was known to the system for years. The system did nothing. On my first day as attorney general, that changes."

## The Strain on Local Law Enforcement

The challenges extend far beyond New York City. Greene County Sheriff Peter Kusminsky told Fox News Digital that his deputies are not trained to handle psychiatric crises and that the county has nowhere to send people who need real care. Deputies are constantly pulled off patrol for mental-health calls, he said, then left to place those in crisis in hotels because there aren't enough hospital beds available.

"We really struggle when we have people in custody who we aren't equipped to handle, and secure beds are even harder to find," Kusminsky said. "It sometimes takes weeks or months to locate one, and when we do, they are maybe on the other side of the state and at an exorbitant cost."

Under New York's Mental Hygiene Law, police and doctors can place someone on a psychological hold if they're deemed a risk to themselves or others — but those individuals are often released back onto the streets within hours. Komatireddy argues this revolving-door approach has created a crisis where known dangerous individuals repeatedly slip through the cracks.

## A Human Tragedy at the Center

The political debate has unfolded against the backdrop of a deeply personal tragedy. Erin Piacenti was a University of Pennsylvania graduate who attended Fordham Law School alongside her future husband, Frank. The couple married on August 10, 2024, and welcomed a baby girl five months before her death. She had just returned from maternity leave and was heading home through Times Square when her path crossed with Cisneros, as [detailed by the New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/09/01/us-news/victim-stabbed-to-death-in-times-square-rampage-was-vp-at-bank-of-america-just-celebrated-2nd-wedding-anniversary/).

"One life, cut far too short by a senseless act of violence," Tisch said at a press conference following the attack.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani also credited the responding officers. "I want to thank the NYPD officers who stepped in and prevented a horrific attack from becoming even worse," he said.

## The Political Stakes

The controversy is unfolding against the backdrop of the November 3, 2026 New York Attorney General election, pitting incumbent Democrat Letitia James — who has served since 2019 and is seeking a third term — against Komatireddy, a Harvard-educated former federal prosecutor and Trump-endorsed Republican challenger, according to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_New_York_Attorney_General_election). A Siena College poll from August 2026 showed James leading Komatireddy 54% to 36% among likely voters.

Komatireddy has accused James of focusing on political battles rather than public safety issues, arguing that the attorney general has neglected the state's mental health crisis. Her campaign message has been blunt: "If New York isn't safe, nothing else matters."

## What's Next

As the Office of Special Investigation conducts its mandatory review of the police shooting, the broader policy debate over New York's approach to severe mental illness is likely to intensify in the run-up to the November election. Komatireddy's proposal for an Office of Civil Commitment represents a significant policy departure that would pair law enforcement with medical professionals in new ways — a model that raises questions about civil liberties and the limits of involuntary commitment.

For now, the families of both Erin Piacenti and Pamela Cisneros are left to grapple with a tragedy that has exposed deep fractures in New York's mental health and public safety systems. The investigation into the police shooting will proceed as required by law, but the political and policy questions it has raised are unlikely to be resolved anytime soon.
