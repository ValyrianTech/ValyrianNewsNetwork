# Supreme Court Allows Trump Ballroom Construction to Continue

A divided Supreme Court on Monday allowed the White House to continue construction on President Donald Trump's $400 million ballroom project as lawsuits play out in lower courts, delivering a significant legal victory for the administration. The 5-4 ruling replaces a [temporary order](https://apnews.com/article/trump-white-house-ballroom-supreme-court-b3eaee672028e584d0c520180c663e2c) issued earlier this month by Chief Justice John Roberts, who wrote a strong dissent arguing the project is "likely unlawful" because it hasn't been approved by Congress. [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-white-house-ballroom-supreme-court-6b9434af0a2dbeffa0ca19f5cdf7d775) reported the decision.

The majority found that the National Trust for Historic Preservation likely lacked legal standing to challenge the project, ruling on procedural grounds rather than the legality of the construction. "Today, we do not pass upon the legality of the government's East Wing project," the court said in its [unsigned order](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/26a203_2b8e.pdf), adding that "the government is likely to prevail in showing that the Trust lacks standing to challenge the project in federal court."

## Context and Background

The project involves demolishing the East Wing of the White House — built in 1902 and expanded in 1942 — to build a 90,000-square-foot ballroom capable of seating up to 999 guests. The administration says crews are working 20 hours a day, seven days a week, and the project is approximately 65% complete. The ballroom will include missile-resistant columns, drone-resistant ceilings, ballistic- and blast-proof glass, plus an underground complex with bomb shelters and medical facilities, according to [Time](https://time.com/article/2026/09/01/white-house-east-wing-ballroom-trump-supreme-court/).

The National Trust for Historic Preservation sued in December 2025, arguing that Trump has no unilateral authority to undertake the work without congressional approval. Federal law prohibits the construction of any structure on federal parks or public grounds in the District of Columbia "without the express authority of Congress." U.S. District Judge Richard Leon ordered a stop to aboveground construction in April, and the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals [upheld](https://media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2026/08/26-5123.pdf) that injunction on August 7 in a 2-1 decision, finding the project was for Congress to decide and "not a matter for Executive self-help," as [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-ballroom-court-ruling-1528247275ec6103da892d271d4ee883) reported.

## The Court's Divide

Chief Justice Roberts, joined by the court's three liberal-leaning justices, argued that the project likely violates the separation of powers. "Today's decision is no victory for the separation of powers," Roberts wrote. He noted that Congress has "explicitly prohibited the construction of any 'building or structure … on any reservation, park, or public grounds of the Federal Government in the District of Columbia without the express authority of Congress.'"

The majority, however, concluded that the only harm the Trust claims is "the offense one of its members will suffer from having to view a structure of the 'scale,' 'height,' and 'massing' the government intends." The court held that "mere offense, disagreement, or distaste does not qualify as a concrete and particularized injury." Legal scholar Jonathan Turley [analyzed](https://jonathanturley.org/2026/09/01/eye-of-the-beholder-aesthetic-injury-and-the-white-house-ballroom/) the standing implications of the ruling.

Trump celebrated the ruling on Truth Social, calling it a decision in favor of the "Ballroom/Military Complex" and saying the project was "under budget and ahead of schedule." Brent Leggs, president and CEO of the National Trust for Historic Preservation, said the group was "deeply disappointed" but noted that "this is not the final decision on the merits of our case."

## Analysis and Implications

The ruling is significant beyond the ballroom itself. Georgetown Law professor Stephen Vladeck told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/01/nx-s1-5950587/supreme-court-trump-white-house-ballroom-separation-powers) that the decision "sets a bigger precedent," noting that the administration has repeatedly tried to spend money Congress has not appropriated. "When presidents spend money Congress has not appropriated, they are not simply expanding executive power but taking away one of Congress' central constitutional powers," Vladeck said.

The court's standing ruling could also have broader implications. Erwin Chemerinsky, dean of UC Berkeley School of Law, observed that "even if the president's actions as to the ballroom are unconstitutional, no one will have standing to sue to challenge them." This is the third time since Trump's return to office that Roberts disagreed with all of his fellow conservatives on an emergency appeal involving spending matters, as [USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/09/01/supreme-court-ballroom-trump-chief-justice-john-roberts/91561990007/) noted.

The project's cost has escalated from an initial estimate of $200 million to $400 million, with internal contractor estimates from Clark Construction suggesting the actual cost could reach $600 million. The Washington Post reported that over half of that total was marked as coming from taxpayer-funded sources, including the US Secret Service and the White House Military Office. [Anadolu Agency](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/us-supreme-court-clears-path-for-trump-s-white-house-ballroom-project/4043156) also covered the ruling's implications.

## What's Next

The court did not rule on the legality of the project — the suit will return to lower courts for further proceedings. However, with construction reportedly 65% complete and crews working around the clock, the project may be largely finished by the time the legal questions are fully resolved. The ballroom is expected to be completed in summer 2028, with the framework expected by November 2026, according to [Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/trump-ballroom-white-house-demolition-construction-east-wing-timeline). The administration's [emergency application](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/26/26A203/419029/20260813211858520_August%20Ballroom%20Application%20-%20FINAL.pdf) argued the project was "vitally required by national security."

Congress has so far refused to authorize the project, and lawmakers may still take action to block or regulate it. The case raises fundamental questions about presidential power over federal property and the limits of executive authority — questions that are likely to persist regardless of the ballroom's ultimate fate.
