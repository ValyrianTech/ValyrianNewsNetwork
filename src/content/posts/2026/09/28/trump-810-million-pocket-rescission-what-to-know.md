# Trump's $810M Pocket Rescission: What to Know

President Donald Trump has moved to withhold $810 million in congressionally appropriated funding through a maneuver known as a "pocket rescission" — an action the federal government's own watchdog says is illegal. The White House [announced](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2026/09/president-trump-takes-historic-action-to-eliminate-wasteful-and-harmful-spending/) the cancellations on Friday evening, September 25, just days before the fiscal year ends on September 30, leaving Congress effectively no time to respond.

It is the second consecutive year Trump has used the tactic, escalating a constitutional confrontation over which branch of government controls federal spending.

## What Is a Pocket Rescission?

Under [Article I of the Constitution](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/essay/artI-S8-C1-2-1/ALDE_00013356/), Congress holds the "power of the purse" — the authority to levy taxes and decide how federal funds are spent. The Impoundment Control Act of 1974, which governs how a president may seek to cancel funding, requires the executive branch to send a rescission request to Congress with 45 days' notice so lawmakers can approve or reject it.

A pocket rescission exploits the calendar. By submitting the request so close to the September 30 end of the fiscal year, the funds expire before Congress can exercise its full review period. The money simply goes unspent, and lawmakers are left with no opportunity to reallocate it.

According to the [Government Accountability Office](https://www.gao.gov/blog/what-pocket-rescission-and-it-legal) (GAO), an independent, non-partisan watchdog, pocket rescissions are not permitted. "Congress holds the power of the purse — approving a budget and appropriating funds," the GAO states. "Presidents and executive branch agencies are responsible for administering those funds." The agency concluded that allowing a president to shorten the period of availability for fixed-term funds would effectively let the executive change the law.

## What Is Being Cut

According to White House briefing materials, the largest single item is **$567 million** from Health and Human Services programs that previously served refugees, asylees, and other non-citizens. The administration argues that Trump's border policies have reduced crossings, making the funds unnecessary.

Other targeted accounts include:

- **$70 million** from Education Department international education grants and fellowships
- **$56 million** from HUD housing counseling support for NGOs
- **$28 million** from HHS research programs the administration calls "overfunded and unaligned"
- **$25 million** from Education Department Special Programs for Migrant Students
- **$15 million** from the Justice Department's Community Relations Service, shut down early in the administration
- **$15 million** from Biden-era DHS programs providing services to undocumented immigrants
- **$10 million** from the Minority Business Development Agency
- **$10 million** from other Biden-era DHS programs
- **$9 million** from Treasury debt-relief programs tied to climate policy
- **$5 million** from the HHS Office of Minority Health

The cuts concentrate on immigration-related services, diversity and equity programs, climate initiatives, and health research tied to race and gender — reflecting the administration's stated policy priorities.

## A Bipartisan Rebuke

The move drew sharp criticism, including from a prominent Republican. Sen. Susan Collins of Maine, who chairs the Senate Appropriations Committee, said in a statement that the action undermines Congress's constitutional authority, CNBC [reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/26/white-house-810-million-pocket-rescission.html).

"This is the most recent attempt by this Office of Management and Budget (OMB) to undermine Congress's Constitutional power of the purse," Collins said. "OMB is an agency of the executive branch. It does not get to decide which programs are worth funding."

She added that the GAO "has concluded that pocket rescissions are unlawful and not permitted by the Impoundment Control Act," calling any effort to rescind appropriated funds without congressional approval "a clear violation of the law."

Democrats were more pointed. Sen. Patty Murray of Washington, the top Democrat on the Senate Appropriations Committee, called the move "theft from the American people, plain and simple," arguing that every Republican who voted for the affected spending bills should be furious because OMB Director Russell Vought is "saying their votes don't count."

Rep. Brendan Boyle of Pennsylvania, the top Democrat on the House Budget Committee, said Trump "knows he can't get these cuts through Congress, so he is illegally making them through the back door" — describing the action as "a blatant attack on Congress's constitutional power of the purse."

Sen. Jeff Merkley of Oregon [told](https://www.newsweek.com/lawmakers-rip-trump-over-illegal-pocket-rescission-move-what-we-know-12492082) Newsweek the move "violates the core separation of powers embedded in our constitution in which it is the Congress, not the president, who decides what programs are designed and at what level they are funded."

The White House did not respond to NPR's request for comment. The OMB, however, defended the action on X: "Wasteful funding? Rescinded. We will use every fiscal tool at our disposal to deliver savings for the American people."

## A Repeat of Last Year

This is not an isolated maneuver. In August 2025, the administration issued its first pocket rescission in roughly 50 years, canceling about [$5 billion](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2025/08/historic-pocket-rescission-package-eliminates-woke-weaponized-and-wasteful-spending/) in foreign aid and international organization funding. CNBC reported that the administration also blocked $4.9 billion in congressionally approved foreign aid through the same mechanism a year ago.

Collins and Sen. Lisa Murkowski of Alaska were the only Republicans to oppose an earlier $9 billion White House rescissions package, and Murkowski previously described the 2025 pocket rescission as "unlawful."

## Analysis: A Test of Constitutional Boundaries

At its core, the dispute is about whether a president can unilaterally decline to spend money Congress has appropriated. Supporters of the administration argue the Impoundment Control Act grants the president rescission authority. Critics, including the GAO, counter that the law never gave the executive the power to let funds lapse — only to propose their cancellation and await Congress's decision.

The timing is central to the tactic's effectiveness. Announcing on a Friday evening in late September leaves only days — not the statutory 45 — for any congressional response, and the funds expire regardless of what lawmakers do.

The practical stakes are significant. The affected programs serve refugees, migrant students, minority-owned businesses, and public health researchers. Whether those services are restored now depends on legal or legislative action that the calendar has made difficult.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is whether Congress, the GAO, or the courts take formal steps to challenge the rescission — and whether such challenges could arrive before the September 30 deadline. Longer term, the episode raises the question of whether pocket rescissions become an annual fixture of the budget process, and whether lawmakers will act to close the procedural gap that makes them possible.

For now, the funds will go unspent. What happens next will help determine where the balance of power over federal spending ultimately rests.
