# SpaceX Starship Reaches Orbit for First Time

SpaceX sent its Starship mega-rocket into Earth's orbit for the first time on Monday, a milestone flight that propels NASA's plans to return astronauts to the moon — even as the mission ended hours early following a thruster failure. The giant vehicle lifted off from the company's Starbase facility at Boca Chica Beach, Texas, at 7:48 a.m. CT (1248 GMT), according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/28/nx-s1-5983418/spacex-starship-first-orbital-flight-14-nasa).

The flight was the 14th test of the fully stacked Starship-Super Heavy system and the first attempt to reach orbital velocity. All previous flights had been deliberately suborbital, flying "passively safe" trajectories that kept debris away from populated areas.

## From Launch to Orbit

Starship's 33-engine Super Heavy booster ignited at liftoff, sending the towering stack skyward. On the launchpad, the rocket stands more than 120 meters (nearly 400 feet) tall, with the Starship upper stage sitting atop the Super Heavy booster, according to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/sep/28/spacex-starship-rocket-orbits-earth-texas). About two-and-a-half minutes after launch, the upper stage separated from the booster.

One of the six engines on the Starship upper stage failed during ascent, briefly casting doubt on whether the vehicle would be able to reach orbit. Mission controllers weighed whether to press on before deciding, about 22 minutes into the flight, that it was safe to continue. A single Raptor engine then burned for roughly 19 seconds to complete the orbital insertion burn, pushing the ship to about 28,000 km/h (17,400 mph).

"Starship is in orbit," mission control announced, confirming the achievement. SpaceX CEO Elon Musk celebrated on X, saying, "First orbital flight of Starship successful!" according to [Space.com](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-megarocket-flight-14-orbital-launch-success).

The Super Heavy booster, meanwhile, simulated an offshore landing in the Gulf of Mexico rather than being caught by the launch tower. SpaceX was testing software and hardware upgrades on the booster, which splashed down about seven minutes after liftoff.

## Starlink Deployment and an Early Return

During the flight, Starship deployed 26 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites, continuing SpaceX's effort to expand its broadband constellation. Three of those satellites carried cameras designed to inspect Starship's heat shield and transmit imagery — data intended to inform future reuse.

SpaceX had originally planned a roughly 10-hour flight with six orbits at an altitude of about 170 miles (275 km). After launch, however, mission managers cut the flight back to three hours, and the vehicle was ultimately directed to a controlled splashdown in the Pacific Ocean north of Hawaii. No one was on board.

## A Milestone with an Early Ending

The early conclusion of the mission underscored that Starship remains in development. An early thruster failure forced the spacecraft to end its flight nearly seven hours ahead of schedule. According to Space.com, the vehicle splashed down and then exploded — an outcome that does not necessarily indicate failure for a test flight designed to push the vehicle's limits.

Martin Barstow, a professor of astrophysics and space science at the University of Leicester, framed the achievement in broader terms. "This is an important milestone for SpaceX, with Starship successfully reaching orbit, and at the first attempt," he told The Guardian.

He added that the flight "marks the beginning of the transition from the testing and development phase to becoming a working vehicle able to deliver the large payloads needed to return to the moon," noting that large launch capability "will also be transformational for the whole space industry."

## Why It Matters

The stakes extend well beyond SpaceX. NASA has selected Starship as the crewed lunar lander for its Artemis program, meaning the vehicle must prove it can fly reliably and at high cadence before astronauts can use it. A successful orbital flight is a prerequisite for those ambitions.

The milestone is also important commercially. SpaceX went public in June 2026, and the company has warned investors that its growth depends on Starship. In its IPO filing, the company said that "any failure or delay in the development of Starship at scale or in achieving the required launch cadence, reusability and capabilities thereafter would delay or limit our ability to execute our growth strategy." Starship is the only vehicle capable of launching the larger V3 Starlink satellites that anchor the company's expansion plans.

## What's Next

Several significant hurdles remain. SpaceX has yet to demonstrate orbital refueling — a capability required to send Starship to the moon and Mars. The company also intends to catch both Super Heavy and the Ship upper stage with the launch tower to accelerate reuse; it has caught the booster on prior flights but has never attempted to catch the Ship.

SpaceX is planning to expand its launch footprint, including a proposed $100 billion facility in Louisiana and future launches from Florida's Space Coast. For now, the first orbital flight stands as a genuine turning point: Starship has left the realm of pure testing and shown it can reach the environment it was built for.
