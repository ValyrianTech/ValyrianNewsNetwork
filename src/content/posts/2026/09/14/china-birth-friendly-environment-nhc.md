# China Promises a More Birth-Friendly Environment

China's National Health Commission (NHC) pledged on September 14, 2026, to further build a "birth-friendly" environment, vowing to expand affordable childcare and lower the costs of having and raising children — the latest step in Beijing's escalating response to a shrinking population. Speaking at a State Council Information Office (SCIO) press conference, NHC Director Lei Haichao said the government would continue advancing integrated nursery-kindergarten development and support communities and employers in setting up childcare institutions.

According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260914/148fd317428541bdb516c720d76a7802/c.html), Lei told the briefing that the goal is to let residents obtain childcare support "nearby, conveniently, and at a relatively affordable price," while raising the childcare enrollment rate and effectively reducing families' childbearing, parenting, and education costs. The conference was part of the SCIO's "Getting off to a Good Start on the 15th Five-Year Plan" thematic series.

## A Shift Toward Supporting Families

The announcement marks the continuation of a policy pivot that has accelerated over the past five years. China has moved from a population growth model to what officials describe as a phase of "reduction and high-quality development," characterized by fewer births, an aging society, and uneven regional population change. In response, Beijing has shifted from merely encouraging childbirth to directly subsidizing child-rearing.

At the center of that effort is a national parenting subsidy launched in late 2025. As [Red Star News](https://m.163.com/dy/article/KNDTV9OS051492T3.html) reported from the March 2026 National People's Congress press conference, eligible infants born on or after January 1, 2022, receive 300 yuan per month for three years — a total of 10,800 yuan per child — with the standard applied uniformly regardless of whether the child is a first, second, or third. Lei noted at the time that the subsidy "is a big fiscal outlay" and likened the benefit to "a child being born with its own rations and its own salary."

At the September 14 briefing, Lei said more than 30 million families had already benefited, with the program reducing their costs of childbearing, parenting, and education, as [Chinanews](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/gn/2026/09-14/10696037.shtml) reported. The figure had risen to 33 million families by March 2026, according to the subsidy details reported by Red Star News, with central government spending exceeding 90 billion yuan and local spending adding more than 10 billion yuan.

## Building Childcare Capacity

The supply side has expanded alongside the cash benefits. Per [Sina Finance](https://finance.sina.com.cn/wm/2026-04-09/doc-inhtwxpa0928746.shtml), China now has about 126,000 childcare service institutions and 6.66 million childcare slots, with 254 municipal-level comprehensive childcare service centers covering more than 61% of prefecture-level cities. Since the start of the 14th Five-Year Plan, the number of institutions has grown by 66,000 (up 110%) and total slots by 3.79 million (up 132%).

Prices have fallen as supply has grown. The same analysis noted that nine provinces have introduced childcare subsidy systems and 1,315 counties have rolled out construction subsidies, operating subsidies, and family consumption vouchers, contributing to a 29% average drop in the price of full-day childcare.

For the first year of the 15th Five-Year Plan, the NHC has proposed adding 150,000 inclusive childcare slots, building on the 890,000 added in 2025, when total slots reached roughly 6.6 million.

## The Cost Question

The measures have drawn a mixed reception. Writing in the state-run Economic Daily and republished by [Guangming](https://life.gmw.cn/2026-03/27/content_38673071.htm), NHC researcher Hao Xiaoning pointed to persistent gaps: insufficient integrated medical-childcare supply, weak coordination mechanisms, and a shortage of professional staff. Over the next five years, China faces an estimated shortfall of about 5.5 million eldercare workers and nearly 1 million certified childcare personnel, she wrote.

Some parents and experts question whether the subsidy is large enough to change decisions. As [Sohu](https://www.sohu.com/a/1031251225_121194703) reported, one Beijing mother of two said the 300 yuan monthly payment "isn't even enough for a can of imported formula" in high-cost cities, while experts estimated the national subsidy covers only a little over 2% of child-rearing costs for Beijing families, limiting its incentive effect on middle-class households.

Proposed fixes include extending the subsidy beyond age three, tying it to household income, and linking eligibility to household registration and medical insurance data to enable automatic "no-application" receipt.

## Policy Ecosystem and Legal Backing

Childcare support now spans several policy levers. The personal income tax deduction for childcare and education has been raised from 1,000 to 2,000 yuan per child per month, and maternity insurance benefits have expanded — with the first seven months of 2026 seeing 784.2 billion yuan in fund expenditure benefiting 25.56 million person-times.

Legal scaffolding is forming as well. A draft Childcare Service Law was submitted to the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress for a first reading in December 2025, and the law was included in the 2026 legislative work plan. The draft sets strict rules on institution licensing, facility safety, and dietary standards, and establishes a permanent industry ban for people with records of abusing infants or other violations.

## What to Watch

The September 14 pledge underscores that stabilizing the newborn population remains a stated national priority under the 15th Five-Year Plan, which emphasizes optimizing birth-support policies and incentives. Yet the gap between policy ambition and family arithmetic — between a 300-yuan monthly transfer and the full cost of raising a child — remains the central test. How quickly China can expand inclusive childcare, close its caregiver shortage, and deliver subsidies more efficiently will determine whether its birth-friendly rhetoric translates into more births.
