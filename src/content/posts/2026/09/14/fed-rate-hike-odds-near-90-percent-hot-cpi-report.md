# Fed Rate Hike Odds Near 90% After Hot CPI Report

Market expectations for a Federal Reserve rate hike at this week's policy meeting surged to nearly 90 percent after a hotter-than-expected August inflation report, setting up the central bank's first increase in more than three years — and a politically charged decision arriving less than two months before the U.S. midterm elections.

According to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/august-cpi-report-inflation-fed-rates/), CME FedWatch data showed the probability of a quarter-point hike jumping to roughly 90 percent, up from about 70 percent the previous day. Other tallies put the odds in the mid-80s: [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/cpi-inflation-august-2026) cited CME FedWatch at 85.6 percent, up from 72.4 percent a day earlier and 59.4 percent the prior week. The Federal Open Market Committee meets Sept. 15-16, with its decision scheduled for 2 p.m. ET on Wednesday, Sept. 16.

## The Numbers Behind the Shift

Headline consumer prices rose 0.4 percent in August and 3.4 percent year over year, matching July but above the 3.3 percent economists had expected, CBS News reported. Core inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy costs, rose 0.3 percent month over month — hotter than forecast and accelerating from July's 0.2 percent. On an annual basis, core prices were up 2.4 percent, down slightly from 2.5 percent in July.

Energy was the dominant driver. Gasoline prices jumped 3.9 percent for the month and 27.4 percent over the year, with the Bureau of Labor Statistics attributing more than one-third of the headline monthly increase to fuel alone. Airline fares climbed 2.7 percent month over month and 23.4 percent year over year. Shelter and food costs continued to climb modestly, underscoring that price pressures have broadened beyond energy.

The reading landed a day after a separate report showed producer prices rising 5.4 percent annually, well above the Fed's 2 percent target, according to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/10/ppi-inflation-report-august-2026.html). Together, the two reports undercut hopes that inflation was reliably cooling.

## A Decision Shaped by War and Politics

The inflation surge is inseparable from geopolitics. Since the U.S.-Iran war began in late February 2026, global energy markets have been roiled by supply disruptions. Recent escalation has sharpened the squeeze: an attack on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil and gas passes, and a drone strike that temporarily shut Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/12/saudi-arabia-shuts-down-oil-pipeline-blaming-drones-from-iraq) reported that Riyadh blamed drones from Iraq for the pipeline shutdown — a route used to export oil via the Red Sea and bypass the Iranian blockade.

The market response has been emphatic. As [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/14/us-says-its-clearing-hormuz-traffic-why-are-oil-futures-beyond-100) reported, Brent crude rose $3.21 to $107.82 a barrel on Sept. 14, while West Texas Intermediate gained $3.17 to $103.22. U.S. diesel prices topped $6 a gallon, up roughly 60 percent from $3.71 a year earlier, a [Euronews analysis](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/09/14/oil-surges-past-107-as-hormuz-attack-and-saudi-pipeline-shutdown-rattle-markets) noted.

Economists warn the August CPI survey may already be behind the curve. The data was collected before the latest fuel price run-up, meaning it does not fully capture the energy-driven inflation now coursing through the economy.

"The renewed march higher in oil, gasoline and diesel prices adds to concerns that higher energy prices could spill over to other goods and services and inflation expectations," said Kathy Bostjancic, chief economist at Nationwide, per CBS News. "As such, we are now looking for the Fed to raise rates by 25 basis points at next week's policy meeting."

Greg Daco, chief economist at EY-Parthenon, put it bluntly to CBS News: "We are changing our Fed call from a hold to a 25bps hike at the FOMC meeting next week."

Others urged caution. Alexandra Wilson-Elizondo, global head and co-chief investment officer of multi-asset solutions at Goldman Sachs Asset Management, told Fox Business that while the report "came in broadly as expected," it "does make next week's rate decision a jump ball," because "the data does not fully capture some of the inflation pressures that have emerged more recently."

## The Warsh Dilemma

The decision belongs to Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, sworn in just four months ago after President Donald Trump urged him to be "totally independent." Trump has since repeatedly pressed for lower rates, and the choice now carries unusual political weight.

In July, the FOMC held rates steady, but three members dissented in favor of a hike — the first such split since 2016 — signaling internal momentum toward tightening. At Jackson Hole in late August, Warsh signaled the Fed might need to raise rates. Holding steady now could damage his credibility with colleagues and markets; hiking risks the president's public ire.

"They really are in a no-win situation where they incur the president's wrath or diminish their credibility in the markets, with consequences for inflation that are probably more severe down the road," Maurice Obstfeld, a former IMF chief economist now at the Peterson Institute for International Economics, told [USA TODAY and Bloomberg](https://www.usatoday.com/story/business/2026/09/13/will-fed-chair-warsh-defy-trump-on-interest-rates/91752334007/). "I don't think Warsh wants to go down as the Fed chairman who buckled to administration pressure when the Fed's mandate was at stake."

Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, framed the bind in blunter terms. "Warsh can't win politically right now," she told USA TODAY and Bloomberg. "If he hikes, he's going to get a tweet, and if he holds steady, he's going to get backlash" from markets. To Fox Business, she added that the problem runs deeper than fuel: "America has an inflation problem and it's more than just high gas and diesel prices.... The Federal Reserve needs to hike in September to prevent this from worsening."

Not everyone frames the politics as purely adversarial. Patrick Harker, a former Philadelphia Fed president now at Wharton, argued a hike could actually help the White House. "By raising rates, that signals that the Fed's on the job," he said. "That might help with what the administration is trying to do, not hurt it."

## Analysis: Why This Matters

A hike would lift the federal funds rate from its current 3.5 to 3.75 percent range to 3.75 to 4 percent — the first increase since July 2023, when the Fed was fighting a very different inflation fight.

The stakes extend well beyond a single meeting. Bloomberg economists Anna Wong and Andrew Sacher warned that markets have already priced in a hike, writing that "if the Fed does not hike, Warsh will lose credibility in the eyes of market participants." A surprise hold could trigger a sharp repricing across bonds and equities.

The decision also collides with the November midterms. Persistent inflation has been a central voter grievance over the cost of living, and a rate hike could inflame those concerns — even as it might help anchor long-term inflation expectations and bring down long-term Treasury yields.

The geopolitical backdrop leaves little room for comfort. So long as the Hormuz disruptions and the Russia-Ukraine war's strikes on energy infrastructure persist, oil and diesel prices are likely to stay elevated, keeping the inflation fire burning. War-risk insurance premiums for Hormuz transits have already jumped from about 0.25 percent of hull value to between 3 and 10 percent, raising shipping costs further, Al Jazeera reported.

## What to Watch

All eyes turn to Wednesday's 2 p.m. ET announcement, followed by Warsh's press conference — where he will be pressed on both the inflation outlook and the Fed's independence. Forecasters are already looking past September: Capital Economics projects a second hike in December 2026 and another in March 2027.

Key questions remain open. Will the Fed signal a sustained tightening cycle or a one-off move? How forcefully will Trump respond? And with energy markets still hostage to conflict, will policymakers be able to get ahead of inflation — or will they spend the coming months chasing it?
