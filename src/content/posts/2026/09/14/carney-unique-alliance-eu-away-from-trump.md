# Carney Pitches 'Unique Alliance' With EU, Away From Trump

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney is seeking a "unique alliance" with the European Union, not membership of the bloc, his office clarified on Sunday after a report suggested Ottawa was exploring an unprecedented "associate membership" status. The episode has laid bare just how far Canada is willing to reach beyond its traditional reliance on the United States.

Speaking to reporters at the Toronto International Film Festival on September 13, Carney drew a sharp line between partnership and accession. "We're not looking to become a member of the European Union," he said. "What we are looking — and will begin discussions for — is a unique alliance between Canada and the European Union. We share the same values, we have the same priorities and we have very complementary strengths."

The remarks came hours after The Wall Street Journal, [citing unnamed EU and Canadian officials](https://www.hln.be/buitenland/weg-van-trump-dichter-bij-europa-canadese-premier-pleit-voor-unieke-alliantie-met-eu~ac172aaf/), reported that Carney was exploring whether Canada could become an "associate member" of the EU — a status that does not currently exist and would have to be created. A senior Canadian government source told CTV News that the report "overstates" what is actually happening, insisting Canada is "not seeking European Union membership in any capacity."

## Why Now?

The timing is no accident. Canada is locked in an escalating trade conflict with the United States. The Trump administration has imposed billions of dollars in import tariffs on Canadian goods, prompting retaliatory measures from Ottawa. Since Donald Trump returned to the White House, relations between the two neighbours have deteriorated sharply — a rupture that has pushed Canada to look east.

Carney has spent months laying the groundwork. In a speech earlier this year he called on "middle powers" to band together in a more fragmented world. In August he announced that Canada would open talks with the EU in the autumn on a "much stronger and deeper security and economic partnership." This week he travels to Strasbourg, France, where he will become the first non-European head of government to attend the European Parliament's "State of the European Union" address before delivering a speech of his own to the 720-seat chamber.

It is a striking symbolic break. The pivot was framed bluntly by Belgium's Het Laatste Nieuws, which reported the story under the headline "Away from Trump, closer to Europe."

## What Is on the Table

According to the Journal's account, the two sides are discussing ways to let goods, services and workers move more freely within strategic supply chains — energy, artificial intelligence, defence and critical minerals. Also under discussion: laying undersea cables, jointly building data centres, cloud storage and satellite networks, and developing infrastructure to ship Canadian energy to Europe.

Carney has spoken regularly with EU leaders, most intensively with French President Emmanuel Macron, about allowing Canadians to live and work visa-free in the bloc, the newspaper reported. The two are expected to meet later in September, with Carney also due to visit Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon, a French territory off the coast of Newfoundland.

The two sides already have some scaffolding to build on. Canada became the first non-EU country to join the bloc's defence procurement programme, known as ReArm Europe or SAFE. The EU ambassador to Canada, Geneviève Tuts, has publicly framed the coming partnership as something "unlike anything that currently exists" — "something unique" that will not look exactly like the EU's arrangements with Switzerland, Norway or the United Kingdom.

"It's more than filling gaps," Tuts told The Canadian Press. "The idea is to go further in the integration, on substance and on the way we are working together."

## A Wide Gap Between Ambition and Reality

The central tension in the story is a semantic one, and it matters. The Wall Street Journal reported membership-adjacent ambitions; Carney and his government deny any such aspiration. The discrepancy suggests either that the initial report over-interpreted internal discussions, or that Ottawa is deliberately downplaying the membership label while pursuing deep integration in substance.

There is also a growing gap between rhetoric and deliverables. Achim Hurrelmann, co-director of the Centre for European Studies at Carleton University, cautioned that the package may amount to a collection of initiatives rather than a fundamentally new relationship. "I wouldn't expect much detail at this stage, because my understanding is that they are really a little bit scrambling for ideas of what to include in this new package," he said. "As always in the Canada-EU relationship, it's easier to come up with big pronouncements of new initiatives than to actually follow through on these announcements."

Geography alone rules out full EU membership: Canada is not a European state. Any arrangement would therefore have to be sui generis. And EU association agreements of the kind the bloc holds with Ukraine and Georgia offer close political and economic cooperation without full membership — but they are not the same as what Brussels appears to be sketching for Canada.

The deeper obstacle may be Europe's own appetite. More than a decade after the EU-Canada trade deal (CETA) was struck, and nine years after it provisionally entered into force, several member states — including Belgium, France and Italy — have still not ratified it. If capitals cannot ratify a trade agreement, sceptics ask, how quickly will they ratify something deeper?

For its part, EU officials have been careful not to promise a finished deal. Tuts has said she expects only discussions in the near term, with the possibility of further detail emerging from the speeches this week.

## What's Next

Carney's Strasbourg address and his meeting with Macron will be the first real tests of whether the "unique alliance" is more than a label. The decisive moment is likely to come on October 29–30, when Canada hosts an EU-Canada summit at which a possible partnership announcement is expected.

Domestic politics may complicate the picture. A March poll by Spark Advocacy found that 58% of Canadians thought joining the EU was worth exploring and about one in four thought it a good idea outright — but Conservative supporters were the most likely to oppose the idea, with roughly a third against it.

What remains unresolved is the most basic question of all: what, legally and practically, would a "unique alliance" actually consist of? For now, both sides are offering vision more than text. The coming weeks will show whether the substance can catch up with the branding — or whether, as Hurrelmann warns, it amounts to another set of announcements that never quite becomes a new chapter in the relationship.
