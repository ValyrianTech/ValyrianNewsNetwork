# China Holds Joint Sea-Air Drills Near Scarborough Shoal

The Chinese People's Liberation Army (PLA) Southern Theater Command announced on Sunday, September 27, 2026, that it had organized joint sea and air exercises in the waters and airspace around Huangyan Dao — known internationally as Scarborough Shoal — describing the drills as a "necessary action" responding to the current conduct of a certain country undermining regional peace and stability, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260927/5de128b5e39c4d7ca0e615c238b9efac/c.html).

The short official statement did not name the Philippines explicitly, nor did it specify the scale, assets, or duration of the exercise. It said the drills were intended to "test and enhance the troops' combat capability to safeguard national territorial sovereignty and maritime rights and interests."

## A Targeted Warning, Experts Say

The exercise's most striking feature, Chinese analysts said, was its clarity of purpose. Zhang Chi, a professor at China's National Defense University, told [Global Times](https://news.ifeng.com/c/8wks6NpsMHy) that the drills were a "solemn warning" against what he called the Philippines' infringing and provocative actions, aimed at strengthening rights-protection and law-enforcement control over the waters and airspace near Huangyan Dao.

"This joint exercise is to draw a line and set rules for the Philippines, to make it clearly realize that no matter what small maneuvers it makes or who it ropes in to take its side, its illegal schemes cannot succeed," Zhang said. He added that if the Philippines "remains obstinate, persists, and continues to provoke, China will definitely play along to the end and implement even stronger countermeasures."

That framing — a public announcement paired with sharply worded expert commentary — is characteristic of Beijing's approach of using military messaging to project both readiness and political intent.

## Context: A September of Escalation

The September 27 drill did not occur in isolation. It followed a week of confrontations that raised tensions across the South China Sea.

On September 10, China announced the designation of Huangyan Dao as a national nature reserve — a move regional analysts viewed as an attempt to claim the moral high ground and create a legal-administrative mechanism for tighter enforcement. The Straits Times [reported](https://www.straitstimes.com/asia/se-asia/manila-to-hold-its-ground-at-scarborough-shoal-even-as-beijing-turns-up-pressure) that Philippine Coast Guard spokesman Commodore Jay Tarriela countered that China was in no position to invoke environmental protection given its own artificial island-building in the South China Sea.

The situation intensified further on September 24, when the Philippine Coast Guard said Chinese naval and coast guard vessels obstructed a resupply mission to the BRP Sierra Madre garrison at Ren'ai Reef. China's Coast Guard said the Philippine side had breached an understanding and that Chinese vessels operated lawfully, while Manila described the Chinese maneuvers as dangerous. A Coast Guard flotilla was also reported to have conducted a law-enforcement patrol in the Huangyan Dao nature reserve, as [Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20260927-9742468) reported.

## The Disputed Shoal

Scarborough Shoal — Huangyan Dao in Chinese, Bajo de Masinloc in Filipino — is a contested atoll in the South China Sea claimed by China, the Philippines, and historically Taiwan. A standoff in 2012 ended with China exercising de facto control over the area.

In 2016, an arbitral tribunal constituted under UNCLOS Annex VII ruled that China's claim of historic rights within its "nine-dash line" had no legal basis and that Scarborough Shoal is a common fishing ground. Beijing rejects the ruling as null and void and maintains that Huangyan Dao is inherent Chinese territory.

## Repeating an Earlier Playbook

The September 27 announcement closely mirrors a near-identical exercise the Southern Theater Command held on August 1, 2026, in the territorial sea, airspace, and surrounding waters of Huangyan Dao — a drill explicitly described at the time as targeting the current South China Sea situation and "a certain country" in the region. That earlier exercise rehearsed reconnaissance and early-warning, deterrence patrol, sea-and-air control, and joint strike missions, according to state media.

The August 1 drill followed Manila's submission of an official chart of Scarborough Shoal and adjacent waters to the UN Secretariat on July 31, an attempt to draw a territorial sea baseline that China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Ministry of National Defense rejected as illegal and invalid.

## Analysis: Messaging Over Mechanics

Read together, the sequence of announcements suggests Beijing is using recurring, publicly disclosed exercises to establish a pattern of presence and to normalize its law-enforcement posture around the shoal, rather than to signal an imminent escalation. The deliberately vague language — "a certain country" — maintains a degree of diplomatic ambiguity even as state-linked commentary names the Philippines directly.

The dual-track messaging serves two audiences. Domestically, it demonstrates resolve to defend what Beijing considers sovereign territory. Regionally and internationally, it tests the reaction of the Philippines and its partners without committing to a specific new operational threshold.

Not all analysts see the trajectory as unmanageable. Hu Bo, director of the South China Sea Strategic Situation Probing Initiative in Beijing, has argued that while tensions at Scarborough Shoal are rising, the situation "should still be within control," characterizing Manila's large-scale deployment of vessels as provocative.

## What to Watch

The immediate focus will be the Philippines' response. Manila has signaled it will not be deterred, with its Coast Guard insisting that patrols and support for Filipino fishermen near Bajo de Masinloc will continue regardless of Chinese pressure.

The broader question is whether the recurring drills and the nature-reserve designation become a durable mechanism for tighter Chinese control — and whether periodic confrontations harden into a sustained crisis. With both sides publicly committed to maintaining their presence, the risk of miscalculation at sea remains, and each new exercise carries the potential to shift the region's fragile status quo.
