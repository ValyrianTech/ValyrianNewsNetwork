# Fortnite World Championship in Antwerp, No Belgians Play

The Fortnite Global Championship — the esports giant's season-ending world finals — is being held in Belgium for the first time this weekend, filling Antwerp's Lotto Arena with thousands of fans for a sold-out, two-day spectacle. Yet even on home soil, the host nation is almost entirely absent from the competition itself: no purely Belgian-flagged player qualified for the tournament.

The event, staged September 26–27, brings together 50 duos — 100 players in total — competing across 12 matches for a $2,000,000 prize pool, with the winning pair taking home $400,000, according to the [Lotto Arena](https://www.lotto-arena.be/en/event/fortnite-global-championship-2026-c1ed5ce1) event page, which lists esports company Blast.tv as organizer. Both days sold out, with tickets available only as a two-day combo.

## A Home Crowd Without a Home Team

Participants arrived from across the globe — from Argentina and Russia to Canada and New Zealand, as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/25/wk-fortnite-strijkt-neer-in-antwerpen/) reported. The notable exception was the near-total absence of Belgian players.

That framing comes with an important caveat. VRT NWS issued a correction to its own headline after publication: 17-year-old "Velofps," a British-Belgian dual national, is competing — but under the British flag. A second Belgian link is organizational rather than national: American player Dominick "Shadow" Ramos (18) competes for Natrix Esports, a Belgian club founded by a 15-year-old.

The nuance matters because it sharpens the story's central question. Belgium can fill an arena for Fortnite but could not produce a player to compete on its own stage.

## "Not Fertile Ground"

Those who follow Belgian esports closely are not surprised. "Belgium is unfortunately not fertile ground for professional esports players," Liesbeth Last, an esports teacher at KTA Brugge — the only secondary-education esports teacher in Belgium — told VRT NWS.

At the heart of the problem, she argues, is a lack of official recognition. "It's a shame that esports are not recognized as a job, nor as a sport, while that is the case for chess, for example," she said, adding that Belgium also lacks "a fiscal and legal framework for esports players."

That gap has concrete consequences. Because esports has no official status, schools cannot offer it as a formal subject — only as an elective. Teams trying to professionalize must either register as non-profits or follow the same administrative path as an ordinary business, with the same social contributions and prerequisites, even without revenue.

As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/pourquoi-l-esport-peine-t-il-a-se-professionnaliser-en-belgique-11621258) detailed, the sector operates in a legal "grey zone." Without a recognized framework, sponsors and investors are deterred. "How do you invest, and expect a return on investment, in a sector that doesn't really exist?" the outlet asked.

## The Cost of Competing Abroad

The financial burden falls hardest on players. Sammy "iDex" Van de Weghe, a Belgian professional Valorant player, told RTBF that the country's high contributions make domestic teams uncompetitive. "With all the contributions required in Belgium, employing a player costs a lot," he said. "We're not competitive against players from other countries, who have esports-adapted statuses."

The result, RTBF noted, is that talented Belgians leave for abroad — often France, where the state treats esports as a driver of innovation. Unlike traditional sports, Belgian esports functions more by linguistic community than by territory: French-speaking Belgians gravitate toward French teams and content, Flemish Belgians toward Dutch or English content. That fragmentation makes any domestic market harder to sustain.

Even so, pockets of ambition remain. Natrix Esports — founded by 15-year-old Noa Mot after he concluded he would not make it as a pro player — reached the world stage through Ramos, who finished 32nd at the Esports World Cup in May, per [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/noa-jeune-belge-de-15-ans-fonde-son-equipe-esport-deja-qualifiee-aux-championnats-du-monde-de-fortnite-11764604). Speaking to De Standaard, Mot remained bullish: "I think we have a chance at the top five."

## Why Fortnite, Why Belgium

That an event of this scale landed in Antwerp at all reflects Fortnite's unusual grip on the Belgian market. As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/anvers-va-devenir-le-centre-du-monde-du-jeu-video-ce-26-et-27-septembre-2026-11789241) noted, the game is free, runs on PC, console and smartphone, and is reinforced by star-studded brand collaborations with the likes of Ariana Grande, Eminem, Star Wars and Lego. Its social dimension — young people gathering online the way earlier generations gathered in the street — sustains its popularity.

Last also points to the broader value of the skills gaming builds. "You learn to think in a solution-oriented way and to communicate efficiently," she said, adding that Randstad research shows gamers score higher on teamwork and critical thinking. Fortnite, that research found, develops the most such skills of any title.

Yet she is clear-eyed about the missed opportunity. "Those platforms have become gigantic," she said of the game and social platforms now subject to the EU's new Kids Act. "We're actually 10 to 15 years too late. We're trying to save what can be saved."

## What to Watch

The Antwerp championship will crown a world champion this weekend regardless of who lifts the trophy, and organizers and the host city will count the sold-out arena as a success. The harder question is whether a world-class event in Belgium's backyard can translate into a functioning domestic ecosystem — one that recognizes esports, retains its talent, and gives the next generation of Belgian players a path to the stage rather than a ticket to watch from the stands.

For now, as Last put it, the country remains a place where esports is played passionately — but professionally, only abroad.
