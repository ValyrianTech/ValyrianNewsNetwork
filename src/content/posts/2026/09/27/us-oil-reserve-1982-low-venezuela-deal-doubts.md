# US Oil Reserve Hits 1982 Low Amid Venezuela Deal Doubts

The United States' emergency crude oil stockpile has fallen to its lowest level in nearly 44 years, and the Trump administration's plan to refill it through a sweeping Venezuela oil partnership is drawing mounting skepticism from energy analysts, legal scholars and even some officials.

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) held 285.36 million barrels in the week ended September 4, 2026, according to [Energy Information Administration data](https://investozora.com/us-strategic-petroleum-reserve-285-million-barrels/) — its lowest weekly level since November 5, 1982. The drawdown follows a massive emergency release earlier this year to counter fuel supply disruptions from the U.S.-Israel war with Iran, which began in late February and sent global oil prices surging.

## A Reserve Worn Thin

The SPR is a federal stockpile of crude oil stored in giant underground salt caverns along the Gulf Coast of Texas and Louisiana, designed to blunt the economic damage of severe petroleum supply disruptions. It has an authorized capacity of 714 million barrels across four sites.

At 285.36 million barrels, the reserve is roughly 40% full. A year earlier, in the week ended September 5, 2025, it held 405.22 million barrels — a decline of nearly 120 million barrels, or about 30%, in 12 months. The drop is even starker measured from March 20, 2026, when the SPR stood at 415.44 million barrels. Since then it has fallen by roughly 130 million barrels, or 31.3%.

On March 11, the Department of Energy announced a 172-million-barrel SPR release — the U.S. portion of a coordinated 400-million-barrel release by International Energy Agency member countries, delivered over about 120 days. Much of the program was structured as an exchange: companies receiving crude must return barrels later, typically with additional oil included. Energy officials have said those exchanges could return roughly 200 million barrels to the reserve over the following year, meaning the current low figure may not reflect the SPR's longer-term size.

Still, experts warn the rapid drawdown carries risks. Samantha Gross, director of the energy security and climate initiative at the Brookings Institution, told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/25/nx-s1-5965797/strategic-petroleum-reserve-low-level-venezuela) that salt caverns can be damaged by pumping too much oil out too quickly. "You can damage them by pumping too much out of them," she said.

Ben Cahill, a nonresident senior fellow at the Atlantic Council, put it more bluntly: "A lot of the things that got us through the last six months, all those buffers, have really been worn away."

## The Venezuela Bet

The administration's answer to the shrinking reserve is a deal announced in late August that President Donald Trump has called "the biggest oil deal in world history." Under the arrangement, Barbados-headquartered North American Blue Energy Partners (NABEP) received concessions to 17 Venezuelan oil fields for 100 years, covering roughly 65 billion barrels of proved reserves — about 20% of Venezuela's estimated 303 billion barrels.

According to a [White House fact sheet](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/08/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-announces-historic-oil-agreement-to-secure-american-energy-dominance-and-drive-venezuelas-economic-recovery/), NABEP granted the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital a 35% equity stake at no cost to taxpayers, and gave the State Department the right to purchase 20% of its output at production cost, plus a right of first refusal on the remaining 80%. The U.S. government can veto board appointments, and a majority of NABEP's board must be U.S. citizens. The White House says the low-cost oil can "facilitate refilling the Strategic Petroleum Reserve."

The deal, [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/05/trump-venezuela-oil-north-america-blue-energy-partners-nabep.html), would make NABEP the second-largest oil company in the world by proved reserves, behind only Saudi Aramco and roughly four times larger than Exxon's holdings. NABEP says it will bring nearly $100 billion in investment to Venezuela's oil sector and aims to exceed 1 million barrels per day of production.

But few historical precedents exist for the U.S. government taking a direct ownership stake in an oil company, let alone one operating foreign fields. "For the American government to get involved with a shady businessman concessionaire in a country that is known for endemic corruption, it just raises all sorts of red flags," said Tyler Priest, an oil historian at the University of Iowa.

Scott Lincicome of the Cato Institute was more pointed. "This is straight up a state-owned enterprise," he said. "De facto control of 100% of output at cost — that's ownership."

## Legal and Logistical Hurdles

A [Guardian investigation](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/26/donald-trump-oil-deal-with-venezuela) published Sept. 26 found the deal faces an array of legal and logistical problems. Venezuelan law does not permit 100-year oil concessions — the maximum is 25 years, a point Venezuela's own government has acknowledged. The Pentagon told lawmakers in a written briefing that it holds "warrants" — rights to purchase shares — rather than the 35% stake itself, and critics say the Office of Strategic Capital lacks legal authority to take equity in private companies.

Energy Secretary Chris Wright pushed back on some of the concerns, telling [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/02/cnbc-transcript-us-energy-secretary-chris-wright-speaks-with-cnbcs-brian-sullivan-on-squawk-box-today.html) that the government would not be the operator of the reserves and that Venezuelan crude would not go directly into the SPR. "We'll swap you a barrel of heavy crude for a barrel of light or medium United States crude," he said.

That is a critical distinction. Venezuela's oil is heavy, high-sulfur crude — "thicker than molasses," in Gross's words — while the SPR caverns largely hold lighter varieties. Many of the 17 fields are undeveloped or need major reinvestment. Francisco Monaldi of Rice University's Baker Institute has said some fields are so far from infrastructure that oil could take 10 to 20 years to flow.

"The increase in production won't be seen for at least three or four years," said Oswaldo Felizzola, an engineer and professor at IESA in Caracas, as [WION reported](https://www.wionews.com/world/venezuela-retains-sovereignty-delcy-rodriguez-defends-sweeping-us-oil-deal-1788076658795).

Venezuela's interim president, Delcy Rodríguez, defended the arrangement, insisting Caracas retains sovereignty. "One thing must be absolutely clear: Venezuela retains ownership and sovereignty over its resources," she said in a televised address. Public sentiment remains uncertain. "We don't know who this will benefit — whether it's Venezuela or (the United States)," said Caracas security guard Jesus Salazar.

## What to Watch

For Washington, the immediate concern is the shrinking buffer. With the SPR at a level last seen in 1982, a fresh supply shock — whether from the unresolved Strait of Hormuz standoff or elsewhere — would leave the U.S. with far less room to maneuver than a year ago. President Trump rejected an Iranian proposal to reopen the strait on Sept. 26, leaving the crisis unresolved.

The Venezuela deal, meanwhile, is unlikely to deliver meaningful new barrels for years, if ever. Former U.S. diplomat John Feeley compared it to "the big beautiful wall that Mexico will pay for," while Venezuelan legal expert Jose Ignacio Hernández called it "just propaganda" that "cannot be implemented as announced."

The next EIA weekly reading will show whether the reserve has stabilized or continued to fall. For now, the world's largest economy is running its emergency oil buffer at a 44-year low — and the plan to rebuild it rests on a deal whose viability remains very much in doubt.
