# Data Center Backlash Reshapes American Politics

A fast-growing, bipartisan backlash against AI-driven data center construction has become a kitchen-table electoral issue across the United States, cutting across party lines, splitting constituencies, and forcing officials in both parties to recalibrate. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/21/nx-s1-5927288/data-center-election-voters-ai), the conflict has moved from zoning hearings to the ballot box, with voters in affected communities organizing against new facilities and candidates staking out positions on the issue.

The scale of the opposition is striking. An Economist/YouGov poll of nearly 1,600 adults found that about two-thirds of Americans oppose new data center construction in their communities, while a March 2026 Gallup poll found seven in 10 Americans oppose construction close to where they live.

## A Grassroots Movement With Real Teeth

The movement's growth has been rapid. NPR reported that some 640,000 people are active in the anti-data center movement, with new groups forming in communities across the country as the issue spreads.

The financial stakes are equally significant. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/15/datacenters-local-elections) reported, residents delayed or cancelled $130 billion worth of data center projects in the first three months of 2026 alone, according to research from the tracking group Data Center Watch.

"Data centers have upended politics as we know it," Darrell West, a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution, told NPR. "It's more than a not-in-my-backyard issue... People just feel like they no longer have control over the technology, [and] that worries them. This is a real grassroots movement. It's ordinary people all across the country, both Republicans and Democrats."

That cross-party quality is unusual in an era of intense polarization. Kasey Hatch, an IT infrastructure engineer who lives near a data center in Sterling, Virginia, told NPR that she and her staunchly Republican cousin "never saw eye to eye on politics," but found themselves "on the same page about everything" when it came to data centers. Nusheen Farahani, a Democrat who lives near the same facility, said her anti-facility group is similarly mixed: "not all of them are liberals or Democrats."

## From Protest to the Ballot

The backlash is increasingly translating into electoral action. As The Guardian reported, the Working Families Party began recruiting local data center activists to run for office in December 2025, and more than 50 candidates have since signed up, with the organization expecting that number to "double or triple" for next year's races.

In Monterey Park, California, organizer Steven Kung helped pass the first U.S. city ban on data center construction and is now running for city council against an incumbent who supported the facility. In Arizona, cybersecurity professional Jackie McGuire won a seat on the Marana town council in July 2026 on an anti-data center platform. And in Oregon, educator Myrna Muñoz upset incumbent Janeen Sollman in a July senate primary where data center backlash was a deciding factor, and now backs a three-year statewide moratorium.

"It's aligning interests in ways that aren't traditional," Chris Borick, a political science professor at Lehigh University, told NPR. "If you're in power right now and you are dealing with this issue, you have a target on your back."

## The Loudoun County Fault Line

Few places better illustrate the political shift than Loudoun County, Virginia, the heart of the nation's data center industry. Northern Virginia is home to 319 mapped data centers — about 19% of the country's facilities — and in Loudoun County alone, data centers generated about $1.2 billion in property tax revenue in fiscal year 2026, roughly 39% of the county's overall budget, according to the [county's own figures](https://www.loudoun.gov/6408/Data-Centers-The-Loudoun-Story).

On Sept. 16, 2026, the Loudoun County Board of Supervisors voted 7-1-1 to advance a 12-month pause on new legislative data center and substation applications, with a proposed retroactive effective date of Sept. 15, according to [Energy News Beat](https://energynewsbeat.co/ai/loudoun-county-the-heart-of-northern-virginias-data-center-alley-advanced-a-12-month-pause-on-new-legislative-data-center-and-substation-applications-on-september-16-2026/). Officials emphasized it is not a legal moratorium under Virginia's Dillon Rule, but a temporary pause on board-level reviews while Phase 2 data-center standards are completed. A final vote on the drafted resolution is expected in October.

The issue has divided even fellow Democrats. Juli Briskman, a member of the Loudoun County Board of Supervisors and a leading critic of data center expansion, announced in August 2026 that she would challenge board chair Phyllis Randall in next year's primary, citing data centers as her top issue, as [NBC Washington](https://www.nbcwashington.com/news/local/northern-virginia/briskman-announces-run-for-loudoun-county-chair-says-data-centers-are-top-issue/4146029/) reported.

"The number one issue is data centers. I think that having a pro-data center chair in Loudoun County is not working anymore," Briskman said. For her part, Briskman told NPR, "I feel like I have been singing this song for a while, and now all of a sudden everyone's grabbing my music and singing along with me." She added, "We are the case study, not just for the region, but for the nation."

## Secrecy Fuels the Fire

What has galvanized opposition as much as any single environmental or economic concern is the secrecy surrounding many projects. A study led by Eric Bonds, a sociology professor at the University of Mary Washington, found that in 31 Virginia localities with an existing, approved, or proposed data center, non-disclosure agreements had been signed for 25 of them.

In Bessemer, Alabama, the city's four-term mayor was forced into a runoff after he, his chief of staff, and the city attorney signed NDAs that included a clause requiring them to destroy communications considered "confidential" by developers. In Wisconsin, a grassroots campaign opposed a $12 billion hyperscale data center in the Madison suburb of DeForest that would have covered more than 2 square miles; the project was abandoned this year.

"I wish we could have had some of the conversations back then, but to be perfectly honest, we had no reason to think that there would be an issue because we've been building these for a long time," John Stephenson, senior vice president for public policy at Vantage Data Centers, told NPR.

## The Economic Counterargument

The industry stresses that data centers bring real fiscal benefits. In Loudoun County, officials confirm that over the past decade the Board of Supervisors reduced the real property tax rate by about 30% as a result of data center revenue, and that vehicle taxes are also down.

"We've seen property taxes go down two years in a row, as well as the vehicle tax rate in the county go down," Dan Diorio of the Data Center Coalition, the main industry trade association, told NPR. "That's money directly back into people's pockets made possible by the revenue from the data center industry."

Opponents counter that these facilities carry enormous electricity requirements and substantial water needs for cooling — burdens that can fall on ratepayers and strain local resources.

## A Political Recalibration

The pressure is now driving policy at the state level. On Sept. 18, 2026, Virginia Gov. Abigail Spanberger — who campaigned in 2025 on qualified support for data center expansion — signed Executive Order 22, enacting a sweeping "Data Center Accountability Framework." According to [WSLS 10](https://www.wsls.com/news/local/2026/09/18/gov-spanberger-unveils-comprehensive-data-center-accountability-standards-signs-executive-order/), the framework bans non-disclosure agreements for commercial data center projects, ends state subsidies for data centers in site development programs, strengthens water-use and backup-generation standards, and requires utilities to allocate a more equitable share of transmission and generation costs to large load customers.

"In the years before I took office, data centers came to Virginia and the Commonwealth did not have a clear or coordinated plan to address their impacts on Virginians — on their electric bills, their water, their land, their air, or their quality of life," Spanberger said. "That changes today."

Similar actions are unfolding nationwide. Frederick County, Maryland, has frozen new data center development pending a state cost-benefit analysis. Hillsboro, Oregon, enacted a moratorium in late July after approving 17 new projects. Restrictions and moratoriums are being debated in states from Texas to California.

As Kathy Cramer, a political science professor at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, told NPR, the issue has become a channel for broader anxieties. "Data centers have become... a way for people to channel their discontent and resentment about bigger things in society that they're really not happy about," she said.

## What to Watch

The coming months will test whether the backlash translates into durable policy. In October, the Loudoun County Board is expected to hold a final vote on its 12-month pause. Virginia's 2027 legislative session will determine whether Spanberger's framework becomes permanent law, and Loudoun's 2027 primary will serve as an early referendum on whether running against data centers is a winning strategy.

For now, the movement's defining feature remains its breadth. As Steve Black, a Maryland tree nursery owner fighting a nearby project, put it: "Some people are Democrats that I'm supporting and some are Republicans, and it no longer matters to me."
