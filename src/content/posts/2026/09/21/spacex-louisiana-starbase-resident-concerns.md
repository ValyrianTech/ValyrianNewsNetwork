# SpaceX's Louisiana Starbase Worries Coastal Residents

SpaceX plans to build its largest rocket launch facility on roughly 125,000 acres of coastal marsh near Pecan Island in southwest Louisiana — a $100 billion project state officials call the biggest capital investment in Louisiana history, and one that has alarmed the residents and wildlife advocates who live nearest to it. The company says the self-sustaining spaceport, dubbed "Starbase Louisiana," could eventually support thousands of launches a year, with construction to begin by the end of 2027 and a first launch targeted for [2029](https://www.opportunitylouisiana.gov/news/spacex-launches-new-era-of-commercial-spaceflight-with-100-billion-louisiana-campus).

Announced on Aug. 25 at an invitation-only event in Abbeville that drew more than 200 attendees, the project would make Louisiana the hub of SpaceX's expanding Starship program. But the deal was negotiated largely out of public view, and the people who call the surrounding marsh home say they are being asked to accept the consequences without a say.

## A facility built for scale

According to the state's Louisiana Economic Development agency, the site will occupy about 200 square miles near the Freshwater Bayou Canal, roughly a 45-minute drive south of Abbeville and about five miles south of Pecan Island. At full buildout it is expected to include five launch complexes with two launch pads each, along with propellant farms, an on-site propellant production facility, power generation, deep-water shipping access, vehicle processing, an airport, and housing for employees and their families.

State and company officials describe a facility capable of launching about 30 rockets a day — thousands per year — as part of SpaceX's plans to expand its Starlink satellite internet service and to power data centers in space. SpaceX President and COO Gwynne Shotwell told the Abbeville crowd the goal is to make rocket launches as routine as commercial air travel. "Starbase, Louisiana, is going to make that future possible," she said, according to [The Daily Advertiser](https://www.theadvertiser.com/story/news/local/acadiana/2026/08/25/spacex-vermilion-parish-launch-site/91454450007/).

The economic case is substantial. The state projects at least 3,000 direct new jobs over 10 years, plus roughly 8,100 indirect jobs, at an average annual salary of $92,600. Shotwell has suggested the real figure could approach 10,000, citing SpaceX's Texas Starbase, where she said 60 to 80 percent of hires come from the local area. Under a payment-in-lieu-of-taxes agreement, SpaceX will pay local taxing bodies about $25 million annually — rising over time — in exchange for avoiding billions of dollars in property taxes over 25 years, and will make a $20 million upfront payment plus a $25 million donation to the Community Foundation of Acadiana.

In an official statement, Gov. Jeff Landry cast the project as a turning point. "For generations, industries came to Louisiana not to build lasting prosperity, but to extract wealth," he said. SpaceX and Musk, he added, are "offering us a permanent future and lasting prosperity."

## Negotiated behind NDAs

Much of how the deal came together, however, remains shielded from public view. State lawmakers involved in the negotiations signed nondisclosure agreements under the code name "Project Osprey," a practice the [Gulf States Newsroom](https://www.wwno.org/local-regional-news/2026-03-27/a-muzzle-on-elected-officials-ndas-cloak-louisianas-biggest-business-developments) has documented extensively under the Landry administration. More than 50 public officials have signed such agreements since Landry took office — compared with none during the final four years of his predecessor, John Bel Edwards.

Asked about transparency, Landry defended the NDAs. "You think I want Texas to steal my thunder? You think I want other states to know what we're working on?" he told reporters at the announcement. "Quite frankly, that's what has continued to happen to Louisiana before I became governor."

The Louisiana Legislature also passed a package of bills this spring at Landry's request exempting aerospace companies from property taxes, from liability and nuisance claims tied to noise, vibrations, heat exhaust and sonic booms, and from public records laws. SpaceX is covered by those exemptions. According to reporting by the Gulf States Newsroom, the property-tax exemption alone is worth an estimated $27.5 billion over 25 years — meaning the company will pay the parish roughly $1 for every $38 in property taxes it would otherwise owe.

Kim Nehrbass, who owns a hunting and fishing camp on Pecan Island, watched the announcement on a livestream and said the process left residents with no real voice. "He didn't ask anybody if they wanted to do it, the people of the state," Nehrbass said of the governor. "He just ramrodded it through."

## Wildlife and a changing coast

The proposed site sits on the Mississippi Flyway, a migration corridor used by roughly 40 percent of all North American waterfowl and millions of migratory birds. It also provides habitat for the endangered whooping crane and for the piping plover and Kemp's ridley sea turtle, both near extinction in the wild, according to the [Louisiana Illuminator](https://www.yahoo.com/news/science/articles/wildlife-groups-warn-not-waive-231524042.html).

Three wildlife groups — the Louisiana Wildlife Federation, the National Wildlife Federation and the Pontchartrain Conservancy — filed comments with the Federal Aviation Administration opposing proposed rule changes, posted July 30, that they say would remove environmental review requirements at the licensing stage. The revisions touch 13 wildlife, water and public health laws, including the Endangered Species Act, the Migratory Bird Treaty Act and the Clean Water Act. "This is precisely the setting in which project-level environmental review matters most," the groups wrote, warning that a facility of this scale "would involve extensive marsh filling, dredging, road and pad construction, water withdrawals and discharges, high-intensity noise and light."

At the announcement, Shotwell acknowledged potential impacts on migratory birds but played them down. "Now the birds might fly away during a launch, but I guarantee they'll come back," she said. "And they come back right away, by the way. As soon as the rocket's gone, the birds come back. It's not like months later."

Resident Brooke Broussard, seated in a boat in the marsh near where the spaceport will rise, was unconvinced. "Why would the birds come back?" she said. "They're smarter than us, in some ways."

Rebecca Triche, executive director of the Louisiana Wildlife Federation, said the group wants more concrete detail. "We did not see details about the reality of the footprint and impacts of launching a reusable and returnable rocket at this site," she said.

Company and state officials say SpaceX has worked with the Louisiana Department of Wildlife and Fisheries and the Coastal Protection and Restoration Authority to limit harm, that unavoidable wetlands impacts must be fully mitigated, and that the company is not developing the entire 125,000-acre tract. SpaceX says it is also evaluating voluntary shoreline restoration beyond what regulations require. Vermilion Parish's coast is eroding rapidly, with officials citing land loss of three to 23 feet per year.

## What to watch

The project now enters a multi-year permitting and construction phase, with the FAA's proposed licensing changes still unsettled and wildlife groups pressing for environmental review to remain intact. SpaceX has pledged to hold town halls and community meetings as work advances, and officials say the state incentives are performance-based — tied to investment and employment benchmarks.

For residents like Broussard, the debate is less about jobs than about what is being traded away. She knows the project is unlikely to be stopped. What she wants, she said, is for people beyond Vermilion Parish to understand what was already there. "I don't want the story to just be: 'Elon Musk came and conquered,'" she said. "I want the other stories to be told."
