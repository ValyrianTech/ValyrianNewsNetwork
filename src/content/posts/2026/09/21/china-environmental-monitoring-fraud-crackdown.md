# China Targets Environmental Data Fraud Across Supply Chain

Five Chinese government departments have issued a joint notice to intensify the crackdown on data fraud by ecological and environmental technical service agencies, extending penalties to the upstream and downstream companies involved in such schemes. The Sept. 21 notice, jointly published by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment (MEE), the Supreme People's Court, the Supreme People's Procuratorate, the Ministry of Public Security and the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR), marks one of Beijing's most aggressive efforts to restore integrity to environmental monitoring, a field long dogged by falsified readings.

The document, titled "Notice on Continuously Strengthening the Supervision of Ecological and Environmental Technical Service Agencies and Deepening the Rectification of Fraud," was published by the [SAMR](https://www.samr.gov.cn/xw/zj/art/2026/art_a29a4c4ddb714f6b95bec6970b796da1.html) and reported the same day by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34114074). It lays out three work priorities and eleven specific measures organized under four areas, signaling a shift from punishing individual violators toward full-chain accountability.

## A Long-Standing “Stubborn Problem”

Third-party environmental technical service agencies — the firms that conduct pollution monitoring, environmental impact assessments and vehicle emissions testing — have repeatedly been found fabricating data. The problem has persisted despite earlier enforcement waves. In July 2018, the MEE publicized five typical cases, including a Fujian sewage plant that falsified chemical oxygen demand readings, a Hubei ceramics company that colluded with a maintenance contractor to tamper with sulfur dioxide data, and a Jiangxi testing firm that issued falsified reports, according to a [China Atmospheric Net](https://www.chndaqi.com/news/277943.html) compilation of the cases. That round signaled zero tolerance two years before the current notice — but it did not end the practice.

At a national deployment meeting in Beijing on Sept. 18, MEE Minister Huang Runqiu acknowledged that the problem remains severe, warning that "the situation is still grim" and that authorities must maintain a "zero-tolerance attitude," according to a ministry [readout](https://www.mee.gov.cn/xxgk/hjyw/202609/t20260919_1166822.shtml). Vice Minister Xu Bijiu presided over the meeting, which brought together judicial, police and market-regulation officials.

## Eleven Measures, Four Fronts

According to [CCTV News](https://news.cctv.com/2026/09/21/ARTI1uwBq7iOtfQBI1WQxTGX260921.shtml), the notice deploys eleven measures across four areas.

The first, broadening the sources of problem leads, calls for deploying big data and artificial intelligence to improve early warning for anomalous data and to carry out what the notice describes as "unobtrusive" and "penetrating" supervision, linking off-site analysis with on-site enforcement. It also encourages internal whistleblowers and public reporting, with improved reward mechanisms.

The second area, increasing case investigation, sets the notice's most striking principle: "strike the source, wipe out dens, destroy networks, cut the chain." Under this approach, authorities will investigate fraudulent technical service agencies and individuals and also punish upstream and downstream affiliated enterprises, achieving what the notice calls "one case, multiple investigations; full-chain accountability." Regulators will also re-review agencies penalized in recent years to prevent a relapse.

The third area focuses on coordination — improving joint inspection and joint case-handling, making technical service agencies a priority target of cross-departmental spot checks, and strengthening the two-way link between administrative enforcement and criminal justice.

The fourth seeks to formalize the system by applying the new Ecological Environment Code, the Ecological Environment Monitoring Regulation and judicial interpretations, tightening industry entry and exit, and refining the circumstances under which those who commit fraud are banned from the profession.

## The Legal Backbone

The crackdown rests on two recent legal instruments. The Ecological Environment Monitoring Regulation, China's first comprehensive administrative regulation in the field, took effect on Jan. 1, 2026. The Ecological Environment Code — the country's second law to be designated a "code," spanning five parts and 1,242 articles — took effect on Aug. 15, 2026, replacing ten laws including the Environmental Protection Law.

As [Environmental Protection Online](https://m.hbzhan.com/news/detail/197440.html) detailed, the Code sharply raises the cost of falsification. Article 1091 imposes fines of 100,000 to 500,000 yuan, rising to 500,000 to 2 million yuan for serious cases, plus license revocation, profession bans and orders to suspend or close businesses. Article 1067 establishes joint and several liability, making a service agency that is at fault jointly liable alongside its polluting client. Article 80 prohibits falsification through sampling interference, sample swapping, altering monitoring conditions, false monitoring and tampering with or fabricating records. Seven articles of the Code specifically address third-party technical service agencies. Huang Runqiu has said the maximum penalty of 2 million yuan, combined with license revocation and profession bans, will "effectively solve the problem" of monitoring data fraud.

## Why “Full-Chain” Accountability Matters

The notice's emphasis on penalizing upstream and downstream affiliates, as reported by [Guangming Net](https://legal.gmw.cn/2026-09/21/content_39012536.htm), reflects a strategic pivot. Rather than pursuing only the testing firm that doctored a reading, regulators intend to reach the polluting client and the supply-chain entities that enable the fraud. The Code's joint-liability provision reinforces this, creating legal exposure for the firms that commission falsified reports.

The phrase "one case, multiple investigations" also hints at an intelligence-driven model. By combining AI-based anomaly detection with cross-source data comparison, authorities aim to spot suspicious patterns before they escalate. The Sept. 18 meeting underscored this, calling for stronger "cross-source data comparison and intelligent screening" and better training for grassroots enforcers to detect fraud, secure evidence and prosecute.

## What's Next

Huang Runqiu outlined a three-stage rollout: establishing a baseline by having agencies self-inspect and building a ledger of problems; carrying out concentrated rectification with classified handling; and normalizing operations through faster management rules, a dedicated monitoring management platform and stricter controls on qualifications, equipment use, conduct and exit. The meeting also stressed that environmental authorities must work closely with market regulators and judicial bodies, promptly transferring suspected criminal cases.

A parallel crackdown is already underway: the MEE and SAMR recently issued a separate opinion advancing reform of motor vehicle emissions inspection agency supervision, with twelve measures across five areas.

Several questions remain unanswered. How "unobtrusive" and "penetrating" supervision will be operationalized — and whether it raises data or privacy concerns for regulated entities — is unclear. Nor is it precisely defined which "upstream and downstream affiliated enterprises" fall within the notice's scope, and no implementation timeline for the three stages has been announced. What is clear is that China is moving from cracking down on individual falsifiers toward a system designed to make the entire environmental-monitoring chain accountable for the integrity of its data.
