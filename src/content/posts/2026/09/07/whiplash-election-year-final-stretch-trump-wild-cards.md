# Election Year Enters Final Stretch as Trump Holds Wild Cards

WASHINGTON — In the earliest days of this election cycle, the United States wasn't at war with Iran. The East Wing of the White House was still standing, and the Kennedy Center wasn't threatened with demolition. Artificial intelligence data centers weren't yet bipartisan shorthand for the tech sector's excess. Democratic socialists were largely confined to the outer edges of political discourse. And Lake Ontario's name wasn't in question.

As the [2026 midterm elections](https://apnews.com/article/trump-midterms-iran-tariffs-data-centers-f07499f53269e37ccd2565564cc01e4e) enter their final stretch with all 435 House seats and 33 Senate seats on the ballot November 3, the political landscape has proven exceptionally volatile — with President Donald Trump often stirring the tumult. From a six-month war with Iran to a trade war with Canada, from a $400 million campaign war chest to a wave of democratic socialist primary victories, the variables reshaping this election cycle are numerous and unpredictable.

## A President at the Center of It All

One thing Democrats and Republicans agree on is that Trump will again be a central player in the election's final stretch. "No. 1, President Trump," said Ari Fleischer, the White House press secretary under George W. Bush, when asked to rank the top issues this year.

House Speaker Mike Johnson acknowledged the uncertainty facing his party. "Some of these things are out of our control," the Louisiana Republican told reporters last week.

The president's approval rating sits at roughly one-third of U.S. adults, according to a July AP-NORC poll — down from 37% in June and well below the 42% who approved when he took office. Majorities disapprove of his handling of the economy and immigration, issues central to his 2024 victory.

## Trump's War Chest: A Wild Card of Its Own

The president is sitting on more than $400 million in his super PAC that could shape the election's trajectory. MAGA Inc. had more than $403 million at the end of July, according to [campaign finance filings](https://apnews.com/article/trump-maga-inc-midterm-elections-spending-0b06b977440348d3b671c756ac94f9fd) — more cash than any of the major party committees. Trump has said he plans to allocate $400-500 million to help Republican candidates, though GOP operatives in battleground states have grown frustrated by the slow deployment of funds.

"Hope springs eternal," said Republican donor Dan Eberhart, one of many waiting for Trump to open his wallet. Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana echoed the concern: "We need help from the president. I'm hoping that the president is going to spend $100 or $200 million in Texas, but he hasn't committed to it yet."

Trump's first major spending move came September 5 with a $10 million ad buy in the Texas Senate race, where scandal-plagued Attorney General Ken Paxton is running against Democrat James Talarico in what was once considered a safe Republican seat.

The president's personal wealth has also become a political liability. Trump reportedly took in $1.2 billion from his crypto businesses last year, according to a federal filing released this summer — wealth that is now threatening a bipartisan Senate effort to advance cryptocurrency regulation. "We've watched this president enrich himself," said Sen. Angela Alsobrooks of Maryland, who has said she won't support the [crypto bill](https://www.semafor.com/article/07/22/2026/democrat-alsobrooks-will-oppose-crypto-bill-absent-stronger-ethics-language) without tougher ethics language.

## The Iran War and Economic Pressures

The U.S. has been at war with Iran since February 28 — a conflict that has killed 18 U.S. service members and caused significant economic disruption. Iran has restricted oil flow through the Strait of Hormuz, sending gas prices soaring. Trump has called the conflict "small potatoes" despite the casualties and economic impact.

"It's hard to get excited about a presidency that has been defined by a complete failure to get prices under control, coupled with a war that is looking increasingly like a fiasco," said Bill Galston, a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution.

Democrats are centering their pitch on affordability. "Life has gotten far too expensive in this country, and Republicans don't give a damn about it," House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries told reporters.

The economic fallout from Trump's tariffs on Canada may also intensify as the election nears, with battleground states like Michigan bearing much of the burden.

## Data Centers: An Unlikely Political Flashpoint

The snowballing opposition to data centers has transformed the midterm landscape in unexpected ways. Tech giants Oracle, Meta, Google, Amazon, and Microsoft expect to plunge more than $700 billion into data centers in the U.S. in 2026 alone. But opposition has united conservative rural communities with left-leaning environmental groups concerned about drought-stricken waterways, pollution in food-growing areas, and shrinking farmland.

Trump remains a strong supporter of data centers, seeing them as critical to winning the AI race against China. But some governors have halted sales tax exemptions for new projects, and New York imposed a one-year ban. In New Mexico, Oracle and OpenAI are building Project Jupiter, a $165 billion complex that has sparked water rights concerns. The [data center controversies](https://apnews.com/article/data-centers-rural-farm-artificial-intelligence-trump-e5a350af1bc12e6a4f470d3b68be51d7) have become a major issue in governors' races across the country.

## Redistricting Battles and the Democratic Socialist Surge

The electoral map itself has been in flux. Texas, California, and Florida have redrawn congressional districts, and Missouri's Republican attorney general is appealing a court ruling that blocked a Trump-backed map. The Missouri Supreme Court unanimously blocked the new congressional districts and ordered a statewide vote in November, with the state now asking the [U.S. Supreme Court](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/05/missouri-supreme-court-congressional-districts.html) to intervene.

Democratic socialists have also won a string of primaries this summer, including in New York, Michigan, Colorado, and Florida — sending shock waves through the party establishment. Most are in reliably Democratic seats, but Republicans are eager to tie the broader party to the far-left flank.

Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear, who chairs the Democratic Governors Association, pushed back on those efforts: "The only person that I see engaged in socialism is Donald J. Trump, who is taking equity in private companies for the United States government. That's the definition of socialism."

## Voting Rights and Election Administration

Concerns about federal election interference hang over many states. Wyoming Gov. Mark Gordon, a Republican, complained about "aggressive" Justice Department election monitors. Oklahoma Gov. Kevin Stitt said he doesn't believe elections should be federalized. Trump's effort to limit mail ballots could add uncertainty just as ballots are being distributed in states like North Carolina, home to a critical Senate race. The administration has asked the Supreme Court to allow restrictions on mail-in voting, with a federal judge having blocked new limits.

## What's at Stake

Midterm elections are historically challenging for a president's party. Democrats flipped 40 House seats during Trump's last midterm in 2018 and 31 seats during the final race of Bush's term in 2006. Republicans gained 63 seats in 2010 under Obama but flipped fewer than 10 seats in 2022 under Biden.

Democrats are confident they can win the handful of seats needed to regain control of the House, and there may be a path to flipping the Senate. "I'm excited about the map and I am bullish that we are going to pick up several seats," said Beshear.

Trump has announced plans for an unusual midterm convention in Dallas later this month to rally Republican voters. But with his approval ratings low, the Iran war ongoing, and economic anxieties persisting, the president's wild cards may ultimately determine whether Republicans can defy historical trends — or whether voters deliver a verdict that reshapes the final two years of his presidency.

As the [Guardian's analysis](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/06/midterm-elections-control-of-congress) notes, the question may not be whether voters cross party lines, but whether they get out of bed on election morning. With less than two months until November 3, both parties are racing to energize their bases in what promises to be one of the most unpredictable midterm elections in recent memory.
