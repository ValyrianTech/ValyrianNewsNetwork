# House Freedom Caucus Faces a Post-Trump Reckoning

At least 10 of the House Freedom Caucus's more than two dozen members will not return to Congress next term, and more could lose their seats in November — a wave of departures that has set off an early contest over the Republican Party's ideological future, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/22/g-s1-144369/freedom-caucus-trump-republicans).

The hardline conservative bloc, founded more than a decade ago as a vehicle to push the GOP rightward on fiscal and social policy, now faces a question with implications well beyond its own ranks: what happens to a movement built on confronting establishment power when its members have become the establishment — and when the combative style it pioneered has been absorbed by the party itself?

## A Bloc Born in Opposition

The Freedom Caucus was formalized in 2015, as Tea Party-aligned conservatives entered Congress frustrated by what they saw as a top-down legislative process. According to NPR, founding member Matt Salmon recalled that under then-Speaker John Boehner, "everything was really being done by a really small group of folks, mostly leadership, and then jammed down the members' throats."

Salmon described how the group took shape after Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio approached him on the House floor, saying members were "really tired of this process where... just a few people kind of make all the decisions." The caucus's central tenet — that rank-and-file lawmakers should play a central role in policymaking — has consistently frustrated Republican leadership ever since.

Boehner's view was considerably blunter. As NPR recounts, the former speaker told POLITICO in 2017 that caucus members "can't tell you what they're for. They can tell you everything they're against," calling them "anarchists" who "want total chaos."

## From Insurgents to Insiders

Over the past decade, President Trump brought several of the group's founders into the highest reaches of his administration. Mick Mulvaney and Mark Meadows both served as White House chiefs of staff, while John Fleming — now Louisiana's state treasurer — was deputy chief of staff.

Fleming framed that trajectory as vindication rather than co-option. "The Freedom Caucus, I would say, was really the predecessor to President Trump," he told NPR. "In fact, I've often said that we were really MAGA before MAGA was cool."

He also acknowledged that the group's original emphasis on executive-branch accountability has faded now that a Republican occupies the White House. "When we started this, Barack Obama was president of the United States," Fleming said. "Today we have a Republican president who really is kind of a breakthrough president when it comes to reforms, new ideas."

Not everyone sees the shift as healthy. Salmon, a co-founder, told NPR the caucus "originally started out with very, very noble motives, but it's just become a political adjunct to the Trump administration." He said the group "morphed from being the entity that really was about getting back to regular order and protecting Article 1 to becoming the chief cheering squad for Trump. And I couldn't be more disappointed."

## Revolving Door in a Record Turnover Year

The departures are unfolding against the backdrop of an unusually turbulent election cycle. According to [NPR's reporting on the 2026 primaries](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/nx-s1-5971264/congress-retirement-2026-house-senate-primary-defeat), nearly one in five lawmakers who began the 119th Congress will not return — 99 seats across both chambers are already guaranteed to have a different occupant, a figure likely to rise after November. Only the 1992 cycle saw more retirements or members seeking other offices, per the Brookings Institution's Vital Statistics on Congress.

Among the Freedom Caucus members departing are two of its most prominent voices: Rep. Chip Roy of Texas and Rep. Ralph Norman of South Carolina, both regular advocates on civil liberties and fiscal issues. Roy told NPR that members are "bound together by wanting to do what we said we would do," adding: "We get a lot of speeches about all this stuff, but then, you know, you got to deliver."

Norman struck a more optimistic note. "It's going to be better because we're at a point now where the Freedom Caucus adds value" on budget questions, he said. "We're losing a lot of seniority, but we're also picking up a lot of people."

## An Ideological Fight Among Alumni

Matthew Green, a professor at Catholic University who wrote *Legislative Hardball* about the group, told NPR that predictions of the caucus's demise have been premature before. "After the 2018 elections, after the 2020 elections, people said, 'Oh, the group doesn't matter, or they've lost their members,'" he said. "It was always, 'this group is destined for the dustbin' and yet they find a way to maintain themselves."

The deeper tension, Green argued, is between two impulses inside the movement: institutionalists who see obstruction as a means to restore a more member-empowered Congress, and Trump-style disruptors intent on remaking politics altogether. "Fear of centralized executive power was the most important thing regardless of party," he said — a principle that becomes harder to hold when your own party controls the executive branch.

"Do you want to quit a group that has influence because you're concerned about some principle of presidential power? Or do you want to take advantage of having a Republican president?" Green asked. "The latter is very tempting, understandably."

At the state level, the movement has already tasted defeat. According to [NPR's reporting on Wyoming's primaries](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/23/nx-s1-5939514/many-wyoming-freedom-caucus-representatives-lose-to-more-moderate-republicans-in-primaries), the Wyoming Freedom Caucus lost its state House majority after more than a dozen aligned members were defeated by more moderate Republicans — a result shaped by a deep-cuts budget push and a controversy known as "Checkgate," in which members were seen accepting campaign donations on the House floor.

"The appetite for some of the more hard-line conservative positions" appears to be narrowing, University of Wyoming political scientist Ryan Williamson told NPR. "When they run on those positions, they're going to struggle."

The movement is not retreating everywhere. The [State Freedom Caucus Network](https://thefederalist.com/2026/09/15/state-freedom-caucuses-chart-the-path-for-making-red-states-great-again/) gathered in Dallas in mid-September for its third annual summit, spanning 15 states and eyeing expansion into Arkansas, Ohio, Tennessee, and Maine. Its president, Andy Roth, told The Federalist that the organization's central mission is exposing "the establishment corruption plaguing state governments," adding that "state politics are able to operate in the dark, and shining a light on that is arguably one of the most important parts of my job."

## What to Watch

Three caucus members are running for governor with Trump's backing — Andy Biggs in Arizona, Byron Donalds in Florida, and Thomas Tiffany in Wisconsin — offering an early test of whether the movement's brand still travels.

But the larger question is what the Freedom Caucus becomes once the current crop of members is gone. Its combative, tear-it-down style now defines mainstream Republican politics, which raises an uncomfortable possibility: that the group's greatest success has also made it redundant.

Fleming, for one, expects the pendulum to swing back. "Most likely the successor to President Trump, whether it's Republican or Democrat, is likely to begin to move back to the status quo, more of the norm," he told NPR. "And that's where I think the Freedom Caucus, again, will likely come in."

Roy, heading for the exits, suggested the fight would simply continue from a different vantage point. "Some of us, we're not going to go far," he said. "We can have a voice, and we can have an influence from outside, maybe every bit as much so as inside. So there's going to be a lot more to do."
