# Abortion Returns to the Ballot in Four States

Abortion is once again a direct question for voters, with measures on the November 3, 2026 ballot in four states — Missouri, Nevada, Virginia and Idaho — [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/22/nx-s1-5974318/election-voting-abortion-policies-roe). Missouri and Nevada are voting on the issue for the second time since 2024, a sign that the post-Dobbs era has not settled the nation's abortion debate so much as re-opened it, state by state.

The votes arrive four years after the U.S. Supreme Court's 2022 decision in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* overturned federal abortion protections, leaving each state to set its own policy. The result has been a patchwork of bans, gestational limits and "shield laws" in pro-access states designed to protect providers from prosecution elsewhere.

## Missouri: A Vote to Repeal

Missouri is the only state voting this year to remove abortion protections already on the books. Its Republican-dominated legislature placed a measure, also numbered Amendment 3, on the ballot that would repeal the reproductive-freedom amendment voters approved in 2024 and replace it with a near-total ban. The new measure would permit exceptions for medical emergencies, fetal anomalies and pregnancies resulting from rape or incest, with rape and incest abortions required before 12 weeks' gestation.

The collision of names has become the defining feature of the campaign. In 2024, a "yes" vote on Amendment 3 added abortion protections to the state constitution; in 2026, a "no" vote keeps them. Kelly McCoomb, a voter in Maplewood, Missouri, kept the yard sign she displayed in 2024 and covered its "YES" with black duct tape reading "NO."

"I have little faith in our Missouri government," McCoomb told NPR. "I hope lots of people see and remember to revote."

The confusion cuts both ways. Reagan Barklage, a vice president of Students for Life of America and treasurer of the Missouri Students for Life Ballot Committee, said she has had to correct anti-abortion voters while canvassing to remind them they are voting "yes" this year after voting "no" last time.

"It is crazy that it ended up being even the same number," Barklage said. "People need to be very clear on what they're voting for."

Margot Riphagen-Dunn, CEO of St. Louis-based Planned Parenthood Great Rivers, described the repeated votes as a deliberate tactic. "Chaos is kind of the point here," she said, "and it is absolutely a tactic that gets in the way of being able to provide."

Missouri was the first state to ban abortion after *Dobbs*, in 2022, and before that, three decades of state regulations — including a 72-hour waiting period, minimum clinic dimensions and admitting-privileges requirements — had already reduced the state to just 150 abortions in 2021, down from 5,772 in 2011. Even after the 2024 amendment passed, it took nearly two years for medication abortions to resume. That return came only after a Jackson County judge [struck down dozens of restrictions](https://www.stlpr.org/health-science-environment/2026-06-18/planned-parenthood-to-resume-missouri-medication-abortions-after-judge-strikes-down-restrictions) in June 2026, a ruling Attorney General Catherine Hanaway has vowed to appeal.

## Nevada, Virginia and Idaho

Nevada's Question 6 first passed in 2024 with 64% support, but the state requires initiated constitutional amendments to pass in two consecutive even-year elections. The measure would not change Nevada's existing law, which allows abortion up to 24 weeks after conception, but it would require voter approval of any new ban across two elections.

"Folks care about it," said Denise Lopez, president of Nevadans for Reproductive Freedom. "They're seeing what's happening at the national level, and they want to make sure that whatever's happening in our neighboring states, like Idaho and Utah, doesn't happen here in Nevada."

Virginia's Question 1 would codify existing access — through two trimesters, or 28 weeks — into the state constitution. In Idaho, where one of the strictest bans in the country is in force, a citizen-initiated measure [qualified for the ballot](https://19thnews.org/2026/07/idaho-abortion-ballot-measure-2026/) that would allow abortion through fetal viability, generally considered around 24 weeks.

## Why It Keeps Coming Back

The recurring votes reflect a deeper question that neither ballot measures nor court rulings have resolved: whether abortion policy can be settled at the state level at all. Kristi Hamrick, vice president of media and policy at Students for Life Action, framed the stakes in constitutional terms.

"I just think there's a constitutional crisis that's coming," Hamrick told NPR. "Can you have 50 state standards on whether or not a human being is a human being? Can you have 50 state standards on whether human beings have legal protection?"

Since *Dobbs*, ten states have established rights to abortion — Arizona, California, Colorado, Maryland, Michigan, Missouri, Montana, Ohio, New York and Vermont. But the Missouri case suggests the victories are not always final. Missouri Republicans hold a supermajority and every statewide office, yet voters have used the initiative process to expand Medicaid, legalize marijuana, raise the minimum wage and restore abortion rights. The 2026 Amendment 3 is widely seen as an attempt to reverse one of those defeats.

Critics argue the measure bundles in a ban on gender-affirming care for minors — largely already addressed by Missouri statute — as "ballot candy" to make the abortion repeal more palatable, and note that at least one television ad for the measure does not mention abortion at all. The 2024 amendment won 51.6% to 48.4%, a margin of roughly 48,000 votes, and drew about 338,000 more votes than Democratic presidential nominee Kamala Harris that year — evidence of significant crossover support among voters who backed President Trump.

## What to Watch

Two forces will determine the outcomes. The first is turnout: whether voters who have now seen the issue on their ballots multiple times still turn out, or whether fatigue sets in. The second is messaging: whether opponents of abortion rights can succeed with a strategy that emphasizes restrictions on gender-affirming care rather than abortion itself.

Missouri's 2026 vote is the only one this cycle that would strip away existing protections, making it a national test case for how far voters will go to reverse a right they recently approved. As both sides prepare for November, the patchwork left by *Dobbs* shows no signs of resolving — only of being contested again.
