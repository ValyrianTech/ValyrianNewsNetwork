# Sikh Trucker Stabbed 17 Times at Wyoming Rest Stop

A Punjabi Sikh truck driver was followed into a rest stop bathroom in southwestern Wyoming and stabbed repeatedly in an unprovoked attack early on Sunday, September 13, an assault that has reignited fears across South Asian and Sikh communities already shaken by a documented national surge in anti-immigrant rhetoric.

The victim, identified only by his last name, Singh, for safety reasons, told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/17/nx-s1-5971866/truck-driver-attack-wyoming-sikh-south-asian) he was attacked from behind at about 3:30 a.m. at the Bitter Creek Rest Area along Interstate 80, east of Rock Springs. He sustained 17 lacerations, including twice each to the neck and chest, and was flown to Wyoming Medical Center in Casper for treatment.

"I don't know why this happened," Singh said from his hospital room. "I don't know why they attack[ed] me."

Andrew Kris Bzdak, 44, was arrested within hours in nearby Carbon County and charged on Monday, September 14, with attempted first-degree murder. He is being held on $1 million bond at the Sweetwater County Detention Center in Rock Springs, [WyoFile](https://wyofile.com/sikh-truck-driver-stabbed-at-wyoming-rest-stop-suspect-faces-attempted-murder-charge/) reported. A preliminary hearing is scheduled for September 24.

## A Rescue and an Arrest

According to court documents, a Hispanic family at the rest area heard the attack and reported it. The family followed the suspect's truck back onto the highway and photographed it, helping investigators identify Bzdak, whom the Wyoming Highway Patrol located in Rawlins. The victim credited the family with saving his life.

"They [gave] me the second life," Singh said.

Camera footage from a nearby parked semi-truck corroborated the family's account, according to an affidavit reviewed by WyoFile. It showed Singh casually walking into the bathroom, followed by another man walking "in a quick manner." Minutes later, the suspect was recorded running out of the building, followed by the wounded victim, who fell at least once.

Authorities say there is no known motive. Sweetwater County Sheriff's Department spokesperson Jeff Sheaman was blunt: "We have zero motive," he told the [Associated Press](https://www.12onyourside.com/2026/09/19/sikh-truck-driver-stabbed-more-than-dozen-times-wyoming-rest-stop-attack/). County and Prosecuting Attorney Daniel Erramouspe cautioned against drawing conclusions prematurely.

"I don't understand the reason why everyone wants to find a motive on this other than to satisfy their own political theories," he told WyoFile. "The investigation is still ongoing. We still have a long ways before trial. And so assumptions don't get anyone further in this case."

## Attack Follows Federal Post Targeting Sikh Drivers

The stabbing came four days after the U.S. Department of Homeland Security posted — and then deleted — a social media image depicting a Sikh man facing off against the Transformers character Optimus Prime, captioned "Get off our roads, you don't know how to drive Mr. Singh." The agency said the post referred specifically to Harjinder Singh, an Indian truck driver charged in a 2025 Florida crash that killed three people.

Community representatives noted that "Singh" is a common surname shared by well over 100,000 Sikhs in the United States and millions worldwide — a point that made the post feel like a broad indictment rather than a reference to one individual.

"The Sheriff's Office has said there is no known motive at this time, but we cannot separate this incident from the climate in which it happened," said Jasjit Singh, an attorney and director of legal services at the Jakara Movement, a California-based organization that serves the Punjabi Sikh community and has supported the victim.

"When our own federal government uses a Sikh name and image to tell people like us that they do not belong on America's roads, our community has every reason to be concerned about the environment that kind of rhetoric creates," he said.

The [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/18/wyoming-truck-driver-stabbing-attack) reported that several prominent South Asian civic and political figures have drawn a link between the attack and the DHS post. Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi (D-Ill.) said he was praying for the driver's recovery and condemned the spread of hate.

"Those spreading this hate, including officials in our own federal government, need to stop," Krishnamoorthi wrote. "Those who turn it into violence must be held accountable. And all of us need to stand together against both."

The office of California Gov. Gavin Newsom said his thoughts were with the victim, who is from Sacramento, and criticized the administration. "The President's now redacted social media post is one proof point — his hateful rhetoric is hurting our communities," his office told NPR.

DHS declined to speculate on a motive in the Wyoming case. "Any attempt to blame DHS for an unrelated attack is RECKLESS and FALSE," the agency told NPR.

## A Pattern of Rising Hostility

The attack landed amid a measurable escalation in hostility toward South Asians and immigrant truckers. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/03/12/nx-s1-5736253/immigrant-truckers-trump-crackdown), slurs used online against South Asians rose by 109% between January 2023 and December 2025, a figure tracked by the advocacy group Stop AAPI Hate. The group attributed the increase to several factors, including the Florida truck crash, President Trump's re-election, debates over H-1B visas, and Zohran Mamdani's New York City mayoral campaign.

The broader political climate has also grown more charged. The Trump administration has pursued a crackdown on noncitizen truck drivers and commercial driver's licensing requirements — a push that, by the Department of Transportation's own estimate, would remove about 200,000 immigrants from the industry. Critics argue there is no data showing foreign-born drivers are less safe.

Sikh advocates say the effects are felt well beyond policy. About 20% of U.S. truckers are Sikh, according to the North American Punjabi Trucking Association, and advocacy groups estimate at least 150,000 Sikh truck drivers work in the country.

"I've been here for the last 25-plus years. I never felt this kind of hate before," Harsimran Singh, CEO of Gillson Trucking in Stockton, California, told the AP. "We are probably going to see more of this. I'm not surprised that it has happened."

Raman Dhillon, head of the North American Punjabi Trucking Association, said drivers have reached out to him with concerns about their own safety. "It's on everybody's mind," he said. "We never thought in this country that we will ever gonna think about things like this."

## A Community's Resilience

The victim, a Sacramento resident who has driven a truck for about three to four years, was headed home on Friday after five days in a Casper hospital. Jasjit Singh, who visited him in person, described him as being in good spirits despite his ordeal, invoking a Sikh concept of enduring optimism.

"There's a concept in the Sikh faith of being in Chardikala — Chardikala refers to being in forever rising spirits, always trying to keep a positive outlook on life," he said. "And he had that, right, despite being stabbed 17 times and literally almost losing his life."

The victim told the AP he hoped to drive his truck across Wyoming again, and he described the state fondly to WyoFile. "It's a beautiful state," he recalled telling someone. "Beautiful place to drive through, calm, quiet."

Still, the attack left the driver worried — both about his recovery and about others like him.

"I am scared and also upset because I have [to] work regularly," he told NPR. "I am just worried … it can happen again."

## What's Next

Bzdak's preliminary hearing, set for September 24, is expected to offer the first fuller account of the evidence against him. Court records indicate he has a prior felony conviction for assault with a deadly weapon from a 2005 Los Angeles County case, according to WyoFile. Efforts to reach him or his public defender for comment have been unsuccessful.

As the case moves through the courts, Sikh community leaders say the broader question — whether the attack was isolated or emblematic — remains unresolved. For now, the driver's own words carry the weight of a community's anxiety: he does not know why he was targeted, only that he nearly was not here to ask.
