# Qin King-Level Tomb Found, Occupant a Queen Dowager

Archaeologists in Shaanxi Province have confirmed the discovery of a Qin king-level tomb in Xianyang, with researchers suggesting the occupant may be Queen Dowager Huayang, the adoptive grandmother of China's first emperor, Qin Shi Huang. The Shaanxi Provincial Institute of Archaeology announced the results of the "Shangzhaoyao Qin Mausoleum" on September 18, 2026, calling it the fourth Warring States-period Qin mausoleum identified on the Xianyang Plain.

## A Royal Tomb Beneath an Airport Expansion Site

The tomb was first encountered in 2018, when the provincial institute surveyed a cultural relic protection unit registered as "Gongling" within land earmarked for the Phase III expansion of Xi'an Xianyang International Airport. According to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/video/20260919/4679198bf7174186bd49199d2c4b20f5/c.html), that survey confirmed an independent, large tomb of the "亞" (ya) cross-shaped form typically reserved for rulers. Excavation of the mausoleum's northern section and supplementary exploration in 2022 essentially clarified its overall layout. Because the site lies within the former Shangzhaoyao village area, it was named the Shangzhaoyao Qin Mausoleum.

The mausoleum is nearly square in plan and organized as a nested "回" (hui) shape, comprising an outer moat, an inner moat, an inner garden wall, the principal tomb, accompanying tombs, attached tombs and sacrificial pits. A truncated-pyramid mound crowns the main tomb, which sits at the center of the inner precinct on the north-south axis, with four sloped ramps positioned at the middle of each side of the chamber.

## 258 Relics and a Royal Designation

Excavations yielded 258 artifacts and assemblages of pottery, bronze, iron, lead and jade, as the Xinhua wire report carried by [China Economic Net](http://www.ce.cn/xwzx/shgj/gdxw/202609/t20260918_3222143.shtml) details. The pottery includes ritual vessels such as ding, boxes, hu and jars, some stamped with inscriptions reading "咸广里高," "咸重成未" and "咸宜都次." Most bronzes came from the attached tombs, including a ding bearing the inscription "宋氏" (Song clan).

Carbon-14 dating of human bones from the accompanying tombs points to a range of 389 BCE to 208 BCE. "Judging from the tomb structure, the mausoleum layout and the characteristics of the unearthed objects, the Shangzhaoyao Qin Mausoleum dates to the late Warring States period and belongs to the Qin king level," said Zhao Hanqing, a staff member at the Shaanxi Provincial Institute of Archaeology.

## Why Researchers Point to Huayang

The occupant's identification is an inference rather than a certainty, resting on the tomb's royal rank, its close resemblance to the nearby Sijiazhuang Qin mausoleum and the historical record. Combining historical texts with late Warring States Qin burial customs, researchers suggest the tomb may belong to Queen Dowager Huayang. Earlier, three Qin mausoleums had been identified in the area — Zhoulingzhen, Yanjiagou and Sijiazhuang — and officials note that Shangzhaoyao closely mirrors Sijiazhuang in form and layout, suggesting a similar date and possibly close ties between their occupants.

Huayang, a noblewoman of the state of Chu, became the favored consort and then principal wife of Ying Zhu, who later reigned as King Xiaowen of Qin. Childless, she was persuaded by the chancellor Lü Buwei to adopt the hostage prince Yiren as her son. That adoption proved decisive: Yiren ascended as King Zhuangxiang, making Huayang Queen Dowager, and when his young son Ying Zheng took the throne, she became a senior figure of the court. She is traditionally said to have died around 230 BCE.

## Evidence From the Servants Buried Outside

A separate peer-reviewed study adds an unusual layer to the discovery. Published in the journal Antiquity in March 2026, the paper examined 43 human bones from 45 small tombs located between the mausoleum's inner and outer moats. All 30 of the sex-identifiable individuals were female, and the study found these women lived longer than contemporary Qin female commoners — evidence of a court affiliation and a structural survival advantage — but bore signs of early-life nutritional stress and adult labor strain.

According to [Shandong University's Archaeology School](https://www.archaeology.sdu.edu.cn/info/1026/5002.htm), the authors concluded the moat-interval population were likely low-ranking palace servants engaged in menial labor such as textile work or cleaning palace quarters. The research, the university says, marks the first time bone archaeology has demonstrated such a functional burial zone at a Warring States Qin mausoleum. The full paper is available through its DOI, [10.15184/aqy.2026.10307](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2026.10307).

## What the Find Adds

Taken together, the two lines of evidence — a royal tomb above and a workforce buried in its shadow — offer an unusually complete picture of how Qin royal mausoleums encoded hierarchy in death as in life. The Shangzhaoyao Mausoleum enriches understanding of late Warring States Qin royal burial practices and the planned, rank-ordered layout of mausoleum precincts on the Xianyang Plain, the dynasty's heartland.

Key questions remain. No inscribed object naming the occupant has been reported, so the link to Huayang rests on inference. There is also chronological tension: the traditional date of Huayang's death, around 230 BCE, sits within but near the edge of the broad carbon-14 range obtained from the accompanying tombs, and the principal tomb itself has not been directly dated by the evidence reported. For now, the tomb stands as the fourth royal Qin mausoleum on the plain — and a vivid window into an empire's formative years.
