# China, US Open New York Trade Talks Ahead of Summit

China and the United States opened a fresh round of high-stakes economic consultations in New York over the weekend, as Vice Premier He Lifeng leads a Chinese delegation to the United States from September 19 to 23, according to an official announcement from China's Ministry of Commerce ([Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20260919/d038fc1cc85c4f89a63e2ebc8dae34fb/c.html)). The talks are the final round of negotiations before a highly anticipated state summit between President Xi Jinping and President Donald Trump, and they will help determine whether the fragile bilateral tariff truce reached in 2025 is extended, narrowed, or allowed to lapse.

Announcing the visit through a spokesperson's Q&A on September 19, the ministry said both sides would be guided by "the important consensus of the two heads of state" and would consult on "economic and trade issues of mutual concern." The Chinese delegation is led by He Lifeng, a member of the Communist Party's Political Bureau and the State Council's Vice Premier, who has served as China's lead economic negotiator in the tariff talks that began last year.

## Talks to Cover AI, Trade and Rare Earths

The New York round is set to last several hours and will cover artificial intelligence, trade, rare earths and other economic issues, according to people familiar with the arrangements cited in Chinese media ([Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/GuoJi%C2%B7ZhanLue/2026_09_19_901232.shtml)). The agenda is being kept flexible to allow for substantive discussion, with working-level talks possibly continuing through Monday ([Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20260919-9700923)).

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who hosts the New York meetings, will be joined by US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, whose office confirmed his participation. Bessent has signaled a willingness to discuss shared AI risks with the Chinese side during the weekend talks, a notable broadening of a bilateral economic dialogue traditionally dominated by tariffs and market access.

The meeting is the highest-level direct economic engagement between Washington and Beijing since the May 2025 Geneva talks that produced the current truce. Under that deal, the United States agreed to reduce its additional tariffs on Chinese imports from 145 percent to 30 percent for a 90-day period — a framework subsequently extended — while China made corresponding reductions on US exports ([The Congressional Times](https://www.thecongressionaltimes.com/story/1569)).

The New York venue reflects a familiar diplomatic pattern: the city hosts the United Nations General Assembly in September, providing a natural window for bilateral meetings away from either capital. Technical or working-level sessions could stretch into early next week as negotiators try to lock in understandings before the leaders meet.

## A Truce Approaching Its One-Year Mark

The central question hanging over the talks is the fate of the tariff truce as it approaches its one-year anniversary. Neither government has publicly disclosed the specific terms under negotiation, including how long an extension might run, and analysts caution that the shape of any deal will become clear only after the leaders meet.

The negotiations are unfolding against a backdrop of deliberate restraint in Washington. According to people familiar with the matter, the US is expected to hold off announcing new tariffs on China and other trading partners until after next week's summit — a delay that preserves the threat of new duties as leverage ([Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/us-said-hold-off-tariffs-062302179.html)). The administration had intended to release a trade report on alleged excess capacity before the meeting that would recommend a 7.5 percent tariff on Chinese goods.

It remains unclear whether the final tariff rate would differ from previously expected levels, which would restore Trump's second-term duties on China to around 20 percent — a level Beijing has previously said is consistent with its trade truce with Washington.

The underlying economic stakes are substantial. The US goods trade deficit with China totaled approximately $150 billion in the first half of 2026, according to US Census Bureau figures. Even under the reduced 30 percent tariff regime, US importers in electronics, apparel and consumer goods continue to face elevated input costs, and the post-truce rates remain a material burden on bilateral commerce.

## From Talks to a State Visit

The New York consultations are a prelude to a state visit that both governments have now begun to formalize. The White House has released Xi's itinerary, under which the Chinese president and his wife, Peng Liyuan, arrive at Joint Base Andrews outside Washington on Wednesday, September 23. Trump is expected to greet Xi at the airport — a rare gesture — and the two will attend a welcome ceremony at the military base ([Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20260919-9701443)).

The Trump-Xi summit itself is scheduled for Thursday, September 24, featuring a Rose Garden review ceremony and a bilateral meeting, followed by a state dinner in the East Room. Guests are expected to include leading US technology executives such as Amazon founder Jeff Bezos, OpenAI's Sam Altman, Apple's Tim Cook, Tesla's Elon Musk and Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang.

On Friday, September 25, the two leaders are slated to have tea at the White House and visit the National Archives before Xi departs for China.

## Analysis: Leverage, Ratchets and the Road Ahead

The timing of the New York talks is no accident. By front-loading the technical negotiations before the leaders meet, both sides are attempting to convert a potentially unpredictable summit into a venue for ratifying deals already sketched out by their negotiators. That sequencing also lets each government manage expectations at home: a deal announced after a summit carries more political weight than one unveiled by working-level officials.

The public reticence on both sides is itself revealing. Washington has declined to commit publicly to the length of any extension, while Beijing has kept its demands close — a posture consistent with the administration's stated approach of using tariff policy as a negotiating instrument rather than a fixed structure. The absence of announced terms means the leverage each side holds will be tested mainly at the summit rather than at the working level.

Rare earths have become a central concern in the dispute. The minerals are indispensable to semiconductors, electric vehicles, defense systems and renewable energy technologies, and China dominates global processing capacity, making supply security a core strategic worry for Washington rather than a technical quibble.

The inclusion of AI on the agenda is a further sign that the two economies are increasingly intertwined in ways that resist simple decoupling. Bessent's willingness to discuss shared AI risks points to a recognition that unmanaged competition in advanced technologies carries hazards for both sides — from safety and security to supply-chain shocks.

China, for its part, has warned against using capacity concerns as a pretext for protectionism. Earlier this year, ministry spokesperson Huang Ling said capacity issues "should be viewed in a comprehensive and fair manner," adding that Beijing would "reserve the right to take all necessary measures" in response to US moves.

## What's Next

The talks in New York are expected to conclude ahead of Xi's arrival, with any agreement most likely to surface through a joint statement or a White House readout. The key milestones to watch are the outcome on the truce extension, the fate of the proposed 7.5 percent tariff, and any concrete movement on rare earths and AI.

For markets and businesses, the immediate question is whether the 30 percent tariff rate survives the truce's anniversary. A short extension would signal continued uncertainty; a longer one would offer relief to importers who have already absorbed a year of elevated costs. For both governments, the summit is an opportunity to stabilize a relationship that has swung between confrontation and accommodation — and to decide whether managed competition, rather than outright rupture, will define the months ahead.
