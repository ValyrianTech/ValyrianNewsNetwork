# GOP's $150M Trump-Backed Ad Blitz Hits Key Races

A constellation of super PACs aligned with President Donald Trump has launched a massive advertising offensive in the closing weeks of the 2026 midterms, reserving more than $150 million in the past two weeks to defend Republican majorities in the House and Senate, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/18/nx-s1-5971202/trump-super-pac-midterm-spending-republicans-democrats). Republican strategist Scott Jennings described the influx as the arrival of the "cavalry," signaling a costly and increasingly negative final stretch before Election Day on Nov. 3.

The spending hands Republicans a substantial overall advantage: GOP candidates and groups have pledged more than $557 million to Democrats' $343 million in the nine most competitive Senate races, an NPR analysis of AdImpact data found. Republican reservations outpace Democratic ones in every contest but Georgia.

## The 'Cavalry' Arrives

Three Trump-aligned groups anchored the September surge: MAGA Inc., the president's main super PAC, along with two new organizations formed on Sept. 1 — No Going Back PAC and Safety and Affordability PAC. The two newcomers list the same treasurer as MAGA Inc., according to [the Associated Press](https://www.mercurynews.com/2026/09/16/trump-money-2026-elections/), which reported the groups had reserved more than $130 million by Sept. 16. No Going Back PAC alone accounted for more than $98.5 million, while Safety and Affordability PAC reserved about $27 million, the AP said.

"Look across the battleground Senate states, look at the House districts, the cavalry has come," Jennings told NPR. "Now, the Republicans have enough money to make an actual argument, to draw a real contrast, to have a real debate. And in some of these districts, in some of these states, it's going to make a difference."

MAGA Inc. reported roughly $403.5 million in cash on hand as of July 31, and Trump has said his political operation could spend as much as $500 million on the midterms, according to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/14/trump-super-pac-midterms-no-going-back-pac.html). In an FEC filing reported over the weekend, No Going Back PAC disclosed roughly $21 million in independent expenditures, targeting Democratic Senate candidates Sherrod Brown in Ohio, Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, Roy Cooper in North Carolina, Chris Pappas in New Hampshire and Jon Ossoff in Georgia.

## Much of the Spending Is Defensive

Beyond the sheer volume, an NPR review of where the money is landing reveals something notable: much of it is defensive, flowing to shore up Republican candidates in conservative-leaning states and districts that have become unexpectedly competitive.

In the Senate, that includes Alaska, Texas, North Carolina and Ohio. In the House, Trump-aligned PAC spending is targeting districts the president carried comfortably in 2024 — Texas' 15th by nearly 18 points, Kentucky's 6th by 15, and Alabama's 2nd by 14. Republicans, in other words, are spending heavily to hold ground rather than to expand.

"Some of these ads are going to be ugly and it's going to be a pretty negative campaign, but that's the nature of a midterm when you've got an environment like the one that the Republicans are facing," Jennings said.

## Texas: The Flagship Battle

Nowhere is the spending imbalance starker than in Texas. Pro-Paxton ad reservations for September alone totaled more than $94 million, dwarfing the roughly $20 million supporting Democrat James Talarico. According to AdImpact, that Republican total for September exceeds what Democrats have spent the entire cycle on advertising in the state.

The Texas PAC, an offshoot of the Senate Leadership Fund aligned with Senate Majority Leader John Thune, has committed roughly $60 million to boost Republican nominee Ken Paxton, the state's attorney general, per [KERA News and The Texas Tribune](https://www.keranews.org/texas-news/2026-09-15/paxton-campaign-makes-first-major-ad-buy-in-senate-race). Paxton's own campaign launched its first substantial broadcast buy on Sept. 15 — about $300,000 per week — with ads leaning into his economic message and a Spanish-language wordplay attacking "Tala-broke."

Talarico, a state representative who had largely owned the airwaves over the summer, responded with a digital ad. "If the billionaires are coming after us, we must be doing something right," he said. A MAGA Inc. spokesman countered that "High Tax Talarico wants to take money out of Texans' pockets," while Talarico's campaign accused "billionaire-backed dark money groups" of lying about its candidate.

## Ohio's Record-Setting Race

The Ohio special election between Republican Sen. Jon Husted and former Sen. Sherrod Brown may prove the cycle's most expensive advertising battle. The contest has already drawn $298 million in advertising, more than any other Senate race this cycle, and Republican groups poured $14 million into the state in a single week, according to [Reuters](https://www.detroitnews.com/story/news/politics/2026/09/18/trump-linked-pop-up-pacs-blitz-ohio-ads-against-brown-costly-senate-race/91822108007/).

Despite an early polling lead, Husted now trails Brown by 3 to 5 percentage points. Brown has also significantly outraised Husted, bringing in $38.6 million versus the Republican's $14.3 million. Brown's campaign manager, Patrick Eisenhauer, said it was "no surprise that giant corporations and billionaires are spending tens of millions of dollars to save Jon Husted's flailing campaign."

Reuters also flagged the rise of "pop-up" PACs — recently registered groups such as Ohio Flyer PAC and Buckeyes PAC that have spent more than $3 million combined but, because of their late registration, will not have to identify donors until mid-October.

## Does Money Move the Needle?

The impact of the spending blitz remains fiercely disputed, and the political fundamentals cut against Republicans. Trump's approval sits around 37 percent, and a Marquette Law School survey released in mid-September found 54 percent of likely voters backing a Democrat for Congress versus 41 percent for a Republican, [TRT World](https://www.trtworld.com/article/42041118291d) reported. Inflation and the cost of living ranked as the top concern for 37 percent of Americans, with only 19 percent approving of Trump's handling of it. An unpopular war with Iran, now spiking fuel prices, compounds the headwinds.

"Money can't change the weather, but it can be an umbrella for them going into this election," said Democratic operative Jesse Ferguson. "It can help insulate them, but it can't change the fundamentals of what voters are feeling."

Analyst David Levine of the University of California was similarly skeptical. "I think that in this year, large amounts of PAC money may not be all that effective," he told TRT World. Voters "get saturated with advertisements," and "the marginal returns will be minimal in most places."

There is also a structural catch: super PACs like MAGA Inc. are charged higher television ad rates than candidate campaigns, and the disparity widens in the final 60 days, when campaigns qualify for broadcasters' lowest rates while outside groups pay rising market prices. That means late money buys less airtime than earlier spending would have.

## The Map and the Math

Republicans hold 53 Senate seats to Democrats' 47, counting two independents who caucus with Democrats. Democrats need a net gain of four for a majority; at 50-50, Vice President JD Vance would break ties for Republicans. The nonpartisan Cook Political Report lists seven Senate toss-ups — Alaska, Iowa, Maine, Michigan, New Hampshire, Ohio and Texas — five of them Republican-held.

In the House, Republicans hold 218 seats to Democrats' 214, with one independent and two vacancies. Mid-decade redistricting in Texas, California, Florida and Ohio moved about 35 million people into new districts, giving Republicans a structural edge of roughly two to six seats compared with the 2024 map.

"[Republicans] will almost certainly continue this trend and will lose Congressional seats," David N. Gibbs, a history professor at the University of Arizona, told TRT World, pointing to a "poor state of the economy" and warning that "the Republican losses in Congress could be even greater than current polls suggest."

## What to Watch

With early voting already underway in several states, the coming weeks will test whether the largest midterm war chest in memory can overcome an unpopular president, stubborn inflation and a costly foreign war. Ad reservations can shift, and the full picture of "pop-up" PAC donors will not emerge until mid-October. Republicans say the money will allow them to draw real contrasts; Democrats and outside analysts argue it cannot change the fundamentals. On Nov. 3, voters will render the verdict.
