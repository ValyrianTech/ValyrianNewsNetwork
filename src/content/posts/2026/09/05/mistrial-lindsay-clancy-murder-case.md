# Mistrial Declared in Lindsay Clancy Murder Case After Jury Deadlocks

A Massachusetts judge declared a mistrial Friday in the murder trial of Lindsay Clancy, the 36-year-old former labor and delivery nurse charged with killing her three young children in January 2023, after jurors failed to reach a unanimous verdict following seven days of deliberations. The case, which has sharply divided Americans over questions of criminal responsibility and maternal mental health, now enters an uncertain legal phase with a hearing scheduled for September 29.

## The Case and the Deadlock

Clancy never disputed that she strangled her three children — Cora, 5, Dawson, 3, and 8-month-old Callan — with exercise bands at the family's home in Duxbury, Massachusetts, on January 24, 2023. Her defense argued she was suffering from postpartum psychosis, a rare mental illness linked to stress, sleep deprivation, and hormonal changes following childbirth, and should not be held criminally responsible. Prosecutors contended she acted deliberately and knew what she was doing, pointing to evidence that she sent her husband out on errands to carry out the killings, as [AP News reported](https://apnews.com/article/lindsay-clancy-postpartum-massachusetts-trial-a939db73e864393eb9c727daa788e7a2).

The 12-person jury — nine women and three men — reported being unable to reach a unanimous decision on three separate occasions. According to notes given to the court, the jury was split 11 to 1. The jury's foreperson told Judge William Sullivan on Thursday that a single juror wouldn't follow the court's instructions about reasonable doubt. After questioning each juror individually and rereading instructions, Sullivan declared the mistrial on the seventh day of deliberations.

Before formally declaring the mistrial, Sullivan allowed defense attorney Kevin Reddington to file an emergency appeal to the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, which was denied. The trial lasted approximately seven weeks, with 21 days of testimony, 85 witnesses, and more than 200 pieces of evidence.

## What Happens Next

Clancy remains charged with murder and will continue to be held at Tewksbury State Hospital, a psychiatric facility, until the case is resolved. Judge Sullivan set a hearing for September 29 that could determine the path forward, as [AP News reported](https://apnews.com/article/lindsay-clancy-mistrial-what-happens-next-e38bef47f5af93bf95aabad272215b45).

Plymouth County District Attorney Timothy Cruz said there would not be an immediate decision about a second trial. "This was and has always been about getting justice for those three little babies," Cruz said after court ended. "This case is not about the healthcare system, or about how women are treated in it, or what diagnoses there are."

Prosecutors have several options: seek a retrial, offer a plea deal, downgrade the charges, or drop the case entirely. Legal experts say a retrial is plausible but not guaranteed. "I think it's entirely possible that they would move forward with a retrial for a couple of reasons," said Jessica Levinson, a Loyola Law School professor. "One, sometimes you see a really big swing in juries from one trial to another. Two, you could charge this in a very different way."

CBS News legal analyst Caroline Polisi echoed that sentiment, noting parallels with the Karen Read case in Massachusetts, where a first trial ended in a hung jury in 2024 before prosecutors retried and Read was acquitted. "I think they'll likely come back a second time," Polisi said, as [CBS News reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/lindsay-clancy-mistrial-what-happens-next/).

## Defense Response and Family Impact

Reddington said he believes Clancy was close to acquittal, suggesting that just one juror stood in the way. "They know they were robbed by one man, for whatever his agenda was, who stole seven weeks of the life of these other jurors that were so attentive, so beautiful, so wonderful," he said outside the courthouse.

David Meier, an attorney representing Patrick Clancy — Lindsay's ex-husband and the father of the children — in a lawsuit over her mental health treatment, released a statement about the emotional toll. "The prospect of reliving this tragedy through another trial is extraordinarily painful — for Patrick, for his family, and for all of us," Meier said, as [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/04/lindsay-clancy-trial-verdict-mistrial).

Boston-based defense lawyer Randy Gioia questioned whether another trial would serve any purpose. "Why put everyone through the trauma again: 16 to 18 jurors, all the witnesses, Patrick Clancy," Gioia said. "What's to be served here when we've had a long trial and long deliberations? You don't have a strong case. You almost lost the case."

## A Case That Divided the Nation

The trial drew intense national attention, with supporters dressed in pink gathering outside the courthouse to advocate for Clancy, while critics argued she should be held accountable for the killings. The case became a flashpoint for discussions about maternal mental health, the healthcare system, and how women's medical concerns are treated, as [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cx2z0xnw2d6o).

Clancy, who attempted suicide after the killings by jumping from a second-story window and is paralyzed from the waist down, had checked herself into a psychiatric hospital in early 2023 but was discharged after a short stay. She killed her children 19 days after being discharged.

## Looking Ahead

The September 29 hearing will likely reveal whether prosecutors intend to pursue a retrial. Both sides could also adjust their strategies based on lessons from the first trial. A retrial would have significant ripple effects, potentially delaying civil lawsuits filed by both Lindsay and Patrick Clancy over her mental health treatment.

For now, the case remains in legal limbo — a reflection, as some legal analysts noted, of a deeply divided public struggling to find consensus on one of the most emotionally charged questions in criminal law.
