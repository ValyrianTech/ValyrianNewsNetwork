# Guangzhou Caps Home Deposits at 5% in Sales Reform

Guangzhou on September 30 introduced new rules for the sale of completed homes, capping buyer deposits at no more than 5 percent of a property's total price and requiring those funds to be held in escrow. The measure, issued jointly by the city's housing bureau, planning authority and the Guangdong arm of the national financial regulator, makes Guangzhou the third Chinese first-tier city to implement a national overhaul of how homes are sold.

The policy follows a national notice issued on August 28 by the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development, the Ministry of Natural Resources and the National Financial Regulatory Administration — known as Jianfanggui No. 3 of 2026 — that aims to shift the market away from pre-sold apartments toward completed-home sales, according to the [full policy text published by The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34177691).

## A Tangible Protection for Buyers

The centerpiece of Guangzhou's rules is the deposit cap. A developer collecting a deposit on a completed home must first obtain the building's construction permit and sign a deposit contract with the buyer specifying the deposit amount, sale price, delivery date and liability for failure to perform. The deposit may not exceed 5 percent of the total price, and it must be deposited in full into a supervised account where it cannot be used until the project passes completion acceptance, has its utilities connected and its public service facilities accepted.

The 5 percent ceiling is the loosest among China's major cities to move so far. As a [comparative analysis by Leju Caijing](https://www.lejucaijing.com/news-7510889656427934858.html) set out, Beijing caps deposits at 1 percent of the total price and Shanghai at 3 percent. On a home priced at 6 million yuan (about $840,000), that translates to a maximum deposit of 300,000 yuan in Guangzhou, versus 180,000 yuan in Shanghai and just 60,000 yuan in Beijing.

Beijing's rules, issued on September 24, and confirmed in a [CCTV report carried by Sina News](https://news.sina.com.cn/o/2026-09-24/doc-inisxnvc0348561.shtml), require a developer to hold a construction permit before signing a deposit contract and let buyers cancel if the developer fails to deliver on time. Shanghai followed on September 28.

## A National Shift Away From Pre-Sales

The three city rules share a common framework: pre-sale of new projects is permitted only after the building's main structure has topped out, buyer funds must be fully supervised, and mortgages are released only after completion filing. Guangzhou adds a requirement that the supervised portion of pre-sale funds cover all remaining construction costs, a "lead bank" system that channels all project money through one bank, and interest-free land payment installments of up to two years, according to the [summary published by Guandian](https://www.guandian.cn/article/20260930/608732.html).

For decades, China's property market ran largely on pre-sales: buyers paid for apartments years before completion, effectively funding construction. That model propelled growth but concentrated enormous delivery risk on households when developers ran into financial trouble — a problem that became acute during the sector's downturn. The completed-home system is designed to reallocate that risk and rebuild buyer confidence by ensuring that what buyers see is what they get.

## Why Guangzhou Went Loosest

Guangzhou's choice of the highest deposit cap reflects a market that is already the most active of the three cities, even as it struggles with deep internal divisions. According to data cited in the research, Guangzhou's new-home online signings in the first half of 2026 reached 31,225 units, the highest among Beijing, Shanghai and Guangzhou, with first-hand commodity housing signing area up 10 percent year-on-year.

But that headline performance masks stark divergence. Core districts such as Tianhe, Haizhu and Huangpu Science City are tight, with Tianhe's sell-through cycle at only 14 months and its first-half transaction area up 47 percent year-on-year. Far-suburban districts including Zengcheng, Conghua and Huadu face sell-through cycles exceeding 40 months and heavy unsold inventory; Zengcheng alone holds 2.8 million square meters, the highest in the city.

The government is also constraining supply at the source. Guangzhou's 2026 residential land supply plan is 327 hectares, down 55 percent from 722 hectares in 2025 — a signal that policymakers want to work down existing stock rather than add to it.

## Reading the Three Cities

Analysts have characterized the three first-tier cities' approaches as three distinct paths under one framework. Beijing is strict on standards but lenient on timing, allowing land payments to be spread over two years interest-free. Shanghai is strict on upfront cash — it issues a first pre-sale certificate only after land payments are cleared — but lenient on financing, opening bonds, equity, asset-backed securities and REITs as channels. Guangzhou is the most demand-side-friendly: it fully removed home-purchase restrictions in late 2024 and offers housing provident fund limits of up to 3.6 million yuan for eligible buyers.

The common thread is caution. All three cities distinguish old projects from new ones based on whether a construction planning permit has been obtained, aiming to avoid disruption to projects already in the pipeline. Guangzhou's rules allow certain in-transit projects to continue under older pre-sale conditions until the end of 2027 if authorities confirm they can be delivered on time.

## What to Watch

The reform's central promise — that completed-home sales and deposit caps will reduce delivery failures — remains untested. A key open question is whether shrinking early cash flow will slow construction for developers already under strain, or whether the land-payment flexibility and lead-bank financing provisions will offset that.

Far-suburban Guangzhou districts, already burdened with inventory lasting more than three years, may prove the hardest test of the transition. And the question of where the national deposit ceiling ultimately settles — and whether second-tier cities follow with their own caps — will shape how quickly China's property market moves away from the pre-sale model that defined it for a generation.

The first test arrives in the fourth quarter of 2026 and through 2027, when the initial batch of topped-out pre-sale projects and completed-home projects reach the market under the new rules.
