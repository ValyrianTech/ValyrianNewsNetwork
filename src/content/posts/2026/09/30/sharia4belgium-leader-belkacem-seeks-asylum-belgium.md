# Sharia4Belgium Leader Belkacem Seeks Asylum in Belgium

Fouad Belkacem, the convicted former leader of the banned extremist group Sharia4Belgium, has applied for asylum in Belgium, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/30/fouad-belkacam-vraagt-asiel-aan-voor-na-vrijlating/). The application, disclosed by Flemish Interest (Vlaams Belang) MP Francesca Van Belleghem, comes as Belkacem approaches the end of a 12-year terrorism sentence and an expected release in 2027 — and appears aimed at preventing his deportation to Morocco.

The news has reignited a charged debate in Belgium over migration, security and the exploitation of asylum procedures, with the country's asylum minister vowing to remove him from Belgian territory while acknowledging that a legal process must first run its course.

## Context: Who Is Fouad Belkacem?

Born in Rumst in Antwerp province in 1982 to Moroccan parents, Belkacem acquired Belgian nationality as a teenager when his parents naturalised, giving him dual Belgian-Moroccan citizenship. He first drew public attention around 2010 as the spokesperson of Sharia4Belgium, a Salafist-jihadist organisation that disrupted public events and promoted the imposition of sharia law in Belgium.

In 2015, an appeals court convicted Belkacem to 12 years in prison and a €30,000 fine as the leader and founder of a terrorist organisation. According to [De Morgen](https://www.demorgen.be/nieuws/voormalig-sharia4belgium-leider-fouad-belkacem-vraagt-asiel-aan-in-belgie-om-uitlevering-aan-marokko-te-voorkomen~b3d88890/), he recruited dozens of young men in Antwerp to travel to Syria to fight alongside the Islamic State, and sought to overthrow Belgian democracy to replace it with sharia. In total, he is expected to have served roughly 15 years behind bars, with his sentence ending in 2027.

A decisive turn came on 23 October 2018, when the Antwerp Court of Appeal stripped Belkacem of his Belgian nationality — a step Belgium permits for people convicted of serious crimes who hold another citizenship. From that point, as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2018/10/23/belkacem/) reported, he retained only Moroccan nationality and became, in principle, deportable to Morocco upon release.

## The Asylum Application

The asylum request, first reported by VRT NWS, would grant Belkacem a legal procedure that places a hold on deportation while it is pending and could, if granted, provide a legal basis for him to remain in Belgium. The Office of the Commissioner General for Refugees and Stateless Persons (CGVS) is handling the file with priority.

According to VRT NWS, Belkacem has behaved as a model prisoner. He has been granted short temporary releases and reportedly visited his Belgian wife and three children without incident — family ties in Belgium that his request may seek to invoke.

The case presents a fundamental tension. Belgium's asylum system exists to protect people facing persecution, not to shelter those convicted of plotting to overthrow the very state assessing their claim. Because Belkacem is a convicted terrorist considered a threat to public order, the grounds for refusing protection on public order and national security grounds are substantial. Yet as long as the procedure is open, a deportation cannot simply proceed.

## Minister Vows to 'Exhaust All Means'

Asylum and Migration Minister Anneleen Van Bossuyt (N-VA) responded forcefully. "I will exhaust all means to remove the man from the territory," she said, as De Morgen reported. "Whoever turns against our society and wants to replace our democracy with sharia has no place here."

At the same time, Van Bossuyt emphasised procedural propriety, noting that "Belgium is a state of law, and his asylum application will first be assessed independently by the Commissioner General for Refugees and Stateless Persons. I am following this closely and give absolute priority to this file." She added that the government will soon introduce a package of measures targeting abuses of procedures filed from prison or closed detention centres.

Van Belleghem, who broke the story, framed it as evidence of a failing system. She said she had already heard that Morocco was reluctant to take Belkacem back, despite a commitment to readmit unlawful residents, calling it "their duty." She further contended that "77 percent of asylum applications are fraudulent, and Belkacem is the textbook example." That figure is attributed to the MP and has not been independently verified.

For its part, the Federal Prosecutor's Office, which prosecuted Belkacem, has described him in stark terms: a "permanent threat to public safety" and a figure whose organisation bore "crushing responsibility" for indoctrinating young Belgians and recruiting them for armed combat in Syria.

## A Question of Deportation

The practical puzzle is what happens at the end of the prison term. Because Belkacem lost his Belgian nationality, he is in principle subject to removal to Morocco. But deportation depends on the receiving country's cooperation — and Van Belleghem's claim that Morocco may decline complicates that path.

The case echoes that of Nizar Trabelsi, a convicted terrorist and former Guantanamo detainee whom Van Bossuyt has also sought to deport. In [September 2025](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/09/03/anneleen-van-bossuyt-over-veroordeelde-terrorist-nizar-trabelsi/), the minister said Trabelsi had "no place in our society" — language she has now repeated for Belkacem. Critics, including Van Belleghem, argue that the government has failed to do the necessary work to return such individuals, pointing to the Trabelsi case as an unfinished precedent.

## Analysis: A Test of Belgium's Legal Instruments

The Belkacem case sits at the intersection of two principles that Belgium, like many European states, struggles to reconcile: the rule of law and the imperative of public security.

On one reading, the asylum application is a procedural manoeuvre — a way to convert a soon-to-expire prison sentence into an open-ended legal stay. If successful, it would effectively neutralise the 2018 decision to strip his nationality and the deportation that decision was meant to enable. On another reading, Belgium's own legal framework created this opening: by allowing deprivation of nationality while retaining an individual's other citizenship, the state set up a scenario in which the person has only one country to go to and can challenge removal through international protection channels.

The wider political stakes are significant. Belgium's migration and security debate has grown increasingly fraught, and cases involving convicted terrorists are potent symbols. Vlaams Belang frames the episode as proof that asylum procedures are abused by criminals; the N-VA minister insists both on a hard line and on respecting due process. The government's promised package of measures on procedural abuse suggests it sees a systemic issue rather than an isolated case.

What the CGVS decides — and how quickly — will matter not only for Belkacem but as a signal of how Belgium handles the intersection of terrorism convictions, nationality law and asylum.

## What's Next

Belkacem is due for release in 2027. Between now and then, several questions will shape the outcome: whether the CGVS grants or refuses protection, whether Morocco agrees to accept him, and how the government's planned measures on procedural abuse affect cases like his. His wife and three children remain in Belgium, a circumstance that may weigh in his case and complicate enforcement even if a removal order is issued.

For now, the former terror leader's bid to stay in the country he once plotted to transform has placed Belgium's institutions — its courts, its asylum authority and its politicians — on the spot. The answer will be closely watched as a test of how far the rule of law and the demand for security can be reconciled.
