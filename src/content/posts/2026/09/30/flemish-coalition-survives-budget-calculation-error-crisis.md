# Flemish Coalition Survives Budget 'Calculation Error' Crisis

Flanders' regional government reached a hard-won budget agreement on Wednesday morning after a dispute over an alleged accounting error briefly tore the N-VA–CD&V–Vooruit coalition apart. The crisis was triggered late Tuesday when coalition partner Vooruit withdrew its approval of a deal it said short-changed the welfare budget, before ministers reconvened the next day and signed off on corrected figures.

Minister-President Matthias Diependaele (N-VA) had announced a deal live on the VRT NWS evening news on 29 September, describing it as "one of the heaviest exercises our Flemish government has ever had to carry out." The agreement set out a balanced budget through 2029, requiring roughly €3 billion in savings and reforms according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/29/wat-staat-in-begrotingsakkoord-vlaamse-regering-inhoud-maatregelen/).

## The 'Rekenfout' That Nearly Derailed a Deal

Within hours of the announcement, the agreement began to unravel. Vooruit said the budget table drafted by Budget Minister Ben Weyts (N-VA) did not match what had been agreed at the negotiating table, and that significantly less money would flow to welfare (welzijn). As [BRUZZ](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/politiek/vooruit-trekt-akkoord-voor-vlaamse-begroting-schending-afspraak-rond-welzijnsbudget-2026-09-30) reported, the party called the discrepancy "a breach of the agreements made," adding: "We are not doing any more night work. We will only give our approval if that money is fully restored to welfare, as agreed at the government table."

Party chairman Conner Rousseau framed the issue in existential terms for the socialists: "Investing in welfare is essential for Vooruit to be able to agree."

Weyts' office acknowledged a lingering "error in the calculation method" but insisted the political agreement itself stood. Diependaele conceded the same point, saying the mistake had been identified and would be resolved. At the centre of the standoff was a gap of roughly €100 million involving the welfare portfolio of Vooruit minister Caroline Gennez — a fraction of the ~€3.1 billion budget total, but a symbolically charged one.

The ministers met again at the Martelaarsplein in Brussels on Wednesday morning with cabinet chiefs convening from 9:00. A new budget table was presented and approved by all parties, clearing the way for Diependaele to deliver his delayed September Declaration at 14:00 in the Flemish Parliament.

"Once the dust settles, the books are in order," Weyts said after the deal. "There is always discussion, theatre and drama. It only shows how important and far-reaching this was."

## What's in the Agreement

The budget package mixes spending cuts with tax and fee increases. More than €1.2 billion is cut from government spending — €500 million in subsidies and over €700 million from the government apparatus itself, including roughly €180 million from personnel via changes to the civil-service statute. VRT NWS reported that two paid public holidays for Flemish civil servants — 2 November (All Souls) and 15 November (King's Day) — are to be scrapped. Local authorities contribute €240 million by 2029 through lower indexation of the municipal fund, and transport company De Lijn sees a promised €50 million boost halved to €25 million.

On the revenue side, the measures directly touch households. Service vouchers (dienstencheques) rise from €10 to €12, reaching €13 each from the 176th voucher per year. Most notably, registration duties on the purchase of a primary home climb from 2 percent to 3 percent — applying to all homes, not only those priced above €500,000 as originally floated. As [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/29/vlaamse-regering-wil-registratierechten-bij-woningverkoop-weer-o/) explained, that adds nearly €4,000 to the cost of an average Flanders home priced at €392,209 — a jump from €7,884 to €11,826 in duties, barely two years after the rate was lowered.

The Groeipakket child benefit will now be paid until age 23 instead of 25 for students living at home, and the exemption for non-studying children up to age 19 is scrapped. The €1,310.75 birth premium is limited to a family's first child. A planned reduction in inheritance tax has been postponed to 2029. Education is notably spared from cuts.

## A Coalition Built on Mutual Suspicion

The €100 million dispute punched well above its weight. Political scientist Carl Devos of Ghent University told VRT NWS that the sum was "small" against a €3.1 billion budget, "but the way it is interpreted shows that something is wrong between these two parties." He described "a great distrust" between N-VA and Vooruit.

That fault line extends beyond Flanders. N-VA and Vooruit also sit together in Belgium's federal coalition, where Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) is seeking roughly €10 billion in savings ahead of a 13 October deadline. The Flemish standoff has raised questions about whether the same tension could complicate the federal talks.

Opposition parties were scathing. Vlaams Belang's Chris Janssens called the episode "a farce, amateurism and bungling," declaring the Flemish government "a muppet show," while PVDA's Jos D'Haese observed that "there is no longer even agreement on whether one has an agreement." Unions and family associations also pushed back. Miranda Ulens of the socialist ABVV said the government "drops young families and ordinary workers like a brick," and Ivo Mechels of the family association Gezinsbond warned that "families are being hit very hard." Employer group VOKA's Frank Beckx welcomed the move toward a balanced budget but questioned the SME subsidy cuts and called a tripling of the industrial wastewater levy a "flat tax."

## What to Watch

Diependaele's September Declaration and the parliamentary debate that follows will test whether the wounds have genuinely healed or been merely patched. Carl Devos' warning of entrenched distrust suggests the coalition's durability — and its ability to hold together through the federal budget round — remains an open question. Commentators across Flemish newspapers have criticised the overnight negotiation ritual as poor governance, a critique unlikely to fade after a deal that nearly collapsed over 3 percent of its own arithmetic.
