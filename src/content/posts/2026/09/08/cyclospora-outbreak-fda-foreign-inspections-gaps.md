# Cyclospora Outbreak Exposes FDA Inspection Gaps

A nationwide [Cyclospora outbreak](https://apnews.com/article/lettuce-cyclospora-fda-cdc-outbreak-mexico-19899950075127fb664282dd73cccfc9) linked to contaminated iceberg lettuce from a Mexican farm has sickened thousands of Americans across more than 20 states, while new data reveals that U.S. regulators have been conducting significantly fewer foreign food inspections for years. The outbreak, traced to lettuce grown by Taylor Farms de Mexico in Guanajuato, has become the largest of its kind in U.S. history, with federal officials confirming 11,458 cases and 495 hospitalizations by late August, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

## Years of Declining Inspections

[Food and Drug Administration](https://apnews.com/article/lettuce-cyclospora-fda-inspections-trump-7eb876d3e37864e8b589aeeff2bca384) inspections of foreign food sites — including the Mexican farm at the center of the current outbreak — are down nearly 35% since 2019, the year before the COVID-19 pandemic, according to agency records reviewed by the Associated Press. Foreign food inspections fell further behind last year, dropping 17% to 1,140 in fiscal year 2025 from the prior year.

The pandemic kicked off a wave of retirements and departures among FDA inspectors that continues to this day. Even before COVID-19, the agency had long failed to meet its congressionally mandated targets for international inspections. Under the Food Safety Modernization Act of 2011, the FDA is supposed to inspect more than 19,000 international food sites annually — but the agency hit an all-time high of only 1,700 in 2019, less than one-tenth of the target, according to the Government Accountability Office.

"When you look at the numbers of inspections balanced against the responsibility of the FDA — in terms of all the firms domestically and internationally under its oversight — the agency just can't achieve what is required," said Michael Rogers, who spent nearly 35 years in various inspection roles within the agency.

## Delayed Response at the Mexican Farm

FDA inspectors arrived at the Taylor Farms site in Guanajuato, Mexico, in mid-August — about one month after the recall was announced on July 17. It was their first visit there since 2019, according to [Food Safety Magazine](https://www.food-safety.com/articles/11732-fda-begins-inspection-at-taylor-farms-mexico-as-official-cyclospora-outbreak-case-count-exceeds-10-000).

Food safety experts say the delay was unacceptable given the scale of the outbreak. "It took us too long to get boots on the ground," said Frank Yiannas, the former top food safety official at FDA. "When these outbreaks happen in fresh produce, you have to respond quickly because these are very complex and changing ecosystems."

The Taylor Farms recall involved thousands of packaged salad products combining iceberg lettuce with other vegetables, shipped to major U.S. restaurant chains including Yum Brands, which owns Taco Bell, Pizza Hut, and KFC.

## A Troubling Test Result and Confusion

The outbreak response was further complicated in July when the FDA was forced to retract a faulty test result that claimed to find Cyclospora on a sample of Taylor Farms' lettuce obtained at the border. The false positive created confusion, with Taylor Farms publicly claiming the FDA had apologized to the company. The agency denied issuing an apology, stating on social media that the false-positive lab sample "does not change the basis for FDA's ongoing outbreak investigation or the overwhelming epidemiological data supporting the current voluntary recall."

Acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas emphasized at a July press briefing: "Our weekend communications around a false positive test result do not change the basis for FDA's ongoing outbreak investigation or the epidemiological data supporting the current voluntary recall by Taylor Farms."

Mexican food authorities, however, say they have not found any positive samples or other red flags at the Taylor Farms facility.

## Record-Breaking Case Numbers

The scale of the outbreak is unprecedented. As of September 3, Michigan alone reported 14,718 cyclospora cases with 366 hospitalizations and two deaths, according to [Food Safety News](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/09/patient-count-in-cyclospora-outbreak-in-michigan-at-more-than-14-700/). The overall CDC count of all cyclospora outbreaks attributed to the parasite is 18,445 cases since May 1 — compared to only 1,180 cases in the same period in 2025. The unofficial state-by-state total exceeds 30,000.

Cyclospora is a microscopic parasite that causes watery diarrhea, severe abdominal pain, nausea, and fatigue. Symptoms can develop between two and 14 days after exposure and can last for a month or more if untreated. Food safety experts say there's no evidence that washing produce will remove the parasite, and it is not killed by chlorine or other treatments.

## Staffing Cuts and Regulatory Concerns

The Trump administration has eliminated more than half the FDA staffers who handle travel bookings, leaving inspectors to handle their own flights, lodging, and other logistics. While inspectors were not among the 3,500 jobs targeted for termination, the agency's food inspection workforce of more than 420 staffers has 10% to 15% of positions chronically unfilled, according to the GAO.

"Until you can better incentivize people to stay in those positions, I think the agency is going to continue to see high attrition, younger and less experienced investigators," Rogers said. "That ultimately may impact FDA's ability to enhance and increase inspections."

The administration has proposed a pilot program of one-day inspections, but Rogers and other officials say abbreviated inspections are not equivalent to traditional ones, which can take a week or more to document serious problems.

## The Delayed Traceability Rule

The 2011 food safety law called on manufacturers to place bar codes on each batch of produce so it can be quickly traced in the event of contamination. The rule was supposed to take effect in January 2026, but the Trump administration delayed its compliance deadline by 30 months to July 20, 2028, after pushback from industry.

"There's widespread acknowledgment that these are complex issues, but when you have 17 years to prepare for a final exam you should be able to pass it," said Brian Ronholm, a former Department of Agriculture official who is now with Consumer Reports.

The timing of the delay has drawn scrutiny. Taylor Fresh Foods, the corporate entity behind Taylor Farms, donated $1 million to the Trump-backing super PAC MAGA, Inc. — one week before the administration delayed the FDA's Food Traceability Rule compliance deadline to July 20, 2028. Rep. Robert Garcia, the Democratic Ranking Member on the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, has demanded answers from Taylor Farms about its response to the outbreak and its communications with federal agencies, as reported by [Food Safety Magazine](https://www.food-safety.com/articles/11684-congress-oversight-committee-probes-taylor-farms-alleged-attempts-to-influence-fdas-cyclospora-outbreak-investigation).

## Business Impact and Consumer Fallout

The outbreak has rippled through the restaurant industry. Taco Bell's U.S. same-store sales dropped 2% in the July-September period due to customer concerns, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/cyclospora-lettuce-taco-bell-restaurants-customers-39083c579b4ba7d3d5d8a23ea3fd5b00). Chipotle expected same-store sales to rise only 1% in Q3 instead of the previously expected 3%. Foot traffic at salad chain Chopt plunged 24% on July 18, and weekly spending at Sweetgreen dropped 10 percentage points compared to last year.

Meanwhile, companies have announced more than 480 food recalls in the current fiscal year, already surpassing four out of the five previous years.

## What's Next

As the investigation continues, FDA officials are studying how many foreign inspections should be conducted annually "to ensure oversight comparable" to the U.S. food supply. Experts say new approaches, including machine learning and remote monitoring of supplier practices, could help alleviate some of the need for inspections.

"We have to rethink how we oversee the safety of foreign producers," Yiannas said. "We can't use the same approaches and limited tools that we've used for the past 30 years."

Taylor Farms has voluntarily suspended all iceberg lettuce sourcing and production from central Mexico and says it invests more than $200 million annually in food safety protocols. The company maintains it uses the latest FDA-validated testing method for Cyclospora.

For consumers, the outbreak serves as a stark reminder of the vulnerabilities in America's imported food supply chain — and the consequences when regulatory oversight falls short. With congressional investigations underway and questions mounting about FDA's capacity to fulfill its mandate, the coming months will be critical in determining whether meaningful reforms follow this unprecedented public health crisis.
