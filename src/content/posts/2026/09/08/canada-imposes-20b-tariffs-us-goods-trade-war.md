# Canada Imposes $20B Tariffs on US Goods as Trade War Escalates

Canada's retaliatory tariffs on approximately $20 billion worth of American goods took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET Tuesday, marking one of the most significant escalations in the trade war between the two nations. The duties, ranging from 15% to 50%, target hundreds of US products including steel, aluminum, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, and electronics, as [reported by AP News](https://apnews.com/article/canada-trump-carney-trade-war-tariffs-4620ffb5ecc029322217207fa6758431).

The measures represent Canada's direct response to Washington's 50% tariffs on $20 billion of Canadian goods that took effect August 22 under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930. Prime Minister Mark Carney has framed the retaliation as a matter of national sovereignty, declaring that Canada will match Washington's tariffs "dollar for dollar in order to protect Canadian workers, farmers, families, and businesses," according to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/8/canadas-retaliatory-tariffs-on-20bn-of-us-goods-take-effect).

## How the Trade War Escalated

The current confrontation traces back to January 2025, when President Donald Trump returned to office and began threatening tariffs on Canadian goods despite having negotiated the US-Mexico-Canada Agreement during his first term. After months of escalating measures, trade talks between the two countries collapsed on August 21 after days of negotiations in Washington. The [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/22/nx-s1-5941584/us-canada-tariffs) reported that Carney likened the situation to a military conflict, saying, "You're at war when you get attacked. We got attacked."

The collapse came after what Carney described as "last-minute changes in the U.S. proposed terms" that were "unfair, uneconomic, and called into question the reliability of any deal." U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer countered that "new demands and walk backs of other commitments by Canada have upended the careful balance reached in the past days."

## The Tariffs in Detail

The Canadian counter-tariffs cover roughly 6% of the $333.6 billion the United States exported to Canada last year. The [Government of Canada's Department of Finance](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/08/list-of-products-from-the-united-states-subject-to-counter-tariffs-effective-september-8-2026.html) published a detailed list of 629 tariff items, with rates matching the US tariffs dollar for dollar. Canadian tariffs on US steel, aluminum, and iron products doubled to 50%, while furniture, clothing, cosmetics, and other consumer goods face rates between 15% and 50%.

Canada has also announced a $7.5 billion (CAD) support package for affected small and medium-sized businesses and workers, as noted by [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/08/canada-retaliatory-tariffs.html). Beyond tariffs, eight of Canada's 10 provinces continue to restrict or ban sales of US alcohol, and US spirits exports to Canada have fallen more than 70% year-over-year.

## Trump's Escalating Threats

In the days leading up to Canada's counter-tariffs taking effect, Trump escalated his rhetoric against Canadian industries. On Monday, he threatened to block Bombardier from selling aircraft in the United States unless the Canadian manufacturer builds its planes in America. "NO MORE SELLING BOMBARDIER IN THE UNITED STATES! Their products aren't good enough!" Trump posted on Truth Social, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-canada-carney-trade-bombardier-aircraft-e65b6eeaaa7ffbd06a67a57fb474b2e9).

Bombardier pushed back, noting that its supply chain includes approximately 2,800 US companies across 47 states, supporting tens of thousands of American jobs. The company's wings are made in Texas, flight control components are produced near Los Angeles, and Honeywell manufactures engines in Phoenix.

Trump has also threatened 50% tariffs on Canadian vehicles, auto parts, and steel next year if Canada does not "fall in line," a potentially devastating escalation for industries built around deeply integrated North American supply chains. "We want the cars to be made in Detroit and in South Carolina and in Tennessee," Trump said.

## A Questionable Legal Foundation

The legal basis for the US tariffs is drawing scrutiny from trade experts. After the Supreme Court struck down Trump's use of IEEPA for broad-based tariffs in February 2026, the administration turned to Section 338 of the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 — a rarely used "zombie statute" from the Great Depression era. According to the [Peterson Institute for International Economics](https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2026/trump-invoked-zombie-statute-justify-tariffs-canada), the last time the International Trade Commission did anything under this statute was cited in its 1943 Annual Report. PIIE's Alan Wm. Wolff argues the courts should reject these tariffs as "patently illegal."

Court challenges could potentially undo more US tariffs, adding another layer of uncertainty to an already volatile trade relationship.

## Political and Economic Implications

The trade war is exacting a political toll on both sides of the border. Only 20% of Americans approved of Trump's tariffs on Canadian goods, according to a Reuters/Ipsos poll cited by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/sep/07/canada-tariffs-us-trump). In Canada, Trump's pressure has backfired, strengthening support for Carney rather than forcing concessions.

"Carney and Canada have become symbolic of resistance to him," Canadian historian Robert Bothwell said. "And what he'll want to do is make an example of Carney and Canada and theoretically terrify everybody else while in fact showing them that they need to remove themselves even further from the United States."

Daniel Béland, a political scientist at McGill University, noted that "the Canadian public is behind the Carney government and the world is watching what will happen next. If Canada's defiant approach succeeds, it could be used as a template for resisting the Trump administration's trade policies."

Economically, the stakes are significant. Canada sends more than 70% of its exports south of the border, and the US economy is roughly 13 times larger. However, Washington has vulnerabilities too — US refineries rely on 4 million barrels of oil per day from Canada, and American farmers depend heavily on Canadian potash fertilizer. Canada's economy grew at a 3.2% annualized rate in Q2 2026, more than twice the US pace of 1.5%.

## What to Watch Next

Several questions loom over the coming months. Will the Section 338 tariffs survive court challenges? Will Trump follow through on his threat of 50% auto tariffs in January 2027? Can Canada maintain public support as economic consequences materialize? And could Canada's approach serve as a template for other countries facing US economic pressure?

Carney is set to address the European Parliament next week, giving him another major international stage to position Canada as a global voice for resisting economic coercion. As Industry Minister Mélanie Joly put it: "I'm convinced that we will be able to show the world that Canada will win this trade war."

Nelson Wiseman, professor emeritus at the University of Toronto, offered a more sober assessment: "Canada-U.S. relations, even without confrontation, will never be what they were before Trump."
