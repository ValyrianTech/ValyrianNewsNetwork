# Belgium's First Lunar Rover to Fly to the Moon in 2029

A space technology company in Zaventem has secured a flight to the Moon for what would be Belgium's first-ever lunar rover, with a launch now targeted for early 2029 aboard a rocket built by Jeff Bezos's Blue Origin.

Space Applications Services, known as SpaceApps, announced on 22 September that its 20-kilogram rover, LUVMI-M, has won a place on Blue Origin's uncrewed MK1 lunar lander, which will be launched by the company's New Glenn rocket. If successful, the rover would operate at the Moon's south pole, becoming the first Belgian vehicle to drive on the lunar surface.

## A rover, not a lander

Despite headlines describing the project as a "lunar lander," the Belgian contribution is a rover — the robotic vehicle that the lander will deploy onto the surface. The lander itself is Blue Origin's MK1, a vehicle that, as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/22/maanrover-luvmi-m-spaceapps-blue-origin/) noted, has never flown.

The LUVMI-M weighs 20 kilograms, can carry 20 kilograms of scientific instruments, and is roughly the size of a built-in oven on wheels. It was entirely designed and built in Zaventem, and it will be driven from SpaceApps' mission control centre there.

The rover is equipped with special wheels designed to keep it from sinking into loose lunar regolith and to handle slopes during ascent and descent. "Our rover should be able to handle hills with a slope of 15 percent," business developer Tom Hoppenbrouwers said in an April 2026 VRT report on [testing at ESA's simulated lunar landscape](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/04/09/belgisch-ruimtebedrijf-test-maanwagentje-op-nagebootst-maanlands/) in Cologne.

## First tests prove successful

In April 2026, the LUVMI-M drove across a 700-square-metre simulated moonscape at the European Space Agency's facility in Cologne, built with 900 tonnes of sand, to assess how it copes with dust, slopes and difficult lighting.

"Those tests were very successful," project manager and space engineer Simon Vanden Bussche said. He added that a next phase will examine whether the rover can survive the extreme temperatures at the Moon's south pole and the vibrations of launch.

That south-pole environment poses unusual challenges. Because the Sun sits low on the horizon there, long shadows stretch across the surface, complicating the camera-based distance estimates needed for autonomous or remote driving. The region is nonetheless a priority for lunar exploration because its permanently shadowed craters may hold water ice — a potentially vital resource for a sustained human presence on the Moon.

## A commercial mission, paid for in-house

The mission is being run entirely on a commercial basis, with no space agency financing its mission or operating costs, according to SpaceApps. Only the development of the LUVMI-M received partial support from ESA. The company declined to say how much it is paying Blue Origin for the flight.

If all goes well, SpaceApps plans to rent out the rover. Space agencies, companies, universities and researchers could install experiments or instruments on it for testing on the Moon, or buy the complete rover to run a mission of their own. The company positions itself as a provider of lunar mobility infrastructure and services.

## Blue Origin's rocky record

The confidence expressed by SpaceApps sits alongside a checkered recent history for its launch partner. The New Glenn rocket has completed only two successful flights. On its third launch, it failed to place a satellite worth tens of millions of euros into the correct orbit; the satellite ultimately crashed and burned up in the atmosphere.

In May 2026, the New Glenn was destroyed in an explosion during a hot-fire test at its launch pad, an incident that left the pad severely damaged. Blue Origin has been repairing the site since. As [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/05/29/ontploffing-raket-blue-origin/), the blast also delayed NASA's lunar plans. Repairing the launch base will take "quite a long time," NASA administrator Jared Isaacman said, while Blue Origin CEO Dave Limp pledged the company would return to space before the end of the year, according to [NOS](https://nos.nl/artikel/2616810-reparatie-lanceerbasis-blue-origin-waar-raket-ontplofte-gaat-lang-duren).

Vanden Bussche remains undeterred. "Failed launches are simply part of new rockets," he said. "That was also the case at the start for SpaceX." He pointed to NASA's recent award of several important lunar missions to Blue Origin as evidence of continued institutional confidence.

## What to watch

The announcement marks a notable step for Belgium's space sector, which has grown increasingly active in both commercial and institutional projects, including work on ESA's larger Lunar Prospecting and Scouting Rover. Only five nations — the Soviet Union, the United States, China, India and Japan — have successfully operated a rover on the Moon, and no European rover has yet done so.

Much now depends on Blue Origin's ability to recover from its setbacks and on whether the MK1 lander, still untested in flight, can deliver the rover safely to the lunar south pole. SpaceApps also faces further environmental testing before launch. If those hurdles are cleared, a small machine built in Zaventem could carry Belgian ambitions to the Moon's surface — and open a new commercial market for renting out lunar mobility.
