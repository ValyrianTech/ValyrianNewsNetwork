# TikTok Settles Alabama Youth Safety Suit for $100M+

TikTok and its Chinese parent company ByteDance have agreed to settle Alabama's youth-safety lawsuit, agreeing to pay the state a minimum of $100 million and to implement sweeping new protections for teenage users — just days before the case was set to go to trial. The agreement, [announced](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/tiktok-alabama-youth-safety-lawsuit-settlement/) Friday by Alabama Attorney General Steve Marshall, marks one of the largest settlements tied to social media youth-safety claims and was reached ahead of a Monday trial date in Montgomery.

Under the deal, Alabama will receive at least $100 million in restitution and remediation, with the potential for up to $300 million "if certain conditions are met," according to the Alabama Attorney General's Office. Local Alabama outlet [WSFA/WBRC](https://www.wbrc.com/2026/09/25/alabama-tiktok-reach-multimillion-dollar-settlement-new-regulations-minors/) reported the state would receive a minimum of $116.2 million within 45 days — a figure that differs from the $100 million cited by CBS News and The Associated Press. Alabama could receive up to an additional $183.8 million if enough other states reach qualifying settlements with TikTok within a set timeframe.

Alabama would have been the first state to argue its claims against TikTok before a jury.

## What Alabama Alleged

Alabama sued TikTok in April 2025, with Marshall [filing a complaint](https://www.alabamaag.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2-Complaint.pdf) alleging the company exploited children by addicting them to harmful content and misrepresented the platform's safety. According to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/26/facebook-found-liable-as-tiktok-settles-for-100m-over-user-safety), the state claimed TikTok's algorithm pushed increasingly violent content to young users, contributing to a teen mental health crisis and causing related emergency room visits to "skyrocket."

The state also accused TikTok of falsely claiming it limits access to inappropriate content in order to secure a teen-safe app-store rating, and of misleading the public about Chinese government access to U.S. users' data. The lawsuit argued that TikTok's "addictive properties" — namely its algorithm that curates highly specific content — were harmful to children and that the company knew of those harms.

## New Protections for Teen Users

The settlement, detailed in a [filing](https://www.alabamaag.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2c218616-c8e5-48a0-8b2f-5e771948385d.pdf) from the Alabama Attorney General's Office, requires TikTok to make substantial changes for teen users in the state:

- A **two-hour daily time limit** for teens, plus expanded parental controls
- **"Productive pauses"** that interrupt users after 15, 60, and 90 minutes of continuous use
- **Nighttime restrictions** blocking access from midnight to 6 a.m.
- Restrictions on **messaging and push notifications** overnight and during school hours
- **Stronger age-verification** measures for young users
- **Enhanced content moderation**, including limits on adult visibility of teen accounts and parental notification of suspicious adult-teen interactions
- A **ban on cosmetic procedure filters** for teens
- An option for teens to select a **non-personalized content feed**
- **Teen accounts set to private by default**

"This is a great day for Alabama parents," Marshall said in a statement. "Tonight, they can rest easier knowing real protections are in place to shield their children from the dangers of social media addiction. TikTok has agreed to give parents real control over what their kids see and how much time they spend on the app."

A spokesperson for the TikTok USDS Joint Venture — the group of domestic investors operating TikTok in the U.S. — said the company's priority has "always been fostering a safe and positive space where people can be creative, discover what they love, and connect with their community," adding that the deal "builds on our commitment and core objective to continually enhance our robust safety tools to protect teens."

## Following the Meta Framework

The Alabama terms are "roughly similar" to changes Meta agreed to make in a landmark settlement reached this summer with nearly every state. On August 26, 2026, New York Attorney General Letitia James and a bipartisan coalition of 50 other attorneys general [secured up to $17.1 billion](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-secures-171-billion-and-groundbreaking-reforms-meta) from Meta, along with transformative changes to Facebook and Instagram for young users. Meta had publicly called on TikTok and YouTube to adopt similar standards; TikTok had not publicly responded before this settlement.

Katherine Robertson, chief counsel in the Alabama Attorney General's Office and the Republican nominee for the state's next attorney general, acknowledged the similarities. "There's been a war on childhood coming from a million different directions," she told the AP. "We recognize that parents and youth are fighting a battle against a bunch of different platforms. They're manipulating kids to get addicted to their product, essentially."

The Meta agreement was explicitly structured so that future settlements with other platforms, including TikTok, would trigger stricter "Phase 2" terms — a 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. access blackout, disabled push notifications, and a 60-minute daily limit per platform. Whether the Alabama deal and other state settlements activate those stricter terms remains an open question.

## Broader Pressure Mounts

At least 27 other states and Washington, D.C., have sued TikTok over similar youth-safety claims, a dynamic central to the "conditions" that could raise Alabama's payment to $300 million. TikTok separately reached a $400 million settlement with the Justice Department in August 2026, ending a 2024 lawsuit alleging the company violated federal children's privacy laws.

The settlement also coincided with a jury verdict in New Mexico finding Facebook liable for violating state consumer protection laws tied to the Cambridge Analytica scandal, in which data from roughly 87 million profiles was harvested. A Meta spokesperson said the company disagreed with the verdict.

## What's Next

Robertson said the Alabama funds will likely go to the state's general fund, though officials hope to direct some toward remediating harm to youth. "Hopefully [we] can make investments in schools and resources and just online safety generally," she said.

Key questions remain: whether the Alabama agreement pressures YouTube to adopt comparable standards, how the "conditions" clause tying extra payments to other state settlements will work in practice, and whether TikTok's platform changes will be independently auditable. For now, the deal stands as the latest in a widening wave of legal and regulatory pressure on social media platforms over their impact on minors.
