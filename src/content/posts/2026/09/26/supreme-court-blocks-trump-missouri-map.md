# Supreme Court Blocks Trump-Backed Missouri Map

The U.S. Supreme Court on Friday blocked a Trump-backed congressional map in Missouri, keeping the state's 2022 districts in place for the November midterms and handing Republicans a setback in their nationwide push to protect their slim House majority. The unsigned decision — the third time the justices have refused to let the 2025 map take effect — warned that switching maps mid-election would usher in "electoral chaos."

## The Ruling

The Court's [unsigned decision](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/26a388_q86b.pdf) held that the District Court and the Court of Appeals "should not enjoin or otherwise prohibit the use of the 2022 map in the 2026 congressional election in Missouri," and likewise should not force the state to adopt the 2025 map. "At this late date, ordering reversion to the 2025 map in the midst of an ongoing election would usher in electoral chaos," the justices wrote.

The Court pointed to the practical reality of an election already underway: "The election is not merely upcoming; it has already started. Ballots using the 2022 map have been printed and mailed. Absentee voting has already begun, as has military and overseas voting." According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/25/nx-s1-5978875/midterms-redistricting-trump-missouri), the ruling ends a months-long legal battle over which districts may be used on November 3.

## How Missouri Got Here

Missouri's Republican-controlled legislature passed the new map, HB 1, in September 2025 at the urging of President Donald Trump, as part of a broader strategy to draw more GOP-leaning seats and prevent Democrats from winning the House. The map was designed to shift Republicans from holding six of Missouri's eight House seats to seven, and it dramatically redrew the Kansas City-area 5th District held by longtime Democratic Rep. Emanuel Cleaver.

Opponents fought back through a constitutional mechanism that became the crux of the case. A citizen group, People Not Politicians, gathered more than 300,000 signatures to place the map on the November ballot as a referendum — now Proposition A. Under Missouri law, a valid referendum petition suspends a law before it can take effect.

According to [St. Louis Public Radio](https://www.stlpr.org/government-politics-issues/2026-09-25/congressional-map-missouri-8th-circle-appeal), the Missouri Supreme Court ruled on Sept. 3 that the petition was valid and that HB 1 "is not and never has been the law," ordering the state to use the 2022 districts. The state court blamed Republican Secretary of State Denny Hoskins for allowing the 2025 map to be used in the Aug. 4 primaries despite the pending referendum.

The dispute then split across court systems. The U.S. Supreme Court twice declined to intervene in the state court's ruling in September. Separately, Republicans sued in federal court, where U.S. District Judge Stephen Clark issued a conflicting order favoring the 2025 map, and the 8th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled for the Trump-backed map on Sept. 21. The [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/25/supreme-court-missouri-maps-trump) reported that at least 16 lawsuits have been filed over the redistricting, making it the longest and most complex fight among roughly a dozen states that sought to redraw their House districts.

## A Narrowed Legal Rebuke

Notably, the Supreme Court's decision also faulted the secretary of state directly. "In an opinion back in May 2026, well before the primary election, the Missouri Supreme Court stated that the 2025 map would not take effect until approved by the voters," the justices wrote. "So in the several-month period before the August primary election, the Missouri Secretary of State was well aware that conducting the August primary election with the 2025 map carried significant risks."

The Court added that its two prior orders in the litigation should have signaled clearly how it would rule: "Given this Court's two prior orders in this litigation, our conclusion that this was error should come as no surprise." The Missouri Supreme Court issued a statement Friday night noting that its Sept. 3 opinion had now been upheld a third time.

## What It Means for November

The decision locks in the 2022 lines for the Nov. 3 general election, with consequences that cut in both directions. Cleaver, who was unopposed in the Democratic primary, is now heavily favored against Republican state Sen. Rick Brattin. The 2022 lines also make the St. Louis-area 2nd District more competitive, potentially boosting Democrat Fred Wellman's challenge to Republican Rep. Ann Wagner, while making Rep. Bob Onder's 3rd District more reliably Republican.

The map's collapse is a personal rebuke for Cleaver's opponents and a relief for Kansas City. The 2025 version had split the city across three districts and cut through Troost Avenue, the historical racial dividing line in the city. "The joy I feel now is generated by the fact that the people won this battle," Cleaver told St. Louis Public Radio, adding that the result spared residents who had worked for years to revitalize Troost.

Republicans framed the outcome sharply. Brattin called it "the most unprecedented form of voter disenfranchisement in American history," arguing that "judges are not kings, and courts are not legislatures." Secretary of State Hoskins called the ruling "a disappointing blow to representative democracy" but said his office would comply. Governor Mike Kehoe said Missouri would "follow the Court's order" while noting his disappointment that the general election would use different lines than the primary.

## The Fight Isn't Over

The ruling settles the map for 2026 — but not the broader question. Missouri voters will still decide Proposition A on Nov. 3, a separate ballot measure that would ratify the 2025 map for future cycles. The [Community Voice](https://www.communityvoiceks.com/2026/09/14/mo-redistricting-prop-a/) reported that an August SLU/YouGov poll found 35% of voters inclined to vote yes, 32% no, and 33% undecided — a sign of enduring confusion over the ballot language and stakes.

Even a "no" vote would not end the matter. The Missouri Supreme Court ruled in March 2026 that lawmakers may redraw districts mid-decade, leaving open the possibility of another legislative attempt ahead of 2028. The [Missouri Independent](https://missouriindependent.com/2026/09/03/missouri-supreme-court-blocks-gerrymandered-congressional-map-orders-referendum-vote/) has followed the state-level litigation closely throughout.

## What to Watch

For now, the ruling delivers certainty to Missouri's election officials, who had prepared for November amid conflicting court orders. Boone County Clerk Brianna Lennon said the clarity meant she could finally assure absentee voters their ballots would count under stable district lines.

Nationally, the decision denies Republicans an expected additional Missouri seat and marginally improves Democrats' prospects as they fight for control of the House. But the redistricting war continues elsewhere: Trump's push prompted states from Texas and Florida to California to redraw their maps early, and the legal battles over those lines are far from settled. Missouri's fight may be over for this cycle — but it remains a template for a contest that shows no sign of ending.
