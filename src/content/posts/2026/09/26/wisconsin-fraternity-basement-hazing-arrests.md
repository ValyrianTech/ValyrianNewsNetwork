# Wisconsin Frat Leaders Charged in Basement Hazing

Two leaders of an underground fraternity at the University of Wisconsin-Madison were arrested and charged with hazing after roughly 30 pledges were found partially clothed in a hot, filthy basement, covered in food and condiments, with several showing injuries consistent with assault, authorities said.

Madison police responded to the Alpha Epsilon Pi (AEPi) house on Langdon Street around 8:20 p.m. Wednesday after an anonymous tip about a possible hazing event, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/wisconsin-fraternity-hazing-basement-rcna599852). Inside, officers found what Capt. Kipp Hartman described as "around 30 partially clothed males covered in food, condiments and other liquids." Most of the pledges were 18 years old. The thermostat was set to 81 degrees Fahrenheit, creating a hot and humid environment in a basement with black-painted walls and debris on the floor.

Fraternity president Brayden Klein and vice president Samuel Vane, both 20, were arrested on suspicion of hazing and disorderly conduct — both misdemeanors in Wisconsin. Each posted $400 bail early Thursday and was released from the Dane County Jail. The Dane County District Attorney's Office will review the case before deciding whether to file formal charges.

## A Warning Sign Police Called 'Fresh'

Several pledges had visible injuries that police did not treat as old or incidental. "Something fresh, potentially like a strike to the body, not an old injury," Hartman said. No one required medical treatment; officers checked the pledges for injuries or incapacitation before escorting everyone outside.

Investigators said the accounts they collected conflicted. "Some statements also did not align with what everybody else's statements were, so there were some concerns with what was being said," Hartman told [WMTV](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/09/24/two-arrested-madison-fraternity-hazing-investigation/). "I would say we had limited cooperation." Police released a photo showing about two dozen shirtless men in the basement, their faces blurred.

The incident occurred during rush week at UW-Madison, a campus of about 52,000 students. Police said they are monitoring what they believe is misinformation spreading on social media and urged anyone with firsthand information to come forward, noting that cooperating witnesses may not be prosecuted. "Our concern is when things are going too far and people's either life and/or physical harm is at stake," Hartman said. "That is definitely when we are going to be stepping in."

## A Chapter the University Cut Loose

The case has drawn attention to the special risks posed by "underground" chapters that operate without university oversight. UW-Madison terminated AEPi's status as a recognized student organization in 2015, after a fourth incident involving events, alcohol or both. The chapter had been suspended in 2013 for hosting a party where a 17-year-old and another person were taken to detox. Since 2016, the university says, there have been seven additional allegations involving the fraternity related to intramural sports, disrespectful behavior, drinking and hazing.

Because AEPi's national organization still recognizes the Madison chapter, it can legally operate unaffiliated with the university. UW-Madison said it has no oversight over the chapter's activities and limited ability to proactively intervene, other than holding individual students accountable under the conduct code. The university's Office of Student Conduct and Community Standards will investigate any students implicated, with sanctions ranging from a written reprimand to expulsion.

On Friday, AEPi's national headquarters issued a cease-and-desist directive to the Madison chapter, suspending new-member meetings, social and brotherhood events and most other chapter activity pending its investigation. "Any member found responsible for criminal conduct in this matter will be expelled from AEPi, and we will support prosecution to the fullest extent of the law," the national fraternity said, as reported by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/26/university-of-wisconsin-fraternity-hazing). National spokesperson Jon Pierce added: "Hazing is a betrayal of everything our fraternity stands for. There is no place for it in AEPi. No exceptions. No excuses."

## The Culture Behind the Basement

Hazing researchers say the Madison case fits a broader and troubling pattern. Dr. Elizabeth Allan, founder of StopHazing, told [WMTV](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/09/26/aepi-suspends-madison-chapter-expert-explains-pressure-behind-fraternity-hazing/) that the dynamics enabling hazing go beyond individual acts. "We see the most lethal hazing happening in fraternities," she said, adding that more than 100 deaths since 2000 have been primarily connected to fraternity hazing. The advocacy site HazingInfo.org tallies almost 130 hazing-related deaths in the U.S. since 2000, an average of about five per year.

Allan pointed to a coercive mix of group pressure and the intense desire to belong. "When you have that group context, you have peer pressure," she said. "You combine that with the strong desire to belong, and you have the ingredients for a more coercive situation." She argued that prevention must be treated as a public health issue rather than a one-time training, because hazing is "so embedded in the culture of many groups and just in society."

Unrecognized chapters deepen the problem, Allan said, because they can operate independently while their members remain enrolled students the university is responsible for. She also warned that students and families often fail to distinguish a university-recognized organization from one operating on its own, and urged schools to communicate clearly which groups are recognized and which are not.

## What's Next

The Madison chapter remains under investigation on multiple tracks — by city police, by the district attorney, and by the university's conduct office. Whether the DA files formal charges against Klein and Vane will determine the legal course of the case, while the national fraternity's internal inquiry could result in expulsions.

Anti-hazing advocates say the episode is a reminder of how much can go wrong when oversight is absent. The case echoed a separate investigation this week at the University of Arizona, where a fraternity is accused of forcing members to swallow live goldfish — a sign, advocates say, that the problem is national in scope, not isolated to one Wisconsin basement.

For now, UW-Madison said it is "fully committed to preventing and addressing hazing." What happened in the basement on Langdon Street will test how far that commitment reaches — and whether an unaffiliated chapter can be held to account.
