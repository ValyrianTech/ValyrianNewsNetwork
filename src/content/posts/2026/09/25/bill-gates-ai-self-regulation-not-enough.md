# Bill Gates Says AI Self-Regulation Isn't Enough and Urges Government Oversight

Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates called for U.S. lawmakers to regulate the development of artificial intelligence, saying that industry self-regulation is insufficient and that governments must step in to monitor the technology. In an interview with NBC News' "Meet the Press," Gates warned that AI is powerful enough to enable events that could kill a billion people, while cautioning that the threat extends beyond any distant apocalypse.

"No one thinks self-regulation is enough," Gates told moderator Kristen Welker, in an interview set to air in full on Sunday, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/politics-news/bill-gates-ai-companies-self-regulating-governments-monitoring-rcna599619). Asked whether legislation is needed in Washington, Gates replied, "Absolutely."

Gates' intervention lands in the middle of an intensifying debate over how—and whether—to govern the world's most powerful AI systems, one that has divided tech leaders, lawmakers, and the two major political parties.

## A Call for Binding Rules

Gates was specific about what he wants, framing regulation as a modest burden rather than a brake on innovation. "You need law enforcement and the politicians to get into the discussion about what safeguards and monitoring look like," he said. "And that has to be a required thing. And it will be a little bit of overhead for the industry, but not a dramatic slowing of what they're doing."

Even as he played down the cost, Gates did not minimize the stakes. He said AI was "certainly powerful enough to drive events that, you know, cause a billion deaths," adding that "there's never been a weapon as powerful as the combination of people with ill intent using the latest AI tools."

Notably, Gates urged policymakers to focus on near-term misuse by bad actors rather than only on the longer-term risk that AI could wipe out humanity in several years—a risk he declined to dismiss but chose not to emphasize.

## A Month of Alarms

Gates is the latest prominent figure to weigh in amid a wave of safety warnings from researchers inside the leading AI labs. Earlier in September, the CEOs of Anthropic and OpenAI agreed publicly that the race to build increasingly powerful AI systems should slow down. Anthropic's Dario Amodei published an essay calling for the industry to "pace the frontier," and OpenAI's Sam Altman endorsed the idea hours later, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-ai-development-rcna597383).

Those statements followed the resignation of Jacob Coxon, a safety researcher who left Anthropic and declared that "neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."

Amodei has since laid out a three-part plan—covered in his essay ["We Must Pace the Frontier"](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier)—that would embed independent evaluators inside AI companies, coordinate standards among democratic nations, and eventually pursue cooperation with China. "The most effective method of pacing is via regulation that targets all U.S. frontier AI companies," Amodei wrote, "as that covers even those who are unwilling to cooperate voluntarily."

## The Industry Is Split

Gates' position puts him at odds with other powerful figures in technology. Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg rejected calls for coordinated action, telling NBC News that each company should police itself. "I don't think that we need some kind of industrywide coordination," Zuckerberg said. "I think that each lab needs to take the time, and when it sees that there are issues, you just take the time that you need internally to basically make sure that you're proceeding safely."

Microsoft President Brad Smith, meanwhile, sided closer to Gates—whose company he once helped lead—by criticizing AI companies for racing against one another. Speaking at a United Nations session, Smith compared the dynamic to airplane manufacturers competing on speed. "Would you say, 'I need to win this race'? Or, would you say, 'I'm going to put the safety of my passengers and the safety of the public ahead of my winning that race'?" he asked. "That is the fundamental question."

The split extends to Washington. House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., declined to bring lawmakers back to pass AI legislation, saying he would defer to the companies and that Congress "can't rush in." Senate and House Democrats, by contrast, have pressed for a regulatory framework.

## Where the Action Is

With federal legislation stalled, the most concrete movement is happening at the state level. California Gov. Gavin Newsom [signed an executive order](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/elections/california-gavin-newsom-ai-order-safety-regulations-kill-switch-rcna598570) creating a board of experts tasked with strengthening the state's AI safety laws—including possibly requiring an emergency "kill switch" to shut down a system in the event of a major problem. Maryland Gov. Wes Moore and New York Gov. Kathy Hochul have released their own AI regulation plans, and governors in Virginia, Nevada, and Pennsylvania have taken related steps.

Newsom framed the state action as a response to federal inaction. "The federal government's abject failure to create any form of meaningful AI oversight or accountability should alarm every American, especially when AI CEOs themselves are begging for regulation," he said.

President Donald Trump has taken a different view, calling recent AI safety warnings a "hoax" and emphasizing competition with China as the priority. "Whoever wins AI wins," he has said.

## Why It Matters

The divide is not simply about risk tolerance; it reflects a genuine strategic dilemma. Amodei and others argue that slowing the frontier too much could hand an advantage to authoritarian governments—specifically China—while slowing too little could outpace the ability to make the systems safe. That tension runs through nearly every proposal now on the table.

Gates' entry carries particular weight because of his unusual position: a technologist and philanthropist who helped build one of the world's largest software companies and has since devoted himself to global public health. His call for mandatory oversight gives advocates a mainstream, business-friendly voice at a moment when the industry's own leaders are publicly divided.

Public opinion may be moving in the same direction as the regulators. According to polling cited in the coverage, roughly half of respondents in the U.S., Canada, and several European countries support slowing the pace of AI development.

## What's Next

The full Gates interview is scheduled to air Sunday, Sept. 27, and could sharpen a debate that has so far produced more warnings than rules. Attention will turn to whether Congress acts on any of the proposals circulating—independent audits, embedded evaluators, capability "checkpoints"—or whether, as seems increasingly likely, states continue to fill the vacuum one executive order at a time.

For now, the strongest signal may be the simplest: a figure as prominent, and as commercially invested in technology's promise, as Bill Gates is now telling governments that the industry cannot be trusted to govern itself.
