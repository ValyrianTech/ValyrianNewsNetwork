# Dutroux Podcast: 30 Years Since Arrest That Changed Belgium

Thirty years after Marc Dutroux's arrest triggered one of the most traumatic episodes in modern Belgian history, VRT NWS has launched a four-part podcast series that reconstructs the summer of 1996 — a period that profoundly reshaped the country's justice system and public consciousness. The special, titled "1996: De Zomer van Dutroux" (1996: The Summer of Dutroux), was released in full on August 5 on VRT MAX as part of the broadcaster's "Onder ons: crimi" podcast series, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/05/podcast-vrt-30-jaar-zaak-marc-dutroux/).

Hosted by justice journalists Philip Heymans and Caroline Van den Berghe, the series revisits the events leading up to Dutroux's arrest on August 13, 1996, when police noticed his van at the location where 14-year-old Laetitia Delhez had disappeared. Inside his home in Marcinelle, investigators discovered a hidden cellar hatch containing not one but two girls — Laetitia and 12-year-old Sabine Dardenne, both alive.

"It is THE moment when the case erupts: the moment Dutroux is arrested," Heymans said on Radio 1's De Ochtend program. "And that turned out to be just the beginning. It later emerged that he had kidnapped 6 girls, 4 of whom did not survive."

## A Nation's Trauma

The arrest exposed a far darker reality. Over the preceding year, Dutroux had systematically kidnapped six girls: Julie Lejeune (8) and Melissa Russo (8), who disappeared in June 1995; An Marchal (17) and Eefje Lambrecks (19), who vanished in August 1995; and Sabine and Laetitia, taken in 1996. Four of the six — Julie, Melissa, An, and Eefje — did not survive. Dutroux also murdered his accomplice Bernard Weinstein.

The discovery of the girls' bodies in the following weeks sent shockwaves through Belgium. As [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2021/08/12/overzicht-dutroux/) documented on the 25th anniversary, the summer of 1996 changed the country forever — children were no longer allowed to play outside alone, white vans became objects of suspicion, and public trust in police and justice plummeted to unprecedented depths.

Heymans, who was 18 when the case broke, recalls how the collective mindset shifted almost overnight. "People's mindsets changed so much," he said. "People suddenly became much more worried, more anxious about what might happen to their children." He described becoming a scout leader shortly after the case and feeling the need to be extraordinarily cautious even about innocent activities. "For things that were actually innocent, you started to become a bit afraid."

## Systemic Failures Exposed

The podcast also examines how the case laid bare deep flaws in Belgium's police and judicial systems. Parents of missing children were dismissed by authorities who told them their children would come home. There was barely any communication with victims or the press, and almost no professional support existed.

"The way victims were treated, for example," Heymans said. "Parents who had to rely on volunteers because the police essentially laughed them off, saying their children would come home."

Van den Berghe, who covered the case from the front row, noted that the anger over these failures is still felt today. "There was barely any communication with victims or the press," she said. "There was barely any professional support and suddenly everyone became very angry about that. That impact is still felt today. The Dutroux case changed a lot, but a lot has also improved."

The public fury culminated in the White March (Witte Mars) on October 20, 1996, when approximately 300,000 people marched through Brussels — the largest demonstration in Belgian history — demanding justice reform.

## A Legacy of Reform

The Dutroux case triggered a wave of institutional reforms that continue to shape Belgian justice today. As detailed in a comprehensive [VRT NWS analysis](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2021/08/10/dingen-dankzij-dutroux/), these include the creation of the Cell for Missing Persons, which has investigated nearly 30,000 cases with a 97% resolution rate; Child Focus, founded in 1998 at the initiative of Jean-Denis Lejeune, father of victim Julie; and the Wet-Franchimont (1998), which granted victims greater rights in criminal proceedings.

The case also led to the Octopus Agreement, which merged Belgium's three separate police forces into an integrated two-level structure; the establishment of the Federal Prosecutor's Office in 2002; and the creation of the High Council of Justice to end political appointments of magistrates. Sentencing courts were established to make conditional release decisions more transparent and remove political influence from the process.

Alain Remue, head commissioner of the Cell for Missing Persons who was involved in the original search, reflected on the case's legacy in an interview marking the 30th anniversary of An and Eefje's disappearance. "We were dragged from one emotion to another," he told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/08/20/30-jaar-na-de-verdwijning-van-an-en-eefje-is-de-politie-gewapen/). "First there was euphoria because we suddenly got 2 girls back alive. We weren't prepared for that. Then that euphoria turned into horror and bewilderment when we found little Julie and Melissa."

Remue acknowledged that even with today's advanced technology — surveillance cameras, mobile phones, social media — a similar tragedy could not be entirely prevented. "You can't prevent a disturbed perpetrator from getting it into his head to kidnap children," he said. "Every so often you have such a madman. And unfortunately, we will see that again."

## The Case Today

Dutroux, now 69, remains in prison under a special security regime — one of the few detainees in Belgium for whom life imprisonment truly means life. In February 2026, it was reported that images of child sexual abuse were found in his cell during a 2024 search of Nivelles prison, and the public prosecutor's office wants to prosecute him for possession of these images, as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/02/24/dutroux-beelden-seksueel-misbruik-gevonden/) reported.

Van den Berghe cautioned against taking Dutroux's explanations at face value. "Dutroux is a pathological liar, a manipulator," she said. "He is one of the few detainees in our country for whom life imprisonment truly means life."

## The Podcast Series

The four-part special, also covered by [TVvisie](https://tvvisie.be/streaming/5augustus2026/vrt-nws-podcast-onder-ons-crimi-1996-de-zomer-van-dutroux-nu-op-vrt-max_141722/), traces the full arc of the case: from the disappearances of the six girls and the failures that allowed them to happen, to the gruesome discoveries and the White March, and finally to Dutroux's dramatic escape in 1998 and the "trial of the century" in 2004, which ended with a life sentence.

The series also addresses the question that has haunted the case for decades: did Dutroux act alone, or was there more behind it? Through personal recollections and archival fragments, Heymans and Van den Berghe aim to reconstruct the case with the knowledge available at the time — the disappearances, the uncertainty, and everything that followed.

As the podcast's [official dossier](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/dossiers/2021/08/zaak-dutroux/) makes clear, the case remains one of the most extensively documented in Belgian history, with its impact still reverberating three decades later. The full series is now available on VRT MAX.

## What to Watch For

As Belgium marks this somber anniversary, the renewed attention on the Dutroux case comes at a moment when questions about justice system effectiveness remain highly relevant. The ongoing prosecution over the images found in Dutroux's cell, combined with the podcast's exploration of how systemic failures allowed such crimes to go undetected, may reignite public debate about whether the reforms enacted after 1996 have gone far enough.

For a new generation of Belgians who did not live through the summer of 1996, the podcast offers a window into a period that fundamentally reshaped their country — a reminder of how one case can expose the fragility of institutions and catalyze profound change.
