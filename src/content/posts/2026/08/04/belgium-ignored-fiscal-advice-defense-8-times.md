# Belgium's De Wever Government Overruled Financial Watchdog on Defense Spending Eight Times

The Belgian government led by Prime Minister Bart De Wever has approved defense procurement dossiers on eight separate occasions despite receiving negative advice from the country's Inspectorate of Finance, according to an investigation by the Belgian news magazine [Knack](https://www.knack.be/nieuws/belgie/legeraankopen-regering-de-wever-negeerde-8-keer-negatief-advies-van-inspectie-financien/). The findings, published on August 4, 2026, raise significant questions about fiscal oversight and accountability in one of Europe's most ambitious military modernization programs.

The Inspectorate of Finance issued 41 negative advisories on defense procurement dossiers during the current legislative term. In eight of those cases, the government proceeded with approval anyway, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/04/onderzoek-knack-adviezen-defensie/).

## The Hangar at the Heart of the Controversy

The most prominent case involves a 6,000-square-meter hangar at Brussels Airport owned by Sabena Engineering. The facility was intended for maintenance of the Belgian military's A400M transport aircraft, which previously required trips to Airbus facilities near Madrid, Spain. The monthly rent of €217,900 was deemed excessive by the Inspectorate, and Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem (CD&V) also issued an unfavorable opinion.

Defense Minister Theo Francken (N-VA) initially removed the dossier from the ministerial council agenda in late 2025. A revised proposal received another negative assessment, yet the council approved the contract on March 6, 2026, anyway. Defense now pays the monthly rent despite the warnings.

"Whoever takes strategic autonomy seriously takes that heavy maintenance in their own hands," Francken said in defense of the decision.

But retired Colonel Roger Housen, a defense expert, dismissed the justification. "Taking that maintenance in-house to be strategically independent is a bullshit argument," Housen told Knack. "Why would you then cooperate with a private company?" He suggested Belgium could instead work with European partners like Germany or France, which operate extensive A400M fleets under the same standards.

## A Pattern of Overruled Advice

The hangar rental is far from an isolated case. The investigation identified several other major defense dossiers where negative advice was overruled, including:

- Approximately €50 million for anti-drone systems, previously reported by the Pano investigative program in [April 2026](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/04/14/counterdroneplan-francken-pano/)
- More than €3 billion for protecting maritime infrastructure, including wind farms and ports
- Fifteen light utility helicopters to replace the aging Agusta A109 fleet
- Eighteen Pilatus aircraft with simulators and infrastructure in Bevekom for pilot training from 2028
- A support package of nearly €1 billion to Ukraine in 2026
- Belgian Defense participation in the European Space Agency

Notably, neither the hangar dossier nor the pilot training facility dossier was submitted to the Parliamentary Committee on Army Purchases, raising concerns about parliamentary oversight of major expenditures.

## The Role of the Inspectorate of Finance

The Inspectorate of Finance is a federal oversight body that reviews government procurement to ensure budgetary soundness, compliance with public procurement regulations, and mitigation of fraud, corruption, and conflicts of interest risks. Its advisories, however, are not binding—the government can legally disregard them.

"Theo Francken doesn't just get an envelope with money that he can start handing out," said Kristof Clerix, the investigative journalist behind the Knack report, speaking on Radio 1's De Ochtend. "The Inspectorate of Finance can reduce the risk of fraud, corruption and conflicts of interest."

A spokesperson for Francken defended the government's approach. "Fortunately we live in a democracy where decisions are made by people who are elected by the people," the spokesperson told Knack. "If the government decides that a dossier is in order, then it is in order. Otherwise the Inspectorate of Finance would rule the country."

Clerix acknowledged the government's legal authority to override the advice but stressed the importance of transparency. "The government has absolutely the freedom to make decisions, because it is elected by the people," he said. "But I think the press may then also write in which dossiers the government apparently doesn't find it necessary to follow advice."

## A Systemic Governance Concern

This is not the first time the pattern has been exposed. An earlier Knack investigation in [August 2025](https://www.knack.be/nieuws/belgie/politiek/controle-op-legeraankopen-inspectie-financien-gaf-47-keer-negatief-advies/) found that the Inspectorate had issued 47 explicitly negative advisories to Defense over 2.5 years, with 46 being ignored by the ministerial council.

The recurring theme has drawn comparisons to the 1988 Agusta scandal, when corruption in the purchase of 46 Agusta A109 helicopters led to convictions of several Belgian politicians. The current replacement program for those same helicopters adds particular sensitivity to the issue.

The De Wever government has made defense a top priority, agreeing to a strategic defense vision for 2026-2034 with [€34 billion in investments](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/06/24/defensieplan-strategische-visie-francken-f35/), the largest defense investment in four decades. The government committed to reaching NATO's 2% GDP target by 2033 and 2.5% by 2034.

## What to Watch For

The investigation comes amid broader scrutiny of fiscal discipline across Belgian governance. Flanders Mobility Minister Annick De Ridder (N-VA) faces similar questions over ignoring Inspectorate advice on regional airport deals, suggesting the pattern extends beyond defense.

As Belgium embarks on its most significant military modernization in decades, the tension between executive authority and expert fiscal oversight will remain a central governance challenge. The question is whether the scale of defense spending—and the stakes involved—demands stronger accountability mechanisms, or whether elected officials should retain the final word on how taxpayer money is spent.

For now, the government maintains its position: the advice is advisory, and the elected government decides. Critics argue that when the advice is ignored as often as it is followed, the system of checks and balances that the Inspectorate was designed to provide may be quietly eroding.
