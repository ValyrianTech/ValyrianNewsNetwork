# Kaylee Goncalves' Youngest Sister Breaks Silence on Idaho Murders

Aubrie Goncalves, the youngest sister of Kaylee Goncalves, has spoken publicly for the first time about losing her sibling in the Idaho college murders, breaking her silence nearly four years after the tragedy that shocked the nation. The 20-year-old appeared on the TODAY show on August 4 alongside her parents, Steve and Kristi, explaining why she decided to speak out now.

"When everything happened, I was a sophomore in high school," Aubrie said, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/kaylee-goncalves-youngest-sister-aubrie-speaks-first-time-words-are-s-rcna590744). "I was only 16 years old, and at the time I kind of just decided no words are going to bring back either one of my sisters, so I didn't feel like saying anything."

Her decision to come forward comes at a pivotal moment in the case. Just days before her interview, Bryan Kohberger — the man who pleaded guilty to killing Kaylee Goncalves, Madison Mogen, Xana Kernodle, and Ethan Chapin — filed a handwritten petition for post-conviction relief in Ada County District Court, seeking to withdraw his guilty plea and undergo a trial.

## A Family's Grief Reopened

Aubrie, who was 16 when her 21-year-old sister was killed in November 2022, explained that she initially chose silence because no words could undo the loss. But as time passed, she realized her voice could serve a different purpose.

"And now that I've gotten older, almost four years has passed, I realized still that no words are going to bring back my sisters, but words are going to keep their memories alive," she said. "I'm not really one to put myself in a situation like this, I don't really like to, but I know that I have to keep Maddie and Kaylee's name alive, and that's why I'm here today."

The interview followed a [Facebook statement](https://www.facebook.com/GoncalvesFamilyTips/posts/a-word-from-our-youngest-aubrie1352-daysthats-how-long-its-been-since-ethan-chap/1034638516202811/) Aubrie released on July 27, the same day Kohberger filed his petition. In it, she wrote: "I wasn't planning on saying anything today. But after seeing that the man who admitted in court to murdering four innocent people now wants to withdraw his guilty plea, I couldn't stay silent."

"For him, this may be another legal filing. For me, it's another day without my sister," she continued.

## Kohberger's Bid to Withdraw His Plea

Kohberger, 31, pleaded guilty in July 2025 to killing the four University of Idaho students at an off-campus home in Moscow, Idaho. As part of the plea deal, he received four consecutive life sentences without parole plus 10 years for burglary, avoiding the death penalty and waiving his right to appeal.

Now, in his [petition filed July 27](https://www.today.com/news/bryan-kohberger-challenging-guilty-plea-conviction-rcna589505), Kohberger claims he was "convinced to falsely confess" and that his lawyers told him to give a "false plea." He alleges ineffective assistance of counsel, saying his attorney grossly misrepresented the conditions he would face on death row and made false promises about what his life in prison would look like.

"My actual innocence is my truth, and the plea, hinged on by false promises and blatant disinformation, MUST be withdrawn," Kohberger told [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/27/us/bryan-kohberger-idaho-college-deaths.html) on July 26.

On August 3, Idaho attorney Gregory Rauch was assigned to represent Kohberger in his bid for post-conviction relief, according to [USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/crime/2026/08/04/bryan-kohberger-new-attorney-withdraw-guilty-plea/91162933007/).

## 'This Will Never Be Over'

The Goncalves family has been unwavering in their response. In a joint statement released July 27, they called Kohberger "a guilty man once again claiming he is innocent," and expressed frustration that the legal system allows a convicted murderer to continue to drag the case back into the spotlight.

Alivea Goncalves, the oldest of the five Goncalves siblings, also spoke on TODAY on August 4, delivering a stark message about the family's ongoing ordeal.

"The fact that someone locked behind bars still has the ability to revictimize their victims, that's something that isn't just very easy to get over," Alivea said. "This will never be over for us until the defendant is no longer breathing on this earth."

In a separate [interview with Vanity Fair](https://www.vanityfair.com/story/alivea-goncalves-interview-idaho-college-murders-documentary-netflix), Alivea described reading Kohberger's filing as both a "gut punch" and almost laughable. "He's arguing ineffective counsel, which could not be farther from the truth," she said. "He was asked during his plea hearing if he had adequate defense and he said yes."

Idaho Attorney General Raúl Labrador has pledged his office will "do whatever is necessary to ensure justice is fully carried out," as [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/bryan-kohberger-challenge-conviction-university-idaho-murders-court/story?id=135126516) reported.

## Remembering Kaylee

Amid the legal maneuvering, Aubrie Goncalves emphasized that the focus should remain on the lives that were lost. She shared warm memories of her big sister, painting a portrait of a vibrant young woman whose future was cut short.

"I think you could see how Kaylee was as a person just by looking at pictures of her," Aubrie said. "She was like the bubbliest human ever. She was always down to have a fun time, she was always looking at the positives, she was one of the hardest workers I've ever met."

"And to this day that is a huge thing that provides me with a constant need to keep going because I know that Kaylee would be doing the same thing had she still been here," she added. "I really am just trying to do half as much as I know that she would have."

Kaylee Goncalves, 21, was a senior at the University of Idaho majoring in general studies. She had accepted a job in Texas after graduation and had recently purchased a new vehicle. Family members said she was excited about beginning the next chapter of her life.

## The Netflix Documentary and Public Attention

The family's story has been thrust back into the public eye with the release of the three-part Netflix documentary "The Idaho Murders: College Nightmare," which premiered on July 29. The docuseries takes a "victim forward" approach, featuring interviews with the families and friends of the victims, people who knew Kohberger, and law enforcement officials.

As [Time magazine](https://time.com/article/2026/07/28/netflix-documentary-idaho-college-murders/) reported, the series traces how police found Kohberger's DNA on a knife sheath left at the crime scene, identified his getaway car, and determined his cell phone went off-grid around the time of the murders. The motive for the killings has never been officially established.

Aubrie Goncalves was direct about the tension between public fascination and personal grief. "Why should the person convicted of taking their voices continue to have one?" she wrote in her Facebook statement. "This isn't a Netflix documentary. This isn't a conspiracy theory. This isn't entertainment. This is my life. These were real people."

## What's Next

Kohberger's petition faces significant legal hurdles. He had 42 days from the date of his sentencing to file an appeal, and the deadline for post-conviction relief is the first week of September 2026. Legal experts have noted that his ineffective assistance of counsel claims face an uphill battle, particularly given that he affirmed the adequacy of his defense during his plea hearing.

For the Goncalves family, the fight continues. Steve Goncalves, Kaylee's father, reiterated on TODAY that he believes the evidence was strong enough for a death-penalty trial and expressed frustration that Idaho did not pursue it.

"That's never changed. I've been consistent through and through," he said. "The evidence was there. I want to honor all the work that the agents did. I think it should've stood."

As the legal process unfolds, Aubrie Goncalves' decision to speak publicly marks a significant moment for a family that has endured unimaginable loss. Her words carry a simple but powerful message: the victims were real people, and their memories deserve to be honored above all else.

"She had a sad death, but she is not a sad thing," Alivea Goncalves said in her Vanity Fair interview. It's a sentiment that captures the family's determination to ensure that Kaylee, Madison, Xana, and Ethan are remembered not as statistics in a true-crime story, but as the vibrant young people they were.
