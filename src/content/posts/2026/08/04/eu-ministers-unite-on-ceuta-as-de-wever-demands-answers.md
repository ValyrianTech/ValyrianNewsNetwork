# EU Ministers Unite on Ceuta as De Wever Demands Answers

European interior and migration ministers from all 27 EU member states held an emergency video conference on Tuesday to address the Ceuta migration crisis, expressing "full solidarity with Spain" while Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever called for a thorough investigation into what he described as the need to "get to the bottom of what happened."

The meeting, convened by Ireland in its capacity as holder of the rotating EU presidency, came after tens of thousands of migrants crossed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta last week in one of the largest mass border crossings in recent European history. Spanish authorities reported that approximately 72,000 migrants entered the territory between Wednesday and Friday, with 70,000 having since returned to Morocco. At least 75 people died during the crossing, according to Spanish officials, though Morocco's interior ministry has reported a sharply conflicting figure of just 11 deaths.

## A Crisis That Shook Europe

Ceuta, a Spanish autonomous city of roughly 84,000 people on the African mainland, was overwhelmed as migrants swam around border barriers or pushed through wire fencing. Ceuta's top official, Juan Jesus Vivas, estimated that about 60,000 people entered the territory—roughly 70 percent of its population—in what he called an "atrocity" perpetrated by Morocco. Spanish authorities said 862 unaccompanied minors were being housed in the enclave, with an estimated 3,000 to 5,000 migrants remaining.

The crisis has exposed deep divisions within the European Union over migration policy. Italy suspended its Schengen open-border arrangements with Spain for one month, while France announced a five-fold increase in police at its border with Spain and Portugal said it would beef up controls at its southern borders. Denmark and Finland called for Spain to be suspended from the Schengen area altogether.

According to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/2/why-has-the-eu-called-an-emergency-meeting-on-spain-migrants-surge), the mass crossing was preceded by a Spanish Supreme Court ruling on July 8 that migrants intercepted at sea cannot be summarily returned. Misinformation about the ruling spread rapidly on social media, with rumors that "Spain was opening its borders." Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez has said the crisis was the result of rumors spread by people smugglers after a misinterpretation of the ruling.

## De Wever Calls for Investigation

Speaking at the start of Tuesday's EU meeting, De Wever said: "We must get to the bottom of what happened." The Belgian prime minister also warned that "Europe must close its borders," adding: "This kind of imagery is harmful to the population. It seems like there's an open door there and that anyone who wants to put pressure on us can use migration as a weapon."

Belgium was represented at the meeting by Minister of Asylum and Migration Anneleen Van Bossuyt (N-VA), who described the discussions as "very constructive." Van Bossuyt told reporters that "no one has called for suspending Schengen. Everyone has expressed their solidarity with Spain. Italy has done so too." She added: "We agree that there is a need for stronger external borders, that we must further combat illegal migration, and that we must break the business model of smugglers."

Van Bossuyt was characteristically blunt about her position on the Spanish government's approach: "Our migration policy is far from the Spanish government's policy. Such mass regularizations have too great a pull effect." She also addressed critics of her stance directly: "I hear some people say: do you want a Fortress Europe? Well, I'd rather have a reinforced fortress than one whose gates are leaking."

## EU Solidarity, But No Concrete Measures

The three-hour meeting produced expressions of solidarity but no concrete policy measures, with ministers describing it primarily as an "exchange of views." EU Migration Commissioner Magnus Brunner acknowledged that the Ceuta incident was a "test" of the EU's resilience and external border security, which he said the bloc "passed." He confirmed that ministers expressed "full solidarity with Spain" and said the crisis was fueled by criminal smuggling networks and disinformation on social media.

Spanish Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska said all countries were "constructive" and acknowledged Spain's immediate response. He emphasized that the EU's Schengen area "was in no way compromised" by the crisis, with "no secondary movement, nor even the possibility of any." He also rejected suggestions that Spain was specifically warned about a potential surge at the Ceuta border.

As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/live/2026/aug/04/europe-drought-extreme-heat-wildfires-climate-crisis-hungary-romania-ceuta-spain-migration-eu-latest-news-updates) reported, Irish Home Affairs Minister Jim O'Callaghan said: "The EU is united and stands ready to support Spain and protect our external borders from illegal migration. We commend Spain for their swift response to the situation in Ceuta and extend our deepest condolences for the tragic loss of life."

## Diplomatic Tensions and the Morocco Factor

The crisis has also highlighted complex diplomatic tensions between Spain and Morocco. Morocco claims sovereignty over Ceuta and Melilla, the two remaining European-held territories on the African mainland, and has previously loosened border controls as a political lever. According to [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/ceuta-eu-calls-borders-a-shared-european-responsibility/a-78220701), Sanchez's recent visit to Algiers—the first by a Spanish head of government in four years—may have angered Morocco, which has a rivalry with Algeria over Western Sahara.

Migration researcher Matteo Villa told [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/jul/31/ceuta-spain-eu-border-africa) that "50,000 people do not come overnight when usually it's hundreds per month. Sanchez going to Algeria is something Morocco didn't really like." Villa suggested Morocco may have loosened the border as a way of showing it has "the upper hand."

Morocco, for its part, has denied any failure of its security services and claims it warned Spain in advance about the risks of the Supreme Court ruling. A senior Moroccan official told AFP that the cost of managing migration flows costs the country some 500 million euros per year.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen has called for "united action" on border security, writing to Sanchez that "the protection of all our external borders is a shared European responsibility." She also offered enhanced technical and financial support to Morocco for border management, describing the kingdom as "an important strategic partner" in combating migrant smuggling.

## Human Cost Behind the Headlines

Behind the political maneuvering lies a profound human tragedy. Faten Ben Omar El Azizi, a 20-year-old talented footballer from Tetouan, Morocco, drowned during the crossing. Her club, Moghreb Atletico Tetouan, said: "Her dream was destroyed before it could begin. She sought neither fame nor adventure, but a better life and a future she thought awaited her on the other side of the border." Her mother told Alyaoum24: "She said always: 'I have no future here.' She worked in cemeteries to make ends meet. She was tired of it all."

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/03/jonge-marokkaanse-voetbalster-een-van-de-vele-dodelijke-slachtof/), the Moroccan Association for Human Rights called for an independent inquiry and described the situation as "a deep social crisis," noting that "so many people are willing to risk their lives to leave the country shows how great the despair is."

## What's Next for Europe

Italy used the meeting to push for EU-funded migrant processing centres and return hubs in third countries, a proposal modelled on its controversial deal with Albania. Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi told ministers that "the time has come to put into practice new hub models for the external processing of asylum procedures and for returns in safe third countries." EU Commissioner Brunner said the idea was mentioned by several countries, with five or six separate proposals on the table.

Belgian PM De Wever, meanwhile, defended Sanchez's right to pursue his own migration policy, saying: "PM Sanchez may be a bit of an outlier in that regard, but he has that right: he was democratically elected."

The next significant moment will be the European Council summit in October, where ministers expect more concrete proposals to be placed on the table. As Van Bossuyt noted, "this meeting was primarily an exchange of views. It was not the intention to come to very concrete recommendations. The next important moment will be the European summit in October."

As [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/08/03/we-demand-solidarity-spain-says-ahead-of-eu-video-call-to-discuss-response-to-uncontrolled) reported, Spanish Foreign Minister Jose Manuel Albares insisted that "the integrity of the Schengen area is absolutely guaranteed" and called for an end to the "deliberate confusion" about how the free-movement area works. He also warned that those who refuse to offer solidarity to Spain "are working against Europe."

Migration expert Gerald Knaus of the European Stability Initiative told DW that the political reaction across Europe demonstrated how quickly border events can create fear: "What we see is that politically, even two days of an event in North Africa create fear in Finland and Denmark and Germany." He predicted that "the most likely outcome is that there will be some grandstanding—that different governments will accuse each other. Spain will respond and will say it is not to blame and there will be bad feelings and no solution."

For now, the immediate crisis appears contained. But as the death toll continues to rise and the political fallout reverberates across the continent, the questions De Wever raised at the start of Tuesday's meeting remain unanswered: What exactly happened at Ceuta's border, and how can Europe prevent it from happening again?
