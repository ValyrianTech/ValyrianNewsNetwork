# Michigan Senate Primary Tests Democrats' Future Direction

Michigan voters headed to the polls Tuesday in a Democratic Senate primary that has become the clearest test yet of whether the party's progressive wing or its establishment leadership will set the direction heading into the 2026 midterms. The race pits progressive epidemiologist Abdul El-Sayed against four-term U.S. Rep. Haley Stevens, with the winner facing Republican Mike Rogers in November in a contest critical to Democrats' hopes of reclaiming the Senate majority.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/live/election-primary-08-04-2026), the race has drawn national attention as one of the most significant contests of the primary cycle. Democrats currently hold 47 of 100 Senate seats, and the open Michigan seat left by retiring Sen. Gary Peters is considered essential to any path back to the majority.

## Two Visions for the Party

El-Sayed, a 41-year-old former Wayne County health director who ran for governor in 2018, has built his campaign around Medicare for All, campaign finance reform, and ending military aid to Israel. He has been endorsed by Sen. Bernie Sanders, Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, and the United Auto Workers, and has positioned himself as an insurgent challenging the party's institutional machinery.

Stevens, a 43-year-old congresswoman from Birmingham who served as chief of staff to President Obama's auto rescue task force, has campaigned on manufacturing, economic experience, and electability. She has the backing of Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer, Gov. Gretchen Whitmer, and retiring Sen. Gary Peters, along with the Jewish Democratic Council of America.

As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/04/michigan-senate-race-abdul-el-sayed-haley-stevens) reported, the race reflects a broader national debate over whether Democrats should embrace more aggressive progressive policies or maintain a more moderate, electability-focused approach. Progressive candidates have surged in Democratic primaries in solidly blue areas across the country in recent months, including in New York and Denver.

## Record Outside Spending and the AIPAC Factor

The race has become the largest test of AIPAC's influence in a single election. According to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/2/how-much-has-aipac-spent-against-michigans-abdul-el-sayed-and-why), AIPAC's affiliated super PAC, United Democracy Project, has spent more than $30 million supporting Stevens—the organization's largest investment in a single race ever. Total outside spending backing Stevens exceeds $54 million, with additional dark money bringing the total to over $60 million.

El-Sayed has outraised Stevens in direct campaign donations, $14.5 million to $11.9 million, but has received only about $3.5 million in independent expenditure support. The spending disparity has become a central theme of his campaign. "Donald Trump is not himself the disease; he's just the first symptom of the disease of our politics," El-Sayed said at a Dearborn rally. "The disease is this system that allows billionaires and corporations and special interests like AIPAC to buy and sell politicians to rig the system against us."

As [Common Dreams](https://www.commondreams.org/news/aipac-2026-midterm-spending) reported, AIPAC has spent at least $104.3 million during the 2026 election cycle, a figure that's likely to grow before the midterms are through.

## A Divided Electorate

The race has exposed deep demographic divisions within Michigan's Democratic coalition. Recent polling shows El-Sayed leading among White voters, college-educated voters, and those under 55, while Stevens leads among Black voters, non-college-educated voters, and those over 55. According to [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/el-sayed-stevens-how-michigan-primary-could-reshape-democrats-12272493), recent surveys have shown El-Sayed with significant leads—a SoCal Strategies poll found him ahead 56-39, an Emerson College survey showed 57-41, and a Tavern Research poll put him up 48-37.

Earlier polling had shown Stevens with a narrow edge. A [Detroit News/WDIV poll](https://www.detroitnews.com/story/news/politics/2026/07/14/michigan-senate-race-poll-democrats-abdul-el-sayed-haley-stevens-edge/90907700007/) conducted July 8-11 found Stevens leading 48-41, though pollster Richard Czuba described the race as "wide open" given the large number of undecided voters.

Michigan's Jewish community has been deeply divided in the race. As [JTA](https://www.jta.org/2026/08/04/politics/lawn-signs-for-abdul-door-knocking-for-haley-michigan-jews-split-in-senate-democratic-primary) reported, some Jewish voters support Stevens due to her strong pro-Israel record and concerns about El-Sayed's rhetoric, while others back El-Sayed through groups like "Jews for Abdul," citing his positions on social justice and their belief that his criticism of Israel is not antisemitic.

## The Campaign's Final Days

Both candidates spent the final days of the campaign pursuing overlapping constituencies in Detroit, where Black voters have long been crucial to winning Democratic primaries. El-Sayed visited Black churches across the city, telling a congregation at Kingdom Covenant Church: "I believe in the kind of America where no matter where you grow up, the color of your skin, how you pray, you deserve equal rights in this country."

Stevens, meanwhile, emphasized her longstanding connections to Detroit-area churches and her work in the Obama administration. "I come here year-round. This isn't just a campaign thing," she said at a church in Pontiac. "Our churches are places of community refuge, community connection."

On election day, El-Sayed danced with a Zumba class at Detroit's Eastern Market, while Stevens cast her ballot at the Baldwin Public Library in Birmingham. "We're getting everybody all excited and we're ready for tomorrow," Stevens said. "I think we're seeing a lot of great turnout."

## What's at Stake

Michigan is one of the nation's closest battleground states. Donald Trump carried it in 2016 and 2024, while Joe Biden won it in 2020. The state has been held by Democrats in the Senate since 2002, and Republicans have not won a Michigan Senate seat since 2001.

As [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/04/nx-s1-5918643/five-states-polls-what-to-know) noted, retaining the open Senate seat is seen as a must-win for Democrats hoping to retake the chamber next year. The winner will face Mike Rogers, a former congressman who narrowly lost the 2024 Senate race to Elissa Slotkin.

The outcome could have implications well beyond Michigan. As [Michigan Public](https://www.michiganpublic.org/politics-government/2026-08-03/el-sayed-and-stevens-close-michigan-senate-primary-offering-different-visions-for-democrats) reported, the party plans to bring its nominees together in the days after the primary for a general election kickoff aimed at presenting a united front heading into November.

Joseph Geevarghese, executive director of Our Revolution, framed the race as a test of whether money can substitute for organizing. "This election is ultimately a test of whether unlimited outside spending can substitute for genuine grassroots organizing," he said. "Regardless of where the money comes from, voters are demonstrating that there are limits to what money alone can buy."

Whoever emerges victorious Tuesday will have less than 100 days to unify a divided party and turn toward the general election—a challenge that will test whether Michigan Democrats can come back together after one of the most bruising primaries in recent memory.
