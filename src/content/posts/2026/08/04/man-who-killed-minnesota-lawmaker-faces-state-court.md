# Man Who Killed Minnesota Lawmaker Faces State Court

Vance Boelter, the man serving two consecutive life sentences plus 40 years after pleading guilty to federal charges for killing a top Minnesota Democratic lawmaker and her husband, made his initial appearance Monday in state court, where he faces a broader set of charges including first-degree murder. The hearing in Hennepin County District Court marked the formal beginning of the state prosecution against Boelter, which had been on hold pending resolution of his federal case, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/hortman-hoffman-minnesota-lawmakers-boelter-court-a72095a6938d87ff3d5a0a2e85c09c4c).

## The Attacks That Shocked Minnesota

Boelter's rampage began in the early hours of June 14, 2025, when he arrived at the Champlin home of state Sen. John Hoffman and his wife, Yvette, impersonating a police officer. He shot John Hoffman nine times and Yvette eight times before moving on to the homes of other Democratic lawmakers. At approximately 3:30 a.m., he arrived at the Brooklyn Park residence of former House Speaker Melissa Hortman, where he shot and killed both Hortman and her husband, Mark, as police officers arrived on scene. The Hortmans' golden retriever, Gilbert, was also shot and later had to be euthanized.

The attacks sparked the largest manhunt in Minnesota history, with heavily armed officers in full combat gear searching suburban streets and country roads before Boelter was arrested near Green Isle on the evening of June 15, 2025, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/minnesota-shooting-lawmakers-timeline-boelter-08189f917904a9e5e79f5df948503a4f).

Federal charging documents described Boelter as acting with "the intent to kill, injure, harass, and intimidate Minnesota legislators." Prosecutors said he aimed to "shift the balance of power" in the state legislature by killing at least four Democratic lawmakers. His SUV contained a list of about 70 potential targets, including abortion providers, pro-abortion rights advocates, and lawmakers across Minnesota, Wisconsin, and other states.

## Federal Sentencing and the State Case

Boelter pleaded guilty to all federal charges in June 2026, nearly a year after the attacks, in exchange for federal prosecutors not seeking the death penalty. On July 23, U.S. District Judge John R. Tunheim sentenced him to two consecutive life sentences without the possibility of parole plus 40 years, calling it "the longest sentence I have imposed after hearing thousands of cases over the years, but I do believe it is deserved," as [reported by the Star Tribune](https://www.startribune.com/vance-boelter-sentenced-to-life-in-prison-as-victims-of-his-political-attack-recount-their-anguish/601870402).

At the sentencing hearing, victims and family members delivered harrowing impact statements. Sen. John Hoffman told the court, "The defendant looked me in the eyes and pulled the trigger. The defendant looked at my wife and chose to do the same. My wife and I now live with injuries. We live with our bodies remembering the gunfire." Colin Hortman, the son of Melissa and Mark Hortman, said, "My days are filled with gray, and the blood that was spilled is the last thing I see before I go to sleep and the first thing I see when I wake."

Now, in state court, Boelter faces charges that are wider in scope than the federal case: two counts of first-degree murder for killing the Hortmans, two counts of attempted first-degree murder for shooting the Hoffmans, one count of attempted first-degree murder for shooting at their daughter Hope, one count of attempted first-degree murder for banging at the door of Rep. Kristin Bahner's home, one count of felony cruelty to an animal for shooting Gilbert, and one felony count of impersonating an officer. If convicted, he faces mandatory life in prison without the possibility of parole. Minnesota abolished capital punishment in 1911.

## An Unusual Request in Court

Monday's procedural hearing took an unexpected turn when Boelter addressed Hennepin County Judge Juan Hoyos directly, asking that U.S. Sen. Amy Klobuchar be compelled to testify in his case. Boelter claimed he had met with Minnesota elected officials in Washington, D.C., over an eight-year span, including Klobuchar, as [reported by the Star Tribune](https://www.startribune.com/vance-boelter-state-murder-case-hennepin-county-attorney-mary-moriarty/601874059).

"Does this court have the authority to bring her in to testify?" Boelter asked. "Or is she above the law?"

Klobuchar responded forcefully in an emailed statement: "We have found no record of my ever meeting or speaking with this murderer who will be spending the rest of his life in prison. This is the second time he has falsely brought me into his tormented web and no one should be giving him more attention."

Boelter also told the judge that his rights had been violated during 14 months in federal custody, citing health deterioration including vertigo, blood in his stool, and losing 36 pounds in his first 49 days, as well as alleged religious freedom violations for being denied access to a pastor. Judge Hoyos deferred any legal arguments to future hearings by Boelter's attorney.

## The Dual Prosecution Strategy

The parallel federal and state prosecutions of Boelter mirror the approach used against former Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin, who was convicted of murder in state court and of violating George Floyd's civil rights in federal court. The dual-track strategy ensures that even a federal pardon could not free Boelter, as [noted by the Star Tribune](https://www.startribune.com/hortman-family-blasts-justice-department-for-abandoning-death-penalty-against-vance-boelter/601871510).

The federal case had been relatively rare—a murder case prosecuted by the federal government in Minnesota. The Justice Department initially pursued the death penalty, but the case fell apart following a controversial ruling in the Luigi Mangione case, in which a federal judge ruled that stalking did not meet the legal definition of a crime of violence. That decision led the DOJ to conclude that an interstate stalking conviction does not constitute eligibility for the death penalty, a development that infuriated some members of the Hortman family.

At his federal sentencing, J. Carroll Hortman, Mark Hortman's 86-year-old father, told the court, "He murdered every living being in that house. He murdered my son at the door. Then he killed Gilbert. Then he terrorized my daughter-in-law, Melissa, while she ran for her life. He gunned her down. Fifteen bullets."

## What's Next

Judge Hoyos kept Boelter's bail at $5 million and declined to rule on where he would be held as the case proceeds through Hennepin County District Court. The Hoffmans, who filed a personal injury lawsuit against Boelter in April, released a statement through their attorney after the hearing saying the state "has an obligation just as the federal government to hold him accountable."

Boelter's next state court appearance is set for September 2, 2026. As the state prosecution moves forward, the case continues to resonate far beyond Minnesota, where the killings of Melissa and Mark Hortman sparked a national conversation about the security of elected officials and the escalating dangers of political violence in American public life.
