# What Flemish See in Wallonia: A Nuanced Portrait

When Belgian political leaders talk about the country's language divide, the narrative is often stark: two communities, two cultures, two competing visions of the state. But a summer series by [La Libre Belgique](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/politique-belge/2026/07/31/ce-que-les-flamands-voient-en-nous-G7KS3EHWWBF27L37T6FKMFFWMM/) asking prominent Flemish personalities what they really think of Walloons and Wallonia paints a far more complex picture — one that has resonated deeply with readers.

## A Series That Struck a Chord

Editor-in-chief Dorian de Meeûs, reflecting on the series in a commentary published July 31, acknowledged that the response has exceeded the newspaper's expectations. "The success of this series with our readers exceeds our hopes," he wrote. "Proof, no doubt, that this Flemish gaze matters."

While stereotypes persist — differences in political culture, relationship to work, to the state, and to language cannot be ignored — de Meeûs argues that "the picture that emerges is far more nuanced than what our political leaders too often reflect back to us."

## Voices from Across the Language Border

What emerges from the interviews is a recurring theme: many Flemish people who have chosen to live in Wallonia have discovered a region that defies the clichés.

Essayist [Luc Devoldere](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/societe/2026/07/28/pour-beaucoup-de-flamands-la-wallonie-est-dabord-le-pays-des-camps-scouts-des-rivieres-et-des-forets-2LV4EHVUGBDVJOVZVYJASRXD5A/), a member of the Royal Academy of Dutch Language and Literature, evokes a land of landscapes rather than politics: "For many Flemish people, Wallonia is first and foremost the country of scout camps, valleys, rivers, and forests."

Flemish writer [Anne Provoost](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/societe/2026/07/27/anne-provoost-les-flamands-ne-veulent-pas-perdre-les-ardennes-GLQKUQNVMBF33LIFXKB5DLKTMU/) goes further. Asked what she would miss most if the language border disappeared, she answered without hesitation: Wallonia. "The Flemish don't want to lose the Ardennes," she insists, arguing that tensions can be overcome by multiplying encounters between French-speakers and Dutch-speakers.

Others offer more pointed observations. Professor [Elisabeth Leijnse](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/2023/08/15/on-a-beau-trouver-neuf-wallons-qui-travaillent-assidument-un-seul-faineant-suffira-pour-confirmer-le-prejuge-cest-toxique-J2TK3YW6JZGFPJ56EBRZ6DKNTA/), who has taught Dutch at UNamur for 30 years, describes the toxicity of stereotypes: "You can find nine Walloons who work diligently, but one single lazy person is enough to confirm the prejudice. It's toxic." She also notes that Walloons often apologize for not speaking Dutch well.

Former KU Leuven rector [Rik Torfs](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/2023/08/13/rik-torfs-ici-quand-on-croise-quelquun-dans-la-rue-et-quil-ne-vous-dit-pas-bonjour-il-y-a-fort-a-parier-que-cest-un-flamand-IIOPFB6NL5EUROUG37WDEUVEHI/), who lives in Walloon Brabant, offers a wry cultural observation: "Here, when you meet someone on the street who doesn't say hello, there's a good chance it's a Flemish person."

For painter [Koen Broucke](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/2023/08/18/koen-broucke-ce-flamand-de-wallonie-de-plus-en-plus-de-flamands-decouvrent-les-tresors-wallons-tombent-sous-le-charme-et-achetent-une-maison-EI7YRCPXQRARLK345SHLN36UOU/), the identity question is personal. When someone wrote "Flemish painter" on his Wikipedia page, he had it changed to "Belgian." "I'm bilingual, the Belgian identity suits me," he says.

Cosmologist [Thomas Hertog](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/2023/08/17/tthomas-hertog-ce-flamand-en-wallonie-ma-rencontre-avec-stephen-hawking-a-ete-une-revelation-6POUBDAOANFRBPQ5SU5MMR5PDE/), a collaborator of Stephen Hawking who lives in Bousval, explains why he chose Wallonia: "We didn't want to return to the fold, under a steeple in Flemish country." Sports journalist [Peter Vandenbempt](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/2023/08/16/je-ne-vois-pas-de-raisons-de-retourner-en-flandre-on-sest-toujours-senti-tres-bien-ici-XCNNJQHLS5GJ5LZC3FBWUX7NYI/) echoes the sentiment: "I see no reason to return to Flanders. We've always felt very good here."

## A Shifting Political Landscape

The softening of rhetoric extends even to the political sphere. In April, the youth wing of the Flemish nationalist party N-VA displayed Walloon flags at the Liège-Bastogne-Liège cycling race, with president Jeroen Bergers declaring that "Wallonia is a beautiful country" and calling for a "free, independent, and prosperous Wallonia" — a striking departure from traditional nationalist discourse, as [reported by La Libre Belgique](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/politique-belge/2026/04/27/a-liege-bastogne-liege-les-jeunes-n-va-sortent-les-drapeaux-wallons-la-wallonie-est-un-pays-magnifique-7V2WVBIBTRELTNPPYPXY2SKTJE/).

The language asymmetry that underpins much of the inter-community dynamic remains a structural challenge. Philosopher [Philippe Van Parijs](https://www.lalibre.be/debats/opinions/2023/07/19/pourquoi-les-flamands-connaissent-le-francais-mieux-que-les-wallons-connaissent-le-neerlandais-et-pourquoi-cela-ne-changera-jamais-7GEARFE5LZCMFBK2QAPHRPV5N4/) has documented that roughly 75% of Flemish people claim to know French at more than a rudimentary level, while only about 20% of Walloons claim the same for Dutch — an asymmetry rooted in history, geography, and education policy.

## Beyond the Political Divide

What makes this series significant is that it moves beyond the institutional debates that have dominated Belgian politics for decades. As [The Belgium Times](https://belgium-times.be/2026/08/ce-que-les-flamands-voient-en-nous-et-ce-que-cela-signifie-pour-la-politique-belge/) notes, understanding these perceptions is essential for grasping the mechanisms that structure Belgian political life — and for maintaining the institutional balance that keeps the country together.

The appetite for this kind of understanding appears genuine. The success of the series suggests that Belgians, both Flemish and Walloon, are hungry for a more human, more nuanced conversation about what unites and divides them. As [Isolde Van den Eynde](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/societe/2026/07/30/les-journaux-flamands-parlent-peu-de-la-wallonie-comme-si-une-partie-du-pays-etait-sortie-du-champ-de-vision-de-lautre-AEGFPGGNBRA4TM4RFKISNMPYZU/), editorialist at Het Laatste Nieuws, put it: curiosity to know each other better remains essential for the future of the country.

## What to Watch For

The interviews offer reason for cautious optimism. Personal encounters across the language border — whether through living choices, professional collaborations, or cultural exchange — are slowly reshaping perceptions. The question now is whether political leaders will follow where their citizens have already led.
