# China's JL-3 Missile Test Marks New Era in Naval Deterrence

China's People's Liberation Army (PLA) Navy conducted its first confirmed submarine-based ballistic missile test in over 40 years on July 6, 2026, launching a strategic missile carrying a training simulation warhead from a nuclear-powered submarine into the Pacific Ocean. The test, which involved a dummy warhead travelling approximately 8,000 kilometers (5,000 miles) to a designated high-seas impact zone, represents a significant milestone in Beijing's naval deterrence capabilities and has drawn strong reactions from regional nations across the Indo-Pacific.

According to [SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/military/article/3362640/why-chinas-jl-3-made-waves-first-submarine-based-missile-test-over-40-years), the launch was China's first confirmed submarine-based missile test since 1982 and the first known firing from a nuclear-powered submarine. While Beijing has not officially confirmed the missile type, military experts and analysts widely assess the weapon as the JL-3 (Julang-3, or Giant Wave 3), China's third-generation intercontinental-range submarine-launched ballistic missile.

## The JL-3 Missile Programme

The JL-3 represents a transformative leap in China's sea-based nuclear deterrent. With a reported range of 10,000 to 14,000 kilometers (6,200 to 8,700 miles), the solid-fueled missile is capable of reaching the continental United States when launched from Chinese coastal waters. It can carry either a single warhead or multiple independently targetable reentry vehicles (MIRVs) and uses astro-inertial guidance with Beidou satellite navigation, according to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JL-3).

The missile made its first public appearance at a massive military parade in Beijing in September 2025, and its development history dates back to a first test flight in the Bohai Sea on November 24, 2018. Chinese military experts quoted by the Global Times assessed that the JL-3 was extremely likely the missile used in the July test, noting that previous tests had covered high and low trajectories and that the missile needed a near-full-range trajectory test to verify the entire weapon system's ballistic performance and operational reliability.

## Strategic Significance

The test's significance extends well beyond the technical achievement. The JL-3's extended range allows Chinese ballistic missile submarines to threaten intercontinental targets while remaining inside heavily defended maritime bastions near the South China Sea and Bohai Gulf, reducing their exposure to hostile anti-submarine warfare forces. As [Defence Security Asia](https://defencesecurityasia.com/en/china-jl3-slbm-pacific-launch-type094a-ssbn-nuclear-deterrence/) noted, this capability significantly complicates American and allied military planning.

Lyle Morris, a senior fellow at the Asia Society Policy Institute, observed that the test shows China's nuclear delivery methods have expanded from land-based to sea-based, and that the Chinese Navy now has the capability to threaten the US homeland from bastion waters near China's coast. RAND Corporation senior policy researcher Beauchamp-Mustafaga similarly noted that the test demonstrates Beijing's intent to show that its emerging triad of nuclear strike forces is credible.

Jeffrey Lewis of the Middlebury Institute told the [New York Times](https://cn.nytimes.com/china/20260706/china-pacific-nuclear-missile-launch/) that the test heralds a new era of testing. "Historically, China has tested ICBMs less frequently than other countries," Lewis said. "I think that was for political reasons, but now the political environment has changed, and I think they are adopting a strategy of conducting more tests. They are now willing to pay the political price that they were not willing to pay in the past."

## Regional Reactions

The launch drew immediate and sharp criticism from across the Pacific region. Australian Foreign Minister Penny Wong described the test as "destabilising to the region," according to [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-07-06/china-test-ballistic-missile-south-pacific/106885076). "Destabilising acts can lead to miscalculation, can lead where we do not want these actions to lead," Wong said. "Pacific Islands Forum leaders have made clear they want the Pacific to be an ocean of peace. We regard this proposed test as counter to that."

New Zealand Foreign Minister Winston Peters called the launch "unwelcome and concerning," warning that Pacific states have no interest in China using the South Pacific as a testing site for missile capability. Japanese Chief Cabinet Secretary Kihara Minoru expressed concerns from the standpoint of Japan's and the region's security, pledging to maintain the highest level of vigilance.

Solomon Islands Prime Minister Matthew Wale, who chairs the Pacific Islands Forum, lodged a strong protest with the Chinese ambassador. "China is a good friend of Solomon Islands, but friends don't do this kind of thing," Wale said. "We don't want anyone to test intercontinental ballistic missiles in the Pacific region. You can be our friend, but don't threaten us."

The Marshall Islands, which has deep historical experience with nuclear testing in Pacific waters, called on China to clearly state its intentions. The US State Department condemned the launch, stating that Beijing's "rapid and non-transparent expansion of its nuclear weapons force is deeply concerning to the region and the world."

## Notification Controversy

A key point of contention surrounds the advance notification process. While China claims it notified relevant countries in advance, the sequence of events suggests a more complicated picture. According to [China-Arms analysis](https://www.china-arms.com/2026/07/china-slbm-test-jl-3-japan-notification-2026/), Japan's Coast Guard was informed a day in advance about a designated hazard zone for "falling space debris" — not identified as a missile test. China's Ministry of National Defense clarified that the notice concerned a ballistic missile launch only about 90 minutes before the launch.

US officials criticized the notification timing, with a State Department official telling Reuters that China notified the US only hours before launch, far short of the standards followed by other nuclear weapons states. [Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20260709-9335875) reported that US officials described the notification as irresponsible and lacking sufficient detail.

## Broader Context

The test came on the same day Australia and Fiji signed the Ocean of Peace Alliance, a mutual defense pact widely seen as countering Chinese influence in the Pacific. It also coincided with the announcement of the China-Russia Joint Sea-2026 naval exercise. The missile reportedly landed in waters near the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone, established under the 1986 Treaty of Rarotonga, which China signed protocols to respect.

China's nuclear modernization continues at a rapid pace. The country's warhead stockpile has grown from roughly 290 in 2019 to more than 600 today, with projections of over 1,000 by 2030. The July 2026 test represents China's second public ballistic missile test into the Pacific since 1980, following a land-based DF-31 test in September 2024.

As [Army Recognition](https://www.armyrecognition.com/news/navy-news/2026/china-submarine-ballistic-missile-test-pacific-2026) noted, the launch demonstrates China's operational maturity rather than a new missile breakthrough. Its significance lies in the combination of an SSBN launch, a Pacific impact area, a dummy reentry vehicle, advance regional notification, and China's public acknowledgement of the event.

## What to Watch

The test signals that China's sea-based nuclear force is moving from symbolic deterrence toward credible, routine deterrent patrols. With the Type 096 (Tang-class) submarine under development and expected to feature significantly reduced acoustic signatures, China's maritime deterrent is likely to become increasingly survivable through the 2030s.

Regional governments will be watching closely for signs of further testing and whether Beijing adopts more transparent notification procedures. The diplomatic fallout from this test — particularly the criticism over notification timing — may shape how China conducts future strategic weapons demonstrations in the Pacific.
