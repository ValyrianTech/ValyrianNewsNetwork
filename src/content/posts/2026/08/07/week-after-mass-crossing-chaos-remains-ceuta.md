# Week After Mass Crossing, Chaos Remains in Ceuta

A week after tens of thousands of migrants surged across the border from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta, the city remains in the grip of a humanitarian crisis. Between 2,000 and 8,000 migrants are still present, most of them unaccompanied minors, as authorities struggle to manage reception centers operating at 2,600 percent of capacity.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/07/ceuta-spanje-marokko-update/), correspondent Miral De Bruyne reported that "a lot is still going wrong" and that it remains unclear how many people have stayed. "In a place where normally about 84,000 people live, many extra people are now walking around," she said. "Those estimates range widely, from 2,000 to 8,000 people. Reception centers cannot handle that number."

## The Mass Crossing

On 30 July, an estimated 70,000 to 72,000 people entered Ceuta from Morocco through the El Tarajal border spit, swimming and walking across in one of the largest mass crossings in the enclave's history. Nearly 90 percent of those who entered progressively returned to Morocco, finding closed businesses, limited food and water, and no path to regularization.

But the crossing exacted a devastating human toll. At least 80 bodies have been received at the Institute of Legal Medicine in Ceuta, with only two identified by fingerprints as of Wednesday evening. Morocco has confirmed 11 deaths on its side of the border. NGOs dispute these official counts: the Moroccan Association for Human Rights (AMDH) estimates nearly 130 deaths, while Caminando Fronteras puts the figure at 141, according to [InfoMigrants](https://www.infomigrants.net/fr/post/72928/mineurs-non-pris-en-charge-subsahariens-dans-limpasse-une-semaine-apres-lafflux-de-migrants-ceuta-confrontee-a-de-nombreux-defis).

## The Crisis of Unaccompanied Minors

The most critical issue facing Ceuta is the fate of unaccompanied minors, who cannot be returned to Morocco under Spanish law and must be taken into state care. The local government estimates approximately 1,100 unaccompanied minors are in the city, with the National Police having identified 528 who have passed to the city's custody. However, NGOs and neighborhood associations report far higher numbers: the Asociación de Vecinos de El Príncipe collected data on 2,672 minor migrants in a single day, and Save the Children estimates between 4,000 and 7,000 minors may still be in the city.

As [RTVE](https://www.rtve.es/noticias/20260806/cientos-menores-vagan-ceuta-semana-despues-entrada-masiva/17181750.shtml) reports, hundreds of minors continue to wander the streets of Ceuta, hiding in parks, beaches, and hills. José Miguel Morales of the Federación Sur Acoge described the heartbreaking reality: "We have desperate families looking for their children in Ceuta calling inconsolably, we have children here who haven't been able to locate their parents, and we have children who don't want to go back and who talk about situations of domestic violence at home." He added: "We're talking about real childhood."

Ceuta's reception centers are overwhelmed. The city normally accommodates 29 minors but now has about 1,107 in care. One school has been converted into an emergency shelter, and Spain has announced 25 million euros in aid for the enclave.

## Warnings of a New Crossing

The Spanish Guardia Civil has issued a document based on open-source intelligence warning of a possible new mass crossing planned for 15 August, organized through social media platforms including Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, and TikTok. The document states: "The risk of materialization is real, but its magnitude is uncertain."

As [RTVE's VerificaRTVE investigation](https://www.rtve.es/noticias/20260806/nueva-entrada-migrantes-ceuta-como-organiza-redes/17182005.shtml) revealed, social media groups analyzed have between 400,000 and 422,560 members combined. The investigation joined three WhatsApp groups and five Facebook groups, analyzing nearly 6,000 messages. Most participants are minors aged 14-17, and smugglers are offering crossings to the Spanish mainland for 1,600-1,700 euros, according to cybersecurity expert Marcelino Madrigal.

The Guardia Civil's rigorous conclusion is not that "there will be an assault on August 15," but rather that "there is an active and potentially mobilizing digital call for that date; its execution, size and authorship are not yet confirmed." Spain has deployed the Army to Ceuta and is considering a maritime barrier, as [El Constitucional](https://www.elconstitucional.es/es/nacional/gobierno-desplegara-barrera-maritima-en-ceuta-acelerara-repatriaciones-entrada-mas-50000-migrantes_6741_102.html) reported.

## Questions of Moroccan Complicity

The crisis has reignited questions about Morocco's role. An investigation by [France24/AFP](https://www.france24.com/fr/info-en-continu/20260803-ceuta-le-maroc-a-t-il-laissé-passer-les-migrants-et-dans-quel-but) suggests Morocco may have deliberately relaxed border controls. Migrants who were turned back said no reinforced checkpoints were deployed on the two roads leading to Fnideq, and a source close to security operations reported "normal gendarmerie controls, no exceptional deployment."

Pierre Vermeren, a researcher and Maghreb specialist, noted that Moroccan police are "very efficient, very organized and very well informed," suggesting they could have blocked the roads if they had wanted to. Riccardo Fabiani of the International Crisis Group said "there are enough elements to estimate that (the power in) Rabat is involved in this crisis," noting that the influx "reminds Spain that Ceuta remains a contested and difficult-to-defend territory and that Madrid needs Rabat to protect its interests."

Haizam Amirah Fernandez, director of the Center for Contemporary Arab Studies in Madrid, said it is "hard to believe the authorities weren't aware of what was happening, or even facilitated a population movement of such magnitude." Morocco denies any "relaxation" at the border crossing and has blamed social media and mafias for the crisis.

## Political Fallout and the Road Ahead

Ceuta's president, Juan Jesús Vivas, has called the mass entry an "invasion" and "an attack on the territorial integrity of Ceuta and Spain," and has asked for "socorro" (help) from the rest of Spain. The political dispute now centers on how to distribute the unaccompanied minors across Spain's autonomous communities, with the PP initially refusing to accept minors in regions it governs and Vox opposing by all legal means.

EU Commissioner Magnus Brunner has stated that no migrant illegally entered the Spanish mainland after the Ceuta crossing, and Morocco has accepted Spain's request to return all unaccompanied minors, removing judicial obstacles that had prevented returns for years.

As [Antena 3](https://www.antena3.com/noticias/sociedad/guardia-civil-alerta-posible-nueva-entrada-masiva-15-agosto-ceuta_202608066a7496cca5690f04736a9a3b.html) reported, police unions warn they are not prepared for another mass entry. The portavoz of the AUGC said "there are no means to stop it" if another crossing occurs, while Jupol's spokesperson said there is "no type of order or operational instruction" regarding reinforcements.

De Bruyne of VRT NWS noted that "now that all of Europe is watching, the Spanish government will do everything to prevent a new wave." But she also warned that such practices will continue as long as people profit from them: "According to the Spanish government, especially human traffickers benefit from that crossing. Chaos arises and they profit from it."

The coming days will be critical. With the August 15 date approaching, Spain is deploying additional troops and strengthening border defenses, while Morocco has processed more than 80 people for their alleged involvement in the crossings. The humanitarian situation in Ceuta remains fragile, and the fate of thousands of minors hangs in the balance as Europe watches closely.
