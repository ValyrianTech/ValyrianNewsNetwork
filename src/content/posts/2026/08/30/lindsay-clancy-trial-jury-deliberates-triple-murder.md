# Lindsay Clancy Trial: Jury Deliberates on Triple Murder Charges

Jurors in the closely watched Lindsay Clancy murder trial ended their second day of deliberations Friday without reaching a verdict, leaving the fate of the Massachusetts mother in the hands of a panel weighing whether postpartum psychosis absolves her of criminal responsibility for killing her three children.

The jury — nine women and three men — began deliberating Thursday afternoon after five weeks of testimony in Plymouth County Superior Court, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/lindsay-clancy-trial-latest-deliberations-7e3d5aca36b47a1676b63dd3716eb5c0). They were excused for the weekend and are scheduled to resume Monday morning.

## The Case

On January 24, 2023, Clancy strangled her three children — Cora, 5, Dawson, 3, and 8-month-old Callan — with exercise resistance bands in the basement of the family's Duxbury, Massachusetts home. She then attempted suicide by cutting her wrists and neck and jumping from a second-story window, an act that left her paralyzed from the waist down.

Clancy, a 36-year-old former labor and delivery nurse at Massachusetts General Hospital, has pleaded not guilty to three counts of first-degree murder by reason of lack of criminal responsibility. Her attorney, Kevin Reddington, does not dispute that she killed her children but argues she was suffering from postpartum psychosis and bipolar disorder at the time and should not be held criminally liable.

## The Defense's Argument

Reddington has painted a portrait of a devoted mother whose mental health deteriorated catastrophically in the months before the killings, despite her repeated efforts to seek help. Clancy saw multiple psychiatrists and nurse practitioners, was prescribed 13 different psychiatric medications, called suicide hotlines, visited emergency rooms, and voluntarily checked herself into McLean Hospital, a highly ranked psychiatric institution, in early January 2023.

"How does this turn into this? How?" Reddington asked the jury during closing arguments, holding up photos of the family before and after the tragedy. "Because of the damn medicine and the lousy medical care that she got."

Defense forensic psychiatrist Dr. Phillip Resnick testified that Clancy was "clearly psychotic" on the day she killed her children, describing her as "almost like a puppet and someone else was pulling the strings." Defense psychologist Paul Zeizel said he believed Clancy was not responsible for her actions due to the depth of her mental illness.

Clancy claimed she heard a disembodied male voice telling her she had no choice but to kill her children and then herself. A chaplain who visited Clancy more than 200 times after the killings testified that Clancy told her she was "glad my children are safe," as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/09/lindsay-clancy-murder-trial) reported.

## The Prosecution's Case

Assistant District Attorneys Jennifer Sprague and Shanan Buckingham argue that Clancy's actions on the day of the killings demonstrated planning and rationality. They point to evidence that she sent her husband out of the house on an errand, called a drugstore to ensure medication was in stock, and calculated how long it would take him to return.

"She was depressed. She was exhausted. She was done," Sprague told the jury in her closing argument, as reported by [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/live-blog/lindsay-clancy-trial-murder-closing-arguments-jury-deliberations-rcna594599). "But those kids, those kids were her protective factor. Those kids were keeping her from ending her misery."

Prosecutors also questioned the seriousness of Clancy's suicide attempt, noting that the cuts to her wrists and neck were superficial and that drug concentrations in her blood were not consistent with someone who had swallowed handfuls of pills. They called forensic psychiatrist Dr. Gregory Saathoff, who testified that Clancy told him she said "Go to God, baby" while strangling each child, suggesting she expected to be reunited with them in heaven.

"She left them discarded on the basement floor, like broken toys, because she was done playing the game," Sprague said. "You know, to a moral certainty, she is guilty."

## The Legal Stakes

The jury can consider first-degree murder, second-degree murder, or manslaughter. They may also find Clancy not guilty or not guilty by reason of lack of criminal responsibility. Under Massachusetts law, the prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Clancy was not suffering from a mental disease or defect that left her without the capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of her conduct.

If convicted of first-degree murder, Clancy faces life in prison without parole. If found not guilty by reason of lack of criminal responsibility, she would be committed to a state mental health facility for an indefinite period.

## Public Support and Broader Implications

The trial has drawn national and international attention, sparking widespread discussions about postpartum mental health and the gaps in maternal healthcare in the United States. Hundreds of supporters, many wearing pink clothing emblazoned with phrases like "Believe," "She Needed Help," and "Peace For Lindsay," rallied outside the courthouse in August, as [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/lindsay-clancy-postpartum-supporters-murder-trial-9ae86dc05b44c78722a47915320e0606) reported.

"Women are being dismissed, neglected and ignored when we speak up," said April Vincent, a 52-year-old paralegal from Providence, Rhode Island, who attended the rally. "We're scared because nobody takes us seriously."

Dr. Nicole Kumi, an expert in postpartum mental health, said the case highlights a significant gap in care for mothers in the postpartum period. "She was crying out for help within her home and within the healthcare system, and all she was met with was medication and redirection," Kumi said. "That's not how we solve problems in maternal healthcare."

Postpartum psychosis is a rare but severe condition affecting 1 to 2 per 1,000 women after delivery. Unlike postpartum depression, it involves psychotic symptoms including hallucinations and delusions.

## What's Next

The jury is scheduled to resume deliberations Monday morning. During their second day of deliberations Friday, they requested to view specific evidence, including a knife Clancy used to cut herself and bags containing empty prescription pill bottles found in her home, according to [WHDH](https://whdh.com/news/jury-in-lindsay-clancy-trial-ends-second-day-of-deliberations-without-a-verdict/).

The request suggests jurors are methodically examining evidence related to premeditation and Clancy's mental state at the time of the killings. Legal experts say a verdict could come as early as next week, given the complexity of the case and the volume of testimony from more than 80 witnesses.

Both Lindsay and Patrick Clancy have filed separate civil lawsuits against healthcare providers who treated Lindsay before the killings, alleging medical malpractice and negligence. Those cases remain pending.

As the nation watches, the jury's decision will not only determine Clancy's fate but may also shape how the legal system and society grapple with the intersection of severe mental illness and criminal responsibility.
