# Heat Kills Hundreds of U.S. Workers Each Year

Extreme heat kills hundreds of workers in the United States every year, yet the true scale of the crisis remains largely hidden in official statistics. A new report from Dallas highlights the human cost, with a U.S. Postal Service carrier among the latest victims of dangerously high temperatures.

## The Scale of the Problem

According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries, an average of 39 workers per year died from exposure to environmental heat over the most recent decade of finalized data (2014-2023). However, a [2021 analysis by Public Citizen](https://climaterig.com/how-many-workers-die-of-heat-stress-each-year-the-2026-data/) estimated the true number of heat-related occupational deaths is likely between 600 and 2,000 per year — roughly 17 to 50 times the official figure. The discrepancy exists because heat is rarely listed as the primary cause of death on death certificates; workers who collapse from heat-induced cardiac events are typically coded as cardiovascular deaths.

Some 28,000 heat-related work injuries occur every year, according to a 2025 study. Agriculture and construction are among the most hazardous jobs for heat-related injuries, according to [Dr. Barrak Alahmad's research at Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health](https://hsph.harvard.edu/environmental-health/news/heat-stress-impacts-workers-and-the-bottom-line/). "Heat-related injuries can come in different forms. It's not just a person passing out," Alahmad said.

## A Postal Carrier Among the Latest Victims

On July 20, 2026, Umeshkumar Thakkar, a 58-year-old rural letter carrier in Little Elm, Texas, collapsed and died while delivering mail during a heat advisory with temperatures reaching about 100 degrees. He had worked for the Postal Service since 2022. According to [The Dallas Morning News](https://www.dallasnews.com/news/article/little-elm-usps-worker-died-amid-extreme-heat-22358025.php), OSHA launched an investigation into his death, which has up to six months to complete.

Thakkar is the third Dallas-Fort Worth postal carrier in just over three years to die on his route amid dangerous heat. In June 2023, Eugene Gates Jr., a 66-year-old Dallas letter carrier, collapsed and died while delivering mail when the heat index reached 113 degrees. An [autopsy](https://www.dallasnews.com/news/2023/09/30/autopsy-reveals-cause-of-death-for-mail-carrier-who-collapsed-in-dallas-heat/) determined he died from heat exposure and heart disease. In June 2025, Jacob Taylor, 51, a 28-year Postal Service employee, collapsed and died while delivering mail in the Dallas Medical District during triple-digit temperatures.

## OSHA's Response and Its Limits

OSHA fined the Postal Service $15,625 for Gates' death, citing the agency for failing to recognize the "hazard of high outdoor heat including high temperature, high humidity and direct sun exposure." The Postal Service contested the citation. As [NBC 5 Dallas-Fort Worth](https://www.nbcdfw.com/news/local/usps-faces-15625-fine-after-heat-related-death-of-dallas-mail-carrier-eugene-gates-jr/3416606/) reported, Gates' widow Carla called the fine "a slap in the face" and "injustice to my husband."

A 2024 report by the Postal Service's Office of Inspector General counted 1,332 heat-related accidents from fiscal year 2022 through June 2024, with more than half resulting in missed work time and potential injury. Letter carriers face unique challenges: aging Grumman Long Life Vehicles with no air conditioning routinely record temperatures above 120 degrees inside.

## A Stalled Federal Standard

OSHA's proposed federal Heat Injury and Illness Prevention Standard has stalled. The rule was published in August 2024, public hearings concluded by late 2025, and the post-hearing comment period closed on October 30, 2025 — but no finalization date has been set, and the rule is not a current administration priority, according to [AlertMedia's analysis](https://www.alertmedia.com/blog/osha-heat-regulations/).

Meanwhile, only about half a dozen states explicitly protect workers from excessive heat. As of 2026, the states with active heat-illness prevention standards are California, Washington, Oregon, Minnesota, Maryland, Colorado, and Nevada. Texas, one of the hottest states, has no state heat standard and prevents local governments from passing their own heat protection laws.

## Calls for Stronger Protections

Labor advocates argue that current measures transfer responsibility onto individual workers while management retains control over start times, route length, staffing, and workload. [David Arkush, director of the Public Citizens Climate Program](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/north-texas-postal-workers-death-215054637.html), noted that delivery drivers face particular risks: "A lot of times, their vehicles aren't air conditioned. Or they're supposed to be air conditioning, but it's broken. They're also out of the vehicle all the time... They don't necessarily get breaks. There's often a lot of pressure on them to deliver quickly."

An anonymous Texas postal worker told the [World Socialist Website](https://www.worldsocialistwebsite.org/en/articles/2026/07/24/qcee-j24.html): "My question is how many people have to die before they realize that the heat is too much or for them to even care about showing some compassion for people and treating us like human beings instead of dogs?"

## What's Next

OSHA's original Heat National Emphasis Program expired in April 2026 and was replaced by a revised, expanded program running through April 2031. Heat inspections have increased from roughly 200 per year to approximately 2,400 per year. But without a federal heat standard, enforcement remains piecemeal.

As temperatures continue to rise due to climate change, the dangers facing outdoor workers will only grow. The question is whether regulators and employers will act before more workers die on the job.
