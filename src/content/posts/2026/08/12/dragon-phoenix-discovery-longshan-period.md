# Dragon-Phoenix Find Rewrites Timeline of Chinese Symbolism

Archaeologists in Inner Mongolia have uncovered a set of "dragon-phoenix" clay figurines in a tomb dating to approximately 2300 BCE, making them the earliest and most clearly defined physical combination of these iconic Chinese symbols ever found in northern China. The discovery, reported by [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260812/ae6bf13433a044908867dfb39a6850ee/c.html), pushes the concrete manifestation of dragon-phoenix imagery back to the Longshan period (c. 2300-1800 BCE).

## The Discovery at Zhaizita Stone City

The figurines were found by the archaeological team of the Inner Mongolia Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology at the Zhaizita Stone City site, located in Jungar Banner, Ordos City, Inner Mongolia. The site, a Longshan-era stone city covering 420,000 square meters, has yielded significant insights into early Chinese civilization.

The clay figurines were discovered in a rectangular earthen-pit tomb, where the occupant was buried in an extended supine position with the head pointing northeast. A jade bi (disc) inlaid with turquoise was placed on the right side of the neck. The "dragon" and "phoenix" figurines were symmetrically arranged on either side of the body—the dragon on the right with its head oriented in the same direction as the tomb occupant, and the phoenix on the left, depicted in a soaring, ascending posture.

## Scientific Analysis and Dating

Radiocarbon dating indicates the tomb occupant died around 2300 BCE, approximately 4,300 years ago. Stable isotope analysis of collagen from the occupant's tooth enamel revealed a diet based on millet agriculture but with significantly higher-than-normal animal protein or special food resources—evidence that the individual enjoyed elevated material resources and social status during their lifetime.

Sun Jinsong, director of the Inner Mongolia Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology, confirmed the significance of the find. "The Zhaizita Stone City site 'dragon-phoenix' clay figurines are the earliest and most clearly defined physical dragon-phoenix combination found in northern China to date, pushing the concrete appearance of dragon-phoenix imagery to the Longshan era," Sun said, as [reported by the Science and Technology Daily](https://www.stdaily.com/web/gdxw/2026-08/12/content_562776.html).

## The Longshan Period and the Hetao Region

The Longshan period, named after the Longshan site in Shandong Province where the culture was first identified in 1928, was a late Neolithic culture that spread along the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River. Known for black pottery, rammed earth construction, and the emergence of early walled settlements, the era is considered a critical period in the development of early Chinese civilization.

The Zhaizita site is part of the broader Hetao region—the area around the Great Bend of the Yellow River in Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, and Shaanxi—which has emerged as a significant center of early Chinese civilization. In 2023, a high-level architectural complex from the Longshan Era was discovered for the first time in Inner Mongolia at the nearby Houchengzui Stone City in Qingshuihe County, as [documented by Go Inner Mongolia](http://www.goinnermongolia.com.cn/2023-12/19/c_949002.htm). Archaeologists have noted that the Hetao region's stone cities represent some of the earliest "city-state" formations along the Yellow River, according to [People's Daily Inner Mongolia](http://nm.people.com.cn/n2/2023/0525/c196687-40429947.html).

## Dragon and Phoenix Symbolism in Chinese Culture

The dragon and phoenix are among the most important symbols in Chinese culture, representing power, harmony, and auspiciousness. The dragon is traditionally associated with imperial power, while the phoenix represents virtue and grace. Together, they symbolize harmony, prosperity, and good fortune.

Previous archaeological evidence of early dragon imagery includes Hongshan culture jade dragons (c. 4000-3000 BCE), painted dragon plates from the Taosi site (c. 2600-2000 BCE), and the turquoise dragon-shaped object from the Erlitou site (c. 1800-1500 BCE). As [Chinese Social Sciences Net](http://english.cssn.cn/skw_culture/CULTURE_Horizontal/202507/t20250710_5885640.shtml) has documented, the dragon is believed to have evolved from snake imagery, with the snake as the primary prototype. The phoenix is often associated with bird imagery and represents transformation and renewal.

This new discovery at Zhaizita provides the earliest physical evidence of dragon and phoenix imagery appearing together as a combination, offering valuable material for understanding how these enduring cultural symbols developed.

## Broader Significance

Sun emphasized the wider implications of the find. "This discovery provides valuable material for studying the spiritual beliefs and burial customs of Longshan-era peoples," he said, as [reported by Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20260812-9506188). "Furthermore, it demonstrates that the Hetao region made unique contributions to early Chinese spiritual and cultural creation, supporting the pluralistic unity of Chinese civilization."

The concept of "pluralistic unity" (多元一体) reflects the understanding that Chinese civilization emerged from the convergence of diverse regional cultures rather than a single point of origin. The Zhaizita discovery adds compelling evidence that the Hetao region played a distinctive role in the spiritual and cultural development of early China.

## What to Watch For

As excavation at the Zhaizita Stone City site continues, archaeologists anticipate further discoveries that may illuminate the religious practices, social hierarchies, and cultural exchanges of Longshan-era communities. The site's 420,000-square-meter expanse suggests significant potential for additional findings that could reshape our understanding of early Chinese civilization and the origins of its most enduring cultural symbols.
