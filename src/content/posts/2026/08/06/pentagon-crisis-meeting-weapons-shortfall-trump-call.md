# Pentagon Crisis Meeting After Trump Fury on Weapons

The Pentagon hastily convened a crisis meeting Friday night after Deputy Defense Secretary Stephen Feinberg received an angry phone call from President Donald Trump about the severe depletion of U.S. weapons stockpiles during the ongoing war with Iran, according to four people with knowledge of the meeting. The gathering of top military and defense acquisition officials was called to address a critical shortfall of U.S. weaponry that now threatens to limit military options against Iran, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/national-security/pentagon-holds-crisis-meeting-weapons-shortfall-angry-trump-call-sourc-rcna590654).

The confrontation has ignited a public battle between the White House and the media, with Trump denying any shortages and threatening to prosecute those who leaked information about the depleted stockpiles.

## The Camp David Confrontation

Trump's frustration boiled over at Camp David last week, where he demanded answers from Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on why he had apparently been misled about extreme munitions shortages, according to two people familiar with the exchange. The president vented that he thought the munitions issue "had been fixed," as [The Washington Post reported](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/trump-hegseth-clashed-at-camp-david-over-iran-missile-depletion-concerns-072937126.html).

When confronted, Hegseth defended himself and blamed his deputy, Stephen Feinberg, for the shortages and for not ensuring Trump was fully apprised of the issue, both people familiar with the conversation said. The exchange underscored Trump's increasing frustration with his defense secretary, who was one of the biggest early advocates for taking military action against Iran.

## Depleted Arsenals

The scale of the depletion is stark. The U.S. has used virtually all of its Army Tactical Missile Systems (ATACMS) and Precision Strike Missiles during the war with Iran, according to two sources with direct knowledge of the matter, as [CBS News reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/u-s-stockpile-long-range-missiles-nearly-depleted-iran-war/). The ATACMS stockpile — a short-range missile also in demand by Ukraine — is drained to the extent that there is basically none left.

Air defense interceptors are also dangerously low. The Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) estimated that by the end of July, the U.S. had between 759 and 827 Patriot missiles left — about a third of the 2,330 available before the conflict began. The U.S. has also used around 60% of its THAAD interceptors, according to [CSIS analysis](https://www.csis.org/analysis/renewed-iran-war-would-test-diminished-interceptor-inventories).

The war's intensity explains the rapid depletion. In the first month of fighting alone, the U.S. fired more than 850 Tomahawk cruise missiles and more than 1,000 Patriot and THAAD systems. From February 28 to April 7, U.S. forces struck more than 13,000 targets in Iran and defended against more than 8,500 incoming Iranian munitions or drones.

## Trump's Denial and Leaker Threats

Trump took to Truth Social on Thursday morning to deny the shortages, writing: "The U.S. has massive amounts of 'munitions,' especially of certain types. Additionally, large amounts are being manufactured and shipped to the U.S. as needed." He added that "the 'leakers' of these treasonous statements are being hunted down. Long term jail sentences will be sought!" as [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cy8mjd19xm7o).

In a second post, Trump said he was "extremely happy with the job that Pete Hegseth is doing" and suggested reporting otherwise might be "treasonous." The president told The Wall Street Journal last month: "We have far more munitions than anyone in the world, and far more than we need."

The White House dismissed reports of the crisis meeting outright. Press secretary Karoline Leavitt said: "This is 100% fake news. Literally never happened. And President Trump has the utmost confidence in Secretary Hegseth." Pentagon chief spokesman Sean Parnell echoed the denial, stating that "Secretary Hegseth did not mislead anyone about our munitions posture, and he did not blame Deputy Secretary Feinberg."

## Strategic Implications

The shortages, particularly in long-range guided missiles and air-defense interceptors, have been part of the reason Trump has pulled back from launching additional massive strikes against Iran in recent days, one source told The Washington Post. Trump said Monday that he had ordered and subsequently called off "the biggest attack since World War II."

The scarcity of defensive munitions has had an impact beyond the Middle East. Ukraine is running out of air defenses, and there are few options to replenish from Western stockpiles, leaving them exposed to Russia's long-range attacks.

The lack of interceptor missiles is also altering how the U.S. decides whether to employ munitions against an incoming threat. U.S. military commanders have been picking and choosing which Iranian missiles and explosive-packed drones to intercept, allowing some attacks through their defenses to preserve the Pentagon's diminishing supply, as [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/national-security/us-military-letting-iranian-projectiles-defenses-rcna588369).

Mark Cancian, a retired Marine Corps colonel and senior adviser at CSIS, warned of the consequences: "We don't have that many left, and there are no good substitutes. If we run out of Patriots, then the missiles get through." He added that "money will not be a problem. The problem is time."

## A Multi-Year Rebuild

Modern weapons have long funding, order, production, and delivery timelines, making it hard to swiftly replace munitions once used. Defense firms "aren't going to start bending metal until you have a contract and congressional appropriations," said Brad Bowman, an Army veteran and senior director at the Foundation for Defense of Democracies.

According to [CSIS analysis](https://www.csis.org/analysis/rebuilding-us-missile-inventory-multiyear-project), it could take until at least mid-2029 to return to pre-war inventory levels for Patriot and THAAD interceptors. The administration's overall defense budget request for the coming fiscal year is a record $1.5 trillion and includes tens of billions of dollars to build missiles, but the proposal has stalled as Democrats object to the massive increase.

Hegseth, Feinberg and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine have asked lawmakers for $67 billion in additional military funding to help cover the costs of the Iran war, but Senate Republicans have yet to agree on a strategy to take up the package.

## Eroding Confidence

The crisis has further eroded confidence in Hegseth's leadership. Senator Thom Tillis (R-NC) said: "I don't have any confidence in him anymore. I think he's all over the place. I think he's got a massive insecurity complex, he has a lack of experience in large organisations, he micromanages too much," as [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/5/why-are-us-republicans-losing-faith-in-war-secretary-pete-hegseth).

Ever since "Signalgate," where Hegseth sent classified information to a chat group that included a reporter, rumors have swirled about how long the defense secretary would remain in Trump's good graces. The stalemate over the Strait of Hormuz and the massive cost and length of the Iran war has eroded Trump's confidence in him, one person familiar with the Camp David conversation said.

## What's Next

More than five months after the start of the conflict, with 18 U.S. service members killed and hundreds injured, Iran and the U.S. remain at a stalemate. The war, which began February 28 with Operation Epic Fury, has seen multiple pauses in fighting, but a deal to fully end hostilities remains elusive.

As the administration continues to deny shortages while simultaneously requesting massive new funding to replenish stockpiles, the gap between public assurances and internal alarm grows wider. The coming weeks will reveal whether the depleted inventories constrain U.S. military options against Iran — and whether the political fallout from the crisis meeting reshapes the Pentagon's leadership.
