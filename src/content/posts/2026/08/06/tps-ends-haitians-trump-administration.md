# TPS Ends for Haitians as Trump Administration Moves to End Temporary Protected Status

The Trump administration has officially ended Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for Haitians, affecting approximately 350,000 Haitian nationals who have been living and working legally in the United States. On August 5, U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes lifted her stay blocking the termination, bringing the case into compliance with a June Supreme Court ruling that sided 6-3 with the administration in [Mullin v. Doe](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/cert/25-1083).

The decision has triggered widespread fear and confusion across Haitian communities nationwide, with reports of ICE check-ins, ankle monitors, arrests, and families facing the agonizing choice between underground life in America or returning to gang-violence-ravaged Haiti. The [Supreme Court ruling](https://abcnews.com/Politics/supreme-court-allows-trump-administrations-cancellation-tps-haitians/story?id=132122359) could potentially be applied to a broader group of 1.3 million people from 17 countries.

## Context: A Program Rooted in Disaster

Haiti was first designated for TPS in 2010 following a devastating earthquake that killed more than 300,000 people and displaced 1.5 million. The designation was extended multiple times by successive administrations from both parties over the following decade and a half, reflecting Haiti's ongoing instability, natural disasters, and escalating gang violence.

In November 2025, then-DHS Secretary Kristi Noem terminated Haiti's TPS designation, certifying that conditions in the country were sufficiently safe for immigrants to return. Lower courts initially blocked the termination, but the Supreme Court's June ruling in Mullin v. Doe determined that the TPS statute bars judicial review of the Secretary's determinations. Justice Samuel Alito wrote the majority opinion, concluding that the law allows "no judicial review of any determination... with respect to the... termination of a TPS designation."

Justice Elena Kagan's dissent was sharply critical, citing statements by President Trump that she described as "so repellent and racially inflected that the majority declines to put them in print." The references, she wrote, "of filth, disease, and primitiveness—are shot through with racial stereotypes and tropes."

## Fear and Enforcement on the Ground

Across the country, the end of TPS has brought immediate and visible consequences. In Salisbury, Maryland—where about 10% of the city's 33,000 residents are Haitian—[church attendance has plummeted](https://apnews.com/article/immigration-haitians-temporary-protected-status-tps-ice-0ea22879cd0ea864242d67041abb3664) as community members stay home, fearing arrest. Pastor Roosevelt Toussaint of the Word of Life Center said Sunday's service drew less than half its normal attendance of more than 500 people.

"We're expecting you to have compassion for our people. They haven't done anything wrong," Toussaint said. "All they came to this country to do is to work, to make a difference in our economy... There's no security at home. If you really send us over there, you send us to die."

ICE has been calling Haitians in for check-ins, fitting some with ankle monitors, and restricting travel to a 75-mile radius from home addresses. In Ohio, where an estimated 15,000 Haitians live in the Springfield area, [residents reported to an ICE field office](https://www.cincinnati.com/story/news/2026/08/02/ice-gives-ohio-haitians-ankle-monitors-after-trump-terminates-tps/91140896007/) in suburban Cincinnati on August 2, with at least two people given ankle monitors. Approximately 550 protesters gathered outside.

"Instead of them heavily descending on communities, they are using tactics to bring those people into their offices to either detain them or fit them with an ankle monitor," said Guerline Jozef, president of the Haitian Bridge Alliance, "and telling them that if they don't self-deport, they will be called back to report within a month."

Viles Dorsainvil, executive director of the Haitian Community Help and Support Center in Springfield, compared the ankle monitors to chains. "They don't want us to be here and now they try to put us in chains as if we did something wrong," he said.

## The Human Toll: Caregivers and the Elderly

The end of TPS is rippling far beyond immigration courts, threatening the care of elderly and disabled Americans who rely on Haitian workers. Around 1 in 5 workers in the U.S. is foreign-born, but immigrants are overrepresented in caregiving roles—one-third of home health aides are immigrants. [Care facilities across the country](https://apnews.com/article/immigration-haiti-nursing-homes-elderly-tps-cb291c13c9c9868ad31092183c8e039d) are bracing for the loss of workers with TPS.

Anna Fischbein, an 87-year-old Holocaust survivor in Sunny Isles, Florida, lost the Haitian health aide who had cared for her for the past decade when Jewish Community Services of South Florida terminated the aide along with 17 others when their TPS lapsed. "I lost not only a lady working for me," Fischbein said, her voice broken by sobs. "I lost a friend."

Solange French, 91, relies on a Haitian home health aide named Martha Nelson for nearly everything—her eggs at breakfast, help in the shower, doctor's appointments, and laundry. "She's with me all the time," French said. "Life would be impossible."

## Economic and Political Implications

Haitian TPS holders contribute an estimated $4.4 billion to $5.9 billion annually to the U.S. economy, according to a letter signed by 104 local officials from across the country. [Boston Mayor Michelle Wu and Councilor Ruthzee Louijeune](https://www.boston.com/news/local-news/2026/08/04/wu-councilor-louijeune-lead-more-than-100-elected-officials-in-urging-congress-to-extend-protections-for-haitians/), the first Haitian American elected to Boston municipal government, led the effort urging the Senate to pass legislation extending TPS for Haitians through early 2029.

"Congress has the power to protect our communities and prevent this devastation for our families and economy by extending TPS," Wu said in a statement. "Thousands of Haitians have lived and worked lawfully in Boston and are part of the fabric of our city."

The House of Representatives passed H.R. 1689 in April 2026 with a 224-204 vote to extend TPS for Haitians for three more years. The Senate companion bill (S.4814) awaits a vote.

Even Republican officials have voiced concerns. Ohio Gov. Mike DeWine called deporting Haitians "a mistake," noting that "the situation in Haiti could hardly be much worse. The violent gangs run most of the country. The government barely functions. And, the economy is in shambles."

The State Department maintains a Level 4: Do Not Travel advisory for Haiti due to gang violence, kidnappings, and civil unrest. Nearly 1.5 million people have been displaced within Haiti because of gang violence, and most Haitians need humanitarian aid.

## What's Next

For the approximately 350,000 Haitians who have lost TPS, the immediate future is fraught with uncertainty. Work authorization expired in late July, and ICE has signaled it will continue enforcement actions. The remaining legal argument—whether the administration's decision was based on racial animus in violation of the Constitution's equal protection clause—continues in litigation, but offers little immediate relief.

Josiane Valsaint, a Haitian TPS holder who wrote about her experience, captured the stakes: "We work hard, we pay our taxes, we give back to our communities and we are a big part of the engine that keeps the United States workforce in many essential industries moving."

As [Truthout reported](https://truthout.org/articles/up-to-350000-haitians-lose-tps-after-judge-lifts-stay-on-scotus-ruling/), the judge's order means that between 330,000 and 350,000 Haitians have lost their legal status even while arguments on racial discrimination continue. The Senate's decision on S.4814 may now be the last hope for hundreds of thousands of families facing the prospect of deportation to a country the U.S. government itself advises against visiting.

For Pastor Toussaint's congregants in Salisbury, the immediate concern is more basic: how to live without fear. "I'm praying this afternoon that God can make you invisible to the enemy," he told them in Haitian Creole. "They put a lot of terror in your heart. But God has come to tell you: Do not be afraid!"
