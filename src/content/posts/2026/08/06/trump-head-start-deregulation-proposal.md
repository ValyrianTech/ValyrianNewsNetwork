# Trump Proposes Sweeping Head Start Deregulation

The Trump administration on Thursday formally proposed a sweeping overhaul of Head Start, the federal early childhood education program serving the nation's poorest children, calling for the elimination of most of the program's regulations and shifting quality standards to state and local governments. The proposal, posted online by the Department of Health and Human Services, would replace the current 133-page set of performance standards with a framework of roughly a dozen pages, leaving most specifics up to state and local law, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/head-start-trump-rfk-standards-18693283cd6791f43e9c181363229a77).

Head Start centers serve approximately 700,000 of the nation's most vulnerable children — including those who are homeless, in foster care, or disabled — through 1,600 operators nationwide, including nonprofits and school districts. The program, created in 1965 as part of President Lyndon B. Johnson's War on Poverty, has long been considered the gold standard in early childhood education, with regulations covering everything from staff-to-student ratios and curriculum requirements to medical and dental screenings and family engagement services. As [AP News reported earlier this week](https://apnews.com/article/head-start-preschool-trump-6207d2fb0934df35722cc2505b679131), the overhaul was anticipated following weeks of deliberations within the administration.

## What the Proposal Would Change

Under the proposed rule, most of those federal standards would be eliminated. Requirements for low staff-to-student ratios, research-backed curriculum, developmental screenings, and services to preschoolers' families would be scrapped, with operators instead instructed to follow state and local childcare licensing requirements — which experts say are generally far more lax. The proposal would also reduce the cap on administrative expenses from 15% to 5% and require more documentation from parents who are homeless or unemployed.

"Federal mandates can certainly hinder local decision-making, drive up costs and limit the program's reach," said Alex J. Adams, an assistant secretary at Health and Human Services, on a call with reporters. "If we get this proposed rule right, we will grow the number of Head Start slots."

The administration estimates the new rules could generate more than $2 billion in savings and potentially allow Head Start to serve up to 236,000 additional children. New requirements include all instruction in English (except tribal immersion programs), new nutrition standards championed by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., and a requirement for 30 minutes of physical activity — outside if weather permits — for every three and a half hours of the program.

"We are removing unnecessary bureaucracy, strengthening nutrition and physical health, trusting parents and local communities, and opening Head Start to hundreds of thousands more children," Kennedy said.

## A Dramatic Shift in Standards

The contrast between Head Start's current standards and typical state licensing requirements is stark. In Mississippi, for example, one Head Start teacher is currently allowed to teach up to four 2-year-olds, while state childcare regulations would permit up to a dozen 2-year-olds to be supervised by one adult.

"Whereas licensing is, like, the bare minimum to keep kids safe, Head Start includes keeping kids safe but also ensuring that kids are developing, growing, learning well," said Shantel Meek, head of the Children's Equity Project at Arizona State University. "It's a totally different purpose."

Critics argue the deregulation would fundamentally alter the program's character. Khari Garvin, who oversaw Head Start under President Joe Biden, said the proposal would leave "the carcass of Head Start." "You might have a program that's called Head Start, but in substance it will not be," Garvin told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/06/nx-s1-5920386/head-start-regulations-trump).

Erica Phillips, executive director of the National Association for Family Child Care, warned that quality would become geographically dependent. "The Head Start performance standards were a consistent … evidence-based set of standards," she said. "It can be helpful to have a nationally recognized benchmark. Quality is now dependent on your ZIP code or on your geography."

## Political Context and Opposition

Head Start has historically enjoyed strong bipartisan support, but it has faced repeated threats under the Trump administration. Russell Vought, Trump's budget chief, had previously called for the program's elimination, and Project 2025 — the conservative policy blueprint — explicitly calls for ending it. Last year, a leaked budget draft proposed eliminating Head Start funding entirely, though it was restored after widespread backlash.

The administration has also laid off much of Head Start's support staff, closed half of its regional offices, and rolled back a Biden-era rule mandating raises for Head Start employees. An early version of the proposal would have barred parents in the United States illegally from enrolling their children, even if those children are U.S. citizens — though it remains unclear whether those provisions made the final draft, as [The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/article/society/head-start-trump/) first reported. The Bulwark's [exclusive reporting](https://www.thebulwark.com/p/exclusive-trump-preparing-new-attack-on-head-start-education-preschool) in July first revealed the administration's plans to gut the program's instructional standards.

Democratic lawmakers and advocates see the deregulation as a "quieter path" to eliminating the program. "They want to do in the Head Start program," said U.S. Rep. Rosa DeLauro (D-CT). "When it starts to fail they'll say Head Start doesn't work." The proposal affects nearly 5,000 Connecticut families served by 22 Head Start programs across the state, according to [CT Insider](https://www.ctinsider.com/news/education/article/trump-head-start-rules-rollback-ct-families-child-22375882.php).

U.S. Rep. Jahana Hayes (D-CT) called it "yet another attack on the poorest individuals." Caitlin Lombardi, an associate professor at the University of Connecticut, described the proposal as "a different kind of political tactic" aimed at achieving the same goal — getting rid of Head Start.

## What's Next

The proposed rule was posted online Thursday and is scheduled to be published in the Federal Register on Friday. The public will have 60 days to comment before it goes into effect, though legal challenges are widely expected — particularly from the ACLU, which has previously sued to block Head Start changes.

Ruth Friedman, who helped draft the current regulations under the Obama administration, argued the proposal contradicts the Head Start Act itself. "If the administration strikes down those standards, it would set back our nation's educational goals and our efforts to reduce poverty, and it is explicitly prohibited by the Head Start law itself," she said.

Tommy Sheridan, deputy director of the National Head Start Association, struck a defiant tone. "When it does come out, we'll be ready to fight back where we need to fight back," he said. "I just really want folks to know Head Start is open. The quality of Head Start children and families truly comes from the people that are running Head Start programs. Those folks are going to be the same. They're going to be able to do that."

For families like Jody Smith's, a Head Start teacher south of Seattle who enrolled her own children in the program, the stakes are deeply personal. Without Head Start, she said, "I would have probably stayed as a poverty line. And I don't know that we would have gotten as far in life as we have."

As the 60-day comment period begins, the future of one of America's most enduring anti-poverty programs hangs in the balance — and the outcome will shape early childhood education for generations to come.
