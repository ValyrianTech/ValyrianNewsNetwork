# Democrats Set 2028 Primary Calendar for Diverse Voters

The Democratic National Committee voted Saturday at its summer meeting in Austin, Texas, to approve a 2028 presidential primary calendar that places South Carolina first and elevates states with significant Black, Latino and union-member populations, formally cementing a dramatic break from decades of tradition that left Iowa out of the early-state lineup for a second consecutive cycle.

Under the approved calendar, South Carolina will hold the first Democratic primary on January 22, 2028, followed by Nevada on February 1, New Hampshire on February 8, New Mexico on February 15, Michigan on February 22, and Virginia on February 29. The expansion from four to six early states marks the first time New Mexico has been among the first states in the country to vote in a presidential primary, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/dnc-2028-presidential-calendar-democrats-south-carolina-be664f26ad5652f054c7f19e483a4d63).

## A Strategic Shift Toward Diversity

The calendar restructuring reflects the party's effort to give greater influence to states that better mirror its diverse coalition, particularly Black voters in South Carolina and Latino voters in Nevada and New Mexico. Roughly a quarter of South Carolina's population identifies as Black, while Latino residents make up 31 percent of Nevada's population and 49 percent of New Mexico's, as [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/15/us-democrats-finalise-major-shakeup-to-2028-presidential-primary-calendar) reported.

DNC Chairman Ken Martin framed the calendar as a way to ensure the eventual nominee can appeal to the full breadth of the party's electorate. "Our 2028 nominee, whoever they are, will have to earn the support across the full breadth of America. Every region, every demographic, every generation," Martin said in his opening remarks to committee members, as [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2028-election/democrats-officially-set-2028-primary-calendar-south-carolina-going-fi-rcna592519) reported. "No shortcuts. No coronation. You want to lead this party? Go earn it. Go and earn it."

The calendar also gives union members a more prominent early voice through Nevada's service industry unions, Michigan's auto industry, and Virginia's federal workforce. Former DNC Chairman Howard Dean, who led the party from 2005 to 2009, endorsed the shift, saying, "These states look more like the country, whereas Iowa and New Hampshire don't."

## The South Carolina Legacy

The decision to lead with South Carolina builds on the state's pivotal role in recent Democratic history. Joe Biden's decisive victory in the state's 2020 primary — where Black voters make up more than half the Democratic primary electorate — revived his struggling campaign and propelled him to the nomination. Biden subsequently pushed to restructure the 2024 calendar to put South Carolina first, a change the DNC implemented for the first time in that cycle.

Jaime Harrison, a former DNC chair who attended the July committee meeting when the calendar was first drafted, said keeping South Carolina first signals the value the party places on its most loyal constituency. "I think we needed a strong statement to say to the most loyal members of our party, which are Black voters, that we're going to stand up with you," Harrison said, as [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/democrats-presidential-nominating-calendar-2028-b3d153a33e39bd9e5ebcce3c58e9e84c) reported. "It was just really important for us to stand up and say, 'We see you, we hear you, but most importantly, we're gonna fight like hell for you. And your voices are important.'"

## Pushback from Iowa and New Hampshire

The calendar change has drawn sharp criticism from leaders in the two states that traditionally led the process. Rita Hart, chair of the Iowa Democratic Party, said bypassing her state sidelines important voter voices and gives a political edge to Republicans, who are expected to keep Iowa atop their calendar.

"I'm deeply concerned that our party has not learned from past mistakes," Hart said at the Austin meeting, as [USA Today via Greenville Online](https://www.greenvilleonline.com/story/news/politics/elections/2026/08/15/democrats-south-carolina-first-2028-presidential-early-voting-primary/91321109007/) reported. "We need a Midwest and truly rural state to be among the first four states if we hope to build sustainable and lasting Democratic majorities that enact real and meaningful change."

New Hampshire Democratic Party Chair Ray Buckley called the decision "disappointing" and defended his state's tradition of first-in-the-nation primaries. "Time and again, New Hampshire has proven we are uniquely suited to battle-test candidates," Buckley said. "Our small, purple state gives candidates a level playing field with the opportunity to meet voters face-to-face, answer tough questions, and earn support one conversation at a time."

The DNC has strengthened penalties for states that violate the calendar, including a $270,000 fine and potential 100 percent reduction in convention representation. Martin made clear the party intends to enforce the new rules. "This is a calendar this party is going to actually protect," he said. "And unlike the past, we're not going to be a wink and a nod, 'we'll let you do what you want, and you can get away with it.'"

## Battle-Testing Candidates

The expanded calendar offers strategic advantages beyond diversity. Party strategist Karen Finney, who has worked on multiple presidential campaigns, said the lineup will force candidates to demonstrate broad appeal. "They have to show that they can go to the South and talk to Black voters and rural voters, as well as out west, talking ranching and mining issues, to a border state, to union members," Finney said. "Whoever survives will be battle-tested."

Of the six early states, all but South Carolina have been competitive in general elections, meaning candidates will spend significant time and resources in states worth a combined 43 Electoral College votes on the way to the 270 needed to win the presidency.

The calendar also offers more scheduling efficiency for campaigns. A swing to the West could hit two early states — Nevada and New Mexico — and include a fundraising stop in California. A South Carolina trip could mean fundraisers and public appearances in Atlanta, Charlotte and Raleigh. Virginia offers proximity to donors and national TV studios in Washington.

## A Party in Transition

The calendar vote comes as the DNC faces significant internal challenges. The party reported about $16 million in cash on hand and more than $18 million in debt, compared to the Republican National Committee's nearly $129 million in cash, as [The Washington Times](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/aug/11/dnc-open-summer-meeting-amid-fundraising-woes-socialist-surge-calls/) reported. Some Democratic lawmakers and operatives have called for Martin's resignation, though he has defenders including Harrison and former interim DNC Chair Donna Brazile.

The party is also navigating a growing divide between progressive and establishment wings, with Democratic Socialists of America-aligned candidates winning several recent primaries, including Abdul El-Sayed's narrow Senate primary victory in Michigan. The new calendar's inclusion of Michigan as an early state could give progressives greater influence in the nominating process.

## What's Next

With the calendar now finalized, attention turns to how Iowa and New Hampshire Democrats will respond. Both states have laws or traditions that could conflict with the national party's schedule, and leaders in both states have not committed to following the new calendar.

Republicans have not yet finalized their 2028 primary calendar but are expected to stick with their traditional early-state lineup led by Iowa and New Hampshire.

The calendar decision is one of the first milestones in what is expected to be a crowded and tumultuous two-year sprint to the Democratic nomination. Without an incumbent president or a dominant front-runner, the states that vote first could once again play an outsized role in determining which candidates gain momentum, attract donors and emerge as viable contenders.

As Martin put it: "This calendar will battle test our candidates, ensure our nominee can appeal to broad swaths of the electorate and this calendar will allow voters, not money, but voters to be the ultimate decider."
