# E-Bike Child Deaths Spur Debate Over Parental Accountability

As traumatic injuries and deaths among children riding e-bikes and other electrified vehicles surge across the United States, a growing national debate is emerging over who bears responsibility when young riders break the law — and whether parents should face criminal consequences for what their children do on the road.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/ebikes-children-safety-laws-injuries-accidents-51e6f9bc6c5a6a6b532ab98bac02da9d), one California prosecutor is cracking down by pursuing criminal charges against some of the young riders' parents, while officials in New York City, New Jersey and other jurisdictions pursue their own strategies to address the crisis.

## A Surge in Injuries and Deaths

The statistics paint a stark picture. At Children's Hospital of Orange County (CHOC), emergency room trauma activations from e-bike injuries exploded from just one patient in 2021 to 201 in 2025, according to a [CHOC study](https://care.choc.org/new-rady-childrens-led-e-bike-study-addresses-speed-related-trauma-risk-for-kids-and-teens/). In Orlando, more than 120 children have been admitted to Arnold Palmer Hospital with e-bike or e-scooter injuries in 2026 so far — compared to about 150 for all of last year, as [ClickOrlando reported](https://www.clickorlando.com/news/local/2026/08/11/a-growing-epidemic-e-bike-injuries-sending-more-kids-to-the-er/).

Nationally, e-bike injuries spiked 357% from 2020 to 2022, according to Mineta Transportation Institute data, while conventional bicycle injuries decreased 22% over the same period. Children ages 11 to 14 account for nearly 62% of e-motorcycle crashes statewide in California, despite a state law prohibiting riders under 16 from operating them.

"The big takeaway is that people are generally more severely injured than they would be if they were on a traditional, pedal bicycle," said Dr. John Austin, a doctor at the University of California San Diego School of Medicine who led a statewide study. "Overall, these patients tend to be younger, they tend to be unhelmeted and, in turn, be more severely injured."

## Prosecutors Hold Parents Accountable

In Orange County, California, District Attorney Todd Spitzer has created a special e-bike and e-motorcycle unit within his Family Protection Division. The office now has 26 open investigations into parents of children suspected of illegally riding e-bikes or e-motorcycles, as the [Orange County Register reported](https://www.ocregister.com/2026/07/31/2-dozen-oc-parents-could-face-criminal-charges-if-children-rode-e-bikes-e-motorcycles-illegally/).

Four active criminal cases have already been filed. Among them is Tommi Jo Mejer, an Aliso Viejo mother charged with involuntary manslaughter after her 14-year-old son fatally injured 81-year-old Vietnam veteran Ed Ashman while illegally riding an e-motorcycle in Lake Forest in April, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/emotorcycle-death-manslaughter-charges-california-parent-ca5a2f1af9eca60c23cc2537f85a6f43). The teen was doing wheelies when he struck the man, then fled the scene.

In another case, Richard Eyssallenne, a Yorba Linda father, was charged with felony child endangerment after prosecutors say he helped his 12-year-old son illegally modify an e-motorcycle to reach speeds up to 60 mph, according to the [Orange County District Attorney's Office](https://ocdistrictattorney.gov/press/yorba-linda-father-charged-with-felony-child-endangerment-and-abuse-contributing-to-delinquency-of-a-minor-after-12-year-old-son-was-critically-injured-while-illegally-riding-e-motorcycle-he-helped-h/). The boy ran through a red light and was critically injured with a fractured skull after colliding with a car.

"The death and devastation caused by e-Bikes and E-Motorcycles has reached epidemic levels," Spitzer said. "If parents refuse to hold their children accountable, then I am going to hold parents accountable when they knowingly break the law."

Other jurisdictions are following suit. In Dublin, Ohio, a Columbus suburb recently issued [new e-bike rules](https://dublinohiousa.gov/motorized-mobility-devices-in-dublin/) making it clear that parents who knowingly allow their children to break the rules are subject to the same penalties as the rider.

## NYC Targets Online Retailers

The debate extends beyond parental accountability to the retailers who sell the vehicles. In New York City, the death of 17-year-old Gabriel Nacato — who crashed into an SUV near City Hall on July 29 while riding an illegal e-moto with a 1,500-watt motor, double the legal maximum — prompted Mayor Zohran Mamdani to take action, as [Streetsblog NYC reported](https://nyc.streetsblog.org/2026/08/05/mamdani-targets-online-illegal-e-moto-retailers).

On August 5, the mayor issued cease-and-desist notices to 42 online retailers, including Amazon, Walmart, Target, Best Buy, Temu and Wayfair, for selling products marketed as "e-bikes" that aren't street legal. Retailers face fines of up to $2,000 per violation if they fail to comply by August 18.

"The burden of whether or not the bike they buy is safe and legal should not fall on working New Yorkers who are simply looking for a quick and low-cost mode of transportation," Mamdani said. "It is the responsibility of retailers to only sell the vehicles permissible under New York law in our city."

From 2017 to 2025, 54% of people killed on e-bikes in New York City were riding illegal vehicles that exceed the 25 mph limit, according to city data. The Nacato crash was detailed in the [New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/07/29/us-news/nyc-teen-killed-in-e-bike-crash-after-ramming-into-suv-near-city-hall/), which reported the vehicle was advertised as reaching 30 mph with a throttle, and the seller's website even gave instructions on how to override the speed limiter to reach 50 mph.

## A Patchwork of Laws

The regulatory landscape remains deeply fragmented. Most e-bikes are classified as nonmotorized vehicles akin to traditional bicycles under federal law, meaning riders don't need a license, insurance or helmet. States have enacted varying requirements, creating what Monica Stafford — a San Francisco-area mother whose teen daughter fractured her skull in an e-bike crash — calls a "total Wild West of laws."

"Being for common sense laws doesn't mean that you're against e-bikes, just like you can be for cars but not think that it's safe to put keys in the hands of a 10-year-old," Stafford said.

New Jersey now requires e-bike riders to be at least 15, obtain a license and register their vehicles. California has no statewide minimum age for lower-powered bikes, though riders must be at least 16 to use e-bikes that can go up to 28 mph. Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis vetoed a bill in June that would have imposed a 10 mph speed limit on e-bikes near pedestrians, saying the measures would lead to "enhanced surveillance" of citizens, as [Jacksonville Today reported](https://jaxtoday.org/2026/06/26/e-bike-veto/).

An Orange County Grand Jury investigation in 2024 found that all 34 cities in the county had different rules for e-bikes, creating inconsistent enforcement and public confusion.

## Advocates Urge Nuance

Cycling advocates caution against overregulation, arguing that many of the problematic vehicles are actually electronic motorcycles falsely marketed as e-bikes. Luke Bornheimer, founder of the San Francisco advocacy organization Streets Forward, warns that conflating the two makes it harder to craft effective policy.

"You kind of muddy the water and make it nearly impossible to figure out what we need better policy and regulations around," Bornheimer said. "It's really important to differentiate between electric motorcycles and e-bikes."

Chloe Lauer, executive director of the San Diego County Bike Coalition, added: "Many of the dangerous behaviors people cite — running red lights, riding on sidewalks, not wearing helmets — are already illegal. We'd rather see those laws enforced than add new restrictions that limit mobility for kids who are riding responsibly."

Anya Dalal, a 17-year-old San Francisco-area high school senior advocating for safety measures, believes the solution requires a multi-pronged approach. "It should be a mix of parental accountability, accountability from the manufacturers and retailers and also education for kids to understand e-bike laws and road rules," she said.

## What's Next

The crisis has prompted school districts to act as well. Newport-Mesa Unified School District in Orange County banned e-bikes for middle and elementary school students starting in fall 2026, with high school students required to complete an e-bike training program.

Amelia Stafford, now 17, who survived a severe e-bike crash in 2023 that left her with a brain injury and epilepsy, has become a leading voice for reform. She and her family lobbied for California's first age restriction on throttle-powered e-bikes, which took effect in Marin County on July 1.

"I don't want somebody to go through the same thing that I had to," Stafford said. "If my voice can make a difference, then that's all I really hope for."

As the August 18 deadline for NYC retailers approaches and Orange County's investigations continue, the debate over who bears responsibility — parents, manufacturers, retailers or the riders themselves — is far from settled. What is clear is that the status quo, with its patchwork of inconsistent rules and rising injury rates, is no longer sustainable.
