# Michigan Senate Primary: Democratic Fights Collide

Michigan’s Aug. 4 Democratic U.S. Senate primary has emerged as the year’s clearest proxy battle for the Democratic Party’s direction, with [NPR reporting](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/01/nx-s1-5904608/michigan-primary-haley-stevens-abdul-el-sayed-democrats) that multiple intraparty fights are colliding in a single race. Progressive Abdul El-Sayed, a physician and former Detroit health director, is challenging moderate U.S. Rep. Haley Stevens for the open seat of retiring Sen. Gary Peters. The winner will face Republican former Rep. Mike Rogers in November, in a contest that could help decide control of the Senate.

## A Proxy Fight for the Party’s Future

Democrats need a net gain of four Senate seats to retake the majority, and holding Michigan is widely seen as essential to that math. The state is home to the nation’s largest Arab American population and a significant Jewish community, and it is set to host an early Democratic presidential primary in 2028 — giving Tuesday’s result outsize national meaning. In 2024, roughly 13% of Michigan Democratic primary voters chose “uncommitted” to protest President Joe Biden’s policy toward Israel, a movement that has shaped the current contest.

## Two Very Different Candidates

El-Sayed, who ran for governor in 2018 and has never held elected office, is campaigning on Medicare for All, removing corporate money from politics, and ending U.S. military aid to Israel. “The problem too often with the Democratic party, we have an instinct to say the right thing, but we’re bought off by the people who are on the side of doing the wrong thing,” he told NPR. “So we’re constantly hedging and pulling punches so we say not much of anything at all.”

Stevens, a four-term congresswoman who flipped a Republican-held suburban district in 2018 and served as chief of staff on the Obama administration’s auto task force, casts herself as a workhorse who can hold the seat and take on Trump. “I’m just beating MAGA four times in my Congressional races, and I’m passing laws and I’m doing damn good work,” she told reporters. “We don’t need wishlists. We need action now. That’s the Michigan way.”

State Sen. Mallory McMorrow suspended her campaign in July after struggling to carve a middle lane, but her name remains on the ballot.

## Record Outside Spending and an Israel Fault Line

The race has also become a referendum on record outside spending. The American Israel Public Affairs Committee and affiliated groups have spent close to $30 million backing Stevens — their largest investment ever in a single race, according to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/aipac-michigan-senate-stevens-elsayed-588bb869eb557643950bf68d6a0d7a10). Outside organizations have poured close to $50 million into boosting her candidacy, including roughly $12 million from one group that has not disclosed its donors.

El-Sayed has made that deluge central to his campaign. “One of the laws of nature in politics is whoever spends the most money wins,” said Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, who campaigned with him in Michigan. “It is an opportunity to show that things other than money can win in America.” Sen. Bernie Sanders was blunter: “She is nothing more than an employee of these billionaires.”

The divide over Israel has been just as sharp. Stevens is a staunch supporter of Israel, while El-Sayed supports ending U.S. military aid to the country. The war in Gaza, which began after Hamas’ Oct. 7, 2023 attacks, has lasted more than 1,000 days and killed more than 73,000 Palestinians, according to Gaza’s Health Ministry. In the closing days of the race, the [Detroit News](https://www.detroitnews.com/story/news/politics/2026/08/01/michigan-senate-primary-abdul-el-sayed-haley-stevens/91090198007/) reported, Stevens accused El-Sayed of wanting to “blame all of your problems on Jewish Americans” — an assertion he rejected. “I love and revere Judaism and the Jewish people,” he said.

## What the Polls and Endorsements Show

Recent polls suggest El-Sayed enters the final weekend with a significant lead. The [Emerson College Polling](https://emersoncollegepolling.com/michigan-2026-poll-abdul-el-sayed-leads-haley-stevens-for-democratic-us-senate-nomination/) survey conducted July 26-27 for WLNS and WOODTV found El-Sayed ahead of Stevens 54% to 39%, with voters under 50 breaking for El-Sayed 67% to 25% and voters over 60 favoring Stevens 53% to 39%. A separate RealClearPolitics polling average, reported by the [Detroit Free Press](https://www.freep.com/story/news/politics/elections/2026/07/30/el-sayed-key-polling-average-means-win-senate-primary/91106158007/), put El-Sayed at 50.2% to Stevens’ 40.8%.

The establishment has largely lined up behind Stevens, including Gov. Gretchen Whitmer, retiring Sen. Peters, former Gov. Jennifer Granholm and former Sen. Debbie Stabenow. El-Sayed has the backing of Sanders, Ocasio-Cortez, Rep. Rashida Tlaib and the United Auto Workers. Sen. Elissa Slotkin, who narrowly won the state in 2024, has not endorsed in the primary.

## What the Outcome Would Mean

Many Democrats see the primary as a test of whether the party’s left can break through against a well-funded establishment. “It’s not moderate versus progressive... it’s going to be people who are going to buck the system and fight or stay and do the same old, same old,” said Debbie Rosenman, chair of the Birmingham-Bloomfield Democrats.

But some strategists question how much the anti-AIPAC message is moving voters. “It’s not that Democratic primary voters love AIPAC. They obviously don’t,” said Adrian Hemond, a Michigan political strategist. “But this doesn’t even register as an issue that’s influencing voting behavior for the overwhelming majority of primary voters.”

The general election is another question. Rogers, unopposed in the GOP primary and endorsed by Trump, lost the 2024 Senate race to Slotkin by fewer than 20,000 votes. Stevens supporters argue she is best positioned to sway independents in purple Michigan; El-Sayed’s campaign counters that his grassroots coalition would expand the Democratic base. If elected, El-Sayed would become the first Muslim U.S. senator.

## What to Watch

The primary ends Tuesday, and the result will be read not only as a verdict on two candidates but as a signal about which faction of the Democratic Party can win in a battleground state. The losing side’s supporters will then face a question nearly as consequential: whether they can unite behind the nominee in time for November, when holding Michigan could determine control of the Senate.
