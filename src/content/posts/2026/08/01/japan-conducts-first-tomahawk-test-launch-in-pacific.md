# Japan Conducts First Tomahawk Test Launch in Pacific

Japan's Self-Defense Forces have carried out their first live-fire test of a U.S.-made Tomahawk cruise missile from an Aegis destroyer, a milestone in Tokyo's push to develop long-range strike options. The launch took place in the Pacific Ocean off the coast of the United States with support from the U.S. Navy, [Japan Today](https://japantoday.com/category/national/japan-destroyer-conducts-1st-test-firing-of-tomahawk-cruise-missiles) reported, citing the Defense Ministry's announcement on July 30.

The exercise grows out of Japan's National Security Strategy, adopted in December 2022, which introduced the concept of "counterstrike capability" — the ability to strike an adversary's missile bases as a self-defense measure. [The Aviationist](https://theaviationist.com/2026/07/31/japanese-warship-launches-first-ever-tomahawk-missile/) notes that Tokyo finalized the purchase of 400 Tomahawk missiles from the United States in January 2024 and that the Kongo-class destroyer JS Chokai, commissioned in 1998, sailed to the United States in the second half of 2025 for modification and crew training.

## Key Developments

On March 27, 2026, the Defense Ministry announced that JS Chokai had acquired Tomahawk launch capability, making it the first Japanese warship configured for the mission. According to [AeroTime](https://www.aerotime.aero/articles/japan-first-tomahawk-cruise-missile-warship-launch), the live-fire test took place on July 29 Japan time (July 28 U.S. Pacific time). The U.S. Navy provided the test range and monitored the surrounding sea and airspace, while the Japanese crew executed the launch. The ministry said the test confirmed that the missile could be launched under operational conditions, including the launch crew's proficiency.

![JS Chokai launching a Tomahawk cruise missile during its first live-fire test](https://theaviationist.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/JapanFirstTomahawkTest_2.jpg)

The ministry's statement said the test "demonstrates steady progress in the Tomahawk project," and that Japan will continue working toward the early establishment of stand-off defense capabilities. Exact details of the test — including the precise location, target type, flight range, number of missiles fired, and whether a Block IV or Block V round was used — were not disclosed.

JS Chokai is expected to return to its home port of Sasebo, Nagasaki Prefecture, around September before being deployed in waters around Japan, Japan Today reported.

## Analysis

Japan is purchasing up to 400 Tomahawks through the U.S. Foreign Military Sales program: roughly 200 Block IV and 200 Block V missiles, at a reported cost of about 169.4 billion yen ($1.03 billion). AeroTime reports the launch makes Japan the third country outside the United States to fire a Tomahawk from a surface combatant, after Australia and the Netherlands.

The test has drawn attention from Chinese state media, which framed the launch as raising regional security concerns. [People's Daily](http://world.people.com.cn/n1/2026/0731/c1002-40771524.html) carried a Xinhua dispatch saying Japan's Self-Defense Forces had conducted their first test-launch of a Tomahawk missile in the Pacific. Chinese military commentator Shao Yongling went further, writing in a [China.com](https://3g.china.com/act/military/13004177/20260731/49647384.html) analysis that while Japan claims these missiles are for defense, "their range and penetration capability make them offensive weapons in essence."

The milestone is part of a broader shift in Japan's military posture. Tokyo is also developing domestic long-range systems, including improved Type-12 surface-to-ship missiles and hypersonic Hyper Velocity Gliding Projectiles, and it continues to debate how quickly to field new capabilities across its fleet. One unresolved question is how many of Japan's eight Aegis destroyers will eventually be armed: Japan Today reported that all eight will carry Tomahawks, while AeroTime said current funding covers five, with the remaining three dependent on future budgets.

## What's Next

The test marks a concrete step in Japan's transition from a force focused largely on territorial and air-defense missions to one capable of striking targets at stand-off range. In the coming months, observers will watch for JS Chokai's return to Sasebo, further deployment decisions, and regional reactions to the missile program — as well as whether Tokyo's parallel investment in homegrown long-range weapons reshapes the balance further.
