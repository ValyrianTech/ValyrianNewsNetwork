# Jury Deliberates Lindsay Clancy Murder Trial After Psychosis Defense

A Massachusetts jury began deliberations Thursday in the murder trial of Lindsay Clancy, a former labor and delivery nurse accused of strangling her three young children in January 2023, after her defense attorney argued she was in a state of deep psychosis and prosecutors contended she planned the killings and knew her actions were wrong.

Clancy, 36, has pleaded not guilty by reason of lack of criminal responsibility, with her lawyers arguing she was experiencing postpartum psychosis when she killed Cora, 5, Dawson, 3, and Callan, 8 months, at the family's home in Duxbury, Massachusetts, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/lindsay-clancy-trial-closing-arguments-5b5f90dbeb5c39fac56d9520201052ab). The rare mental illness is linked to the stress, sleep deprivation and hormonal changes that follow childbirth.

## The Killings and Their Aftermath

On January 24, 2023, Clancy strangled her three children with exercise bands in the basement of the family's home, then slashed her wrists and neck and jumped from a second-story window in a suicide attempt. She survived but remains paralyzed from the waist down.

Clancy had sought treatment from multiple providers in the months before the killings, was prescribed 13 different psychiatric medications, and had voluntarily checked herself into McLean Hospital, a psychiatric facility in Belmont, Massachusetts. She killed her children just 19 days after being discharged, as [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/live-blog/lindsay-clancy-trial-murder-closing-arguments-jury-deliberations-rcna594599) reported.

## Closing Arguments: Two Competing Narratives

During closing arguments on Thursday, defense attorney Kevin Reddington spoke for about 50 minutes, arguing that Clancy was overmedicated and received inadequate medical care. "Because of the damned medicine and the lousy medical care that she got," Reddington said, referencing the mental health providers she saw and drugs prescribed in the months before the killings.

Reddington described Clancy's deteriorating mental state, showing jurors examples of her handwriting that had become "little chicken scratches because her mind was gone." He held up a copy of the book "Good Moms Have Scary Thoughts" found at the Clancy home, noting that a healthcare provider had given it to her. "This woman lived for her kids," he said.

"This young lady is not guilty of the killing of her children because she was suffering from a disease and defect," Reddington concluded, as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/27/lindsay-clancy-closing-jury-deliberations) reported.

Prosecutor Jennifer Sprague, speaking for approximately an hour, argued that Clancy's actions showed planning and premeditation. She pointed to evidence that Clancy sent her husband on errands to create time to kill the children, checked a map to calculate how long the errands would take, and called the pharmacy to ensure the medication was in stock.

"It was a choice to take them with her. It was a horrible choice. It is a choice that we don't want to think about that a mother could make, because that doesn't make us feel safe or comfortable in this world," Sprague told the jury. "But it's a choice she made."

Sprague dismissed the defense's focus on the mental healthcare system, calling it "a distraction to get you angry and passionate about an important issue, but an issue that's not on trial here." She argued that Clancy had access to abundant care and resources, saying, "This wasn't a woman suffering from a lack of care, a lack of resources."

## The Central Legal Question

The trial, which has been called "America's saddest murder trial" by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/09/lindsay-clancy-murder-trial), has drawn national attention to postpartum mental health and the treatment of women in the healthcare system.

Under Massachusetts law, a person is not criminally responsible if they have a mental disease or defect and lack the substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality or wrongfulness of their conduct or to conform their conduct to the law. The burden of proof is on the prosecution to prove criminal responsibility beyond a reasonable doubt.

The five-week trial featured approximately 80 witnesses and sharply divided expert testimony. Defense experts, led by forensic psychiatrist Dr. Phillip Resnick, testified that Clancy was "clearly psychotic" on the day of the killings and was not in control of her actions. Resnick described her as "a puppet and someone else was pulling the strings."

Prosecution experts, including forensic psychologist Dr. Kirk Heilbrun, testified that Clancy had bipolar disorder and depression but retained awareness of the illegality of killing others. Heilbrun concluded she was criminally responsible and killed her children "out of love" so they wouldn't suffer after her suicide.

## Public Reaction and Broader Implications

The case has deeply divided the public. Hundreds of supporters, many wearing pink clothing with phrases like "Believe," "She Needed Help," and "Peace For Lindsay," rallied outside the courthouse. Clancy has emerged as a symbol of gaps in maternal healthcare, with the trial sparking debate on podcasts, social media, and in conversations beyond the Massachusetts courtroom.

Postpartum psychosis is a rare but severe mental health condition estimated to affect 1 to 2 per 1,000 women after delivery. Experts at the Cleveland Clinic describe it as a mental health emergency affecting someone's sense of reality. Most people with the condition do not harm their children, but mothers with severe cases may try to do so and may also try to harm themselves.

Dr. Nicole Kumi, a postpartum mental health expert, said the trial highlighted "a significant gap in care for moms in the postpartum period." She noted that Clancy was actively seeking help: "She knew something was wrong, but she didn't know what was wrong."

## What's Next

The jury of three men and nine women must decide among five options: not guilty, not guilty by lack of criminal responsibility, guilty of first-degree murder, guilty of second-degree murder, or manslaughter. Judge William Sullivan allowed the option of a manslaughter verdict, which could help the jury reach consensus.

If convicted of first-degree murder, Clancy faces life in prison without parole. If found not guilty by reason of lack of criminal responsibility, she could be committed to a state mental health facility.

Judge Sullivan praised the jury's dedication as deliberations began, telling them, "I don't think I've ever seen a more dedicated group of jurors in any trial that I've ever been involved in." The case, which has gripped the nation for over three years, now rests in their hands, with the verdict expected to carry profound implications for how the justice system addresses postpartum mental illness.
