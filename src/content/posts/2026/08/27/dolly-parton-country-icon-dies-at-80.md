# Dolly Parton, Country Icon, Dies at 80 After Cancer Battle

Dolly Parton, the legendary country music icon whose soaring vocals, poignant songwriting and sparkling costumes defined her rise from a log cabin in the Tennessee mountains to the heights of global stardom, has died at the age of 80. Her publicist, Marcel Pariseau, confirmed that Parton passed away on Tuesday, August 25, at the Vanderbilt-Ingram Cancer Center in Nashville, surrounded by loved ones, after "bravely facing a brief battle with cancer," as [AP News reported](https://apnews.com/article/dolly-parton-death-country-icon-87156f3e6a1547b88bf414529b644ad3).

Parton had been admitted to the cancer center on Friday, August 21. Her death was announced by her nephew Bryan Seaver, who served as her head of security for more than two decades, in a prerecorded video posted to her Instagram account. "It is an honor, an honor that is mixed with absolute pride and great sadness, but sadness lies with us, not with Dolly," Seaver said. "Dolly has lived in the light and is in the arms of Jesus."

## A Life From Log Cabin to Legend

Born on January 19, 1946, in a one-room log cabin in Sevier County, Tennessee, as the fourth of 12 children, Parton's journey was the quintessential American rags-to-riches story. Her father, who never learned to read, worked as a farmer and construction worker—a detail that would profoundly shape her philanthropic legacy. Her first musical performances were in church, where her grandfather was a preacher, and by age 10 she was singing on local television. At 13, she appeared at the Grand Ole Opry in Nashville, where [Johnny Cash introduced her](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2026/aug/25/dolly-parton-country-singer-dead) as "a little girl here from up in East Tennessee."

With a suitcase of songs, Parton moved to Nashville after graduating high school in 1964. Her partnership with country star Porter Wagoner beginning in 1967 proved pivotal, and she released her first country No. 1 solo single, "Joshua," in 1971. Then came 1973's "Jolene"—the song that made her career—followed by "I Will Always Love You" in 1974, written as a farewell to Wagoner. She famously turned down Elvis Presley's request to record the latter because she refused to share publishing rights, a decision that secured her financial independence for decades to come.

Parton wrote hundreds of songs totaling more than 100 million worldwide sales and more than 1 billion online streams. She scored a record 25 US country No. 1 singles and 47 albums in the country Top 10, with 55 Grammy nominations and 10 wins, making her the third-most nominated woman in Grammy history. Her crossover success included the 1980 film "9 to 5" with Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin, and her duet with Kenny Rogers, "Islands in the Stream." She was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame and, in 2022, the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame.

## A Legacy of Giving

Beyond her music, Parton's philanthropy was as legendary as her career. Her Imagination Library, founded in 1995, has donated more than 330 million books to children across the United States, Canada, the UK, Ireland and Australia. "My father could not read and write, and I saw how crippling that could be," she once explained, as [AP News detailed in its philanthropy profile](https://apnews.com/article/dolly-parton-philanthropy-country-music-obituary-6c10fced8ebea114cebbad1851e95d4c).

Her $1 million donation to Vanderbilt University's Medical Center helped fund research that contributed to the development of Moderna's COVID-19 vaccine. After the devastating 2016 Smoky Mountain wildfires, she established The My People Fund, providing $12 million in monthly payments to families who lost their homes. She quietly paid for band uniforms at many Tennessee high schools and bought a Nashville strip mall with her "I Will Always Love You" royalties to support the surrounding Black neighborhood. Her Dollywood theme park in East Tennessee became a major economic driver in Appalachia.

"I just give from my heart," Parton said in her 2022 Carnegie Medal of Philanthropy acceptance speech. "I never know what I'm going to do or why I'm gonna do it. I just see a need and if I can fill it, then I will."

## An Outpouring of Tributes

Tributes poured in from around the world following the news of her passing. President Trump announced he would lower the American flag throughout the United States for one week in her honor, writing on Truth Social: "There has never been anyone like her, and never will. This is a true loss for millions of people," as [CBS News reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/dolly-parton-death-reactions/).

Miley Cyrus, Parton's honorary goddaughter, wrote: "The feeling of losing my Aunt Dolly is beyond what feels right to share. Our most sacred moments were private and they'll stay there behind the stage, beneath the glamour & between our hearts." Taylor Swift said in a statement that "a world without Dolly doesn't feel possible, real, or right," adding that Parton possessed "true grace and true grit, all in one dazzling person," as [The Guardian compiled in its tribute roundup](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2026/aug/25/dolly-parton-death-celebrity-reactions).

Beyoncé, who collaborated with Parton on a reimagined "Jolene" for her 2024 album "Cowboy Carter," called her "the barometer. The North Star." Reba McEntire wrote: "There will never ever be another you." Jane Fonda, her "9 to 5" co-star, remembered her as "the voluptuous embodiment of empathy." Paul McCartney praised her as "a very clever business woman who understood the value of her music." The Empire State Building lit up in pink as a tribute, and fans gathered at murals and statues across Tennessee to leave flowers.

## A Cultural Force Like No Other

Parton was among the most beloved personalities in music and beyond—the rare celebrity whose appeal transcended generations, geography and politics. In tumultuous election years, it wasn't uncommon to see "Dolly for President" shirts. Yet she remained famously apolitical, once saying: "I don't do politics. I have too many fans on both sides of the fence."

She was also considered a feminist role model for holding the reins of her own career—writing her own songs, owning her content and managing her finances in an entertainment world dominated by men. Her self-deprecating wit and glamorous persona made her a cultural touchstone, as [Vanity Fair noted in its obituary](https://www.vanityfair.com/culture/story/dolly-parton-dies).

Parton was preceded in death by her husband of nearly 60 years, Carl Dean, who died in March 2025 at age 82. She had recently canceled her Las Vegas residency due to health issues, and her final public appearance came in June at the opening of Dolly's Tennessean Travel Stop in Cornersville, Tennessee, as [USA Today reported](https://www.usatoday.com/story/entertainment/celebrities/2026/08/25/dolly-parton-cause-of-death/91464295007/).

## What's Next

The Broadway musical "DOLLY: A True Original Musical" is set to open January 19, 2027, on what would have been her 81st birthday. Her family has asked that, in lieu of flowers, donations be made to the Imagination Library. A private funeral service will be held for immediate family, with no public memorial plans announced yet.

Parton once said she hoped people would remember her simply as "a good ol' girl" who "tried hard, she worked hard and she tried to make the world a better place and try to make people happy." By that measure, and by every other, she succeeded beyond measure. As [Al Jazeera noted](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/25/dolly-parton-beloved-us-country-music-icon-dies-at-80), "Parton's legacy is not celebrity, but humanity." The world has lost one of its brightest lights, but her songs, her generosity and her spirit will endure for generations to come.
