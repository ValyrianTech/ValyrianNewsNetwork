# Bulldozers Clear Big Bend National Park Land for Border Barrier

Construction crews have begun clearing pristine desert land at Big Bend National Park in Texas to make way for border barrier work along the Rio Grande, escalating a federal border security project that has ignited fierce opposition from environmentalists, local residents, and even some Republican officials.

Heavy machinery from Southwest Valley Constructors Co., a Kiewit Corp. subsidiary awarded a $1.7 billion federal contract, was first spotted inside the park near Cottonwood Campground on August 4 and near Santa Elena Canyon on August 6, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/14/nx-s1-5927276/big-bend-border-texas-rio-grande). Bright pink ribbons now mark a construction path where willow and mesquite trees once stood.

## A Landmark Contract in a Protected Landscape

The project, known as Big Bend 4 (BBT-4), calls for 17 miles of noncontiguous, low-profile vehicle barriers plus improvement and maintenance of about 205 miles of patrol roads and detection technology, according to [Engineering News-Record](https://www.enr.com/articles/63469-17b-border-wall-project-work-begins-in-big-bend-national-park). The contract was fully obligated in May 2026 and is one of the largest-ever federal border security contracts.

Big Bend National Park, formally created in 1944, spans about 801,000 acres of desert and mountains along the Rio Grande corridor. The park's southern boundary forms about 118 miles of the U.S.-Mexico border, and it draws more than 500,000 visitors per year. The [Big Bend Sentinel](https://bigbendsentinel.com/2026/05/20/largest-border-wall-contract-in-us-history-to-span-big-bend-national-park/) reported that the wall project in the park alone is slated to cost just under half of the 2025 operating budget for the entire National Park Service.

The Trump administration has bypassed environmental laws for the project, waiving NEPA, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act, the National Historic Preservation Act, the National Park Service Organic Act, and numerous other requirements. A federal judge declined to block construction in early August 2026.

## Local Opposition and Environmental Concerns

At Santa Elena Canyon, local river guide Billy Miller addressed a crowd of nearly 150 people on Wednesday, some standing in the river's waters and others on the banks holding signs that read 'don't bulldoze my church' and 'don't tread on Big Bend.'

"The river's being threatened as we speak," Miller said. "A lot of people might think, 'Well, we saved it from the 30-foot wall, we won the battle.' The battle is just now beginning."

Laiken Jordahl with the Center for Biological Diversity, a national conservation nonprofit currently suing the federal government over its border barrier projects, warned of lasting damage. "If we lose places like Big Bend, we never get them back," Jordahl said. "The destruction that will be inflicted here will truly be irreparable."

[USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/08/11/big-bend-border-wall-trump-protests/91248748007/) reported that Tiernan Sittenfeld, president and CEO of the National Parks Conservation Association, called the project "an unconscionable attack on one of America's greatest treasures." Rio Grande river guide Mike Davidson, who has lived in the area for nearly 50 years, described it as "horrifying. An invasion. Occupation."

## CBP Defends the Project

CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott has defended the work, saying in a [statement](https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/announcements/statement-cbp-commissioner-rodney-s-scott-correcting-record-big-bend) that the improvements will help agents "detect illegal crossings, respond faster, and assist visitors in danger." Scott said the agency is not building a 30-foot wall in the park and that current work constitutes "survey and design work" rather than full construction.

"We are securing this park to protect its legacy," Scott said. "Keeping it safe, secure, and pristine so American families can enjoy the beauty of our country, free from fear of cartel activity, for generations to come."

Activists dispute CBP's characterization. The advocacy group No Big Bend Wall said in a [statement](https://nobigbendwall.org/2026/08/03/letter-to-senator-cornyn-senator-cruz-and-governor-abbott-protect-big-bends-river-recreation-economy-public-river-access-and-texas-natural-heritage-stop-needless-border-barrier/) that "Washington doesn't get to contradict what we can see with our own eyes," citing "an unprecedented 80 miles of new road cut through our National Park" and "pointless clear-cutting alongside existing park roads."

## Border Crossings at Historic Lows

Opponents argue the project is unnecessary given the region's low border crossing numbers. Apprehensions in the Big Bend Sector fell by 74% in fiscal year 2025 and accounted for just 1.3% of apprehensions across the southern border, according to [Houston Public Media](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/texas/2026/08/13/559457/cbp-commissioner-defends-agencys-border-project-in-big-bend-national-park/). Within the national park itself, apprehensions averaged between 100 and 200 per year since 2023, according to the advocacy group Keep Big Bend Wild.

Former Big Bend National Park superintendent Bob Krumenaker, now chair of Keep Big Bend Wild, called the project "an absolutely senseless road that would all but destroy Mariscal Mountain, which is pretty much the most remote area in Big Bend National Park."

"There's no logic," Krumenaker said. "It looks like they're gonna do all sorts of things, which are almost the most destructive things that they can do for virtually no purpose."

## Political and Legal Challenges Mount

Republican Sen. John Cornyn of Texas has urged DHS to hear directly from local stakeholders before proceeding with additional construction, according to [Houston Public Media](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/texas/2026/08/11/559234/texas-border-wall-big-bend-national-park/). "The requests from local officials and community leaders for coordination and communication appear very reasonable to me," Cornyn wrote to DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin.

Texas Democrats in Congress have also penned letters demanding answers about the project, and more than 2,000 people gathered at the Texas Capitol in April to protest. Five border county sheriffs have spoken against the plans.

The legal challenges are expanding. The Center for Biological Diversity, Friends of the Ruidosa Church, and a local river guide are suing over the DHS waivers. And on August 14, the People of La Junta for Preservation and two Lipan Apache worshippers filed a novel religious freedom lawsuit, arguing the construction violates the Religious Freedom Restoration Act by cutting them off from sacred sites and ecosystems fundamental to their spiritual practices, as reported by the [Texas Observer](https://www.texasobserver.org/indigenous-group-sues-big-bend-border-wall/).

Christina Hernandez, director of People of La Junta and a Lipan Apache Tribe of Texas member, said the river is central to their ceremonies. "We use the river for coming-of-age ceremonies that we are reintroducing into the community, and then we also harvest sacred plant material—willows, cattail pollen—and that all we get specifically from the banks of the river," Hernandez said.

## What's Next

The project represents an unprecedented escalation of border security infrastructure within protected federal lands. As bulldozers continue their work, the key questions remain: Will the courts block or delay the project? Will Sen. Cornyn's request for stakeholder meetings be honored? And what will be the long-term environmental impact on one of America's most pristine national parks?

For now, the battle over Big Bend is just beginning. As river guide Billy Miller warned, the fight to save the park from what opponents call irreversible destruction is far from over.
