# Beijing to Exclude Red Light Waits from Delivery Deadlines

Beijing will pilot a program starting in September in which time spent waiting at red lights will not count toward food delivery time limits, a move aimed at improving road safety for delivery riders by removing the incentive to run red lights. The initiative, called "Red Light Stop Clock" (Hongdeng Tingbiao), was announced at the second "Breaking the Rat Race for Good" consultation meeting organized by the Beijing Municipal Market Regulation Administration on August 13, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/government/20260814/b00e64b7f50f4283926d337feb5230da/c.html).

## Context: The Rider Trapped in the System

Chinese food delivery riders have long faced intense pressure from platform algorithms that calculate delivery times down to the minute, leading to widespread dangerous riding behaviors including running red lights, riding against traffic, and speeding. The phrase "delivery riders trapped in the system" has become a widely discussed social issue in China, reflecting how algorithms transferred uncontrollable factors affecting delivery time—such as traffic congestion and red light wait times—onto riders themselves.

## Key Developments

The pilot will begin in September in Beijing's Chaoyang District, Tongzhou District, and the Economic-Technological Development Area, with plans to gradually expand citywide. Three major platforms—Meituan, Taobao Flash Purchase, and JD Food Delivery—will implement the mechanism, as reported by [China News](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/sh/2026/08-13/10676890.shtml).

Under the new mechanism, time spent waiting at red lights will be excluded from delivery time limits. For electric bicycle deliveries, delivery time will be calculated at an average speed not exceeding 15 km/h, with additional time compensation for adverse weather and complex road conditions. The mechanism also moderately increases delivery assessment time for riders who accept transferred orders and ensures that orders delayed due to slow restaurant preparation do not affect a rider's service score.

Meituan has already launched the first official version of the Wait Light Stop Clock feature in Beijing, with pilot road testing underway. According to [IT Home](https://www.ithome.com/0/989/145.htm), the feature relies on two types of data: rider location trajectory signals and real-time traffic light status data. When a rider has multiple orders while waiting at a red light, the waiting time counts toward each order's delivery time.

Meituan also released its first national rider AI assistant, Tuanbao, in Beijing, providing five functions: red light reminders, accident-prone road alerts, green wave passage, wrong-way detection, and intelligent speed reminders.

## Analysis: Algorithmic Goodness or Surface-Level Fix?

The initiative has drawn both praise and skepticism. Zhang Yi, a professor at the University of International Business and Economics, told Xinhua News that the core of platform algorithms lies in balancing efficiency and fairness, and considering both safety and speed. He described measures such as moderately increasing rider delivery assessment time and the Red Light Stop Clock as "substantive protection of riders' labor rights" and "a vivid practice of using algorithmic goodness to push the industry from speed competition toward quality competition."

An editorial from [China Youth Daily](https://www.163.com/dy/article/L4860J8R0514CDBK.html) praised the measures as reflecting a shift in internet platform rules from efficiency-only to humanized governance, noting that in the past, algorithms transferred uncontrollable factors affecting delivery time onto riders, trapping workers in a system composed of data and algorithms.

However, critical voices question whether the measure truly addresses fundamental problems. A commentary from [Huaxi Metropolis Daily](https://www.163.com/dy/article/L48QAC3T0514TQ42.html) argued that the Red Light Stop Clock is a typical big company culture response that complicates simple solutions. The commentary noted that the rat race is driven by the entire algorithmic incentive system—ranking, elimination, and differential order allocation—not just red light timing, and pointed to past instances where platforms announced the cancellation of overtime fines but then introduced service score systems that penalize riders indirectly.

## What's Next

Beijing's adoption follows Suzhou's pioneering move in July, when the city became the first in China to formally include red light waiting time in delivery time calculation. Meituan and Suzhou jointly launched the pilot on July 31, with plans to roll out in 20 cities nationwide. Over 20 additional cities are being evaluated for rollout, and the initiative is expected to cover over 1 million riders once fully deployed.

The pilot represents a significant step in China's broader regulatory push against platform "rat race" competition. Whether it will meaningfully reduce dangerous riding behaviors and improve rider welfare—or prove to be another surface-level adjustment in a deeply entrenched algorithmic system—will become clearer as the September pilot unfolds and its results are measured.
