# Nevada Swing Voters Open to Socialist Candidates Amid Economic Anxiety

Ten of 12 Nevada swing voters who switched from Joe Biden to Donald Trump between 2020 and 2024 said they would not automatically think negatively of a politician who identifies as socialist, according to focus groups observed by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/13/nx-s1-5929406/nevada-economy-socialism-focus-groups). The findings, released this week, signal growing economic frustration among some of the most consequential voters in American politics and suggest shifting dynamics in a key battleground state.

The focus groups, conducted Tuesday by messaging firm Engagious and marketing research group Nelson Recruiting as part of the [Swing Voter Project](https://www.npr.org/2026/03/15/nx-s1-5743778/inside-our-swing-voter-project-politics), featured 12 participants with a mix of political affiliations: six self-described independents, four Republicans, and two Democrats. Rich Thau, president of Engagious, moderated both sessions and said he was surprised by the level of openness to socialism among voters he described as "tend[ing] to be attracted to more centrist political positions."

"I was struck by the receptivity that they had toward democratic socialism as a concept, that they weren't willing to automatically dismiss it," Thau said. "And I thought generally that they were way more open to socialism than any swing voter otherwise would be."

## Economic Anxiety as the Driving Force

The connection between financial distress and openness to socialist ideas emerged as the central theme of the discussions. Ten of the 12 participants reported feeling more economically anxious now than when Trump took office last year, and seven said they are carrying a higher debt load than a year ago.

Marianne, a 57-year-old independent voter, described the pressure of rising grocery prices and supporting her adult son, who has worked on the Las Vegas Strip for 22 years but cannot make ends meet. "I used to pay cash for groceries," she said. "Sometimes I put it on credit cards because the price has just gone up so much in certain areas."

Thau characterized the openness to socialism as "the economic version of any port in a storm." He added: "These people are struggling economically. So they need help. And they think that socialism apparently is going to offer them the help that they're looking for."

## A Distinction Between Socialism and Communism

While participants were open-minded about socialist candidates, they drew a clear line at communism. None said they would be open to communist candidates, and several expressed visceral discomfort with the term. Justin, a 39-year-old independent, said his reaction to communism is "ingrained from childhood. Living in the '90s, communism, it's very ingrained that it is negative."

This distinction may explain why [Trump often uses the term "communist"](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/02/nx-s1-5914789/trump-attacks-dsa-candidates-as-communists) rather than "socialist" when attacking far-left candidates, a rhetorical choice that appears designed to trigger a more visceral negative reaction among voters.

## Broader Context: Democratic Socialists on the Rise

The Nevada findings come amid a broader surge of democratic socialist candidates winning competitive primaries across the country. A [recent Pew Research Center survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/06/30/about-a-third-of-us-democrats-like-political-leaders-who-identify-as-democratic-socialists/) found that about a third of Democratic voters view democratic socialist leaders positively, while 56% neither like nor dislike them. Among all Americans, 17% like such leaders, 38% dislike them, and 43% are neutral.

Democratic socialist candidates have [won competitive primaries](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/01/nx-s1-5872747/democratic-socialists-are-on-a-winning-streak-heres-what-that-means-for-democrats) in Philadelphia, New York City, Denver, and Washington, D.C., this year, sparking debate within the Democratic Party about whether embracing left-wing candidates helps or hurts the party's chances in November. The focus groups took place on the same night that democratic socialist Francesca Hong [narrowly lost](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/12/nx-s1-5927119/2026-minnesota-governor-primary-election-crowley-hong) the Wisconsin Democratic gubernatorial primary to moderate David Crowley, suggesting that while voters may be open to socialist ideas, they may still prefer more moderate candidates in actual elections.

## Implications for the 2026 Midterms

The findings carry significant implications for Nevada's competitive 2026 races, including the governor's race between incumbent Republican Joe Lombardo and Democratic challenger Aaron Ford, as well as competitive congressional seats. Nevada has a history of progressive politics within the Democratic Party: Bernie Sanders won a landslide in the 2020 Nevada caucuses, and DSA-aligned candidates subsequently took over leadership positions in the state party, prompting the entire staff to [walk out in 2021](https://theintercept.com/2021/03/08/nevada-democratic-party-dsa/).

Conservative analysts have taken note of the focus group results as well. [Nevada News and Views](https://nevadanewsandviews.com/socialist-creep-finds-an-opening-with-nevadas-swing-voters-focus-group-shows/) warned that "when voters feel desperate, they sometimes stop asking how a solution works and start asking only whether someone is finally paying attention to them," urging conservatives to address kitchen-table economic issues more effectively.

## What to Watch For

As the November midterms approach, the question of whether economic anxiety translates into electoral support for socialist candidates remains open. The Wisconsin primary result suggests voters may draw a distinction between openness to socialist ideas and actually voting for socialist candidates. But the Nevada focus groups indicate that the political landscape is shifting, and both parties will need to address the economic concerns driving voters toward alternative approaches.

For Democrats, the challenge is balancing the momentum of progressive candidates with the electability concerns raised by centrists. For Republicans, the findings serve as a warning that economic frustration could push even their own voters toward government-heavy solutions. How candidates in Nevada and across the country respond to these pressures may well determine control of Congress in November.
