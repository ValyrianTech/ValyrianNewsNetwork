# Floods and Heatwaves: Weather Shocks Hit Europe and Asia

Extreme weather events are striking simultaneously across the globe, from record-breaking floods in Japan to a historic heatwave engulfing Europe. The concurrent shocks have killed dozens, displaced hundreds of thousands, and forced shutdowns of critical infrastructure, underscoring the accelerating impact of climate disruption on communities worldwide.

## Japan: Unprecedented Rainfall Devastates Chiba

Record-breaking rains hit Chiba prefecture, east of Tokyo, on Thursday night, triggering the Japan Meteorological Agency's first-ever highest level-5 warning for heavy rains and landslides in the region. Some areas received three times more rain in half a day than they typically see for the entire month of August, with 115mm falling in a single hour in some locations, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/14/g-s1-138727/europe-asia-floods-heatwaves-world-weather).

At least five people have died, with several victims trapped in submerged cars on flooded roads, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/14/japan-deadly-rain-evacuation-thousands-stranded-tokyo-airport-weather). Evacuation orders were issued for more than 400,000 people, and approximately 45,000 homes lost power. Nearly 7,000 passengers were stranded overnight at Narita International Airport, while Japan's Ground Self-Defence Force evacuated 4,000 people from Soga train station.

"This case was an extremely unusual situation," Chiba Governor Toshihito Kumagai told reporters. "I have responded to many disasters in the past, but I have never experienced a case like this."

The flooding struck during the Obon festival, one of Japan's busiest travel periods, compounding the disruption. Soldiers have been dispatched to assist with relief efforts, and while rain had eased by Friday, authorities warned of ongoing flood risks.

## Europe's Fifth Heatwave: A Continent Under Pressure

Across Europe, the summer's fifth heatwave is affecting approximately 300 million people — more than half the continent's population — and is lasting longer than the deadly 2003 heatwave that killed tens of thousands, according to [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2026/08/13/millions-across-europe-swelter-through-a-new-wave-of-extreme-temperatures). Europe is warming twice as fast as any other continent due to atmospheric and oceanic currents and its proximity to the Arctic.

In France, Italy and Spain, some 92 million people are sweltering in temperatures exceeding 95°F (35°C) in cities largely without air-conditioning. France recorded 42.5°C in central Chateaumeillant — the country's highest reading on Thursday — while Spain recorded 42.4°C at Miranda de Ebro. More than 135 million people across Europe were forecast to face temperatures above 35°C.

The UK recorded its hottest day of the year on Thursday, with a provisional 38.1°C at Kew Gardens in west London, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/aug/13/uk-records-hottest-day-of-the-year-fifth-summer-heatwave-peak). July was the hottest month on record in the UK, and 2026 is on track to be the country's hottest year ever. Drought has been declared in three-quarters of England and all of Wales, with some water companies warning they could run out of water within six weeks.

Firefighters have been stretched to their limits. In the West Midlands, fires destroyed homes and forced dozens to evacuate, with a child and three firefighters among those hospitalized. "The apocalyptic scenes in Norton and across Stourbridge are sadly just a taste of what is to come if we don't act," said local councillor Andrew Tromans.

## Nuclear Infrastructure Vulnerable to Climate Stress

The drought has exposed critical vulnerabilities in Europe's energy infrastructure. Romania shut down the last working reactor at its only nuclear plant, Cernavodă, after the Danube River fell to record lows, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/14/romania-nuclear-plant-shut-down-cernavoda-danube-river-water-drought). The plant's two 706-megawatt reactors account for 20% of Romania's power output.

"We do not foresee a restart within the next 10 days," plant director Romeo Urjan told AFP, as reported by [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cqlxpq5q799o). Romania has declared a state of energy emergency throughout August, and carmakers Dacia and Ford have paused production until 19 August.

The shutdown poses immediate problems for neighbouring Moldova, which relies on Romanian power to cover up to 60% of its electricity shortages. "I think we should be ready for a worsening situation, including power outages," Moldovan energy expert Sergiu Tofilat told Radio Chisinau.

France, which relies on nuclear power for about 70% of its electricity, recorded a more than 20% shortfall in atomic production capacity this week — a record deficit caused by outages linked to drought, extreme heat and even a jellyfish invasion. Hungary's Paks nuclear plant has been partially shut down since late July, producing only 25% of its usual output.

## Asia's Record Heat

The heatwave gripping Asia has been equally punishing. South Korea's Yangsan recorded 42.5°C on August 2 — the highest since modern observations began in 1904 — with the heat blamed for at least 31 deaths nationwide, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/aug/13/asia-heatwave-south-korea-potatoes-boil-ground-record-heat-hong-kong-japan). More than 900,000 livestock and 1.4 million farmed fish have died.

South Korean President Lee Jae Myung told his cabinet the extreme weather was "unlike anything we have experienced before," calling for a "fundamental overhaul of the national crisis system."

In Japan, temperatures reached 40°C or higher at multiple locations, and the country recorded its first-ever "kokushobi" (cruelly hot day) — a new category introduced this year for temperatures above 40°C. Hong Kong posted its highest temperature since records began in 1884, reaching 36.9°C on August 9.

Scientists at Japan's Weather Attribution Centre found that the country's July heat would have been "virtually impossible" without global warming. Without human-caused warming, such a heatwave would occur once every 10,000 years.

## A Global Pattern of Climate Disruption

The simultaneous weather shocks across continents are consistent with what climate scientists have long predicted. The World Meteorological Organization reported that Asia's warming pace between 1991 and 2025 is roughly twice that of the preceding three decades. The World Weather Attribution group has confirmed that Europe's drought has been exacerbated by human-induced climate change.

Prof June-Yi Lee, a climate scientist at Pusan National University and coordinating lead author of the UN's most recent climate assessment, warned of an "urgent need for rapid, large-scale reductions in greenhouse gas emissions to limit further global warming together with well-planned adaptation measures."

## What to Watch For

As the summer continues, several developments bear watching: whether Romania can restore nuclear capacity as Danube levels fluctuate; how European water utilities cope with unprecedented demand; whether Japan's flood recovery proceeds amid ongoing weather warnings; and whether governments accelerate adaptation measures in response to what scientists describe as the "new normal" of climate disruption. The economic toll is already mounting — Italy's agricultural trade association estimates climate-related damage could exceed €3bn, while France's Environment Minister warns heatwaves could rack up €10bn to €15bn in costs.

Julia King, who chairs the adaptation work of the UK Climate Change Committee, has warned that extreme weather "gets worse from here" until net zero is reached. The events of this week across Europe and Asia may be the clearest demonstration yet of what that warning means in practice.
