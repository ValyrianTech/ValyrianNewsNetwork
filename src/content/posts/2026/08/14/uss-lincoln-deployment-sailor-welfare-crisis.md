# USS Lincoln Deployment Sparks Crisis Over Sailor Welfare

The USS Abraham Lincoln has been at sea for more than 260 consecutive days, a modern-era record for a U.S. aircraft carrier, as its crew of over 5,000 sailors and Marines supports U.S. operations in the war against Iran. The extended deployment, which began as a scheduled seven-month Pacific rotation in November, has been repeatedly extended amid the conflict, leaving families in limbo and prompting sharp criticism from lawmakers who say conditions aboard the ship have deteriorated to a breaking point.

Sen. Ruben Gallego (D-AZ), a Marine veteran who deployed to Iraq, has emerged as a leading voice demanding oversight, telling NPR that the Pentagon's handling of the deployment reflects a failure of leadership. "They're just kind of winging it right now," Gallego said in an interview with Morning Edition. "That's not proper leadership." The senator has been in contact with the spouse of an experienced sailor aboard the carrier who described deteriorating morale and difficult working conditions.

## Record Deployment Under Wartime Conditions

The Lincoln departed San Diego on November 21, 2025, initially bound for the South China Sea. It was redirected to the Middle East in January, shortly before Operation Epic Fury began against Iran on February 28. Since then, the crew has had only two days on land—in Guam in December and Oman in July—according to reports from [Navy Times](https://www.militarytimes.com/news/your-military/2026/08/11/multiple-uss-abraham-lincoln-sailors-have-tried-to-go-overboard-amid-extended-deployment-families-say/). The deployment was originally scheduled to end in May but has been extended multiple times with no publicly announced return date.

The carrier's prolonged time at sea has set a record that surpasses even the COVID-era deployments when ships were intentionally kept away from port, as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/14/nx-s1-5930567/reports-about-mental-health-supply-issues-aboard-uss-lincoln-raise-concerns). The crew has been supporting U.S. and Israeli operations in the war against Iran, which has entered its sixth month with no clear end in sight.

## Reports of Deteriorating Conditions

Family members and military publications have reported a cascade of problems aboard the Lincoln. Rep. Mike Levin (D-CA), whose district covers part of San Diego County, described conditions including moldy showers, broken toilets, laundry machines down for weeks, long stretches without hot water, and meals reduced to half a cup of rice and two tortillas. The ship's store reportedly ran out of soap, deodorant, and toothpaste.

More troubling are reports of sailors attempting to jump overboard. [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/12/uss-abraham-lincoln-overboard-extended-deployment) that multiple attempts have occurred, citing Navy Times and Stars and Stripes reporting. Annabelle Loma, whose husband attempted to jump from the carrier, told Navy Times: "He's scared. He thinks he'll get a dishonorable discharge, and just because he was burnt out his 13-year career is ruined, just like that. That's not fair, that's not right."

In a separate incident, a sailor on watch intervened to stop a shipmate from going overboard, according to the sailor's wife, Maria Rodriguez. CNN reported that a sailor went overboard last month and was rescued and medically evaluated. The [Wall Street Journal reported](https://nypost.com/2026/08/13/us-news/pentagon-quizzed-about-conditions-aboard-uss-abraham-lincoln-after-reports-of-sailors-trying-to-jump-overboard/) that one person went overboard in early August but was quickly rescued.

The Navy disputes reports of an increase in suicide attempts. "We have not observed an increase in suicidal ideations or attempts aboard the ship," the Navy said in a statement. "We take every service member's well-being seriously." The service says religious, medical, and mental health professionals are available as part of a comprehensive support network.

## Families in Limbo After Bahrain Evacuation

The crisis extends beyond the Lincoln's crew. When the war began, Iran struck Naval Support Activity Bahrain—home to the Navy's 5th Fleet and less than 130 miles from Iran—with missiles and drones. At least 1,500 troops and their families were ordered to evacuate, according to [NPR reporting](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/13/nx-s1-5929463/evacuation-bahrain-troops-iran-war).

Months later, many evacuees remain in a state of uncertainty, living out of duffel bags in hotel rooms and moving between bases. They are on temporary duty status, ineligible for base housing or rental assistance. One military spouse told NPR she and her husband have been in their seventh hotel room, five months after being told they'd be relocated "for a short period of time." Adm. Karl Thomas, commander of Fleet Forces, told NPR that reopening Bahrain to families seems unlikely in the near future.

## Congressional Pressure Mounts

Democratic lawmakers have intensified their demands for accountability. Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-CT), a member of the Senate Armed Services Committee, sent a letter to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao demanding a formal accounting of conditions. Blumenthal noted the Lincoln has not made a port call in over 200 days and cited "widespread reports of shortages of basic supplies, water contamination, plumbing issues, deteriorating mental health, deck safety concerns, and disruptions in the mail system."

Rep. Seth Moulton (D-MA), a Marine veteran and member of the House Armed Services Committee, told NPR the reports are "horrific" and accused the administration of failing its troops. "These brave young Americans signed up to serve our country, not be pawns for Pete Hegseth in a disastrous war that he can't figure a way out of," Moulton said. Sen. Mark Kelly (D-AZ), a former naval officer, called reports of sailors attempting to jump into the sea "disturbing," noting that "long deployments are hard. They are a lot harder when the mission isn't well defined and there is no end in sight."

Gallego is among lawmakers seeking to visit the carrier, calling for a bipartisan congressional delegation to conduct an oversight investigation.

## Pentagon Response and Replacement Plans

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has dismissed reports of poor conditions as "completely misrepresented." Speaking from Panama, Hegseth said: "We make sure every ship, every crew, every captain has everything we can provide them at every single moment." He praised sailors enduring "high seas" and "austere conditions with less port calls," saying "I have more respect and gratitude for those sailors than anybody."

[Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/13/reports-of-us-sailors-in-middle-east-trying-to-jump-ship-spark-outcry) that the USS George Washington, based in Japan, is heading toward the Middle East to replace the Lincoln, with the USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier strike group also being prepared as relief. No timeline has been provided due to operational security concerns.

Rear Adm. Benjamin Nicholson, commander of Navy Sealift Command, acknowledged the logistical challenges that have contributed to supply shortages. "In this conflict, those logistics hubs were not available to us for a number of different reasons," Nicholson told NPR. "So we had to adapt our strategy of how we would resupply the fleet."

## Broader Questions About Military Readiness

The Lincoln controversy has raised fundamental questions about the sustainability of extended carrier deployments. The Navy traditionally plans deployments for roughly six to seven months, with port visits providing essential breaks for sailors. A 2024 Naval Postgraduate School study of 873 sailors linked worse mood to shorter, poorer-quality sleep, while a separate Navy study found mood declining over time during extended periods at sea.

As Blumenthal wrote in his letter: "These reports warrant immediate attention, but they also raise a broader question: whether the Navy can sustain the operational tempo now being demanded of its carrier force." The senator gave Pentagon officials until August 27 to respond to his six questions about fatigue metrics, morale tracking, and the long-term effects of repeated deployment extensions.

For the families of sailors aboard the Lincoln and those evacuated from Bahrain, the uncertainty continues. As one father of a sailor told Navy Times: "I don't want to worry that the circumstances of this deployment will get the best of my son, and he doesn't make it back home. That's an unbearable burden on my heart."

The coming weeks will determine whether the Navy can restore confidence in its handling of the Lincoln's crew—and whether the broader pattern of extended deployments during the Iran war will be reassessed. What's clear is that the human cost of the conflict extends far beyond the battlefield.
