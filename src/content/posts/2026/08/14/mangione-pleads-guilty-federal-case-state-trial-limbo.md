# Mangione Pleads Guilty in Federal Case, State Trial in Limbo

Luigi Mangione, the 28-year-old accused of killing UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson, pleaded guilty on Friday to two federal charges of interstate stalking resulting in death, dramatically reshaping the trajectory of one of the most closely watched criminal cases in recent American history. The guilty plea, entered before Judge Margaret Garnett in Manhattan federal court, came just weeks before his state murder trial was scheduled to begin on September 8, and his defense team immediately filed a motion to dismiss the state case on double jeopardy grounds.

## The Guilty Plea

In a hastily scheduled 45-minute hearing, Mangione admitted to shooting Thompson outside the New York Hilton Midtown hotel on December 4, 2024. "I shot Mr Thompson in Manhattan and he died," Mangione told the court, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/14/nx-s1-5930700/mangione-federal-charges-guilty-plea). "When I did so, I understood that my actions would place him in fear of death or serious bodily injury. I knew what I was doing was illegal."

Mangione entered an open plea, meaning there was no agreement with prosecutors. U.S. Attorney Jamie McDonald confirmed that the government had made no accommodations or concessions and would seek the maximum penalty. "Now [Mangione] stands convicted in federal court and faces life in prison," McDonald said, as reported by [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwy0nlq1l2wo).

In his allocution, Mangione detailed the planning behind the attack, telling the court he had emailed UnitedHealthcare posing as an investor to gather information about their annual investor conference. He said he was driven to kill Thompson "after years of enduring severe pains from a broken back" and navigating the U.S. healthcare system. Mangione admitted to crossing state lines with intent to kill and using a 3D-printed gun equipped with a silencer and magazine.

Judge Garnett scheduled sentencing for December 18. Mangione faces a maximum sentence of life in prison without parole, with the government indicating it believes the applicable sentencing range is 292 to 365 months, or approximately 24 to 30 years.

## The Double Jeopardy Battle

Within minutes of the federal guilty plea, Mangione's defense team filed a motion to dismiss the state murder case, arguing that New York's strict double jeopardy law prohibits prosecution for the same conduct twice. "With today's guilty plea, Mr Mangione has accepted full responsibility for the death of Brian Thompson," defense attorney Karen Friedman Agnifilo said outside the courthouse, according to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/14/luigi-mangione-hearing-federal-case). "It was a single tragic event, yet he's being prosecuted twice for the same conduct."

New York state has especially stringent double jeopardy protections under CPL 40.20, which prevent a defendant from being tried more than once on related charges. The same rules prevented state prosecutors from bringing fraud charges against former Trump campaign manager Paul Manafort in 2019, when a panel of state judges ruled he had already been convicted on similar counts in federal court.

Legal experts say the federal plea could have profound implications for the state proceedings. "If a federal prosecution has been completed on a matter, the state cannot prosecute the matter on those same facts," Mitchell Epner, a lawyer and former federal prosecutor, told [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/ckg3763gy2ro).

## State Charges and Trial Uncertainty

Mangione still faces eight state charges in New York, including second-degree murder, possession of a forged instrument, and six counts of criminal possession of a weapon. He has pleaded not guilty to all state charges. If convicted, he faces 25 years to life in prison.

The state trial was scheduled to begin September 8 with jury selection, and the judge had previously ruled the 12-person jury would remain anonymous due to intense media scrutiny. The Manhattan District Attorney's office had maneuvered to hold the state trial first to avoid potential double jeopardy complications from a federal conviction.

In a statement, the Manhattan DA's office said it remains committed to pursuing the state case. "We are encouraged that Mr. Thompson's family received a measure of accountability today," the statement said. "While federal sentencing is pending, we are prepared to litigate the defense motions."

## A Case That Captured the Nation

The killing of Brian Thompson, a 50-year-old father of two, sparked a five-day nationwide manhunt that ended with Mangione's arrest at a McDonald's in Altoona, Pennsylvania. Shell casings found at the murder scene bore the words "delay," "deny," and "depose" — a reference to a well-known phrase in the insurance industry describing how insurers avoid paying claims.

The case has drawn intense global attention, with Mangione viewed by some as a folk hero for those critical of the U.S. healthcare system. Supporters have donated more than $1.5 million to his legal defense fund, and a [YouGov poll](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/15/nx-s1-5853110/luigi-mangione-court-hearings-growing-support) found that Americans under 30 are more likely to view him favorably than unfavorably.

Elected officials across the political spectrum have condemned the killing. NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch offered a pointed rebuke to Mangione's supporters: "Violence is not a cause. Murder is not a message, and a killer is certainly not a hero."

Thompson's family, who were present in the courtroom during the plea hearing, issued a statement calling the guilty plea "an important step toward justice." "While nothing will ease the pain of losing him, we are grateful that the federal justice system has held the person responsible for this heinous act accountable," the family said.

## What's Next

The federal sentencing is scheduled for December 18, where Mangione could receive a sentence of 24 to 30 years or life in prison. The fate of the state murder trial now rests on how Judge Gregory Carro rules on the double jeopardy motion.

In January, Judge Garnett dismissed two of four federal charges against Mangione, including the death penalty-eligible murder charge, ruling that U.S. Supreme Court precedents made it "incompatible" with the stalking charges. This removed the possibility of capital punishment, as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/01/30/g-s1-108032/judge-rules-luigi-mangione-should-not-face-death-penalty) at the time.

The [Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/luigi-mangione-pleads-guilty-connection-his-murder-united-healthcare-ceo-brian) confirmed that Mangione pleaded guilty to one count of interstate stalking resulting in death and one count of cyberstalking through use of interstate facilities resulting in death, each carrying a maximum sentence of life in prison.

As the legal proceedings continue to unfold, the case remains a flashpoint in the national debate over healthcare, justice, and the limits of public sympathy for those who resort to violence. Whether the state trial proceeds, is dismissed, or is significantly altered by the double jeopardy motion will be determined in the coming weeks — a decision that could reshape the outcome of one of the most consequential criminal cases of the decade.
