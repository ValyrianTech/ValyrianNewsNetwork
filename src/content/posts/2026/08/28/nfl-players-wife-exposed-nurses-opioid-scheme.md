# NFL Player's Wife Helped Expose Nurse's Alleged Opioid Scheme

A new [Associated Press investigation](https://apnews.com/article/dea-opioids-joan-rubinger-nurse-practitioner-e575853a66d0ef726351338932d073ff) reveals how the wife of a former NFL star helped expose an alleged prescription drug scheme operated by a California nurse practitioner who treated some of the biggest names in professional sports. The case has brought renewed attention to opioid abuse and prescription drug diversion in the United States.

## A Nurse Practitioner to the Stars

Joan Rubinger, 49, built a prominent sports medicine practice called All Pro Sports Medicine that catered to elite professional athletes. Her [website](https://allprosportsmedicine.com/) touted a client list including 169 NFL Pro Bowl selections, 29 NBA and WNBA champions, and 15 Olympic medalists. Notable patients included Kobe Bryant, Dwyane Wade, Fernando Tatis Jr., Drew Brees, Saquon Barkley, and Odell Beckham Jr.

But behind the scenes, the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration alleged in court filings, Rubinger doled out thousands of illegal prescriptions for highly addictive painkillers. Over a four-year period, federal authorities said, the nurse practitioner prescribed 260,000 pills containing controlled substances to patients in more than 20 states.

## The Scheme Expands Beyond Athletes

The DEA alleged in a seizure warrant that Rubinger's scheme expanded "from professional athletes" to include members of an Ohio-based street gang, a confessed New Jersey drug trafficker, and a New York City model. Along the way, federal authorities alleged, Rubinger took deliberate steps to avert DEA suspicion, coaching clients over an encrypted messaging app on ways to avoid having their prescriptions flagged by pharmacies.

New clients were required to follow instructions contained in a document Rubinger called "THE RULES," court records show. "I put these rules in place for a reason: to minimize the attention we attract from the DEA," she told one prospective client, according to a DEA seizure affidavit. Getting a prescription, she told clients, was "like at McDonald's, you gotta pay for your burger before they hand it to you."

## How an NFL Player's Wife Broke the Case

The DEA began investigating Rubinger in 2022 after being tipped off by Kelly Davis, then the wife of former All-Pro Carolina Panthers linebacker Thomas Davis. Kelly Davis told authorities and the AP that she visited an emergency room near her home in Charlotte, North Carolina, complaining of chest pains. As she was being evaluated, a doctor informed her she had been flagged for receiving excessive pain medication prescriptions.

Unbeknownst to her, Rubinger had written 23 oxycodone prescriptions for her husband over a two-year period listing her as the patient, according to the DEA and Kelly Davis. Authorities alleged Thomas Davis received more than 13,000 oxycodone and Percocet pills from Rubinger between November 2019 and March 2024.

"She preyed on these guys' pain," Kelly Davis told AP. "They had money, they had injuries, they had mental-health issues."

Kelly Davis confronted Rubinger over Instagram. "This is insane," she wrote, according to DEA records. "You clearly know he has a problem. Why would you do this?"

## Legal Consequences and Settlement

Citing what an administrative law judge called "egregious conduct," the DEA revoked Rubinger's ability to prescribe opioids in July 2026. That action followed Rubinger's agreement in March to pay $1.4 million to settle a parallel [Justice Department civil complaint](https://www.justice.gov/usao-edca/pr/stockton-nurse-practitioner-agrees-pay-14-million-resolve-alleged-controlled-substance) that accused her of dispensing controlled substances like oxycodone, Percocet, and Xanax on hundreds of occasions without any legitimate medical purpose. Rubinger did not admit any wrongdoing as part of the settlement and agreed to never again prescribe controlled substances.

The Justice Department alleged Rubinger violated the Controlled Substances Act at least 900 times. A permanent injunction was entered on March 26, 2026, requiring Rubinger to surrender the ability to dispense controlled substances.

Notably, no criminal charges were filed against Rubinger; the case was handled as a civil matter. James Rafalski, a retired DEA investigator who reviewed court records, expressed surprise the case did not result in criminal prosecution. "Most doctors who abuse their DEA registration just write scripts without a thorough examination of the patient," Rafalski told AP. "She knowingly took steps to evade detection by the DEA and instructed her clients to do the same."

Eric Grant, the U.S. attorney in Sacramento, said in a statement that "the significant financial penalty" and ban on Rubinger prescribing controlled substances "grants the public meaningful protections against the unlawful dispensing of dangerous drugs."

## A Remarkable Comeback Story

Rubinger's life took a near-tragic turn in 2015 when she plummeted 20-30 feet over the edge of a cliff while riding a mountain bike. The crash broke 19 bones, punctured her lungs, and left her with spinal cord injuries that confined her to a wheelchair. In a [personal account](https://allprosportsmedicine.com/pages/what-happened) of her recovery, she described her struggle: "I don't know who I am anymore. I've lost my identity. It terrifies me."

Despite her injuries, she returned to work within three months. Following her comeback, several NBA players, including Dwyane Wade, wore orange wristbands in her honor on the court. Atlanta Hawks guard Buddy Hield wrote in a testimonial on her company's website: "You would look at Joan sitting quietly in her wheelchair and never know that she is the secret weapon behind the best athletes in the world."

## The NFL's Long Struggle with Opioids

The NFL has well-documented problems with painkillers. A 2011 study found that 52% of the league's retired players used opioids during their career. More recently, a 2020 investigation of the head trainer for the Washington Commanders found he was illegally distributing oxycodone to players. The trainer accepted a $10,000 fine under a deal with the Justice Department.

Since 2019, NFL players have been required to report any prescriptions they receive from outside their team. A study from 2021-22 showed only 10% of NFL players received prescriptions for opioids, according to [Pain News Network](https://www.painnewsnetwork.org/stories/2025/1/16/opioid-pain-medication-infrequently-used-by-nfl-players).

## Thomas Davis's Public Reckoning

Thomas Davis, a 2014 Walter Payton Man of the Year and one of the most beloved figures in Carolina Panthers history, discussed his opioid addiction publicly for the first time in a May 2026 episode of The Pivot Podcast. "There's a lot of shame and guilt," he said, holding back tears. "I've always been a person and a player that said that I would never allow anything to control me."

Davis, who played 14 of his 16 NFL seasons with the Panthers and is believed to be the first and only NFL player to play after tearing the same ACL three times, said his opioid abuse contributed to the demise of his marriage. The couple divorced in 2025. Kelly Davis said her husband's use of opioids contributed to the breakup.

"I take full ownership of every single component of everything that's happened in my life," Davis said on the podcast. "But at the end of the day, I know there were some factors that took place that ultimately led me to a space that I didn't anticipate being in."

## What's Next

The case raises important questions about prescription drug oversight and the treatment of elite athletes dealing with chronic pain. While Rubinger has agreed to never prescribe controlled substances again, her spokesperson, Erick Mullen, suggested she had been pressured by dangerous clients. "You'll have to imagine what kind of people threatened, intimidated, bullied and cajoled a paraplegic nurse practitioner and single mother into this kind of situation," he wrote in an email to AP.

Federal prosecutors declined to comment on why they did not pursue criminal charges. The case highlights ongoing concerns about opioid abuse and prescription drug diversion in the United States, particularly within the high-pressure world of professional sports where athletes face intense physical demands and chronic pain.

As the NFL continues to enhance its monitoring of prescriptions and athletes face increasing scrutiny over pain management, the Rubinger case serves as a cautionary tale about the intersection of sports, medicine, and the opioid crisis.
