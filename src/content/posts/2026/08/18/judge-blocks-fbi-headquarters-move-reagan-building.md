# Judge Blocks FBI Headquarters Move to DC Reagan Building

A federal judge has blocked the Trump administration's plan to relocate the FBI headquarters to the Ronald Reagan Building in Washington, D.C., ruling that the move unlawfully abandoned a previously approved plan to build a new facility in Greenbelt, Maryland. The decision, issued Monday by U.S. District Judge Theodore D. Chuang, delivers a significant legal setback to the administration's cost-cutting initiative while restoring the path forward for Maryland officials who spent more than a decade competing for the project.

## The Ruling

Judge Chuang, an Obama appointee, issued a permanent injunction that prevents the administration from moving the FBI to the Reagan Building, renovating the facility for bureau use, or redirecting federal funds designated for the headquarters project. In a 47-page opinion, the judge found that the administration's decisions were "arbitrary and capricious and not in accordance with law" and exceeded statutory authority, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/fbi-headquarters-maryland-reagan-building-f5a77411bf54f3e17beb9cd41fd046a8).

The ruling centers on congressional requirements enacted in 2022 and 2023 that directed the General Services Administration (GSA) to select the new FBI headquarters from three short-listed sites outside Washington: Greenbelt, Maryland; Landover, Maryland; or Springfield, Virginia. The judge found that the Reagan Building selection violated these statutory requirements, noting that "the text provides no conditions under which the selection could be unilaterally rescinded or switched to a nonconforming site," as [PJ Media](https://pjmedia.com/david-manney/2026/08/17/trump-tried-to-save-billions-on-fbi-headquarters-a-judge-blocked-him-n4956250) reported.

The injunction also blocks the diversion of $555 million in funds approved by Congress for the Greenbelt project. Maryland had alleged the administration was attempting to divert over $323 million that Congress had set aside for the relocation, according to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/fbi-reagan-building/).

## A Decades-Long Saga

The FBI has operated out of the J. Edgar Hoover Building on Pennsylvania Avenue since 1975. The brutalist-style structure has fallen into serious disrepair, with protective nets surrounding the facility to protect pedestrians from falling debris. The bureau long ago outgrew the space, forcing employees to work out of annexes throughout the region.

For approximately 15 years, officials debated where to relocate the headquarters, with Maryland and Virginia competing intensely for the massive federal project. During his first term, President Trump considered relocating the FBI but ultimately dropped a proposal that would have moved it to one of three sites in Maryland or Virginia, instead pushing for a smaller headquarters in Washington.

In November 2023, the GSA under the Biden administration selected Greenbelt, Maryland, ending the lengthy debate. The site was chosen over Landover, Maryland, and Springfield, Virginia, with the GSA citing Greenbelt as the most cost-effective of the three options.

However, in July 2025, FBI Director Kash Patel announced the bureau would instead move to the Ronald Reagan Building, a federal office complex a few blocks from the existing FBI headquarters. Patel called it "the most cost-effective and resource-efficient way to carry out our mission," arguing the move would save taxpayers billions in new construction costs and avoid more than $300 million in deferred maintenance at the Hoover Building, as [Internewscast](https://internewscast.com/news/us/judge-deals-blow-to-fbi-headquarters-move-to-reagan-building/) reported.

The Reagan Building had been home to the U.S. Agency for International Development until 2025, when the Trump administration consolidated USAID into the State Department and allowed Customs and Border Protection to take over the building's lease.

## The Lawsuit and Its Aftermath

In November 2025, Maryland Attorney General Anthony Brown and Prince George's County filed suit against Patel and the FBI, alleging the administration attempted to "unlawfully sabotage" the Greenbelt headquarters project and illegally divert funding designated by Congress.

Maryland officials celebrated the ruling as a major victory. "Maryland and Prince George's County committed more than a decade of work to earn the FBI headquarters and pledged hundreds of millions of dollars," Brown said in a statement. "By blocking the Trump Administration's unlawful attempt to move the FBI to the Reagan Building and divert the funds Congress set aside for this project, the Court has cleared the path back to Greenbelt."

Maryland Gov. Wes Moore echoed that sentiment, saying, "Now it is time to stop the games and get to work building the world-class FBI headquarters that our public servants deserve, where it belongs: in Prince George's County, Maryland."

The economic stakes are substantial. Prince George's County officials estimate the Greenbelt project could represent roughly $4 billion in investment and create more than 7,500 jobs. Maryland and the county had committed more than $300 million toward transportation, parking, and other improvements tied to the site, as [MyBigPlunge](https://mybigplunge.com/politics/us-politics/trump-presidency/judge-blocks-trump-administrations-fbi-headquarters-move-in-major-maryland-showdown/) reported.

The FBI, however, sharply criticized the decision. "This is not the first time courts have tried to undermine the administration in its goal to make government more cost-effective for American taxpayers," the bureau said in a statement. "The court has chosen to impermissibly intervene for political reasons. This FBI is mission focused and will continue the best course of action to meet the needs of law enforcement."

## Analysis and Implications

The ruling rests on the principle that Congress controls federal property and spending. The judge determined that the 2022 and 2023 congressional measures created binding requirements for the site selection process, and the administration lacked statutory authority to unilaterally rescind the Greenbelt selection or switch to a nonconforming site, according to [The Washington Times](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/aug/17/federal-judge-blocks-kash-patels-attempt-move-fbi-hq-reagan-building/).

The judge's injunction can be revisited if Congress rescinds or changes the site-selection provisions, meaning the administration could potentially appeal the ruling or seek congressional action to alter the law. Conservative commentators have expressed concern that a single district judge can freeze a presidential administration's decision based on congressional language written years earlier, arguing that Congress should have to explicitly reaffirm the Greenbelt decision if it wants to proceed.

## What's Next

The ruling represents a significant legal setback for the Trump administration's broader efforts to reduce federal spending by repurposing existing buildings. It also highlights the ongoing tension between the executive branch and the judiciary over the administration's cost-cutting measures.

For Maryland, the ruling restores the path to the Greenbelt project, though questions remain about the timeline for construction and whether Congress will take further action. The legal battle could continue as both sides weigh their next moves in a fight that involves billions of dollars, thousands of jobs, and the future home of one of America's most powerful law-enforcement agencies, as [NY Ledger](https://thenyledger.com/news/federal-judge-blocks-patels-attempt-to-move-fbi-hq-to-reagan-building/) noted.

Observers will be watching closely to see whether the administration appeals the ruling, whether Congress intervenes to clarify or change the site-selection requirements, and how this decision affects other federal building repurposing initiatives across the government.
