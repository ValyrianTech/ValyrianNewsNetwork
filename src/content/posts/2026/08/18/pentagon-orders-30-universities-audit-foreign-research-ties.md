# Pentagon Orders 30 Universities to Audit Foreign Ties

The Department of Defense has ordered 30 U.S. universities to conduct comprehensive audits of their partnerships with foreign institutions, particularly those in China, Russia, and Iran, as the Trump administration escalates its scrutiny of academic collaborations that could pose national security risks. The institutions have until August 31 to complete the reviews and terminate any problematic partnerships or risk losing eligibility for future federal research funding, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/pentagon-audit-china-harvard-mit-738832f9f20186dc5f9ef1aa39914636).

## The Directive and Its Scope

The Pentagon formally notified the 30 universities on Monday, instructing them to review their academic, financial, and research connections to foreign entities deemed of concern under Section 1286 of the Fiscal Year 2019 National Defense Authorization Act. The directive targets partnerships with 130 foreign institutions on the Pentagon's updated list, published July 23, which includes universities in China, Russia, and Iran, as well as organizations associated with rebranded Confucius Institutes.

"The Department of War has zero tolerance for academic partnerships that compromise our national security," Emil Michael, Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering and the Pentagon's chief technology officer, said in a statement. "Institutions that receive funding from American taxpayers must uphold the highest standards of research security, and these mandated audits will ensure accountability across the board."

The full list of 30 schools was not publicly disclosed by the Pentagon but was obtained by [DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2026/08/17/pentagon-probes-30-u-s-academic-institutions-for-potential-ties-to-adversaries/) from a U.S. official. It includes elite institutions such as Harvard, MIT, Johns Hopkins, Cornell, Duke, Georgetown, NYU, UC Berkeley, UCLA, and UC San Diego, as well as smaller regional universities including Bryant University, Drake University, and Northeastern State University in Oklahoma.

## The Harvard Controversy

The directive follows a House committee report released August 13 alleging that Harvard created a nonprofit subsidiary—Harvard Global Research and Support Services—to circumvent federal foreign-gift disclosure requirements, routing $630 million in Chinese funding around Section 117 of the Higher Education Act. The report, released by the House Select Committee on China and the House Education and Workforce Committee, identified more than 140 academic publications co-authored by Harvard-affiliated researchers and scholars associated with Chinese universities linked to the People's Liberation Army.

"Harvard created an entity in a blatant attempt to get around federal foreign funding disclosure laws," Rep. Tim Walberg (R-Mich.), chairman of the House Education and Workforce Committee, said in a statement. "That's unacceptable, especially given Harvard's history of failing to comply with these requirements."

Rep. John Moolenaar (R-Mich.), chairman of the House Select Committee on China, added: "From allowing violence against anti-China protestors to collaborating with entities linked to the PLA, Harvard has made serious mistakes and must act now to prevent more."

Harvard has disputed the committee's characterization, with a spokesperson saying the university "shares the Committees' concern about geopolitical risks, including threats posed by the Chinese Communist Party, and is committed to continuing to conduct research and academic programming with protections that prioritize U.S. interests and national security." The Justice Department is also investigating whether Harvard is allowing Chinese donors to create scholarships that exclude American students, as reported by [The Epoch Times](https://www.theepochtimes.com/china/house-committees-detail-harvard-ties-with-chinese-entities-in-new-report-6075178).

## Expanding the Section 1286 List

The July 2026 update to the Section 1286 list expanded it to 130 institutions, adding three of China's most prominent civilian research universities—Fudan University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and Shandong University. This marked a significant widening of the campaign, which had previously concentrated on institutions with direct ties to the People's Liberation Army, particularly the Seven Sons of National Defense—a group of seven elite Chinese universities administered by China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology.

Timothy Heath, founder of the research firm Perceptum and former China analyst for RAND and the U.S. military, said the expansion reflects a genuine intelligence assessment. "The new listings show that the Department of War recognizes the depth of cooperation between Chinese civilian academies and the military," Heath said. "As that collaboration deepens, civilian universities are becoming increasingly entangled in Chinese defense priorities."

Dr. Joseph Jewell, Assistant Secretary of Defense for Science and Technology, emphasized the importance of protecting research investments. "Universities are critical partners in executing a wide range of Department of War research programs, and the department must therefore ensure that our research investments are well protected from foreign exploitation," Jewell said.

## Pushback from Academia and Beijing

The directive has drawn criticism from both academic institutions and the Chinese government. Sarah Spreitzer, vice president at the American Council on Education, which represents college and university presidents, expressed concern that the announcement implies wrongdoing without evidence.

"We don't appreciate the implication that we are not good partners on research security, given that we helped create this list, and given that we've always partnered with the federal government when there has been national security concerns," Spreitzer said, as reported by [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/harvard-nyu-among-30-universities-hit-pentagon-foreign-tie-audit-deadline).

A spokesperson for the Chinese embassy in Washington rejected what it described as the politicization of normal academic exchanges. "The U.S. side should abandon the Cold War mentality and foster an open, fair, and non-discriminatory environment for educational, scientific, and people-to-people exchanges between China and the United States," the spokesperson said, according to the [South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/news/us/article/3364337/pentagon-orders-30-us-universities-scrutinise-ties-chinese-research-partners). "Scientific innovation and academic development require open cooperation; restricting normal research exchanges will ultimately harm the interests of all parties, including US researchers and institutions."

## Enforcement and Compliance Risks

The compliance stakes extend well beyond future grant eligibility. The Department of Justice has shifted its enforcement approach from the criminal China Initiative—shuttered in 2022—to civil enforcement under the False Claims Act, which allows the government to seek treble damages against universities for failures to disclose researcher affiliations with Section 1286-listed institutions in federal grant applications.

This means universities that have been receiving DoD funding while failing to disclose collaborations with blacklisted institutions face potential civil liability not only for future compliance gaps but for past grant cycles in which non-disclosure occurred. The 30 named institutions collectively receive billions of dollars annually from DoD research arms including DARPA, the Office of Naval Research, and the Air Force Research Laboratory, as noted by [TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/324814/20260818/harvard-nonprofit-exposed-dod-orders-30-universities-audit-foreign-research-ties.htm).

## Academic Freedom vs. National Security

Critics within the national security establishment have raised concerns about the scope of these restrictions. Captain Roger Herbert (U.S. Navy, ret.), former professor at the U.S. Naval Academy, argued in a June 2026 Proceedings article that academic freedom is itself a national security imperative, pointing to how displaced European scholars including Albert Einstein, Enrico Fermi, and Edward Teller brought their knowledge to American institutions in the 1930s and 1940s.

The directive also represents a precedent-setting expansion of the compliance model to named U.S. institutions. By identifying specific schools and imposing a specific deadline backed by a specific funding threat, the Pentagon is creating an institutional accountability record that prior Section 1286 rulemaking never required.

## What's Next

The August 31 deadline is fast approaching, and the 30 universities must now determine how to respond. The Pentagon did not disclose why each specific school was included in the audit, and the inclusion of smaller regional universities alongside elite research institutions suggests the screening cast a wider net than headline names might imply.

The audit comes amid broader Trump administration-China tensions, including concerns about Chinese ownership of ports at either end of the Panama Canal, infrastructure projects funded by China's Belt and Road initiative, and Chinese investment in the telecommunications sector, as [The Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/american-politics/article-905894) noted.

As the deadline approaches, universities face a difficult balancing act between maintaining international research collaborations and complying with federal security requirements. The outcome of this audit process could set a precedent for how the U.S. government manages academic partnerships with foreign institutions for years to come, with implications for scientific cooperation, academic freedom, and national security policy alike.
