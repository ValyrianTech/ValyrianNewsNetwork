# Florida Executes 13th Inmate in Busiest Death Chamber

MIAMI — Florida is set to carry out its 13th execution of 2026 on Tuesday evening, as William Frances Silvia, 61, is scheduled to receive a three-drug lethal injection at Florida State Prison near Starke. The execution would solidify Florida's position as the nation's busiest death chamber, with the state having carried out more than half of the 22 executions nationwide so far this year, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/florida-execution-william-silvia-9ece9e5bba0484cdd83e53fd0918145a).

The U.S. Supreme Court denied a request to halt the execution on Tuesday, clearing the way for the procedure to proceed at 6 p.m. local time. Silvia was convicted of first-degree murder and attempted first-degree murder for the September 2006 shooting death of his estranged wife, Patricia Silvia, and the wounding of her mother, Betty Woodard, at the family's Orlando-area home.

## A Crime Rooted in Domestic Turmoil

According to court records, Silvia purchased a 12-gauge Mossberg Persuader shotgun and four boxes of shells on September 22, 2006, the day after he was fired from his job. He had been homeless since his wife separated from him two months earlier, living in his truck and various motels. That evening, during a family cookout at the Woodard home, Silvia told Patricia "You will be sorry" before retrieving the shotgun from his truck, firing two shots into the air, and then shooting Betty Woodard in the face as she opened a door. He then shot and killed Patricia inside the kitchen, firing approximately seven rounds before fleeing.

Silvia was arrested the next day and confessed during transport, saying he was angry "because she spent all of their money and then started dating her ex-husband." He was convicted in 2008, with a jury recommending death by an 11-1 vote.

## A Complex Legal Journey

Silvia's path to the death chamber has been anything but straightforward. He was first sentenced to death in 2009, but his sentence was later vacated following the U.S. Supreme Court's landmark 2016 ruling in *Hurst v. Florida*, which required capital sentences to be found unanimously by juries. After being granted a new penalty phase in 2017, he was again sentenced to death in 2018.

However, a critical complication emerged from Silvia's decision in 2012 to waive his right to postconviction appeals and discharge his attorneys. The Florida Supreme Court ruled that this waiver barred him from relief under the new constitutional standard, as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/18/florida-13th-execution-william-frances-silvia) reported.

His attorneys also argued that the Capital Collateral Regional Counsel, the state agency providing legal representation to death row inmates, could not provide adequate legal assistance because it was simultaneously handling another death row inmate's case. The Florida Supreme Court rejected this appeal on August 12, according to [Florida Today](https://www.floridatoday.com/story/news/crime/2026/08/18/florida-execute-william-silvia-election-day-schedule-details/91339387007/).

## Mental Health and Mitigating Factors

Both prosecution and defense experts agreed that Silvia suffered from chronic psychological disorders and paranoia that served as mitigating factors. Defense expert Dr. Day diagnosed Silvia with a delusional disorder and testified he may have experienced a trauma-related dissociative episode during the shooting. Even the State's psychiatric expert, Dr. Danziger, acknowledged Silvia's longstanding paranoid thinking and significant personality pathology.

Silvia's case overview, detailed by [Floridians for Alternatives to the Death Penalty (FADP)](https://www.fadp.org/william-frances-silvia-case-overview/), documents a childhood steeped in violence and instability, multiple head injuries including a car crash that knocked him unconscious and an assault causing facial fractures, and severe psychological deterioration in the months before the crime. He lost his job, became homeless, and was increasingly fearful for his own safety.

Maria DeLiberato, Legal and Policy Director of FADP, said: "Serious mental illness shaped William Silvia's life, the crime and the legal decisions that followed. When the consequence is execution, a decision made in the throes of his mental illness should not permanently close the courthouse door to a constitutional protection recognized years later."

Research from Cornell Law School Professor John Blume found that 88 percent of the 106 capital defendants studied who waived their legal reviews had documented mental illnesses or severe substance abuse disorders, with at least 30 having prior suicide attempts, as reported by [Tampa Free Press](https://www.tampafp.com/florida-sets-execution-date-for-william-silvia-amid-push-for-commutation/).

## Florida's Record-Breaking Execution Pace

Silvia's execution would mark the 13th in Florida this year, following a record-breaking 19 executions in 2025 — the most in a single year since the death penalty was reinstated in 1976. Florida executed more people in 2025 than Texas, Alabama, and South Carolina combined, according to [Mother Jones](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/02/florida-death-penalty-desantis/).

Gov. Ron DeSantis, in his final term, has signed death warrants for 32 people since taking office in 2019, with approximately 250 people remaining on Florida's death row. In July, Florida executed two prisoners in one day — the first state in nearly a decade to do so — as [USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/nation/2026/07/28/double-execution-florida-james-duckett-dominick-occhicone/91072108007/) reported.

Two more executions are already scheduled for September: Harold Gene Lucas, 74, on September 1, and Daniel Owen Conahan Jr., 72, on September 10, as [FOX 35 Orlando](https://www.fox35orlando.com/news/floridas-upcoming-executions-three-long-standing-capital-cases) detailed. The same legal team handling Silvia's case is also representing Lucas and Conahan, creating overlapping litigation deadlines within a 34-day window.

## Lethal Injection Controversies

Florida's aggressive execution schedule has drawn scrutiny over its lethal injection protocol. An investigation by Mother Jones found documented issues in half of Florida's 2025 executions, including underdosings of required drugs, use of expired drugs in at least four executions, drug substitutions not in the official protocol, and flaws in drug logs maintained by the Department of Corrections.

In at least three executions in 2025, witnesses reported troubling physical signs including chest heaving, eye rolling, and twitching. Autopsy reports found pulmonary edema — which mirrors the feeling of drowning — in at least one executed inmate.

Justice Sonia Sotomayor has expressed concern about Florida's execution secrecy, noting that inmates are caught in a "catch-22" — they cannot prove cruel and unusual punishment without access to execution records they are denied. "By continuing to shroud its executions in secrecy, Florida undermines both the integrity of its own execution process and, potentially, this Court's ability to ensure the State's compliance with its constitutional obligations," she wrote.

## Advocacy and Opposition

FADP has launched a petition urging DeSantis to commute Silvia's sentence to life imprisonment without parole, and the [Florida Conference of Catholic Bishops has called on the governor to show mercy](https://www.fadp.org/stay-of-execution/stay-the-execution-of-william-silvia/). Peaceful vigils were planned outside Florida State Prison beginning at 5 p.m. on execution day, with additional vigils planned across the state.

DeLiberato also criticized the compressed timeline for Silvia's legal review: "This is not a matter of attorneys simply having a heavy workload. The State has placed the same defense team under overlapping deadlines in three cases where every minute matters and every decision is irreversible. Florida cannot manufacture an emergency and then pretend that equates to meaningful legal review."

Hannah Gorman, a death penalty law professor at Florida International University's College of Law, framed Florida's execution pace in broader terms: "Florida is an outlier in the U.S. But this is also a massive message coming out of America."

## What to Watch For

Silvia's execution, if carried out, would bring Florida's 2026 total to 13 — already more than double the combined executions of all other states this year. With two more executions scheduled for September, Florida shows no signs of slowing its pace under DeSantis, who has said he is doing his "part to deliver justice" to victims' families.

As the state continues its aggressive execution schedule, questions about lethal injection protocols, mental health considerations in capital cases, and the broader implications of Florida's death penalty policies are likely to remain at the forefront of national debate.
