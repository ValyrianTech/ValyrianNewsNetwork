# Health Lecture Scams Target China's Seniors, Report Warns

A new report from [People's Daily](http://paper.people.com.cn/rmrb/pc/content/202608/03/content_30172743.html) has exposed the pervasive threat of health pseudo-science scams targeting elderly consumers in China, after a family member lost more than 5,000 yuan ($700) at a single "health lecture." The report, published Sunday, highlights how predatory marketing disguised as science popularization continues to exploit seniors' health anxieties despite intensifying regulatory crackdowns.

## A Mother's Costly Lecture

The report centers on a netizen identified as Mr. Zhou, who filed a complaint on the People's Daily Leadership Message Board after his mother attended a health lecture where an "expert" promoted a substance called "clam peptide," claiming it could "prevent cancer, fight cancer, and treat the three highs" — high blood pressure, high blood sugar, and high blood lipids. His mother spent over 5,000 yuan on several boxes of the product before Mr. Zhou discovered the packaging was labeled as a "solid beverage," not a medicine or health supplement.

"Saying a solid beverage can cure diseases — isn't this kind of science popularization just false advertising?" Mr. Zhou asked.

## A Widespread Problem

Health is the hardest-hit area for pseudo-science in China. In May, the Central Cyberspace Administration's Reporting Center and the China Association for Science and Technology jointly released the "Top 10 Cases of Resisting Pseudo-Science" at the [China Internet Civilization Conference](http://www.81.cn/zt/2026nzt/2026nzgwlwmdhwmwlkjayfjll/yw_252772/16462382.html) in Nanning, with 8 of the 10 cases directly related to health. According to China Association for Science and Technology statistics, approximately 35% of previously released debunking content was concentrated in the health field.

Experts say the problem is driven by the vast commercial potential of health-related content. As platforms tighten restrictions on direct product sales, some unscrupulous operators have shifted to using "science popularization" as a cover for marketing, [CCTV Health reported](https://jiankang.cctv.com/2026/05/09/ARTIrvbu4HxvmSs4cbLjhyM4260508.shtml).

## Tactics of Deception

The report identifies several recurring patterns in health pseudo-science scams:

**Manufacturing anxiety.** Scammers exploit fears about children's growth and chronic diseases. Some self-proclaimed "big health IP incubators" openly boast about their approach. "What we sell is not products, not professionalism, but pain points and anxiety," one anonymous influencer said. "Whether you dare to speak harshly determines whether you can collect the money."

**Exaggerating efficacy.** Pseudo-science promoters use technical-sounding jargon to package ordinary foods as miracle cures. Reader Mr. An described working at a company that used "health lectures" to promote a substance called "interventional factor" as a treatment for diabetes, hypertension, and cancer — when it was actually just ordinary food. Other scams have marketed "quantum pendants" and "quantum insoles" as medical devices.

**Fake expert personas.** Most promoters are not medical professionals but product sellers who deliberately craft professional identities — claiming to be medical doctors, former pharmaceutical executives, or adopting titles like "Doctor" or "Director" while wearing white coats on camera. Some "incubator" agencies sell ready-made scripts and marketing playbooks for as little as a few yuan online.

**"Real experts, fake science."** The report also documents cases where genuine doctors lend their authority to questionable practices. One reader spent 8,000 yuan on medication recommended by a "verified doctor" from a top-tier hospital on a social media platform for HPV treatment — only to find it ineffective and develop new health problems. The reader had been redirected to private WeChat channels where assistants, not the doctor, conducted the consultations.

## Regulatory Response

Chinese authorities have stepped up enforcement against health product scams targeting the elderly. In 2025, the [State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR)](https://www.samr.gov.cn/xw/zj/art/2025/art_2f89ddb830844b64aced096f4bfce8a1.html) launched a special rectification campaign targeting false advertising of drugs and health products for the elderly. Nationwide, [6,175 cases were investigated and penalized](https://news.dayoo.com/society/202601/27/140000_54921567.htm), with fines and confiscations totaling 103.5 million yuan.

In May 2026, SAMR, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, and the Ministry of Civil Affairs jointly launched the "Health Food Protection for the Elderly Special Action." Additionally, four departments issued the "Notice on Regulating Medical Science Popularization Behavior of Self-Media" in 2025, requiring classification and verification of account qualifications and prohibiting unqualified accounts from producing professional medical content.

Despite these measures, scammers continue to adapt. Some training manuals now analyze platform review algorithms and maintain lists of "banned words" to help promoters use more subtle language, according to the report.

## Expert Recommendations

Professor Wu Shenkuo of Beijing Normal University Law School argues that regulation must target the root incentive. "Pseudo-science creators want traffic, so regulation must hit the 'vital point,'" he said. He recommends platforms strengthen content review, improve content labeling mechanisms, and impose account bans and traffic restrictions on violators, while cyberspace, public security, and market regulation departments conduct coordinated enforcement.

Health sector readers have also suggested that hospitals take responsibility for their doctors' science popularization activities. "Since the hospital's background endorses the authority of these bloggers, the hospital has the responsibility to positively guide doctors' science popularization behavior," one reader said.

## What's Next

As China's population ages, the vulnerability of elderly consumers to health scams remains a pressing concern. [Analysts note](http://guoqing.china.com.cn/2026-03/23/content_118396148.shtml) that scammers increasingly operate through private channels — WeChat groups and closed livestreams — that are difficult for regulators to monitor. The challenge ahead lies in closing these regulatory gaps while ensuring that legitimate health information remains accessible to the public.

For families, the report's message is clear: vigilance at home may be the most effective defense. As Mr. Zhou's experience shows, a single lecture can cost a family thousands of yuan — and the emotional toll of discovering a loved one has been deceived is often far greater than the financial loss.
