# Venezuela, Dominican Republic Restore Diplomatic Ties

Venezuela and the Dominican Republic have agreed to begin a gradual process of normalizing their diplomatic and consular relations, ending a two-year rupture that followed the disputed 2024 Venezuelan presidential election. The joint announcement, issued on August 2, establishes a two-phase roadmap for rebuilding ties between the two Caribbean nations.

## A Two-Phase Roadmap

According to the joint communiqué, the normalization process will unfold in two stages. The first phase prioritizes the restoration of consular services, allowing citizens of both countries to process passports, visas, and legal certifications. The second phase will advance toward the full normalization of diplomatic relations according to a mutually agreed timetable, as [reported by Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/2/venezuela-and-the-dominican-republic-say-they-will-work-to-restore-ties).

The Venezuelan Foreign Ministry described the decision as responding to "the shared interest of strengthening institutional channels of communication to benefit people of both countries," calling the agreement the result of "sustained dialogue" between both nations. The joint communiqué further affirmed that "both governments reaffirm their willingness to conduct this process through dialogue, mutual respect and adherence to the principles of international law," according to [Ultimas Noticias](https://en.ultimasnoticias.com.ve/politica/venezuela-y-republica-dominicana-normalizan-relaciones-diplomaticas-y-consulares/).

## Background: The 2024 Rupture

The Dominican Republic was among seven Latin American countries that refused to recognize the results of Venezuela's July 28, 2024 presidential election, in which Nicolás Maduro was declared the winner amid widespread allegations of fraud. On July 29, 2024, Venezuela's Foreign Ministry recalled its diplomatic personnel from these countries and demanded their diplomats leave Venezuela, as [noted by TeleSUR](https://www.telesurenglish.net/venezuela-and-the-dominican-republic-agree-to-normalize-bilateral-relations/).

The rupture was not the first between the two nations. In 2019, Venezuela and the Dominican Republic suspended normal relations when the previous government of Danilo Medina recognized Juan Guaidó as president of Venezuela. However, calls for normalization have persisted. As far back as February 2023, Dominican Republic congresspeople advocated for restoring bilateral relations, citing the deep historical ties between the two peoples, as documented by the [Orinoco Tribune](https://orinocotribune.com/dominican-republic-congresspeople-advocate-for-normalizing-relations-with-venezuela/).

## A New Political Landscape

The agreement comes against the backdrop of Venezuela's dramatic political transition. Maduro was abducted by US forces in a military raid on Venezuela in January 2026, and the interim government led by Delcy Rodríguez has pursued renewed engagement with formerly estranged partners as part of a larger realignment with US interests in the region, as [reported by LBC Group](https://www.lbcgroup.tv/news/world-news/950282/venezuela-dominican-republic-agree-to-restore-ties-after-dispute-over/en).

This normalization is part of a broader trend. Chile announced the restoration of diplomatic relations with Venezuela on July 31, 2026, with Chile's President José Antonio Kast stating, "As of today, Chile and Venezuela are beginning a new phase," according to [BSS News](https://www.bssnews.net/international/410457). Venezuela has also announced the gradual restoration of consular relations with Peru, as [Prensa Latina reported](https://www.prensa-latina.cu/2026/08/02/venezuela-y-republica-dominicana-acuerdan-normalizar-relaciones/).

## Voices from the Diaspora

For the significant Venezuelan diaspora in the Dominican Republic—estimated at over 60,000 people—the restoration of consular services carries immediate practical significance. Venezuelan residents have expressed cautious optimism about the announcement. "Let the embassies open. Let it not remain a simple communiqué, like what happened in February when a communiqué was issued but we never saw the embassy here open," said Greisy, a Venezuelan resident in the Dominican Republic, in an interview reported by [El Nacional](https://www.elnacional.com/2026/08/venezolanos-en-republica-dominicana-apoyan-el-reinicio-de-las-relaciones-entre-ambos-paises/).

Another resident, Nathali, described the agreement as "progress," noting that "in my case, my daughter is Dominican, but she doesn't have Venezuelan papers, so it's complicated to enter the country without those documents." The reopening of consular offices would allow citizens to regularize their migration status and access essential services.

## Analysis and Implications

The agreement signals the interim government's commitment to repairing bilateral relationships severed during the Maduro era. The normalization efforts align with the broader strategy of closer ties with Washington and US interests in the region, including opening Venezuelan energy sources to US investment and greater access to natural resources, as [El Nacional reported](https://www.elnacional.com/2026/08/venezuela-y-republica-dominicana-acordaron-iniciar-un-proceso-de-normalizacion-gradual-de-sus-relaciones/).

The foreign ministries indicated that no exact dates have yet been set for high-level governmental bilateral political meetings in the short term. The technical teams are actively working on the implementation details, with the roadmap to be developed by mutual agreement, according to [Infobae](https://www.infobae.com/america/agencias/2026/08/02/venezuela-y-republica-dominicana-acuerdan-normalizar-relaciones-diplomaticas-y-consulares/).

## What to Watch For

The key question now is whether the agreement will translate into concrete action. The February 2026 agreement to restore flights between Caracas and Santo Domingo, along with consular services, was met with skepticism when the promised embassy reopening did not materialize. As the diaspora community has made clear, the proof will be in the opening of embassy doors and the resumption of consular services on the ground.

Observers will also be watching whether other countries that severed ties with Venezuela in 2024 follow the path of Chile, Peru, and now the Dominican Republic. With Venezuela's interim government pursuing a policy of regional reintegration, further normalization announcements may be on the horizon.
