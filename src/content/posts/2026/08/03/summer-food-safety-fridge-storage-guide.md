# Summer Food Safety: Why Your Fridge Isn't a Safe

As summer temperatures soar across China, health officials are warning families that their refrigerators are not the food "safes" many believe them to be. In a practical guide published by [People's Daily](https://www.peopleapp.com/column/30052829014-500007630842), food safety experts explain that low temperatures only inhibit microbial growth—they cannot kill bacteria. Improper storage can lead to food spoilage and pose serious health risks, particularly for the elderly, children, pregnant women, and those with weakened immune systems.

## The Science Behind the Warning

Wang Bei, Vice Dean and Professor at the School of Food and Health at Beijing Technology and Business University, told [People's Daily Online](http://health.people.com.cn/n1/2026/0803/c14739-40772632.html) that microorganisms multiply rapidly between 5°C and 60°C, causing food to spoil. While refrigerator cold storage can lower food temperature below 4°C and slow bacterial growth, it cannot fully suppress it.

More concerning is Listeria monocytogenes, a bacterium that can grow slowly even in refrigerated environments. Commonly found in ready-to-eat meat products and cold dishes, it poses a serious threat to people with weakened immune systems, according to Wang.

## Three Key Principles for Safe Storage

### 1. Classify Foods and Refrigerate Appropriately

Not all foods belong in the refrigerator. Perishable items—cooked meats, fish and shrimp, eggs and dairy, soy products, cut fruits and vegetables, and leftovers—have high moisture and nutrient content and breed bacteria easily, so they need prompt refrigeration.

However, some foods should stay out. Tropical fruits like bananas and mangoes suffer cold damage at low temperatures, turning black and losing their texture. Potatoes, onions, and garlic are better stored in cool, dry, ventilated places, as [China News Service](https://www.chinanews.com/jk/2026/08-03/10671009.shtml) reported.

### 2. Separate Storage Zones to Prevent Cross-Contamination

One of the biggest hidden dangers in refrigerator storage is cross-contamination between raw and cooked foods. Juices from raw meat and seafood can carry pathogenic bacteria that contaminate ready-to-eat items.

The guide recommends following the "cooked on top, raw on bottom" principle: upper shelves for cooked foods and ready-to-eat items, lower shelves for raw meat and seafood. The freezer should be kept at -18°C and follow the same principle for pastries, ice cream, and fresh ingredients.

Additional tips from the guide include storing food in small portions to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles, leaving 30% of refrigerator space empty for proper air circulation, and promptly cleaning up spills to prevent mold growth.

### 3. Rationally Assess and Safely Consume Leftovers

Whether leftover food can be eaten has nothing to do with whether it's been "overnight." What matters is refrigeration timeliness, sealing, storage duration, and reheating effectiveness, as [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/sci-tech/20260803/69a78550ade842549b2cd30140816a48/c.html) detailed.

Before eating, check the food's condition. Discard immediately if it shows discoloration, stickiness, mold, or an off smell—do not rely on reheating to kill bacteria in spoiled food. Leftovers in normal condition must be thoroughly reheated before eating.

In summer at room temperature, meat dishes and leafy vegetable leftovers should not be kept for more than 2 hours. When temperatures exceed 32°C, this should be shortened to 1 hour. Stewed dishes and cooked meats can be refrigerated for 3-4 days, while meat soups and juices should be consumed within 1-2 days.

## A Broader Food Safety Context

This guidance comes at a critical time. According to Chinese health authorities, June through September is the peak season for foodborne illnesses, with events and cases during this period accounting for approximately two-thirds of the annual total. About two-thirds of foodborne illness events occur in households.

At a [National Health Commission press conference](https://news.ycwb.com/ikimvkntim/content_54139818.htm) in late May, Fan Yongxiang, Deputy Director of the National Food Safety Risk Assessment Center, emphasized three household safety habits: habitual cleaning, separating raw and cooked foods, and cooking thoroughly.

## What This Means for Your Kitchen

Wang Bei's advice is straightforward: "Refrigerator freshness preservation focuses on controlling temperature, time, and cleanliness." In summer, not all foods need refrigeration. Promptly refrigerating perishable foods, storing cold-sensitive foods in ventilated dark places, separating raw and cooked items, and properly storing and reheating leftovers can effectively avoid dietary risks.

The article, which has been viewed over 1 million times on the People's Daily app, reflects growing public concern about food safety during China's hot summer months. As temperatures continue to rise, these practical guidelines offer a simple framework for keeping families safe—one meal at a time.

As the guide emphasizes, the refrigerator is a tool for slowing spoilage, not a magic box that makes food safe indefinitely. Understanding its limitations and following proper storage practices is the key to preventing foodborne illness this summer.
