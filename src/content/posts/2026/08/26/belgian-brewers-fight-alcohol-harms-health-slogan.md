# Belgian Brewers Launch Campaign Against New 'Alcohol Harms Health' Slogan

Belgian brewers have launched a high-profile campaign against a new mandatory health warning slogan, "Alcohol harms health," that would appear on all alcohol advertising in the country. The industry argues the labeling requirement is disproportionate and could unfairly damage Belgium's renowned beer sector, which was recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage in 2016.

## Open Letter and Industry Pushback

The umbrella organization Belgische Brouwers (Belgian Brewers) published an open letter in Belgian newspapers on August 26, 2026, titled "Are these harmful moments?" ("Zijn dit schadelijke momenten?"), calling on the federal government to roll back planned stricter alcohol advertising rules. The letter was signed by 100 brewers and approximately 1,000 other stakeholders from the sector, including TV personality Viktor Verhulst, TV chef Piet Huysentruyt, and Anders party leader Frédéric De Gucht, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/26/belgische-brouwers-kanten-zich-tegen-slogan-alcohol-schaadt-de/).

The open letter argues that the new slogan would stigmatize an entire sector. "We support the fight against alcohol abuse," the association emphasized. "But we can also wage that fight without nailing an entire sector to the pillory." The letter also highlights that Belgium would become one of the first countries to explicitly label beer as harmful, as [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/opvallend-protest-van-belgische-brouwers-in-open-brief-zijn-dit-schadelijke-momenten~ac84865a/) reported.

## The New Regulation

The Belgian Council of Ministers approved the stricter rules on March 27, 2026, following a proposal by Minister of Health Frank Vandenbroucke (Vooruit). The new mandatory slogan "Alcohol schaadt de gezondheid" ("Alcohol harms health") would replace the current "Alcoholmisbruik schaadt de gezondheid" ("Alcohol abuse harms health") on all alcohol advertising. The health message must be clearly visible in a contrasting color and occupy at least 5% of the advertising surface, though it would not be required on bottle labels or packaging.

The new rules also ban free alcohol giveaways with magazines and prohibit influencers and media with large young audiences from advertising alcoholic beverages, as detailed by [Gondola Foodservice](https://www.gondolafoodservice.be/post/alcohol-schaadt-de-gezondheid-de-waarschuwing-wordt-wel-degelijk-verplicht-op-alle-reclame-in-be). The measures will take effect one year after publication of the Royal Decree, which is currently with the Council of State for advice, and must first go through a European Union notification procedure.

## Industry Concerns and Economic Context

Krishan Maudgal, CEO of Belgische Brouwers, said the warning "unnecessarily stigmatizes an entire sector that provides investments, thousands of jobs, and social cohesion." The industry generates nearly 4 billion euros in turnover, contributes approximately 1% to Belgian GDP, directly employs 6,605 people, and supports nearly 50,000 indirect jobs.

The sector is already under pressure. Beer consumption in Belgium fell 3.2% in 2025, and over the past decade, the decline is nearly 20%—about half a billion fewer glasses per year. Exports, which account for nearly 70% of Belgian beer production, fell 4.8% in 2025, according to [Horeca Magazine](https://horecamagazine.be/belgische-brouwers-luiden-de-alarmbel/). The number of breweries dropped from 411 in 2024 to 395 in 2025.

Xavier Vanhonsebrouck of Kasteel Brouwerij Vanhonsebrouck in Izegem was particularly blunt: "Admit it: this is over the top, isn't it? The government is already patronizing enough, now they also want to stigmatize our beautiful product," he told [KW.be](https://kw.be/nieuws/economie/horeca/zijn-dit-schadelijke-momenten-belgische-brouwers-verzetten-zich-in-open-brief-tegen-nieuwe-slogan-alcohol-schaadt-de-gezondheid/).

## Health Experts Defend the Measure

Frieda Matthys, chair of the Permanent Working Group Mental Health at the Hoge Gezondheidsraad (Superior Health Council), defended the new slogan. "From more recent scientific research we know that any use of alcohol carries some risk," she said. "A little alcohol carries a little risk for health, a lot of alcohol carries a lot of risk." The council changed its recommendation from "misbruik" (abuse) to "gebruik" (use) based on this evidence.

The Vlaams Expertisecentrum Alcohol en andere Drugs (VAD) also supports the mandatory health message. "Public health must take precedence over the profit margins of the alcohol industry," the organization stated, adding that "'Alcohol harms health' is a fact, and that should not be swept under the rug," as reported on the [VAD website](https://vad.be/artikels/vad-steunt-verplichte-boodschap-alcohol-schaadt-de-gezondheid/).

Minister Vandenbroucke defended the measure, saying: "Enjoying a drink together on a terrace, we all enjoy that. That's what we Belgians are known for. But alcohol is harmful. And we all actually know that." He emphasized that the goal is to inform, not to dictate: "Everyone decides for themselves how much they drink, but as a government we want to inform young people and adults correctly."

## Political Tensions and Lobby Concerns

The debate has exposed tensions within the governing coalition. The Knack magazine reported in February 2026 that Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) said the government was in contact with the sector, while Minister Vandenbroucke's office denied such consultation, as noted by [Knack](https://www.knack.be/nieuws/belgie/politiek/strengere-regels-alcoholreclame-de-wever-dialoog-sector-vandenbroucke-niet/). The alcohol lobby's influence on Belgian health policy has also come under scrutiny, with investigations documenting systematic pressure on ministers and cabinets.

## What's Next

The measures still face an EU notification procedure and require the Royal Decree to be finalized before taking effect. The brewers have called for dialogue with the government to discuss alternative measures, emphasizing their support for initiatives like the BOB campaigns against drinking and driving. Meanwhile, health organizations like VAD argue the tightening should go even further, calling for a complete ban on alcohol advertising and raising the minimum purchase age to 18.

As the debate continues, the core question remains: can Belgium balance its cherished beer culture—celebrated by UNESCO and integral to its social fabric—with growing scientific consensus that no level of alcohol consumption is risk-free? The answer will shape not only advertising rules but the future of a sector that defines much of the country's identity.
