# D.C. National Guard Deployment Extended Through 2029

It has been one year since President Donald Trump declared a "crime emergency" in Washington, D.C., and deployed National Guard troops to the nation's capital, pledging to make the city "safe and beautiful." But what seemed like a temporary mission has now been [extended through January 2029](https://apnews.com/article/national-guard-deployment-extended-trump-dc-39b886397af1a335694630d27930eb7b), raising profound questions about the federal government's role in the city's affairs, the effectiveness of the military presence, and the future of D.C. autonomy.

## A Deployment That Would Not End

On August 11, 2025, Trump announced a [federal law enforcement surge](https://apnews.com/article/trump-washington-crime-national-guard-homelessness-655bc22834223c7dc93115bbcb2b215c) in Washington, invoking Section 740 of the D.C. Home Rule Act to take control of the city's police department and activate National Guard troops. The initial deployment of 800 D.C. Guard members quickly expanded as troops from more than 20 states and territories were rotated through the capital.

As of August 4, more than 4,600 military personnel from D.C. and 24 states and territories remained deployed, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/national-guard-deployment-washington-trump-crime-66e7c6ab6beccd4146f39cff514f99c8). Approximately 9,700 National Guard troops have been rotated through the city over the past year, [Stars and Stripes reported](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2026-08-10/national-guard-dc-one-year-deployment-22510103.html).

The Pentagon confirmed in July that the mission will continue until January 20, 2029 — Inauguration Day — "or until terminated by the President," as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/15/nx-s1-5894757/national-guard-deployment-in-d-c-extended-until-2029). The deployment was previously set to expire at the end of 2026.

## The $1.4 Billion Question

Extending the deployment through 2029 will cost approximately $1.4 billion, according to a [Defense Department estimate](https://apnews.com/article/trump-national-guard-washington-dc-64017d169872fe730ef2976504302e7b) provided to Congress. The Congressional Budget Office projected the first year alone would cost around $660 million, based on a monthly troop level of about 2,900.

Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-MA), who obtained the cost estimate, criticized the expense: "While American families are getting flattened by skyrocketing costs, Donald Trump is spending $1.4 billion to keep troops on the streets in Washington for years on end. The National Guard are not pawns and taxpayer dollars are not a piggy bank for Trump's political stunts."

D.C. Council Chairman Phil Mendelson echoed the concern, noting that "taxpayers are paying more than a million dollars a day to have them walk around" and that "the presence of armed soldiers on American streets is not a good look."

## Mixed Results on Crime

Federal officials point to significant enforcement results. The U.S. Marshals Service reported over 16,000 arrests since the surge began, including 35 homicide suspects and 3,200 narcotics arrests, along with nearly 2,000 illegal firearms seized. The U.S. Attorney's Office for D.C. reported a 67% increase in charged cases, [FOX5 DC noted](https://www.fox5dc.com/news/one-year-later-mixed-reviews-dc-national-guard-deployment-amid-crime-emergency).

Metropolitan Police Department statistics show overall crime was down 20% through August 7 compared with the same period in 2025, including a 32% drop in homicides. However, crime was already falling before the deployment began — violent crime reached 30-year lows in 2024 and was down an additional 26% in 2025.

A [May 2026 report from the Niskanen Center](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/11/dc-residents-debate-safety-gains-a-year-into-trumps-guard-deployment), a nonpartisan think tank, found the deployment produced roughly a 24% reduction in opportunistic property crimes but had little to no effect on violent crime. The report noted the Guard's footprint was "misaligned with the geography of violence," with troops concentrated in tourist areas rather than high-crime neighborhoods east of the Anacostia River.

"It was an expensive tool deployed in the wrong places for the wrong types of crime," said Richard Hahn, co-author of the Niskanen report.

## Legal Challenges and Civil Liberties Concerns

D.C. Attorney General Brian Schwalb sued the Trump administration in September 2025, claiming the deployment violated the Posse Comitatus Act, which restricts the use of military personnel for domestic law enforcement. U.S. District Judge Jia Cobb ruled to end the deployment in November, but a federal appeals court granted a stay as litigation continues.

"There's a deep irony that here we are in the capital of the free world, and our president is treating it like a police state," said Scott Michelman, legal director of the ACLU of the District of Columbia. "D.C. residents have lived with our National Guard troops in our neighborhoods for about a year, despite the absence of any genuine emergency and really any reason for them to be here."

The ACLU also filed a claim in July on behalf of retired Army Captain Anna King, who alleges she was violently detained by Idaho National Guard members for her vocal criticism of the deployment.

A Senate investigation authored by Senators Gary Peters and Andy Kim in February expressed concerns that the deployment risked harming local trust in law enforcement, politicizing the armed forces, and reducing troop preparedness.

## A City Grappling with Autonomy

The deployment has become a defining issue in D.C. politics. The law enforcement surge played a major role in June's Democratic primary elections, elevating candidates who have made D.C. autonomy and statehood top priorities. Janeese Lewis George, a democratic socialist likely to become the city's next mayor, and Robert White Jr., the Democratic nominee for Congress, both campaigned on pushing back against federal encroachment.

White told AP News he plans to build "an army of residents" to target swing districts and work at "un-electing" people critical of the district — a strategy similar to one former D.C. delegate Walter Fauntroy successfully pursued in 1972.

Outgoing Mayor Muriel Bowser, who has sought to protect Home Rule while avoiding direct confrontation with Trump, called the surge an "unprecedented federal intervention" that highlighted the city's lack of statehood and autonomy. "Like all Americans, Washingtonians deserve local control and accountability," she said. "My North Star remains clear: protect Home Rule, protect D.C.'s autonomy, and protect D.C. residents."

## Concerns Ahead of Elections

As the city looks toward the midterms and the 2028 presidential election, some residents worry about what the continued military presence means for democratic processes.

"The last time this president was supposed to come out of power, Jan. 6 happened," said Alex Dodds, co-founder of FreeDC, an activist group that has protested the deployment. "Imagine if that happens again but this time he has 5,000 troops standing at the ready to be part of that situation. You know, that's our worry."

Donna Powell, a D.C. resident, put it more simply: "We're already safe and beautiful. We don't need all these National Guard here."

## What's Next

The deployment's future remains uncertain. The Pentagon has said the mission will continue through January 2029 unless terminated by the president, and the White House remains steadfast in its support. "President Trump has transformed D.C. from a crime-ridden city into a safe and beautiful haven for residents and visitors alike," White House spokeswoman Abigail Jackson said.

But with legal challenges pending, a new generation of D.C. leaders poised to take office, and questions about the $1.4 billion price tag, the debate over the National Guard's presence in the nation's capital is far from settled. As D.C. Council member Brianne K. Nadeau put it: "The fact that we still have the National Guard walking our streets a year later with no end in sight is really demoralizing."

For Washingtonians, the question is no longer whether the deployment was justified — but when, and how, it will end.
