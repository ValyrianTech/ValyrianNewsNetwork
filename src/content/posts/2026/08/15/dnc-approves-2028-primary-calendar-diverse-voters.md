# DNC Approves 2028 Primary Calendar for Diverse Voters

The Democratic National Committee approved its 2028 presidential primary calendar on Saturday at its summer meeting in Austin, Texas, cementing a dramatic shift that places South Carolina at the front of the nominating process and elevates states with significant Black, Latino, and organized labor constituencies. The [approved calendar](https://apnews.com/article/dnc-2028-presidential-calendar-democrats-south-carolina-be664f26ad5652f054c7f19e483a4d63) marks the second consecutive cycle in which the Palmetto State leads the Democratic nominating contest, following the 2024 overhaul championed by then-President Joe Biden.

The early-state lineup is South Carolina on January 22, Nevada on February 1, New Hampshire on February 8, New Mexico on February 15, Michigan on February 22, and Virginia on February 29. The schedule pushes Iowa out of the early-state window entirely for the second straight cycle, breaking with decades of tradition in which the Hawkeye State's caucuses led the Democratic calendar.

## A Strategic Shift Toward the Party's Coalition

DNC Chairman Ken Martin framed the calendar as a deliberate effort to ensure the party's nominee can appeal to the broad coalition Democrats need to win the White House. "You have to be battle-tested in a way that you already have campaigned in front of and for the support of various communities throughout the country," Martin said in an interview.

The calendar prioritizes states where Black voters—who can yield more than half of South Carolina's Democratic primary electorate—and Latino voters in Nevada and New Mexico will have an outsized say early in the process. It also elevates organized labor, with Nevada's service industry unions, Michigan's auto workers, and Virginia's federal workforce all playing prominent roles.

"They have to show that they can go to the South and talk to Black voters and rural voters, as well as out west, talking ranching and mining issues, to a border state, to union members," said party strategist Karen Finney, who has worked on multiple presidential campaigns. "Whoever survives will be battle-tested."

## A Break From Iowa and New Hampshire Dominance

The decision represents a continuation of the seismic change first implemented for the 2024 cycle. For decades, Iowa and New Hampshire—two states with overwhelmingly white electorates—led the Democratic nominating process. The [Rules and Bylaws Committee vote](https://apnews.com/article/democrats-presidential-nominating-calendar-2028-b3d153a33e39bd9e5ebcce3c58e9e84c) in late July set the stage for Saturday's full DNC ratification.

Howard Dean, the former DNC chairman and Vermont governor who helped solidify Nevada and South Carolina as early states behind Iowa and New Hampshire during his tenure, endorsed the latest change. "These states look more like the country, as a whole, whereas Iowa and New Hampshire don't," Dean said.

The shift is not without controversy. New Hampshire state law requires its presidential primary to be held at least seven days before any other state's similar contest, setting up a potential conflict with the DNC calendar. New Hampshire Democratic Party Chair Ray Buckley has been openly critical, saying the decision shows that "many party insiders have learned nothing from 2024."

Iowa Democrats have also voiced frustration. Rita Hart, chair of the Iowa Democratic Party, called the calendar vote "very messy" and a sign that the national party is "out of touch with voters."

## Progressive vs. Establishment Dynamics

The calendar could have significant implications for the party's ideological balance. South Carolina's Democratic primary voters have historically favored more moderate candidates—twice rejecting Vermont Sen. Bernie Sanders in favor of Hillary Clinton (2016) and Joe Biden (2020). In 2020, 56% of South Carolina Democratic primary voters were Black, and they preferred Biden over Sanders 61% to 17%.

Boyd Brown, a former DNC member from South Carolina, suggested the state's voters are distinct from those in other early states. "I think our primary voters up north are probably a little different than primary voters in South Carolina," Brown said. "It's not the woke ideologies that we just can't get away from in other places."

Nina Smith, a former Buttigieg adviser who works closely with progressive causes, offered a more nuanced view. Smith said Southern Black voters are "more progressive than they're given credit for" on policy but also "realists" about what is possible. "Living through Jim Crow and other oppressive structures will force you to take that approach," she said.

With South Carolina leading off and many Southern states following on Super Tuesday soon after the early lineup concludes, progressives will need to tailor their messages carefully. "A lot of Black voters don't necessarily see themselves in the ideas and arguments that progressives put forward," Smith said.

## Unions and Battleground Strategy

The early-state lineup gives organized labor a prominent platform. Nevada's Las Vegas service industry unions, Michigan's auto industry and manufacturing support industries, and Virginia's large federal workforce will all be central to the early contest.

"Labor is still so important," Dean said.

Beyond seeking union endorsements, campaigns will look to organized labor for help in spreading Democrats' message and mobilizing voters.

The calendar also has clear electoral strategy implications. Of the six early states, all but South Carolina have been competitive in recent general elections. Combined, they are worth 43 Electoral College votes—not insignificant on the path to the 270 needed to win the presidency.

Television markets also create spillover effects. Ads targeting parts of South Carolina reach portions of North Carolina and Georgia, while Virginia offers proximity to donors and national TV studios in Washington, D.C.

## A Crowded Field Ahead

The calendar decision arrives as Democrats prepare for what is expected to be a large and competitive presidential field. There has not been a truly open Democratic presidential primary—without an incumbent president or a dominant front-runner—in almost two decades.

Potential candidates who have expressed interest include Kentucky Gov. Andy Beshear, former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg, former Vice President Kamala Harris, California Gov. Gavin Newsom, Michigan Gov. Gretchen Whitmer, and Sen. Ruben Gallego of Arizona, among others. The [2028 Democratic primaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2028_Democratic_Party_presidential_primaries) are shaping up to be a wide-open contest.

## What's Next

The calendar's approval comes amid internal party turmoil, with some members calling for Martin's replacement over fundraising concerns. However, no major movement to pressure him to step down has materialized, and Martin has defended his leadership by citing the party's success in the 2025 elections.

New Hampshire and Iowa have not committed to following the new calendar, and the DNC has said penalties such as fines, loss of delegates, and potential loss of access to DNC-sanctioned debates will serve as deterrents. Martin acknowledged the uncertainty, saying the party will "have to cross that bridge when we get there."

Republicans, meanwhile, are expected to maintain their traditional early-state lineup led by Iowa and New Hampshire.

As the first milestone in what promises to be a turbulent two-year sprint to the Democratic nomination, the calendar sets the stage for a nominating contest that will test candidates' ability to connect with the diverse constituencies that form the backbone of the Democratic coalition. The states that vote first will play an outsized role in determining which candidates gain momentum, attract donors, and emerge as viable contenders.

For Democrats seeking to reclaim the White House after President Donald Trump's second term, the message is clear: candidates will need to prove themselves in states that reflect the party's full coalition—from the Black voters of South Carolina to the Latino communities of Nevada and New Mexico to the union halls of Michigan and Virginia.

"This is not a social or a debate club. It is a political party," Brown said. The point, he added, is to put a Democrat in the White House.
