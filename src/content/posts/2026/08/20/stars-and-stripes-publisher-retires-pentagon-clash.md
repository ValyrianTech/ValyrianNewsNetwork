# Stars and Stripes Publisher Retires Amid Pentagon Clash

The longtime publisher of Stars and Stripes, the U.S. military's independent newspaper, has announced his retirement, citing fundamental differences with Pentagon leadership. The departure of Max D. Lederer Jr., effective September 30, comes less than four months after the Pentagon fired the newspaper's ombudsman and amid escalating efforts by the Trump administration to exert editorial control over the storied publication.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/stars-stripes-pentagon-publisher-retire-journalism-policy-ae5277e248f51d10dfcb49d7796e7d2f), Lederer, the second full-time civilian to hold the publisher position since taking the helm in 2007, wrote in a memo to staff that it had "become clear that my philosophy of leadership, and my understanding of the value and mission of Stars and Stripes, differ in fundamental ways from the direction the leadership of the Department of Defense has for the organization."

## A Historic Institution Under Pressure

Stars and Stripes was first published by Union troops during the Civil War in 1861 and has been published continuously since World War II. The newspaper reaches approximately 1.4 million readers daily, mostly online, while maintaining a print edition overseas for service members in remote locations with unreliable internet access.

The publication's mission statement emphasizes that it is "editorially independent of interference from outside its own editorial chain-of-command" and is unique among news organizations tied to the Defense Department in being "governed by the principles of the First Amendment." Congress mandated this independence in the late 1980s after lawmakers were alarmed by attempts to suppress unfavorable news coverage of the Iran-Contra affair, and created the position of ombudsman in 1991 to safeguard the arrangement.

## Pentagon's Push for Control

The retirement announcement marks the latest chapter in a year-long confrontation between the Pentagon and the newspaper. In January 2026, chief Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell posted on X that the department would "refocus" Stars and Stripes content away from what he called "woke distractions that syphon morale." On the same day, the Pentagon rescinded the federal regulation that had given the newspaper legal protection from interference.

In March, the Pentagon issued an eight-page memo titled "Modernization of Stars and Stripes Operations," which expanded Defense Department oversight, limited wire service use, barred comics and syndicated features, and required that content be consistent with "good order and discipline"—a phrase borrowed from the Uniform Code of Military Justice. As [Stars and Stripes reported](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2026-03-13/pentagon-modernization-plan-stars-and-stripes-21051529.html), the memo was written without the newspaper's input, and Lederer said he was not contacted directly by anyone from the Pentagon about the changes despite reaching out to them.

Lederer expressed particular concern about the planned transition from print to digital, telling Stars and Stripes, "I don't feel that there's a full recognition of the value of multiple platforms versus only a digital platform." He feared the move would limit accessibility for troops in remote locations.

## Ombudsman Fired, Legal Challenges Mount

The Pentagon fired Jacqueline Smith, the newspaper's ombudsman, in April—just days after she published a column titled "The Pentagon is trying to silence me." In that column, which was published in [Stars and Stripes](https://www.stripes.com/opinion/2026-04-23/stripes-former-ombudsman-pentagon-trying-to-silence-21465037.html), Smith wrote: "No one should be surprised that they're kicking out the one person charged by Congress with protecting Stars and Stripes' editorial independence." She added: "This newspaper has a long history of commitment to the military community and to journalistic values. Please don't let it be controlled by Pentagon brass."

Smith has since filed a lawsuit challenging her termination as retaliatory and a violation of her First Amendment rights. Two members of the newspaper's publisher advisory board also filed a federal lawsuit in June alleging that the Pentagon's actions violate federal law and "erode [Stripes'] longstanding editorial independence and First Amendment protections," as [Stars and Stripes reported](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2026-06-03/lawsuit-pentagon-independence-stripes-21862545.html).

## Pentagon Reportedly Considering Early Dismissal

According to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/pentagon-considers-firing-stars-stripes-publisher/), Pentagon officials have discussed firing Lederer before his scheduled retirement as the administration strategizes an overhaul of the military news outlet. CBS News reported that about $15 million of Stars and Stripes' $29 million annual budget comes from tax dollars, with the remainder derived from subscriptions and advertisements.

The pressure on the newspaper has intensified following its recent reporting on conditions aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln aircraft carrier, which has been on an extended deployment in the Middle East. Stars and Stripes was among the first outlets to report on deteriorating conditions, low morale, and suicide attempts among sailors—concerns that President Donald Trump has downplayed.

Editor-in-chief Erik Slavin has publicly defended the newspaper's independence, telling [CBS News Sunday Morning](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/the-battle-over-stars-and-stripes/) that the "foxhole" he would be willing to die in would be over editorial independence from the Pentagon. "We need to be able to provide independent news to service members," Slavin said. "If we can't do that, if we were turned into something other than that, if we were public relations? Yeah, that's the foxhole."

## Congressional and Press Freedom Concerns

The Pentagon's actions have drawn bipartisan concern from lawmakers. In April, Senators Elizabeth Warren, Richard Blumenthal, Ruben Gallego, and Tammy Duckworth sent a letter to Deputy Secretary of Defense Steve Feinberg warning that the new policy "threatens the credibility of Stars and Stripes, and the reliable flow of unbiased news to service members, and contradicts decades of Congressional reforms that guard against censorship at the paper." Representative Jamie Raskin also sent a letter to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, signed by 38 other House members, expressing "great alarm" about political interference.

Press freedom advocates have echoed these concerns. Timothy Richardson, program director of journalism and disinformation for PEN America, said the Pentagon's actions "clearly show that the Pentagon is trying to increase its influence over content decisions at Stars and Stripes. That's a threat to the First Amendment, certainly, and the independence that the Congress has long guaranteed for the newsroom."

The broader context includes the Pentagon's wider efforts to restrict media access, including imposing new restrictions on journalists working inside the Pentagon, declaring its press office a classified space, and requiring journalists to be accompanied by escorts on Pentagon grounds.

## What's Next

As [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/20/stars-and-stripes-publisher-firing-uss-abraham-lincoln), the Pentagon's reported consideration of firing Lederer before his scheduled retirement raises questions about whether the administration will move more aggressively to reshape the newspaper. Lederer has said he will "do everything I can to make sure the transition is smooth" until the end of September.

For the 1.4 million daily readers of Stars and Stripes—and for the broader principle of press freedom—the coming months will be critical. The newspaper, which Gen. John Pershing once called "your paper" for the soldiers it serves, faces an uncertain future as the Pentagon seeks to redefine its mission. Whether the institution can maintain its editorial independence will depend on the outcome of ongoing legal challenges, congressional oversight, and the resolve of its journalists.

As Jacqueline Smith warned in her final column: "What you can worry about is the future of Stars and Stripes." The question now is whether the storied publication can survive the pressure being exerted upon it.
