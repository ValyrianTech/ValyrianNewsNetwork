# Sunflowers on the Rise: Flemish Brabant Leads Belgium in Sunflower Fields

Flemish Brabant has emerged as Belgium's leading province in sunflower cultivation, with more than 60 percent of the total Flemish sunflower harvest now grown on its territory. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/07/zonnebloemen-vlaams-brabant-faunamengsel-subsidies/), the province's sunflower fields have more than doubled since 2024, reflecting a broader shift toward sustainable and climate-resilient agriculture across Flanders.

![Sunflower field in Flemish Brabant](https://cdn.assets.prezly.com/b4ab6380-5520-40f9-9f04-d1ada545cb4a/-/preview/-/format/auto/DSCN2435.JPG)

## A Province-Wide Phenomenon

Of the 55 hectares of dedicated sunflower cultivation across all of Flanders, approximately 33.5 hectares now lie in Flemish Brabant—more than double the area recorded in 2024, according to figures from the Flemish Agency for Agriculture and Fisheries. "The popularity of sunflower cultivation has been increasing in recent years throughout Flanders, but it is especially a Flemish Brabant story," said Els Gils of Praktijkpunt Landbouw Vlaams-Brabant, the region's agricultural knowledge center.

The trend extends well beyond dedicated sunflower fields. Fauna-friendly seed mixtures—blends of grains, herbs, and flowers including sunflowers—now cover 2,534 hectares in Flemish Brabant, up from 1,998 hectares the previous year. These mixtures serve as winter shelter and food sources for farmland birds, attract bees and butterflies, and improve soil quality by preventing erosion and nutrient runoff.

"The vast majority of the yellow fields you see are in fact a fauna mixture and not real sunflower cultivation," Gils noted.

## Climate Resilience Drives Interest

The growing interest in sunflowers is closely tied to climate change. Sunflowers are naturally drought-resistant, with deep, strong roots that can reach up to two meters, allowing them to thrive in the dry, warm summers that are becoming increasingly common in Belgium. As [the Province of Flemish Brabant](https://pers.vlaamsbrabant.be/genieten-van-een-zonnebloemenzee-in-vlaams-brabant) highlighted in its earlier project launch, "climate change is making itself felt and presents us with challenges. Farmers are looking for answers to how to deal with weather extremes. And that is where the sunflower comes into the picture."

![Sunflower field research in Flemish Brabant](https://cdn.assets.prezly.com/4fafda42-780a-41d9-9798-092765637f1f/-/preview/-/format/auto/20220708_zonnebloem_5%20(2).JPG)

This climate rationale was a key driver when the Praktijkpunt Landbouw first launched its sunflower cultivation project in 2022. As coordinator Rana Yurduseven explained at the time, "in the context of climate change, we are looking for crops that grow well in dry and warm years." The project received €110,000 in funding from Europe, Flanders, and the province, as [reported by Made in](https://www.made-in.be/vlaams-brabant/landbouwers-in-de-regio-experimenteren-met-zonnebloemen/).

## Economic Pressures Reshape Farmland

The sunflower surge comes amid significant economic pressures on Flemish agriculture. Potato acreage has fallen 23 percent due to oversupply and low prices, while sugar beet area is down 13 percent amid a European sugar surplus. As [VILT reported](https://vilt.be/nl/nieuws/landbouwers-ruilen-aardappelen-en-suikerbieten-voor-ecoregelingen-maar-is-dit-verstandig), farmers are increasingly turning to biodiversity-friendly crops as a secure income source, with the area of such crops exceeding 10,000 hectares for the first time in 2026.

Farmers can receive up to €1,500 per hectare for fauna-friendly seed mixtures under the EU's eco-regulation scheme, though the maximum amount is not guaranteed and depends on available budget. "The preliminary 2026 areas are proof that our Flemish farmers are true entrepreneurs," said Jo Brouns, Flemish Minister of Agriculture and Environment, as quoted by VILT. "At the same time, they also choose to make extra efforts for the environment and nature, with positive effects for farmland birds and soil health."

## A Farmer's Perspective

For some farmers, sunflowers are about more than subsidies. Farmer Guy Renders planted two hectares of sunflowers for crop rotation and soil health, deliberately declining government subsidies so he could harvest and sell the flowers. "If you do that, you are no longer allowed to harvest or sell the flowers," he told VRT NWS. "We prefer to cut them. That way we sell 30 to 40 bouquets per day."

The field has become a local attraction, drawing passersby who stop for the flowers and often pick up potatoes or cherries along the way. "People become happy when they pass by here," Renders said. "And whoever gets a flower, is happy and comes back."

## Beyond the Blooms

While the visual appeal of sunflower fields is undeniable, their practical value extends further. Sunflower seeds are primarily used for animal feed and birdseed, with only very limited local production of sunflower oil. Large-scale sunflower oil production—such as that used by chip manufacturers—does not yet occur in Belgium.

However, the trend shows no signs of slowing. As [Boerenbond](https://www.boerenbond.be/meerboerdanjedenkt/waarom-akkers-soms-vol-bloemen-staan) notes, flowering fields offer multiple benefits: they protect soil from erosion, improve soil structure through deep root systems, store carbon in the ground, and create attractive landscapes that draw visitors. Similar trends are visible in the Netherlands, where [Omroep Brabant](https://www.omroepbrabant.nl/nieuws/6022066/dit-is-waarom-je-steeds-meer-zonnebloemvelden-ziet-in-brabant) reports that farmers are increasingly using sunflower fields as winter food sources for wildlife.

## What to Watch For

As climate change continues to reshape agricultural conditions, sunflowers are likely to play an increasingly prominent role in Belgian farming. The key question will be whether the eco-regulation subsidies can keep pace with demand—in 2024, so many farmers enrolled that the maximum payment could not be disbursed. With fauna mixture areas continuing to expand, the sustainability of the subsidy system itself may become the next challenge for Flemish agriculture.

For now, the golden fields of Flemish Brabant stand as a vivid symbol of how farmers are adapting to a changing climate while supporting biodiversity and soil health—one sunflower at a time.
