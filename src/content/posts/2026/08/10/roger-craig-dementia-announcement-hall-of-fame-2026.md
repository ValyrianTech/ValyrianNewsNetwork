# Roger Craig Reveals Vascular Dementia in Hall of Fame Speech

CANTON, Ohio — Roger Craig, the former San Francisco 49ers star running back, revealed during his Pro Football Hall of Fame induction speech on Saturday that he has been diagnosed with vascular dementia, saying his doctors believe the condition "may be related, at least in part, to the concussions" he experienced during his playing career, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/roger-craig-dementia-49ers-hall-of-fame-3946ac71bd8989389a9a373b44dd6be2).

The 66-year-old made the announcement nearly eight minutes into a nine-minute prerecorded video message played while he sat on stage wearing his gold jacket at Tom Benson Stadium in Canton, Ohio. Presented by former teammate Jamie Williams, Craig received a standing ovation from the crowd and hugs from the many Hall of Famers on stage.

"The changes were gradual," Craig said. "At first, they were easy to dismiss as normal aging. Over time, my family noticed, too."

## A Pioneering Career

Craig's induction capped a remarkable career that redefined the running back position. Selected by the 49ers in the second round of the 1983 draft as a fullback out of Nebraska, Craig became one of the [most versatile players](https://apnews.com/article/hall-of-fame-roger-craig-c3756a7fcf3e1b95df1c44ac61322f9d) in league history. He excelled as a runner, receiver and blocker, helping the 49ers win three of their five Super Bowls.

In 1985, Craig became the first player in NFL history to record 1,000 yards rushing and 1,000 yards receiving in a single season — a feat only two other backs have matched since. He was named the AP NFL Offensive Player of the Year in 1988 and was a first-team All-Pro that same season.

Craig waited 28 years for his Hall of Fame induction, elected as a seniors finalist. He entered the Class of 2026 alongside Drew Brees, Larry Fitzgerald, Luke Kuechly and Adam Vinatieri.

## A Courageous Revelation

In his speech, Craig addressed his diagnosis with characteristic grace, acknowledging the complex relationship between the game that defined his life and the health challenges he now faces.

"Two things could be true," Craig said. "Football gave me opportunities beyond anything I can imagine. It helped shape the man I became. At the same time, we know more today about brain health than when I played. I hope sharing my story encourages people to pay attention to their health and seek help when something doesn't feel right."

He added: "I want people to know that this diagnosis doesn't define me. I am still Roger Craig. I am still a father, still a husband, a teammate, a friend and someone who loves this game and the people who brought it to my life. If sharing my story helps even one family or one former player, then it's worth it."

Craig's decision to go public was deeply personal. His son, Rogdrick, told [USA TODAY Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/nfl-greats-said-backstage-roger-113116970.html) that the family believed it was "super important" to share the diagnosis to help other players.

"There are a lot of players that deal with this, and they don't say anything," Rogdrick said. "By the time they get older, it's already too late. We're big proponents of getting on offense early and making sure you're talking to your doctor. He just wants to help as many people as possible. He's the bravest person I know. My hero. I'm so proud of him."

## Reactions from Football Royalty

The announcement resonated deeply among the Hall of Famers gathered in Canton. Emmitt Smith, the NFL's all-time leading rusher, shook his head backstage and offered a stark assessment: "It's a brutal game. You learn a lot. It teaches you. But damn."

Former teammates paid tribute to Craig's pioneering role in the sport. Steve Young, the Hall of Fame quarterback who played alongside Craig, called him "a pioneer" whose versatility changed what was expected of running backs.

"Our offense allowed for new things to happen, and it demanded running backs who could catch the football, and run routes, and be dynamic and still line up in the eye and go downhill off tackle," Young said. "It asked more of anyone and Roger answered that."

Christian McCaffrey, the current 49ers running back who matched Craig's 1,000-1,000 feat in 2019, said: "I think about him as a pioneer and someone who set the standard for guys like myself."

## Broader Concerns About Brain Health

Craig's announcement highlights ongoing concerns about brain health in professional football. In the past year, the families of several former NFL players have donated their brains for examination for Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy (CTE), with results publicly revealed for Dallas Cowboys defensive end Marshawn Kneeland and Hall of Fame Bears player Steve McMichael — both of whom were found to have the condition.

Vascular dementia, the second-most common type of dementia behind Alzheimer's disease, is a decline in thinking skills caused by conditions that block or reduce blood flow and oxygen to the brain. Craig was one of the league's most durable tailbacks in his prime, missing just six games across his eight-year 49ers career, and he acknowledged suffering multiple head injuries as a player.

## A Legacy Beyond the Field

Craig finished his career with 8,189 rushing yards, 4,911 receiving yards and 73 total touchdowns. He was a four-time Pro Bowl selection and won three Super Bowls with the 49ers. His 1985 season remains one of the most remarkable individual achievements in NFL history.

As he takes his place among football's immortals, Craig's decision to share his diagnosis may prove as impactful as anything he accomplished on the field. His message was clear: awareness, early action and openness can make a difference.

"Football gave me everything: opportunity, discipline, lifelong friends and the platform to grow as a man," Craig said. "This honor belongs to my parents who started it all."
