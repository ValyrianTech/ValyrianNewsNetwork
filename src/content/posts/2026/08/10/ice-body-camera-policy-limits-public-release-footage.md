# ICE body camera policy limits public release of footage

Immigration and Customs Enforcement is moving to equip all field officers with body-worn cameras by the end of September, but the agency's policy gives its director broad discretion over what footage the public will ever see, specifying that releases must be in the agency's "best interests." The rollout comes after recent fatal shootings by ICE officers intensified pressure for accountability that the cameras could provide.

## A Policy Shift Toward Discretion

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/ice-body-cameras-officers-shootings-dab1b294c8dc412d9ef8557ccbebfd57), ICE's body-worn camera policy, issued in February 2025, states that the agency will promptly release video of shootings and other encounters in which agents cause death or serious injury only after determining "it is in the best interests of the agency" to do so. The policy gives the ICE director authority to release recordings that reflect well on the agency's image while potentially keeping others secret, according to experts on body camera policies.

The February 2025 policy represents a significant departure from ICE's 2023 standard, which directed the agency to expedite the public release of body camera footage in certain circumstances. The new "best interests" standard reverses that presumption: instead of a default toward release, it creates a default toward discretion.

Christopher Schneider, a professor at Brandon University in Canada and co-author of the book "Police Body-Worn Cameras: Media and the New Discourse of Police Reform," said the policy's explicit language is unusual. "In some ways, ICE is saying the quiet part out loud," he told AP. "Body-worn cameras are being used as contemporary image-work tools to present the police to the public in the most favorable way possible."

Katherine Hawkins, senior legal analyst at the nonprofit Project On Government Oversight, was more blunt. "Without any clear way for the public to get access to it, it doesn't do much good," she said. "It's not unheard of for agencies to delay or withhold or selectively edit footage at all, but to say that explicitly just really gives the game away."

## Shootings That Prompted the Rollout

The camera deployment follows two fatal shootings in July that were not captured on video. On July 7, an ICE officer shot and killed Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 52-year-old Houston homebuilder who was driving to work and was not the target of the ICE operation. Days later, on July 13, ICE officer David Brouillette fatally shot Johan Sebastián Durán Guerrero, a 25-year-old Colombian man in Biddeford, Maine. Brouillette had a documented history of violent behavior toward family members.

Neither shooting was recorded with body cameras, angering members of Congress who had given ICE $20 million to purchase body cameras in a bill passed to end a partial government shutdown on April 30. ICE spent $30.9 million in July to purchase body camera equipment from Axon, the Arizona-based company that also manufactures Tasers, federal records show. The spending began one day after the Maine shooting.

An investigation by [The Intercept](https://theintercept.com/2026/07/16/ice-maine-shooting-body-cameras/) revealed that ICE officers at the Maine shooting scene were wearing Motorola SVX Video Remote Speaker Microphones — devices with camera capabilities that were only being used as radio mics because of the Axon contract. The cameras on these devices had covers clipped over the camera lenses, suggesting that even when officers appear to be wearing cameras, the recording function may not be active.

## ICE Defends the Policy

Acting ICE Director David J. Venturella pushed back against the AP report, saying the policy was "mischaracterized." In an [official statement](https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/acting-ice-director-david-j-venturella-issues-statement-mischaracterization-ices-body), Venturella said the approach mirrors other federal law enforcement agencies and is fully consistent with the Freedom of Information Act and the Privacy Act.

"ICE policy permits expedited release when appropriate — and clarifies that it's not appropriate to release footage when doing so could compromise investigations or privacy," Venturella said. He added that ICE is "on track to equip every officer and agent in the field with body-worn cameras by the end of August," ahead of the original September timeline.

A DHS spokesperson told [KSAT](https://www.ksat.com/news/local/2026/08/08/ice-to-require-body-cameras-policy-says-footage-will-be-released-in-agencys-best-interest/) that body camera footage "must be reviewed on a case-by-case basis due to privacy concerns for innocent bystanders," and noted a more than 1,300% increase in assaults against ICE officers and a 3,300% increase in vehicle attacks.

The Freedom of Information Foundation of Texas responded sharply: "The standard of 'in the agency's best interests' has never been an allowed argument to withhold information and creates in the agency an entity whose interests and priorities are independent from and above those of the constituent public. On its face, this standard is designed to foreclose transparency."

## Congressional Pressure Builds

The issue has become politically charged. Sen. Susan Collins (R-ME), who helped secure the $20 million in funding, warned that if ICE does not reverse its policy, she would introduce legislation to reinstate the 2023 expedited-release standard. Sen. Angus King (I-ME), who led a 38-senator letter demanding accountability, said the policy "defeats the whole purpose of body-worn cameras and makes a mockery of congressional action to fund the body-worn cameras in the first place."

White House border czar Tom Homan has defended the cameras as a tool that helps officers. "I think the American people need to see what that officer sees and hears," he said on CBS's "Face the Nation" in July. "And if you look at the data, body cameras actually exonerate agents more than they, you know, provide evidence of wrongdoing."

Chuck Wexler, executive director of the Police Executive Research Forum, said transparency is essential. "The community has a right to know, and very often it helps put the actions of the agents in some perspective," he said. "ICE could go a long way in building credibility with the larger community by being more transparent."

## A Pattern of Delays

ICE began testing body cameras in 2021 during President Joe Biden's administration and distributed 1,400 devices three years later. After returning to office in 2025, President Donald Trump rescinded a Biden order requiring federal law enforcement agencies to use body cameras. ICE did not buy body cameras even after receiving a historic $75 billion infusion in 2025, and the July purchases from Axon were made through an existing government contract that began in 2023.

Jason Houser, who was ICE chief of staff under Biden and helped implement the agency's pilot program, said the deployment was unnecessarily slow. "This is not hard to deploy, but they've slow-rolled it," he said. "This doesn't fix the problem of putting officers in a place where they're not trained or equipped to carry out the style of operations that the administration is asking them to do."

ICE's sister agency, Customs and Border Protection, has not released footage of the January shooting of Alex Pretti in Minneapolis even though it was captured on agents' cameras, as [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/ice-will-only-release-body-camera-video-when-in-agencys-best-interests-policy-says) reported. CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott told lawmakers the video was under investigation and would be made public "when it's appropriate."

## What's Next

The question of whether ICE will release footage from the Houston and Maine shootings remains open, though experts say it is unlikely under the current policy. Sen. Collins has threatened legislation to reinstate the 2023 expedited-release standard if ICE does not voluntarily reverse its policy. Courts could also compel footage disclosure even when the agency refuses to release it voluntarily.

ICE says body cameras have been sent to more than half its field officers, with the rest to receive them by the end of September. At least one member of every arrest team will be required to have a camera. But as the policy stands, the public may never see what those cameras record — unless the agency decides it serves its own interests.
