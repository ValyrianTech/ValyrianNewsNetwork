# National Guard Deployment in DC Extended Through 2029

It has been 12 months since President Donald Trump announced a crime emergency in Washington, pledging to make the city "safe and beautiful" and deploying the National Guard. But a mission that seemed temporary has been [extended through January 2029](https://apnews.com/article/national-guard-deployment-washington-trump-crime-66e7c6ab6beccd4146f39cff514f99c8), leaving residents and a new cadre of local officials questioning why troops remain on the streets of the nation's capital.

"There's a deep irony that here we are in the capital of the free world, and our president is treating it like a police state," said Scott Michelman, legal director of the ACLU of the District of Columbia. "D.C. residents have lived with our National Guard troops in our neighborhoods for about a year, despite the absence of any genuine emergency and really any reason for them to be here."

## A Temporary Mission That Never Ended

On Aug. 11, 2025, Trump announced he was launching [a federal law enforcement surge](https://apnews.com/article/trump-washington-crime-national-guard-homelessness-655bc22834223c7dc93115bbcb2b215c) in Washington aimed at fighting crime. He invoked Section 740 of the District of Columbia Home Rule Act in an executive order to take over the city's police force and signed a directive for Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to activate the National Guard.

As of Aug. 4, more than 4,600 military personnel from D.C. and 24 states and territories were still deployed alongside unknown numbers of less visible federal law enforcement officers. During the summer of 2026, around the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence, troop numbers surged to approximately 5,000.

Small groups of armed guard members have become a routine presence on the streets. They have supported arrest operations but are also seen patrolling tourist areas, such as along the Tidal Basin during the Cherry Blossom Festival and in neighborhoods like Georgetown where violent crimes are rare. They've helped with medical emergencies, snow removal and beautification projects.

## A $1.4 Billion Price Tag

Extending the deployment into 2029 will cost approximately [$1.4 billion](https://apnews.com/article/trump-national-guard-washington-dc-64017d169872fe730ef2976504302e7b), according to a Defense Department estimate for fiscal years 2027-2029. The estimate assumes an average of 2,500 Guard members through January 2029. The Congressional Budget Office projected the first-year cost at around $660 million based on monthly troop levels of about 2,900. Current costs exceed $3 million per day, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/14/nx-s1-5892498/national-guard-deployment-dc-2029).

"While American families are getting flattened by skyrocketing costs, Donald Trump is spending $1.4 billion to keep troops on the streets in Washington for years on end," said Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-MA), who obtained the cost estimate. "The National Guard are not pawns and taxpayer dollars are not a piggy bank for Trump's political stunts."

The Project on Government Oversight estimates total costs could reach $2.5-$3.4 billion.

## The Crime Debate

The White House has touted the deployment as a success. "President Trump has transformed D.C. from a crime-ridden city into a safe and beautiful haven for residents and visitors alike," White House spokeswoman Abigail Jackson said in an email.

Figures posted to the U.S. attorney's office for the District of Columbia credit the surge with more than 13,000 arrests. Metropolitan Police Department statistics show overall crime was down 20% through Aug. 7 compared with the same period in 2025, including a 32% drop in homicides, from 97 to 66.

But there's a debate over how much of that success is owed to Trump's mission or to years-long local efforts. Crime was already falling before the surge began, and two studies have found the National Guard presence has had little to no effect on violent crime. The [Niskanen Center study](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/04/nx-s1-5845073/national-guard-washington-dc-crime) found a 24% decrease in "opportunistic" crimes — like property crimes and vehicle break-ins — but called the deployment a "blunt and expensive instrument."

## Legal Challenges and Local Opposition

A [lawsuit challenging the deployment](https://apnews.com/article/national-guard-trump-dc-4a0268d1185017c042d425c3133f0322) is pending. In November 2025, U.S. District Court Judge Jia Cobb ruled that Trump's military takeover illegally intrudes on local officials' authority. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit temporarily paused that ruling in December 2025. Deployments to other cities, including California and Illinois, have been blocked or paused by courts.

"The fact that we still have the National Guard walking our streets a year later with no end in sight is really demoralizing," said D.C. Council member Brianne K. Nadeau. "There isn't really any political liability for national leaders to mess with D.C. because nobody really cares."

Because Washington is a congressionally established federal district, Trump has much greater influence over the police and D.C. National Guard, which he controls directly, including its commander. The Home Rule Act, passed in 1973, allows residents to vote for local leaders, but Congress retains oversight over the local budget and review authority of all laws passed.

## Political Fallout and New Leadership

The law enforcement surge played a major role in [Democratic primary elections](https://apnews.com/article/washington-dc-primaries-bowser-norton-trump-8d4aa81d46e089de5c2c83c718d7fe07) in June, which elevated candidates who have made Washington's autonomy and statehood top priorities. Janeese Lewis George, a democratic socialist likely to be the city's next mayor, has already rankled Trump, who threatened to put the city under federal control if she wins. Robert White Jr., another pro-statehood candidate, won the Democratic primary for the city's seat in Congress.

"My election means we're going to keep our independence and we're going to get statehood," White told the Associated Press. "People know I'm not going to lay down. I'm going to fight."

White said he plans to build "an army of residents" and allies to target swing districts and work at "un-electing" people critical of the district.

Outgoing Mayor Muriel Bowser has taken a cautious approach, avoiding the kind of biting rhetoric typical of other high-profile Democratic leaders. "We're more than monuments and politics," Bowser said in a video pinned to her X account. "We don't have voting representation in Congress. We don't control the D.C. National Guard, and we don't have full access to full American democracy."

## Concerns Over Troops During Elections

The deployment has raised concerns among some over what the upcoming midterms and the 2028 presidential election will look like with troops on the streets. "The last time this president was supposed to come out of power, Jan. 6 happened," said Alex Dodds, co-founder of FreeDC, an activist group that has protested the deployment. "Imagine if that happens again but this time he has 5,000 troops standing at the ready to be part of that situation. You know, that's our worry."

Elizabeth Goitein of the Brennan Center for Justice echoed that concern. "An emergency is a sudden, unforeseen, and temporary state of affairs," she said. "Announcing that an emergency will last for another 2 1/2 years means it's not an emergency. Given what happened in January 2021, that should send up major red flags."

## What's Next

As the deployment enters its second year with no clear end in sight, the continued military presence in the capital will be a major test for the new batch of political candidates expected to take office after the midterms this fall. Residents will look to them to push back on the federal government's encroachment into the city's affairs — although they are limited in what they can do to counter it.

Donna Powell, a D.C. resident who opposes the deployment, put it simply: "We're already safe and beautiful. We don't need all these National Guard here." The money, she said, could be better spent "on healthcare and food for families that need it."

With the deployment now extended through Inauguration Day 2029, the question of how long armed troops will patrol the streets of the nation's capital — and what that means for American democracy — remains unresolved.
