# Bullet Engraving Shows Kirk Targeted for Politics

SALT LAKE CITY — Prosecutors in the killing of conservative activist Charlie Kirk pointed Tuesday to an engraved bullet as evidence that the Turning Point USA founder was targeted for how the defendant perceived his politics. The inscription, which reads "Hey Facist! CATCH!" along with directional arrow symbols, was found on ammunition recovered with the suspected murder weapon.

The evidence is central to the case against Tyler James Robinson, 23, who faces charges including aggravated murder in the fatal shooting of Kirk on September 10, 2025, at Utah Valley University in Orem, Utah. Prosecutors are seeking the death penalty, citing aggravating factors including political targeting and whether the shooting endangered thousands of people who attended Kirk's outdoor campus event.

## The Political Targeting Argument

In a filing submitted late Tuesday, prosecutors argued the engraved bullet found in the suspected murder weapon was an "overt political reference," according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/charlie-kirk-tyler-robinson-politics-death-penalty-d93454f7a018005c60ac86ae30054fdc). They also cited an alleged text message from Robinson about Kirk: "I had enough of his hatred. Some hate can't be negotiated out."

The engraved cartridges were recovered alongside an older-make Mauser-type bolt-action rifle chambered in .30-06, wrapped in a dark-colored towel and hidden in a wooded area near the campus. Investigators found at least four shell casings with inscriptions, including references to the Italian anti-fascist song "Bella ciao," a furry subculture meme, and a mocking message directed at investigators, as [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/internet/charlie-kirk-shooting-suspect-fascism-memes-bullets-tyler-robinson-rcna230950) reported.

Prosecutors contend that Kirk's opposition to same-sex marriage and gender-affirming care stood in sharp contrast to Robinson, who was reportedly in a romantic relationship with his roommate, Lance Twiggs. Twiggs told investigators he had considered a gender transition around the time of Kirk's killing, according to prosecutors.

## The Death Penalty Dispute

The prosecution and defense have filed dueling documents focused on whether the crime included aggravating factors that would make Robinson eligible for the death penalty if convicted, as [AP News reported](https://apnews.com/article/charlie-kirk-assassination-death-penalty-tyler-robinson-2e486bce004797c86f0bab4533a96a6f). Those factors include political targeting and whether the shooting endangered thousands of others who attended Kirk's event.

Robinson's attorneys have argued the shooter "hit the intended target" and that the bullet traveled above the crowd, meaning no one else was endangered. They also dispute claims that Robinson targeted Kirk over his political views, saying the alleged text message is not strong enough evidence to prove political motivation.

In a 19-page filing, prosecutors rejected those arguments, saying the evidence "easily establishes at least probable cause" that Robinson knowingly created a great risk of death to others, with numerous people within a "zone of danger" around Kirk, as [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/us/prosecutors-hammer-tyler-robinson-bid-derail-death-penalty-charlie-kirk-murder-case) reported.

"Defendant does not challenge the substance of the evidence demonstrating that he was the person who crawled to the sniper's perch on the Losee Building rooftop, fired the fatal shot that killed Mr. Charlie Kirk, hid the rifle in a wooded area, got rid of some of the clothing he was wearing, and told his roommate to delete their texts about the shooting," prosecutors wrote.

## Evidence and Timeline

Kirk was fatally shot in the neck with a single bullet from a sniper positioned on the roof of the Losee Center, approximately 142 yards away, while speaking to about 3,000 people at an outdoor campus event. A handwritten note that prosecutors allege Robinson left for Twiggs read in part, "I had the opportunity to take out Charlie Kirk, and I took it."

DNA analyses linked Robinson to both the suspected murder weapon and a tool he allegedly used to engrave the bullet cartridges, according to evidence presented during a five-day preliminary hearing in July. Robinson turned himself in a day after the shooting after his parents recognized him from released photographs and convinced him to surrender.

In a recorded interview shown in court, Twiggs said he had never heard Robinson talk about Kirk before the shooting, and that Robinson did not talk much about gender issues or LGBTQ+ rights, but did sometimes discuss politics, including Trump.

## What's Next

Judge Tony Graf will decide if the case should advance to trial after hearing from both sides on September 1. The judge will determine whether prosecutors have shown enough probable cause to send the case to trial and on what charges.

The case has drawn national attention as a prominent example of political violence in the United States. Kirk, 31, was a close ally of President Donald Trump and a highly influential figure in the MAGA movement, described by The New York Times as "something of a kingmaker." His assassination followed a series of violent political incidents across the country and has intensified debates about political extremism and the safety of public figures.

As the September 1 hearing approaches, the engraved bullet evidence and the question of political motivation will remain central to determining whether Robinson faces the death penalty if convicted. The case also raises broader questions about the intersection of online extremism, political rhetoric, and violence in American public life.
