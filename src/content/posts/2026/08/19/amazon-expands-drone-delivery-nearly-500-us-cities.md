# Amazon expands drone delivery to nearly 500 US cities

Amazon announced Wednesday plans to expand its Prime Air drone delivery service to nearly 500 U.S. cities and towns by the end of 2026 — a roughly sixfold increase from its current footprint. The expansion brings ultrafast drone delivery to communities with tens of millions of customers, marking one of the most significant milestones yet in the company's decade-long effort to make airborne package delivery a mainstream reality.

Customers could receive drone deliveries in as fast as 30 minutes, with most orders arriving around 60 minutes after checkout, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/amazon-drone-delivery-expansion-walmart-20db399ba65e8bc36a76b547f990b118). The drones can carry packages up to 5 pounds that fit in a large shoebox, making more than 60% of Amazon's most frequently purchased items eligible for delivery.

## A milestone 13 years in the making

The announcement represents the culmination of a vision first unveiled by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos on CBS's "60 Minutes" in December 2013, when he showed off a prototype drone and declared, "I know this looks like science fiction — it's not." As [GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-finally-set-to-deliver-on-its-13-year-old-drone-promise-reaching-nearly-500-u-s-cities-and-towns/) noted, both the technology and the regulations took far longer than the company initially expected.

Prime Air currently operates from 11 drone delivery sites across 10 U.S. metro areas in seven states: Arizona, Florida, Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, Nebraska, and Texas. Amazon plans to launch soon in the Chicago, Syracuse, Cleveland, Atlanta, and Boise metro areas, with more communities to follow later this year, according to the company's [official announcement](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/transportation/amazon-prime-air-drone-delivery-expansion).

## How the service works

Each Prime Air site serves an area of approximately 175 square miles, and multiple drone launching locations are needed to serve a large metro area. The drones fly primarily over suburban areas, allowing them to avoid the tall buildings and crowded airspace that complicate flights over dense urban centers.

Drone delivery is free for Prime members on orders of $50 or more. Prime members with orders under $50 pay $2.99, while non-Prime customers pay $4.99. Amazon said it has already delivered hundreds of thousands of packages by drone this year, with thousands of deliveries made daily at Prime Air sites.

"Customers already turn to Amazon for fast Same- and Next-Day Delivery, and Prime Air provides them an even speedier option when they need it, with deliveries in as fast as 30 minutes," said David Carbon, vice president of Amazon Prime Air, as reported by [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/19/amazons-prime-air-is-taking-off-in-nearly-500-u-s-cities/). "We've already delivered hundreds of thousands of packages to customers by drone this year, and by the end of 2026 we plan to reach customers in nearly 500 cities and towns."

## Safety and regulatory framework

Amazon operates Prime Air under Federal Aviation Administration Part 135 certification — the same regulatory framework used for commercial air carriers — and has been awarded waivers to fly drones beyond the line of sight of pilots. The company says its drones feature an industry-leading Detect-and-Avoid system that continuously monitors airspace and allows the aircraft to make real-time safety decisions.

The expansion comes despite a history of operational setbacks. In October 2025, two Amazon drones collided with the boom of a crane near its same-day site in Tolleson, Arizona, prompting a temporary suspension of operations and investigations by the NTSB and FAA, as [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/02/amazon-to-resume-drone-delivery-following-crash-in-arizona/). Amazon resumed service the following Friday after completing an internal review.

The federal government has [proposed a rule](https://apnews.com/article/drones-trump-duffy-line-of-sight-rule-bdbc54ca3b8ef2ead9ccfc62f3762f4c) that would allow more drone operators to fly beyond the horizon, but that rule has not yet been finalized. Amazon's existing waivers give it a head start over competitors in scaling operations.

## Competitive landscape intensifies

The expansion intensifies the battle between Amazon and Walmart to provide consumers with the fastest delivery times. Walmart and Alphabet's Wing division have been building what they describe as the largest drone delivery network in the U.S., adding seven new markets in June — including Phoenix, Philadelphia, and the Bay Area — and planning to reach 270 stores and more than 40 million people by 2027, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/drones-delivery-orders-available-walmart-amazon-e1dbd3638c60e464d6533e74eb046ce6).

Amazon CEO Andy Jassy outlined the company's ambitions in his [annual letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-ceo-andy-jassy-2025-letter-to-shareholders) in April, writing that Prime Air "now has a design that'll scale, plans to serve communities with 30 million customers by year-end, and expects to deliver half a billion packages by the end of this decade."

## Expert perspectives and challenges

Despite the ambitious targets, analysts caution that significant hurdles remain. Sucharita Kodali, a retail analyst at Forrester, told AP News that "it's still an experiment. It's still in test and learn mode." She noted that the expansion plan "does seem aggressive but remember that lots of the 500 may be small towns located in close proximity to one another."

Noise concerns have been a recurring issue for residents near drone operations. Amazon says its drones are quieter than an idling delivery truck during drop-off and comparable to a window fan while in flight. Operational challenges also persist, from tree cover and landscaping to inflatable pools that can make it difficult to find a good drop zone.

It's also not clear that drone delivery is cheaper than trucks at this point, since a team of people is needed to make drone delivery possible — and those people may need to be licensed drone pilots. Amazon's goal appears to be less about cutting costs and more about keeping customers happy with quicker deliveries, banking on faster delivery attracting more shoppers.

## What's next

Even after this expansion, drones will still handle only a fraction of the hundreds of millions of package deliveries Amazon makes each year. But the move signals that drone delivery is transitioning from experimental novelty to a permanent part of the e-commerce logistics mix.

As [DroneDJ](https://dronedj.com/2026/08/19/amazon-drone-delivery-where-how/) observed, with competitors like Walmart, Zipline, Wing, and Flytrex continuing to expand their own networks, the race to redefine last-mile logistics is heating up. If Amazon's timeline stays on track, drone deliveries could become a familiar sight in hundreds more American communities before 2026 comes to a close.

The coming months will reveal whether Amazon can overcome regulatory hurdles in each new community, manage the operational complexity of scaling to hundreds of sites, and win over skeptical consumers — challenges that have proven far more difficult than the original vision suggested.
