# USS Lincoln Families Demand Answers Over Crew Conditions

Families of sailors aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln are condemning President Trump's dismissal of the aircraft carrier's record-breaking deployment, saying the commander-in-chief should be "ashamed" of the conditions crew members have endured during nearly nine months at sea supporting the war against Iran.

When asked by reporters Friday whether the deployment had gone on too long, Trump said it was "not nearly long enough." Asked whether families were worried about conditions on board, he replied, "No, they're not." The comments came as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/15/nx-s1-5932539/uss-lincoln-trump-dire-conditions) that the Lincoln has been at sea for more than 260 consecutive days, setting a post-Vietnam-era record for carrier deployments.

## A Record Deployment Under Extreme Strain

The USS Abraham Lincoln departed San Diego in November 2025 with approximately 5,000 sailors on board. Originally expected to end in May, the deployment was extended indefinitely as the carrier became a central asset in the U.S.-Israeli war against Iran that began in late February. The ship has logged more than 10,000 aircraft flights and expended 1.5 million pounds of ordnance while enforcing a naval blockade on Iranian ports.

Reports describe food shortages, plumbing problems, moldy showers, water contamination, and declining mental health among crew members. There have even been reports — which NPR has not independently confirmed — that sailors have attempted to jump overboard. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/16/navy-families-denounce-trump-hegseth) interviewed three naval families who described relatives losing 20 to 30 pounds since being sent to the Middle East, with one sailor described as "emaciated" by his mother.

Karen Bramlett, whose grandson serves on the ship, told NPR her grandson is "just depressed" and that his dreams have been "shattered." "Donald Trump needs to be ashamed of himself to let them suffer the way that they're doing," she said. "They're over there fighting for our country. And this is the thanks they get? They can't even get help from him or his administration."

## Administration Defends Mission, Families Push Back

Acting Secretary of the Navy Hung Cao acknowledged in a statement Friday that "a small number of mental health cases were treated with no loss of life" but defended the extended mission, saying the conflict with Iran demanded it. "There is no question our young men and women have been pushed to their limits — but they have never broken," Cao said.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has dismissed media reports about conditions on the ship as "misrepresented" and said the U.S. can indefinitely maintain a naval blockade on Iran. But critics note that more than 200 family members attended a meeting in San Diego with Acting Navy Secretary Cao, and that Hegseth has not provided specific examples of what was misrepresented.

Julie Roland, a former Navy helicopter pilot who flew missions from the Lincoln, questioned Hegseth's denial. "Why did he not provide a single example of anything that was misrepresented," she told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/14/nx-s1-5931543/no-privacy-little-downtime-life-aboard-the-uss-abraham-lincoln). Roland described long lines for food, water, and bathrooms due to plumbing issues, and said sailors are dealing with "super long lines for the chow hall" and broken equipment.

## Lawmakers Demand Investigation

Democratic lawmakers are calling for Pentagon investigations and oversight visits. Sen. Ruben Gallego (D-AZ), a Marine veteran who deployed to Iraq, accused the Trump administration of "winging it" and said the Pentagon's handling of the deployment constitutes "not proper leadership." Speaking to [NPR's Morning Edition](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/14/nx-s1-5930365/uss-abraham-lincoln-iran-navy-ruben-gallego), Gallego called for a bipartisan delegation to visit the sailors.

Sen. Mark Kelly (D-AZ), a former Navy combat pilot and NASA astronaut, said he spoke to a grandmother of a sailor aboard the Lincoln who said her grandson has lost 20 to 25 pounds. "When you have crew members jumping off because of the conditions — I mean, literally, jumping off the ship to end their lives — that's a problem," Kelly told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/17/nx-s1-5933015/sen-mark-kelly-discusses-conditions-aboard-the-uss-lincoln). "And it needs to be investigated."

Rep. Mike Levin (D-CA) was more direct: "Respectfully, Mr. President, I'd like to see you survive a week on that ship."

## Logistics Challenges Compounded by War

The supply problems extend beyond the Lincoln. Iran attacked the Navy's main regional hub in Bahrain in March, complicating resupply efforts across the fleet. Rear Admiral Benjamin Nicholson, commander of Navy Sealift Command, acknowledged that traditional logistics hubs "were not available to us" in this conflict.

The USS Tripoli, an amphibious assault ship, also faced supply issues with sailors losing significant weight, and the USS George H.W. Bush has been at sea 146 days with reported shortages. The Strait of Hormuz shipping has dropped from 130-140 ships daily to fewer than a dozen, [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/17/uss-lincoln-crisis-how-it-compares-to-past-aircraft-carrier-deployments).

Harlan Ullman, a retired senior naval officer, told Al Jazeera that extended deployments "erode the readiness and capability of that ship the longer it's at sea." He noted that nuclear-powered carriers require maintenance that can only be performed in port.

## Relief on the Way

The USS George Washington is en route to relieve the Lincoln in the Middle East, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/17/nx-s1-5933068/uss-washington-to-relieve-uss-lincoln-after-concerns-about-the-crews-mental-health). The Washington's arrival will mark the end of one of the most demanding carrier deployments in modern U.S. history — but questions remain about how the Navy will sustain its blockade operations and whether the crew will receive adequate care upon return.

Admiral Brad Cooper, CENTCOM commander, visited the Lincoln and praised the crew, saying "history will record this deployment as one of the most operationally intense and consequential of the modern era." He also claimed the Lincoln has "among the lowest number of cases related to mental health" of the Navy's 11 aircraft carriers.

## What's Next

As the Lincoln's sailors prepare for their long-awaited return, lawmakers and veterans groups are pressing for accountability and comprehensive mental health support. Janessa Goldbeck, a Marine Corps veteran and CEO of Vet Voice Foundation, warned that "you cannot keep extending deployments, grinding down crews, and gaslighting families without destroying trust in the institution and its leaders."

The deployment has also raised broader questions about the sustainability of the Navy's operational tempo amid an open-ended conflict. With the war against Iran now in its sixth month and peace talks stalled, the strain on America's carrier fleet — and the families who support it — shows no sign of easing.
