# Colombia Earthquake Death Toll Rises to 289, Over 180,000 Affected

The death toll from the devastating magnitude 7.4 earthquake that struck Colombia on August 10 has risen to 289, with 143 people still missing and more than 4,100 injured, according to President Abelardo de la Espriella. The disaster has affected over 180,000 people across 15 departments and 472 municipalities, triggering one of the largest humanitarian responses in the country's recent history.

President de la Espriella delivered the latest situational report from the western port city of Buenaventura, confirming 186,016 people affected and 120,328 families impacted by the quake, as reported by [Caracol Radio](https://caracol.com.co/2026/08/16/victimas-del-terremoto-en-colombia-ascienden-a-289-segun-confirmo-presidente-de-la-espriella/). "The priority remains the search for the missing, because the most important thing here is to save lives," the president said. The National Unit for Disaster Risk Management (UNGRD) separately reported 287 confirmed deaths, 4,147 injured, and 194 missing as of August 16, according to [El Tiempo](https://www.eltiempo.com/colombia/otras-ciudades/balance-oficial-de-la-ungrd-este-16-de-agosto-tras-terremoto-de-7-4-en-colombia-289-fallecidos-4-187-heridos-y-143-desaparecidos-3578742).

## The Earthquake

The earthquake struck at 07:34 local time on August 10, with its epicenter approximately 20 kilometers from San José del Palmar in the department of Chocó, at a depth of 96 kilometers according to the Colombian Geological Survey, or 110.3 kilometers per the USGS. The [initial report](https://caracol.com.co/2026/08/10/atencion-fuerte-temblor-en-bogota-hoy-10-de-agosto-2026-epicentro-y-magnitud/) described the quake as being felt across much of western and central Colombia, as well as in parts of Ecuador, Panama, and Venezuela. The Colombian Geological Survey called it the strongest earthquake in the country in the last decade and the largest since the 1979 Tumaco earthquake.

The earthquake, which lasted approximately two minutes, was caused by strike-slip faulting at an intermediate depth within the subducting Nazca plate, according to [Wikipedia's comprehensive account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Colombia_earthquake) of the event. More than 100 aftershocks have been recorded, the largest with a magnitude of 5.0. The event is the deadliest earthquake in Colombia since the 1999 Armenia earthquake, which killed more than 1,000 people.

## Widespread Destruction

The impact has been devastating across multiple regions. In Valle del Cauca, at least 133 people died, with about 5,000 homes destroyed. Cali, the region's largest city, recorded at least 95 deaths and 949 injuries, with more than 5,000 buildings damaged or destroyed and 40 collapsed. Mayor Alejandro Eder captured the scale of the tragedy when he said, "This is not the same Cali we had a week ago."

In Risaralda, at least 101 people died in Pereira, where 65 buildings collapsed and the Matecaña International Airport partially collapsed. The department of Chocó, where the epicenter was located, recorded at least 14 deaths and 119 injuries. Infrastructure damage across the country includes 127,608 homes damaged, 26,392 homes destroyed, 197 collapsed buildings, 304 health centers affected, and 3,207 educational centers affected, according to the UNGRD balance reported by El Tiempo.

## Government Response and Political Context

The disaster struck just three days after President de la Espriella, a conservative, was sworn into office on August 7, following a tense transition from the previous administration. The government declared a national disaster on the day of the earthquake, as [Caracol Radio reported](https://caracol.com.co/2026/08/10/gobierno-declara-desastre-nacional-ante-emergencia-provocada-por-terremoto-de-74-en-colombia/), and subsequently declared an economic emergency and proposed a "Miracle Fund" for reconstruction. Three days of national mourning were declared, and a World Bank loan of up to US$450 million was activated.

However, the response was initially complicated by coordination challenges. Political scientist Sandra Borda of Universidad de los Andes noted that "without a transition and with that kind of personnel change, it was perfectly normal for these short circuits to occur," referring to the delayed appointment of a UNGRD director until August 12.

## International Aid Controversy

Colombia's handling of international assistance became the first controversy of the new government. The country initially limited or refused some international rescue offers, accepting teams only from the US, El Salvador, Ecuador, and Israel. As [RNZ/CNN reported](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/world/991730/colombia-limits-foreign-rescue-teams-as-earthquake-survival-window-narrows), this delay was considered significant as the critical 72-hour window for rescuing survivors narrowed. The Chinese ambassador to Colombia, Zhu Jingyang, publicly urged the government to establish an official coordination channel for international assistance.

Foreign Minister Omar Bula defended the government's approach, insisting the country has "not excluded absolutely any country in terms of the offers that have been made to us," adding, "We work with countries without any ideological or political consideration." Eventually, international aid began flowing, including search and rescue teams from Virginia and California in the US, 19.5 tons of aid from Mexico, volunteers from Peru, and rescue forces from Israel.

## Humanitarian Relief and Reconstruction

Humanitarian organizations have mobilized extensively. World Central Kitchen has been working with local restaurants to prepare and distribute food, while the Colombian Red Cross has deployed specialized urban search and rescue teams, as [Caracol Radio detailed](https://caracol.com.co/2026/08/16/colombia-mantiene-la-ayuda-a-las-comunidades-afectadas-por-el-terremoto/). The hospital ship Bencos Biohall resumed operations on August 14, and over 260 kilograms of medications and medical supplies have been sent to Buenaventura.

The scale of reconstruction ahead is enormous. Cali's Mayor Eder announced that the city's reconstruction will cost approximately 10 trillion Colombian pesos and take about three years, according to [El Tiempo's analysis](https://www.eltiempo.com/colombia/cali/el-reto-de-reconstruir-a-cali-tras-el-terremoto-tres-anos-de-trabajo-y-una-inversion-de-10-billones-para-superar-la-emergencia-3578662). The plan includes a humanitarian crisis phase of 3-6 months, followed by physical and social reconstruction over 2-3 years. Nearly 600 buildings in Cali remain evacuated pending structural studies, with about 30% potentially requiring reinforcement or demolition.

Economic projections vary widely. Oxford Economics estimated direct economic damages of US$990 million to US$1.98 billion (0.2% to 0.4% of GDP), while the USGS estimated the toll at near US$40 billion (7% of GDP).

## What's Next

As rescue operations transition to recovery, the focus is shifting to the long road ahead. Classes will resume virtually while schools are assessed, and the government is working to stabilize essential services across the affected regions. The international community has pledged continued support, and the government's "Miracle Fund" proposal aims to channel both national and international contributions toward rebuilding infrastructure.

President de la Espriella's promise to Buenaventura — "Buenaventura will no longer be the land of oblivion" — reflects the broader challenge facing Colombia: not just rebuilding what was lost, but building back more resilient communities in a country that sits on one of the world's most seismically active regions. The coming months will test whether the new administration can translate its emergency response into a sustained recovery effort that reaches all 180,000 affected people.
