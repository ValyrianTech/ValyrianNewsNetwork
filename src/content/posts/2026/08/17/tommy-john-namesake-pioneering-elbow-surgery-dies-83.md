# Tommy John, Namesake of Pioneering Elbow Surgery That Saved His Career, Dies at 83

Tommy John, the left-handed MLB pitcher whose revolutionary elbow surgery saved his career and changed baseball forever, died Saturday night at his home in Bradenton, Florida. He was 83. His agent, Mike Maguire, confirmed the death on Sunday, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/16/g-s1-138941/tommy-john-elbow-surgery-baseball-pitcher-yankees-dodgers). John had been receiving hospice care.

John won 288 games during a 26-year major league career that spanned from 1963 to 1989, making him a four-time All-Star with a 3.34 ERA and 2,245 strikeouts. He pitched for Cleveland, the Chicago White Sox, the Los Angeles Dodgers, the New York Yankees, the California Angels, and the Oakland Athletics. As [USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/mlb/2026/08/16/tommy-john-death-obituary-baseball-surgery/91227505007/) noted, his legacy extends far beyond his statistics on the mound.

## The Surgery That Changed Baseball

In 1974, at age 31 and in his 12th major league season, John tore the ulnar collateral ligament in his pitching elbow while throwing for the Dodgers against the Montreal Expos. At the time, such an injury was effectively a death knell for a pitcher's career. John allowed Dr. Frank Jobe, the Dodgers' team physician, to attempt an experimental procedure: replacing the damaged ligament with a tendon from John's right forearm. Jobe estimated the odds of success at 100-to-1.

The four-hour surgery on September 25, 1974, involved drilling holes in the humerus and ulna bones of John's left arm and threading the replacement tendon through them in a figure-eight pattern, as described in the [MLB.com glossary](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/injuries/tommy-john-surgery). The recovery was arduous—John's left hand initially shriveled, and he lost feeling in several fingers due to ulnar nerve damage, requiring a second surgery to reroute the nerve. His arm remained in a cast for four months.

John's successful return in 1976 proved the procedure could work. He pitched 14 more seasons and won 164 more games after the surgery—more than the 124 he won before it. His final major league appearance came days after his 46th birthday. As [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/sports/baseball/former-mlb-pitcher-tommy-john-dies-age-83-rcna592800) reported, John became known as the "Bionic Man" following his thrilling return from what should have been a career-ending injury.

## A Legacy That Saved Thousands of Careers

Since John's pioneering operation, more than 2,600 big leaguers and thousands of amateur players have undergone UCL reconstruction, now universally known as Tommy John surgery. The procedure has saved the careers of some of baseball's greatest modern stars, including Shohei Ohtani, Justin Verlander, Gerrit Cole, Jacob deGrom, and John Smoltz—the first Tommy John recipient elected to the Hall of Fame. As [USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/sports/mlb/2026/08/16/baseball-pitchers-careers-tommy-john-surgery/91330352007/) detailed, 12 legendary pitchers had their careers saved or extended by the groundbreaking procedure.

"If you put it in dollars and cents, I think there's no question that Tommy John is the most valuable reconstructive procedure there is," Dodgers team physician Dr. Neal ElAttrache told The Associated Press in 2024.

The surgery became so widely recognized that Merriam-Webster added "Tommy John surgery" to its dictionary in 2019. In June 2022, John donated the original cast from his 1974 surgery to the Smithsonian Institution, signed by his 1974 Dodgers teammates, Dr. Jobe, and Hall of Fame announcer Vin Scully.

## A Farewell Message

Just one week before his death, on August 8, the New York Yankees released a social media post from John during their 78th Old-Timers' Day, in which he said goodbye to friends and fans. As [MLB.com](https://www.mlb.com/yankees/news/tommy-john-misses-old-timers-day-for-health-reasons) reported, John had been battling health issues from his home in Bradenton. The farewell message was also covered by [13WHAM](https://13wham.com/news/nation-world/tommy-john-namesake-for-surgery-changing-mlb-dies-at-age-83-major-league-baseball-ucl-tear-ulnar-collateral-ligament-dodgers-yankees-angels-athletics-white-sox-cleveland-pitchers), which noted John's decision to undergo the experimental operation in 1974 changed baseball and helped save the careers of thousands of pitchers.

"I want to thank the Yankees organization & the Steinbrenner family for giving me this opportunity to say goodbye to everyone, along with all the friends & fans who followed me throughout my 26-year career," John wrote. "I will never forget you."

In his message, John also shared the advice his father gave him when he left for his first pitching job: "Good luck, Tommy. Just remember one thing: whether you make it big in the big leagues or you don't, you'll always just be Tommy John from Terre Haute, Indiana. I've never forgotten those words." [Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/articles/tommy-john-shares-emotional-message-023956199.html) reported on the emotional farewell, noting that John thanked Dr. Jobe "who saved my arm and made it possible for me to continue pitching."

## A Career of Excellence Overshadowed

While John is best known for the surgery bearing his name, he was also an exceptional pitcher in his own right. His 288 career wins are the second-most by any pitcher since 1900 not elected to the Hall of Fame. He won 20 games in 1977 and finished second in Cy Young voting that year to Steve Carlton, and again in 1979 to Mike Flanagan.

John was the opening day starter for both the 1966 Chicago White Sox and the 1989 New York Yankees—23 years apart, a testament to his remarkable longevity. From 1995 through 2009, he appeared on the Hall of Fame ballot but never received more than 31.7% of the votes, well short of the 75% required for election.

"Tommy was a wonderful person who competed to the utmost and did what no player had ever done to save his career, in conjunction with the esteemed Dr. Frank Jobe," former Dodgers general manager Ned Colletti posted on X. "The HOF is missing Tommy John."

Dodgers president and CEO Stan Kasten said in a statement: "Tommy was an exceptional pitcher throughout his career in Major League Baseball, and his courageous role in becoming the first to have surgery that would go on to bear his name can't be overstated. His impact both on and off the field has been felt by ballplayers of all ages and will be for generations to come."

The New York Yankees also issued a statement, calling John "a bedrock of our rotation during his time in pinstripes and a longtime favorite of Yankees fans." The team noted that "his incredible longevity and post-surgery achievements changed the fortunes and trajectory of countless athletes around the world, but it also overshadowed his excellence as a pitcher."

## Mixed Feelings About His Medical Legacy

John had complicated feelings about how common the surgery bearing his name became, particularly among young athletes. In July 2018, he tweeted that he was "sick" that his name was attached to a surgery happening more frequently in children than professionals, as youth pitchers increasingly threw harder and suffered UCL injuries.

His oldest son, Tommy John III, became a chiropractor and wrote a book about preventing young athletes from needing major sports-related surgery.

## Final Years and Family

John faced significant health challenges in his later years. He was hospitalized with COVID-19 in December 2020 and later developed Guillain-Barré syndrome, which left him paralyzed in his lower extremities for several months, as he told the New York Post in May 2022.

Born Thomas Edward John Jr. on May 22, 1943, in Terre Haute, Indiana, John graduated as valedictorian from Gerstmeyer High School in 1961. Despite being recruited by the University of Kentucky to play basketball, he chose baseball and signed with Cleveland. His hometown roots remained central to his identity throughout his life, as [WTHI](https://www.wthitv.com/2026/08/16/tommy-john-terre-haute-native-mlb-pitcher-dies-83/) reported in its coverage of his passing.

John and his first wife, Sally Simmons, had four children: Tamara, Tommy III, Travis, and Taylor, who died of a prescription drug overdose at age 28 in 2010. The couple divorced in 2013. John is survived by his second wife, Cheryl, his children, and his son-in-law Patrick Mannelly, a retired NFL long snapper who played 16 years for the Chicago Bears.

## What's Next

Tommy John's impact on baseball will endure for generations. As Yankees pitcher Gerrit Cole, who had Tommy John surgery himself, said: "The surgery itself is saving us, but the player had to step up to the table and do it. Having gone through the process, I can imagine that he was just as diligent—if not more diligent—to the rehab process, and really writing the book for all of us, to a certain extent."

John's courage in becoming the first pitcher to undergo UCL reconstruction not only extended his own career by 14 seasons but paved the way for thousands of pitchers who followed. His name will forever be synonymous with second chances in baseball.
