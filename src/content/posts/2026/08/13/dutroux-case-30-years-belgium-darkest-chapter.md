# Dutroux Case 30 Years On: Belgium's Darkest Chapter

Thirty years ago today, on August 13, 1996, Belgian police arrested Marc Dutroux, his wife Michelle Martin, and accomplice Michel Lelièvre in Marcinelle, near Charleroi. The arrest, following the disappearance of 14-year-old Laetitia Delhez in Bertrix, would unravel one of the darkest chapters in modern Belgian history—a case that exposed systemic failures in the justice system and permanently altered the national psyche.

## The Horror Summer of 1996

What began as the search for one missing girl quickly revealed a nightmare of unimaginable proportions. On August 15, just two days after the arrests, Sabine Dardenne (12) and Laetitia Delhez (14) were found alive in a hidden cell in the basement of Dutroux's house in Marcinelle. Sabine had been missing for 81 days after her abduction on May 28, 1996.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/07/09/mijn-haren-gaan-nog-altijd-rechtstaan-als-ik-die-beelden-zie/), justice journalist Caroline Van den Berghe recalls the moment vividly: "I still get goosebumps when I look at it." The joy of the rescue, however, quickly turned to grief. On August 17, the bodies of Julie Lejeune (8) and Mélissa Russo (8) were discovered buried in the garden of Dutroux's property in Sars-la-Buissière. The girls had died of hunger and thirst in Dutroux's cage. On September 3, the bodies of An Marchal (17) and Eefje Lambrecks (19) were exhumed from a property in Jumet—they had been abducted on August 23, 1995, after attending a hypnosis show at a casino in Blankenberge, and were buried alive.

## A System That Failed

The case exposed profound dysfunctions in Belgium's judicial and police systems. Dutroux had been convicted in 1989 for the abduction and rape of five minors but was released early by a decision of the Minister of Justice. Multiple warning signs and information about him had been neglected or poorly handled by investigators.

As [RTL Info](https://www.rtl.be/actu/belgique/societe/marc-dutroux-arrete-il-y-30-ans-revelations-mobilisation-et-reformes-dans-une/2026-08-13/article/797136) reported, the case triggered an immediate wave of national and international shock, revealing that Dutroux—already convicted for sexual assaults—was on conditional release at the time of the crimes. Investigators had also failed to properly exploit several alerts about him.

Investigator Michel Demoulin, who interrogated Dutroux and obtained his confessions, told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/06/30/30-jaar-losbarste-zaak-dutroux-waar-ging-het-over/) that he had to build a trust relationship with the psychopath. "Through his slip of the tongue, I knew he had also abducted Julie and Mélissa," Demoulin said. It was a breakthrough that would eventually lead to the discovery of all the victims.

## The Spaghetti Arrest and the White March

The case took an even more dramatic turn in October 1996 when investigating judge Jean-Marc Connerotte was removed from the case by the Court of Cassation—because he had attended a spaghetti fundraiser dinner for victims' families. The so-called "Spaghetti Arrest" triggered unprecedented public outrage.

"People were furious. It sometimes got very violent. It was as if a revolution was about to break out," Van den Berghe recalled. Spontaneous demonstrations and strikes broke out across the country. Students took to the streets, workers stopped work, and eggs and spaghetti were thrown at courthouses.

This fury culminated in the White March of October 20, 1996, when more than 300,000 people marched through Brussels in one of the largest demonstrations in Belgian history since World War II. Paul Marchal, father of victim An Marchal, told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/08/22/30-jaar-geleden-verdwenen-an-en-eefje-dankbaar-om-de-steun-die/) that the march carried him and his wife through their grief: "My wife and I felt carried by all those participants in the White March, and thanks to them, we carried An with us."

## A Nation Transformed

The Dutroux case fundamentally changed Belgian society. Child and youth psychiatrist Peter Adriaenssens told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/13/peter-adriaensens-sinds-dutroux-meer-angstige-maar-ook-meer-a/) that it was "the day all parents lost their innocence." The case changed parenting styles, making parents more anxious but also more alert and realistic. "Before Dutroux, people thought adults always do the right thing," Adriaenssens said.

Van den Berghe echoed this sentiment: "There is a before and after Dutroux. Those were different times, we lived more carefree."

The case also triggered sweeping institutional reforms. As [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/judiciaire/2026/08/13/affaire-dutroux-ce-moment-ou-tout-a-bascule-dans-la-confiance-des-belges-2IB4SDGJXJH7NOKM6NQXCBJPP4/) noted, the affair was not just a crime story—it permanently altered Belgium's police and judicial landscape. Reforms included the creation of Child Focus (the national center for missing children), the Cell Missing Persons, the Franchimont law strengthening victims' rights, the integration of police forces, the High Council of Justice (1999), and the tribunal for the application of sentences (2007).

The brief escape of Dutroux from the Neufchâteau courthouse in 1998—when he was recaptured within hours by a forest ranger—accelerated these reforms and led to the resignation of Justice Minister Stefaan De Clerck and Interior Minister Johan Vande Lanotte.

## The Trial and Its Aftermath

The trial began at the Assize Court in Arlon on March 1, 2004, after eight years of investigation. On June 22, 2004, Dutroux was sentenced to life imprisonment. Michelle Martin received 30 years, Michel Lelièvre 25 years, and Michel Nihoul 5 years for drug trafficking and criminal association (acquitted of abduction charges).

Dutroux remains the only one of the four convicted individuals still in prison. As [Paris Match Belgique](https://www.parismatch.be/actualites/societe/2026/08/12/30-ans-plus-tard-laffaire-dutroux-nest-toujours-pas-finie-soit-on-a-affaire-a-des-policiers-incompetents-soit-on-ne-veut-pas-nous-dire-la-verite-W7U7EX3C6RDOLCOIKPEX7Q52SU/) reported, the case is far from closed. In July 2024, a search of Dutroux's cell at Nivelles prison found approximately 200 photos of nude women, some possibly depicting minors. He was re-indicted in February 2026 for possession of child sexual abuse images.

Jean-Denis Lejeune, father of victim Julie, has expressed deep frustration at the slow pace of the investigation. "Either we're dealing with incompetent police officers, or they don't want to tell us the truth," he said. "I feel like I'm reliving the same kind of situation as when the girls were abducted in June 1995."

## What to Watch For

As Belgium marks this somber 30th anniversary, the investigation into the images found in Dutroux's cell continues. A graphological expert examination has been ordered to determine whether annotations on the photos were written by Dutroux. The case serves as a continuing reminder that even three decades later, the wounds of the horror summer of 1996 remain open—and that the pursuit of justice, for the victims and their families, is far from over.

For [Knack](https://www.knack.be/nieuws/belgie/maatschappij/an-zal-altijd-en-overal-bij-ons-zijn/), Paul Marchal's words capture the enduring pain: "We think about An every day, she is always with us." The legacy of the Dutroux case is not just one of institutional reform, but of a nation forever changed by the knowledge that evil can exist in the most ordinary of places.
