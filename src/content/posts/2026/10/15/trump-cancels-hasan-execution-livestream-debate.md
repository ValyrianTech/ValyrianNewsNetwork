# Trump Cancels Hasan Execution Livestream, but Debate Lingers

President Donald Trump has canceled plans to livestream the firing-squad execution of Nidal Hasan, the former Army psychiatrist convicted of killing 13 people at Fort Hood, Texas, in 2009. The reversal, announced in a Truth Social post on Thursday, followed intense bipartisan criticism at home and abroad — but scholars and critics warn that the episode may already have crossed a line by pushing a previously fringe idea into the mainstream of American political discourse.

"I think streaming the execution for all to see might have been a strong deterrent for the World to witness how America deals with Terrorists and SCUM like Hasan," Trump wrote, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/donald-trump/trump-no-livestream-execution-nidal-hasan-rcna603697). Still, he added, "many very good and well meaning people would rather not watch him die, through streaming, or through any other visual device. He will die, as scheduled, by firing squad, but this event will not be streamed."

The execution itself remains scheduled for the afternoon of Dec. 3, 2026, at Fort Hood — the same time of day as the 2009 shooting, according to [CBS News Texas](https://www.cbsnews.com/texas/news/fort-hood-shooting-survivor-nidal-hasan-execution-livestream-october-2026/). Families of the victims may view it in person. Trump said they "deserve to witness JUSTICE," [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/crx23qlnj27qo).

## How the Episode Unfolded

Hasan, a U.S.-born Muslim and Army psychiatrist, opened fire at Fort Hood's Soldier Readiness Processing Center on Nov. 5, 2009, killing 13 and wounding 32 — the deadliest non-combat attack on a U.S. military base. In his 2013 court-martial, where he acted as his own lawyer, he admitted he was the shooter, saying he had "switched sides" to protect Muslims and the Taliban from U.S. military actions, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/07/nx-s1-5993539/firing-squad-execution-nidal-hasan-fort-hood). A military jury convicted him on all counts and sentenced him to death. He is now 56, paralyzed from the chest down, and uses a wheelchair.

The sequence that led to this week's reversal began in early October, when Pentagon spokesperson Sean Parnell announced that Trump had approved the execution by firing squad and Army Secretary Adam Telle issued an order setting the date. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth then said the execution would be made public and livestreamed, telling a right-wing streaming network that "people need to understand that there's serious consequences for these types of things."

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/15/nx-s1-6001794/fort-hood-execution-hegseth-christian-nationalist), the livestream was Hegseth's idea and caught Trump, senior White House officials, and Army officials by surprise. NBC News reported that Hegseth also surprised officials by recommending the firing-squad method and the Fort Hood location, when the Army had been preparing for a lethal-injection execution elsewhere.

As criticism mounted, Vice President JD Vance publicly doubted the plan, saying he would not watch. Trump at first said he "absolutely" approved of the livestream, then hedged, saying a final determination was still pending.

## A Rare Convergence of Opposition

The pushback spanned the political and religious spectrum. On Wednesday, 72 retired generals, admirals, and other flag officers released an open letter urging cancellation, arguing the broadcast would violate constitutional protections against cruel and unusual punishment and turn a "solemn and grave punishment" into "a carnival for the enjoyment of voyeurs and spectators," [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cmp3gnngv3e9o).

"This type of exploitative broadcasting — that debases human dignity — will taint the honor of the military that employs it, and a nation that allows it," they wrote, warning it would make Hasan "a martyr for attacking and killing his fellow soldiers."

Lawmakers from both parties objected. Sen. Thom Tillis, R-N.C., who supports the death sentence, called a livestream "beyond disgusting," saying public executions are "what oppressive dictatorships like Iran and Afghanistan do." Sen. Jack Reed, D-R.I., the ranking member of the Senate Armed Services Committee, called the plan "a gift to anti-American terrorist recruiters." House Speaker Mike Johnson told CBS he worried a video could become a recruiting tool.

The Vatican and U.S. Catholic leaders also weighed in. Archbishop Paul S. Coakley, president of the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops, called it "a barbaric promotion of a culture of death," while Archbishop Timothy Broglio, who serves Catholics in the U.S. military, instructed any Catholics assigned to the firing squad to request an excusal. The United Nations called the plans "abhorrent."

## The Religious-Nationalist Undercurrent

Beyond the immediate outcry, religious scholars interviewed by NPR raised a deeper concern: that Hegseth's promotion of public execution reflects a Christian-nationalist worldview that may now be reshaping military justice.

Hegseth affiliates with the Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches, whose leader identifies as a Christian Nationalist. Julie Ingersoll, a professor of religion at the University of North Florida, said that within that tradition, capital punishment — including public execution — enjoys near-unanimous endorsement. "Just saying it out loud moves the boundary," she said of the livestream proposal.

Matthew Taylor, a visiting scholar at Georgetown University's Center on Faith and Justice, was more pointed, calling Hegseth "a religious extremist" who is "trying to implement those ideas within the U.S. military structure within the Pentagon."

Joshua Salaam, president of the Association of Muslim Chaplains, questioned why Hasan would be punished differently from others on military death row. "The question is, why is he being punished differently than everyone before him?" he asked. "And the answer that most people from my community will say is it has something to do with his religious beliefs, his religious identity."

Even the cancellation has not quieted those concerns. John Galligan, one of Hasan's lawyers, told the BBC that Hegseth's initial decision violated the Army's own regulation and that "the damage has already been done." "It will permanently remain a stain on our military justice system," he said.

## A Historical Threshold

The execution, if it proceeds, would be the first U.S. military execution since 1961, when Pvt. John Bennett was hanged, and the first by firing squad since 1945, when Pvt. Eddie Slovik was shot for desertion during World War II. The last high-profile public execution in the United States was Rainey Bethea's 1936 hanging in Kentucky, which drew roughly 20,000 spectators and prompted a backlash that ended the practice.

Robin Maher, executive director of the Death Penalty Information Center, noted that public executions are "typically held in authoritarian countries to instill fear in the population," and that there is no known precedent for a U.S. military execution to be livestreamed.

Hasan could still delay the proceeding by filing a habeas corpus appeal in federal court. Burke Butler, co-executive director of the Texas Defender Service, told CBS that lawyers are likely to argue that a public execution would constitute cruel and unusual punishment.

## What's Next

The immediate question is whether the Dec. 3 execution proceeds on schedule or is stalled by litigation. But the broader debate — over the role of religion in military justice, the modern revival of firing squads, and the boundary between punishment and spectacle — is unlikely to fade with the cancellation of a single livestream.

For survivors and victims' families, the episode has already reopened old wounds. Alonzo Lunsford, a former Army sergeant shot seven times in the attack, said he wants to attend the execution in person but opposes a public broadcast. "People have to be held accountable for their sins, but I don't want this to be some pay-per-view event, like some John Wick movie," he told CBS News Texas. "As a country, we're better than that."

Lelia Hunt Willingham, whose brother Army Spc. J.D. Hunt was killed at Fort Hood, put it more bluntly. "It's becoming a circus again," she said, [speaking to CBS News Texas](https://www.cbsnews.com/texas/news/lost-brother-fort-hood-shooting-nidal-hasan-planned-execution-10-12-26/). "People keep saying 'Do this for the families,' and, you know, I want to say, 'You can't speak to what brings us closure.'"
