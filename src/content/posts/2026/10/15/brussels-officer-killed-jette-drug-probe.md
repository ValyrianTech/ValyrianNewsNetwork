# Brussels Officer Killed in Jette Drug Probe

A veteran Brussels police inspector was shot dead in the municipality of Jette on the night of 12 to 13 October while taking part in an operation linked to a violent carjacking, an incident that has laid bare the growing risks facing Belgian officers and the spread of drug-related firearms violence in the capital.

The victim, 41-year-old Nordine El Talbi, was a member of the Anti-Overvalbrigade (AOB), a specialised anti-robbery unit of the Brussels Capital/Elsene police zone. He was killed by a single gunshot to the head as he carried out a lone, plainclothes reconnaissance of a stolen vehicle, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/labo-csiteam-voert-nieuw-sporenonderzoek-uit-in-jette~1791870783608/). On 15 October, the federal Labo CSI forensic team returned to the scene for a fresh round of evidence gathering, examining verges and searching sewers, while the Brussels prosecutor's office declined to comment on the investigation or the detained suspects.

## How the Night Unfolded

According to the timeline set out by prosecutors, the events began shortly after 11pm on 12 October, when a 35-year-old man from Anderlecht reported that his vehicle had been violently carjacked in Tubeke, in Walloon Brabant. He told police that three or four armed men had stolen his car.

Police distributed the stolen plate, which was later detected by ANPR cameras on the Brussels Ring. The trail led officers to the Carton de Wiartlaan in Jette, near the Belgica metro station. Two AOB teams took covert positions near the vehicle, and El Talbi was sent on foot, in plainclothes, to reconnoitre ahead of a possible intervention. His colleagues waited for a signal that never came.

When they went to check on him, they found him unconscious on the pavement, [BRUZZ reported](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/veiligheid/doodgeschoten-agent-ging-alleen-op-verkenning-maar-gaf-nooit-signaal-aan-collegas-2026-10-13). He had been struck by a single bullet to the head and died shortly afterwards. Notably, his colleagues heard no gunshots — leaving open the question of whether a silencer was used.

## Weapons, Drugs and Arrests

At a press conference on 13 October, Brussels public prosecutor Julien Moinil confirmed that several weapons had been found at the scene, as well as "a considerable quantity of drugs." Three pistols were recovered, though Moinil stressed these were handguns rather than war weapons. It remains unclear which weapon fired the fatal shot, or whether El Talbi managed to draw his own service weapon.

A judicial investigation has been opened for murder with aggravating circumstances — the fact that the victim was a police officer carrying out his duties — an offence that carries a potential life sentence. An investigating judge has been appointed, and several suspects have been arrested.

According to [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/onderzoek-naar-doodgeschoten-inspecteur-nordine-41-houdt-rekening-met-ander-scenario-was-carjacking-een-ripdeal-tussen-drugsbendes~a062949e/), the suspects are reportedly three brothers aged 26, 28 and 31 who live on the very street where the shooting occurred, only houses away from the scene. The family denies any guilt, saying the middle brother, a taxi driver, had just finished his shift and was about to go home when he was arrested.

Under Belgian criminal procedure, a decision on whether to keep the suspects in custody had to be taken within 48 hours of their detention, with clarity expected on the night of 14 to 15 October.

## A Possible 'Rip-Deal'

One line of inquiry that has gained attention is the hypothesis that the reported carjacking was in fact a "rip-deal" — a robbery in which criminals steal drugs from rivals. Under that scenario, the vehicle would have been used by people from the drug milieu and would have contained a substantial consignment of narcotics.

If accurate, that theory would also cast the original complainant in a different light: the man who reported the carjacking could himself become a suspect, with his report potentially intended to conceal the true circumstances. The prosecutor has confirmed a link to the drug milieu but has not confirmed the rip-deal hypothesis.

According to HLN, citing a police source, the suspects may not have realised they had shot a police officer until they took El Talbi's badge and service weapon. A separate trace reportedly led to a pizzeria in Sint-Jans-Molenbeek, where one suspect allegedly entered with bloodstained hands saying he had killed a police officer — a claim that remains unconfirmed.

## The Victim and a Force in Mourning

El Talbi joined the police zone in 2009 as an inspector and, after a decade in first-line services, realised his ambition of joining the AOB in 2019. Police chief Michel Goovaerts described him as "a true Brussels lad" and an experienced, highly valued colleague.

"This night I received the call that every corps chief fears," Goovaerts said. "Nordine ... realised his dream: to join the AOB. He did that with great conviction and professionalism. What I can guarantee is that we will keep the memory of Nordine alive."

The death — reportedly the 36th line-of-duty death of a Belgian officer in 25 years — has triggered an outpouring of grief across the country. Officers gathered at the Poelaertplein in Brussels at midnight to commemorate their colleague with their vehicles' blue emergency lights illuminated, and a condolence register opened at Brussels City Hall on 14 October. Police zones across Flanders flew flags at half-mast. A large commemorative ceremony is planned for 16 October at the Cinquantenaire Park, and El Talbi's burial prayer was announced for central Brussels before burial at the multiconfessional cemetery in Evere.

## A Wider Reckoning

Prosecutors and politicians have tied the killing to a broader escalation of drug-related violence and the growing circulation of firearms in the capital. Brussels prosecutor-general Frédéric Van Leeuw pointed to weapons flowing from conflict zones, including Ukraine, as well as cheap firearms produced in Turkey.

"Firearms enter our country not through large containers like cocaine, but in small quantities, for example in the boot of vehicles. Or they come back from conflict zones two hours' flying time from Brussels," he said, [according to BRUZZ](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/samenleving/procureur-van-leeuw-na-dood-agent-criminelen-gaan-sneller-over-tot-extreem-geweld-2026-10-14). "The value of drugs is enormously high, and that goes hand in hand with an increase in overt violence. It is no longer discreet criminal organisations, but groups that use open violence to intimidate rivals."

The killing has reignited a long-running debate about police safety and the resources available to justice. Police unions have argued that violence against officers is escalating and that political responses have been inadequate. "This was a straight-up murder," said Vincent Houssin, vice-chairman of the VSOA police union. "We regularly sound the alarm ... but we get little hearing from the political authorities."

Interior Minister Bernard Quintin promised that lessons would be drawn, and Justice Minister Annelies Verlinden expressed her condolences, as did Prime Minister Bart De Wever. The Flemish nationalist party Vlaams Belang called for the harshest possible punishment, demanding that the perpetrators "must get life and must never be released."

## What to Watch Next

Several key questions remain unanswered. Investigators have not disclosed how many shots were fired in total, which of the three recovered pistols was the murder weapon, or whether the drugs were already in the car at the time of the alleged Tubeke carjacking. The precise type and quantity of the narcotics found have also not been made public.

The custody decision for the arrested suspects was expected imminently, and the possibility that the rip-deal thesis could reframe who is considered a suspect remains a significant thread. Meanwhile, the CSI team's return to Jette on 15 October suggests investigators are still working to reconstruct the final moments of an officer who never gave the signal his colleagues were waiting for.

As Brussels mourns, the case stands as a stark illustration of the pressures bearing down on a capital at the centre of Europe's drug-violence problem — and of the human cost borne by those tasked with confronting it.
