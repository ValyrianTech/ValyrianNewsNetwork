# OCC: Big Banks Debanked Customers Over Politics

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency concluded that the nine largest U.S. banks maintained policies that allowed accounts to be denied or closed based on customers' political and religious views, according to a report released Wednesday. The findings, which cover 2020 through September 2025, revive a long-running fight over fair access to financial services and prompted the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau to open its own investigation the same day.

Comptroller of the Currency Jonathan V. Gould said the [47-page report](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2026/nr-occ-2026-90.html), titled "Guaranteeing Fair Banking for All Americans," provides "irrefutable evidence that the nation's largest banks have denied, debanked and discriminated against customers and lawful businesses."

"Americans should have access to financial services regardless of their beliefs, affiliations, or political views," Gould said. "Fair access to financial services is not a mantra—it is a mandate."

## Which Banks Were Named

The review covered the nine largest national banks supervised by the OCC: JPMorgan Chase Bank, Bank of America, Citibank, Wells Fargo Bank, U.S. Bank, Capital One, PNC Bank, TD Bank, and BMO Bank. Together they represent a central pillar of the U.S. financial system.

The OCC said it examined nearly 100,000 complaints and collected roughly 50,000 documents totaling about 420,000 pages. It found that all nine banks had policies that "required, encouraged, or otherwise influenced" decisions to deny or limit financial services between 2020 and September 2025.

The mechanism, according to the agency, was "broad reputation risk policies" that lacked clear standards and objective, risk-based criteria—leaving room for decisions to be shaped by a customer's politics, religion, or line of business.

## The Evidence in the Report

The report cites specific episodes. In February 2021, WePay—a payment processor acquired by JPMorgan Chase in 2017 and now defunct—closed the accounts of merchants selling merchandise promoting President Trump and the MAGA brand. About two years later, a bank declined services to a public education organization because of its partisan leanings, the report said.

The OCC also found that banks restricted entire industries, including oil and gas exploration; coal mining or coal-powered plants; firearms and ammunition manufacturing or distribution; detention facility construction and operation; tobacco and e-cigarette manufacturing and retail; and digital asset activities.

The findings were not one-directional. [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/debanking-top-us-banks-occ-report/) noted that the OCC found some banks had also refused to do business with Democratic-leaning organizations—including, in one 2023 case, an abortion-rights group. The report described one bank business line that treated "partisan activity" as presumptively high reputation risk until 2025, serving organizations deemed "bipartisan" while declining others.

## Banks Push Back

JPMorgan Chase disputed that its policies discriminate. "Our policies prohibit discrimination against customers based on political or religious views," a spokesperson said, adding that WePay's processes "did not meet our expectations. We identified it, corrected it, and the business no longer operates."

The bank also offered a broader argument: years of operating under a regulatory environment that "often second-guessed decisions contributed to a culture of caution across the banking industry," and "that system needs fixing."

The Bank Policy Institute, which represents the industry, said banks "support fair access to banking services" and backed efforts to prioritize "material financial risk" over "reputational risk." Citibank and Wells Fargo declined to comment, [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/article/federal-watchdog-says-9-large-us-banks-denied-service-to-customers-over-political-or-religious-views-160634194.html) reported.

## A Second Front: The CFPB

Hours after the OCC report landed, the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-announces-new-initiative-to-address-occ-uncovered-debanking-by-large-banks/) announced it would investigate whether the banks violated federal consumer financial laws, including the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and prohibitions on unfair or deceptive practices.

"It is appalling that these banks targeted American consumers for their political, social, or religious beliefs that did not align with the Biden Administration or its leftist allies," said CFPB Acting Director Mark Paoletta. He said the agency would "fully investigate" the institutions named in the report. No new monetary penalties have been announced by either agency; a report and an investigation are not findings of legal liability.

## How We Got Here

The review stems from [Executive Order 14331](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/08/guaranteeing-fair-banking-for-all-americans/), signed in August 2025, which directed federal banking regulators to identify institutions that declined business with clients over their views. The OCC announced the review in September 2025 and issued [preliminary findings](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2025/nr-occ-2025-123.html) in December 2025.

The issue is entangled with President Trump's personal grievances. Trump and his family have said accounts were closed after the Jan. 6, 2021 Capitol riot; Melania Trump wrote in her memoir that her account was shuttered and that Barron Trump was later denied one. Trump sued Capital One in 2025 and JPMorgan Chase and CEO Jamie Dimon in [early 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/01/22/why-did-trump-sue-jamie-dimon-jpmorgan/).

Lawmakers responded quickly. Senate Banking Committee Chairman Tim Scott (R-S.C.) called the report "damning" and said, "Debanking Americans over faith or politics is simply un-American." Rep. Andy Barr (R-Ky.), lead sponsor of the FIRM Act—which would bar regulators from weighing "reputational risk"—said no law-abiding American should be debanked for their beliefs, [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/politics/us-regulator-accuses-nations-largest-banks-debanking-customers-over-political-views) reported.

## Analysis: A Fight With Two Sides

The report lands in a contested space. Debanking is real, and it is not always improper: banks close accounts for fraud, overdrafts, and legal risk every day. The OCC's central claim is narrower—that broad, vague reputation-risk policies let non-risk factors seep into decisions that should rest on objective criteria.

The political framing is more complicated than a single narrative. The OCC's own evidence documents debanking of both conservative- and liberal-aligned customers and industries, undercutting a purely one-sided story even as officials describe the practice in partisan terms. That tension could matter as the CFPB investigation and any legislation move forward.

Gould signaled the OCC is not finished. "Notwithstanding certain efforts by the largest banks to rein in these practices, I am not confident that they will be enough," he said, adding that the agency will assess "further changes to the regulatory and supervisory framework."

## What to Watch

Several threads will determine how far this goes. The CFPB's investigation could produce enforcement action or fizzle without penalties. The FIRM Act and related proposals could codify parts of the executive order—or stall in Congress. And the courts will weigh in through Trump's lawsuits against Capital One and JPMorgan. For now, the report has done what regulators rarely achieve: turned a quiet compliance question into a national debate over who gets to use the banking system, and why.
