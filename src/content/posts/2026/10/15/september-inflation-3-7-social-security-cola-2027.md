# September Inflation Hits 3.7%, Lifting Social Security

U.S. consumer prices rose 3.7% over the year in September, a hotter-than-expected reading that will trigger a 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) for Social Security recipients in 2027, the Social Security Administration announced Wednesday. The same inflation surge is eroding household purchasing power and raising the odds of another Federal Reserve rate hike just weeks before the November 3 midterm elections.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/14/nx-s1-6000593/inflation-consumer-prices-cpi-social-security-benefits), the annual increase — up from 3.4% in August — was driven largely by an energy spike tied to the U.S. war with Iran. Prices rose 0.6% from August to September, the largest monthly jump in five months.

## A Bigger Check for Retirees

Shortly after the Labor Department released its inflation report, the Social Security Administration [confirmed](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/press/releases/2026-10-14.html) a 3.6% benefit increase for 2027 — the largest annual boost in four years. The average retirement benefit will rise by about $75 a month, to roughly $2,170.

Nearly 72 million Social Security beneficiaries will see the increase beginning in January 2027, and about 7.3 million SSI recipients will get higher payments starting December 31, 2026 — together reaching roughly 76 million Americans.

"Social Security will implement this cost-of-living adjustment for 76 million Americans, giving them greater financial stability," Commissioner Frank J. Bisignano said in the agency's announcement.

The COLA is calculated automatically under the Social Security Act, tied to the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W) measured over July, August, and September.

## Seniors Tread Water

Advocates for older Americans welcomed the increase but warned it may not be enough. AARP CEO Myechia Minter-Jordan said the adjustment "helps millions of Americans keep up with rising costs for groceries, routine bills, healthcare and other everyday expenses."

Critics argue the CPI-W formula fails to reflect seniors' real spending, which skews toward healthcare. As [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/social-security-cola-2027-increase/) reported, some advocates are pressing Congress to switch to the Consumer Price Index for the Elderly (CPI-E).

"While a 3.6% COLA is certainly welcome, it wouldn't necessarily restore the purchasing power seniors have already lost," said Shannon Benton, executive director of the Senior Citizens League. "The COLAs are designed to keep pace with inflation, not make up for years of falling behind."

Max Richtman, president of the National Committee to Preserve Social Security and Medicare, told NPR the increase "won't be enough for most seniors to even tread water financially." Nancy Altman, president of Social Security Works, put it bluntly to CBS: "People are getting crushed by prices."

## What Drove Inflation Higher

Energy was the story. Heating oil jumped 74% over the past year, a particular burden for New England families who rely on it to stay warm. Gasoline prices were up 34.2% annually, and [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/14/us-inflation-rises-before-midterm-elections-fuelling-rate-hike-fears) reported that a gallon of gasoline averaged $4.33 nationally, with diesel at $6.29.

"The directionless war in Iran continues to disrupt global supply chains, driving up the prices of food and fuel," said Kyle Moore, chief economist at The Century Foundation.

Core inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy costs, also ticked up to 2.6% annually. Grocery prices were a rare bright spot, holding roughly flat for the month, though poultry, fruit, and juice all rose while milk and beef fell.

The artificial intelligence boom is another factor. Memory chip costs have climbed, and Al Jazeera noted that prices for computer software and accessories have jumped 28% year-over-year. Apple raised prices on older-model iPhones by $100 last month.

## Paychecks Falling Behind

Perhaps the most politically charged detail: average wages rose just 3% in September, meaning the typical worker's buying power actually shrank over the year.

"People are kind of saying, 'Something isn't right with that,'" economist Brian Bethune of Boston College told NPR. "I'm working harder. I'm more productive. And this is not a square deal."

Beth Hammack, who heads the Cleveland Federal Reserve Bank, worries the cumulative shocks — tariffs and war — could make inflation self-perpetuating. "This could be a sign that an inflationary mindset is taking hold," she said. "It can become a self-fulfilling prophecy that makes it harder for monetary policy to bring inflation back down."

## The Political and Policy Stakes

This was the final inflation report before the midterms. Al Jazeera reported that a recent Reuters/Ipsos poll found about 78% of Americans blamed President Trump's policies for rising prices. Markets dipped after the release, with the Nasdaq down 0.8% and the S&P 500 off 0.6%.

The Federal Reserve, which raised rates by a quarter point in September — its first hike in three years — meets again in late October. Per CME FedWatch data cited by Al Jazeera, traders saw a 52.9% chance of holding steady versus a 47.1% chance of another hike.

Meanwhile, Social Security's own finances loom. Its trust fund is projected to become insolvent in about six years, and absent legislative action, all recipients could face a benefit cut of more than 20% in 2032.

Medicare's 2027 Part B premiums have not yet been announced. Because those premiums are deducted directly from Social Security checks, they could absorb part of the COLA before retirees ever see it. Tyler End, a certified financial planner and CEO of Retirable, noted that healthcare inflation runs about 5.8% annually — far above the COLA's long-term average.

## What to Watch

Beneficiaries can view their personalized COLA notices online starting in late November through their my Social Security accounts; those not enrolled in paperless notices will receive them by mail in December. SSI recipients will see their first higher payments on December 31.

The bigger question is whether a 3.6% bump — against 3.7% inflation and rising senior poverty, now at 15.4% — can do more than keep pace. For now, as Benton put it, seniors are "getting a slightly bigger check to pay bills that have gotten even bigger."
