# Liangzhu Tombs Yield Possible 5,000-Year-Old Offering Hall

Archaeologists working at the Liangzhu ruins in eastern China have discovered and reconstructed the remains of a group of high-status buildings directly east of the site's royal tombs, with experts suggesting the largest structure may have served as an offering hall (xiangdian) used for sacrificial rites. The find, announced at a forum in Hangzhou, points to the possible emergence of a formal tomb-and-shrine system at Liangzhu as early as 5,000 years ago, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/culture/20261021/b5c40e48ec1e423db418a308f7dabe94/c.html), which reported the discovery on 21 October 2026.

## Context: A 40-Year Landmark Site

The buildings sit beside the Fanshan site in present-day Yuhang District, Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province. Fanshan was discovered roughly 40 years ago by the Zhejiang Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology and is regarded as the highest-ranked cemetery of the Liangzhu culture found to date, popularly known as the Fanshan Royal Tombs.

Its most celebrated tomb, M12, yielded the massive Jade Cong King — carved with the complete deity-beast motif — and the Jade Yue King, a large ceremonial axe. As [China National Radio](https://news.cnr.cn/native/gd/20190706/t20190706_524682050.shtml) reported, the discovery of these objects played an era-defining role in decoding Liangzhu jade, revealing the beast-face motif to be a full deity image bearing a feathered crown.

Liangzhu itself was a key Neolithic culture of the Yangtze River Delta, centred on Liangzhu Ancient City and dating roughly 5,300 to 4,300 years ago. It is renowned for advanced rice agriculture, sophisticated jade work and large-scale hydraulic systems, and the ancient city was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site on 6 July 2019.

## What Was Found

With approval from the State Administration of Cultural Heritage, re-excavation of the Fanshan site restarted in 2024, when researchers found a rectangular enclosure wall on the site periphery — initially judged possibly to be a cemetery wall. Carbon-14 dating of the east and south enclosure walls placed their construction in the same period as Tomb M12, roughly 5,000 years ago.

Building remains were found east of the royal tombs in 2025 and reconstructed this year. The reconstruction drew on 120 post holes discovered in the area. "These 120 post holes correspond to three timber buildings," said Li Songyang, an ancient-architecture researcher at the Zhejiang institute. "The largest has an elliptical plan, 25 metres east-west and 16 metres north-south."

The largest structure, designated F1, is interpreted as possibly a stilt building with three rings of posts, reconstructed with two tiers of eaves reaching about 15 metres in height. To accommodate the terrain, its interior floor was raised roughly to adult waist height. East of it stand two smaller buildings — one nearly circular, one roughly square — reconstructed as simpler thatched huts. The team based their designs on large wooden components of mushroom-tree and oak species unearthed at the Zhongjiagang site, as well as architectural motifs incised on Liangzhu pottery.

## An Offering Hall Hypothesis

Wang Ningyuan, director of the Science and Technology Archaeology Office at the institute and head of the Liangzhu Ancient City and water-system project, said the enclosure wall may be the remains of a cemetery's outer wall, while the buildings may be the remains of an offering hall used exclusively for sacrifice.

"The Liangzhu polity may already have had a tomb-and-shrine system at that time," Wang said, according to Xinhua. The interpretation builds on the team's 2025 revelation at the third Liangzhu Forum of a possible cemetery garden, with a chain of courtyards distributed along a long ridge — described at the time as possibly one of China's earliest royal cemetery gardens.

## Analysis: Reading Ritual from Ruins

The significance of the find lies less in a single building than in what its arrangement implies. An offering hall positioned beside a paramount elite cemetery suggests that Liangzhu society had already codified the relationship between the dead and the living — between burial and ongoing ritual obligation. If that reading holds, it would push the conceptual origins of the formal imperial tomb-and-shrine system back some five millennia.

It also reinforces a picture of Liangzhu as a highly organised state-like society. The discoveries rely on the team's integrated space-sky-ground-data archaeology paradigm, combining aerial remote sensing and drones, satellite imagery, traditional excavation and GIS-based digital recording — a method the team has used to identify more than 1,000 water-dam remains across Zhejiang.

## What's Next

Caution is warranted. The identification of the structures as an offering hall, the 5,000-year dating framework and the tomb-and-shrine framing are expert interpretations pending peer-reviewed publication, and English-language wire pickup of the announcement has been limited. Further excavation and analysis at Fanshan — and the wider question of whether similar structures accompany other elite Liangzhu cemeteries — will be the key tests of the hypothesis.

For now, the reconstructed buildings offer a striking preview of what ceremony may have looked like at the dawn of one of East Asia's earliest complex societies.
