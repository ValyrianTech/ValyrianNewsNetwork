# FAA Certifies Boeing 737 Max 10, Ending Years of Delays

Nearly a decade after Boeing unveiled its largest 737 Max jet, the Federal Aviation Administration approved the Max 10 for passenger service on Tuesday, ending years of delays and clearing a major hurdle in the embattled planemaker's effort to restore its reputation and financial footing. The certification, presented at Boeing's factory in Everett, Washington, allows the single-aisle aircraft to be sold to domestic and foreign carriers, though deliveries are not expected to begin until 2027.

The approval represents another milestone in Boeing's long recovery from years of safety, quality and production crises, and completes the lineup of four 737 Max models after the smaller Max 7 was cleared for service in August.

## A Decade in the Making

The Max 10 was introduced at the Paris Air Show in 2017 to help Boeing compete with rival Airbus, and was originally due to enter service around 2020. But technical and regulatory hurdles — compounded by the fallout from two deadly crashes involving the smaller Max 8 that killed 346 people in 2018 and 2019, and the 2024 midair blowout of a door plug on an Alaska Airlines Max 9 — repeatedly pushed certification back.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/21/g-s1-149032/faa-boeing-737-max-10), the delays held up a key part of Boeing's business: more than 30 customers worldwide have ordered 1,555 Max 10s that Boeing has yet to deliver, accounting for more than 30% of its total 737 Max backlog. Major customers include United Airlines, Delta Air Lines, American Airlines and Ryanair. Alaska Airlines joined the list in January, ordering 105 Max 10s with options for 35 more.

## A Milestone, and a Business Bet

The Max 10 typically seats 185 to 210 passengers, according to Boeing, compared with 175 to 195 on the Max 9 and 160 to 180 on the Max 8. It is intended to compete with Airbus's A321neo family, which entered service in 2017; the largest A321neo can carry more passengers and fly farther without refueling than the Max 10.

Still, Boeing designed the Max 10 to carry more passengers while burning less fuel than older aircraft — a potentially attractive combination for airlines facing soaring jet fuel costs amid the U.S. war with Iran. Those rising costs have put added pressure on carriers to control expenses.

At the Everett plant on Tuesday, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the certification followed "extensive safety reviews," including thousands of hours of test flights, and called it a major win for American manufacturing. "To think that it took us almost 10 years to get here. We all probably had a little less gray hair 10 years ago," Duffy said.

Stephanie Pope, president and CEO of Boeing Commercial Airplanes, said the certification "clears the path to deliver an airplane that will set a new standard in commercial aviation." "Today is a very, very special day. It caps years of hard work and dedication by many," she said, adding that it "marks another major milestone in our recovery, and it unlocks a key to our future growth."

## What Certification Means — and What Comes Next

Certification means the FAA has determined that the Max 10's design meets safety standards, following engineering reviews and flight testing. But Boeing must continue to show it can consistently build the aircraft to the approved design, and each individual plane must be cleared before it can carry passengers. The Max 10 also required significant design improvements, including updates to the flight control system with an enhanced angle-of-attack sensor — faulty angle-of-attack data was a factor in both fatal Max 8 crashes — and these changes will be applied to the existing fleet in the coming years.

The moment reflects a broader easing of regulatory pressure on Boeing. In the wake of the fatal crashes, the FAA overhauled how it certifies Boeing aircraft. But over the past 18 months the agency has gradually raised production limits and, in July, again allowed Boeing to issue airworthiness certificates for its 737 Max and 787 planes at the end of production.

Deliveries are expected to begin in 2027, with the first Max 10s potentially going to Canada-based WestJet early next year, pending certification by Transport Canada and the European Union Aviation Safety Agency — approvals Boeing expects within a couple of months. Some of the 737 Max certification team will now shift to helping Boeing certify the 777-9, its largest jetliner in production, which is six years behind schedule.

For Boeing, the question now is whether the Max 10 can help it claw back ground lost to Airbus in the lucrative single-aisle market — and whether a company still working to prove its manufacturing discipline can deliver on the order book without new setbacks.

## What to Watch

Investors offered a muted reaction, with Boeing shares slipping slightly on the day of the announcement. The coming months will test whether Boeing can ramp up production smoothly at its Everett and Renton lines, secure foreign certifications, and rebuild the trust that years of crises eroded. With more than 1,500 orders waiting, the first deliveries — and how smoothly they arrive — will say much about whether this certification marks a true turning point or simply a long-delayed first step.
