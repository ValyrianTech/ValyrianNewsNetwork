# What If an AI Bubble Bursts and Accelerates Job Losses?

It is the scenario investors dread and workers may have even more reason to fear: an artificial intelligence investment bubble bursts, sparking a recession — and that recession, rather than protecting jobs, accelerates the replacement of human workers with AI. NPR Planet Money economics correspondent Greg Rosalsky calls it "the AI double whammy," in an analysis published October 20 on [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2026/10/20/g-s1-148305/what-if-an-ai-bubble-bursts-and-that-accelerates-ai-job-losses).

"First, the bust hammers our retirement savings," Rosalsky writes. "Then, as businesses scramble to cut costs, many of our jobs are permanently lost."

## The Mechanics of a Bubble

The thesis rests on two strands of economic research: the history of financial bubbles and the relationship between recessions and automation. Drawing on economist Charles Kindleberger's 1978 book *Manias, Panics, and Crashes* — itself built on Hyman Minsky's theory of financial instability — Rosalsky notes a recurring pattern. Revolutionary technology sparks excitement, investors over-bet and borrow heavily, asset prices fall, debts remain, and a crash can spiral into a broader financial crisis.

The warning is not that a bubble definitively exists, but that the conditions are present. A J.P. Morgan analysis earlier in October found that just 42 AI-related companies accounted for roughly two-thirds of the S&P 500's stock price gains since early 2024. Meanwhile, AI-related firms are borrowing hundreds of billions of dollars to finance data centers and energy infrastructure. Rosalsky is careful to stress that this does not prove a bubble or that the debt is a ticking time bomb — the dot-com bust, after all, produced a relatively mild recession because debt played a smaller role.

## Why Automation Clusters Around Recessions

The second half of the argument is the more counterintuitive. Research suggests automation does not advance gradually but in bursts that cluster around downturns. As Rosalsky reports, a study by economists Nir Jaimovich and Henry Siu found that between 1990 and 2017, 88% of the decline in the share of Americans employed in automation-vulnerable jobs occurred during recessions or in the months immediately before or after them.

The logic follows that a recession is precisely when cost-cutting pressure is strongest — and when AI tools, if available on the cheap, become an attractive substitute for payroll. A separate [working paper on recessions and technological transformation](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24373), by economists Alexandr Kopytov, Nikolai Roussanov and Mathieu Taschereau-Dumouchel, offers a related explanation: because adopting a new technology disrupts production, firms prefer to make the switch during downturns, when profits are already low — meaning recessions can speed up adoption even as they do short-term damage.

## The Pricing Irony

There is an unsettling twist. Fierce competition among AI model providers could drive down prices and make the technology cheaper for businesses to adopt — even as it squeezes the profits of the AI companies themselves. Technology analyst Benedict Evans has questioned whether model providers can maintain pricing power or will collapse into "low-margin commodity infrastructure," as he wrote in his July 2026 essay ["Ways to think about token pricing"](https://www.ben-evans.com/benedictevans/2026/7/9/ways-to-think-about-token-pricing). As Rosalsky puts it: "Investors could lose their money, and workers could still lose their jobs."

## A Contested Premise

Rosalsky does not present the double whammy as a forecast. He frames the piece as an exercise in "dark thoughts," repeatedly acknowledging uncertainty about whether AI profits will justify the spending, whether a bust would trigger a recession, and whether historical automation patterns will repeat. He also notes an offsetting force: a downturn could leave businesses with less money to invest in AI, and AI may yet prove more useful for making workers more productive than for outright replacing them.

## Policy Responses on the Table

If the scenario unfolds, the policy toolkit matters. Rosalsky points to unemployment insurance and other safety-net programs as damage limiters. He also highlights a more provocative proposal from Bill Gates, who has argued for taxing AI and robots much like human payroll — making it more economical to keep employing humans while generating revenue to strengthen the safety net. Rosalsky flags the idea of taxing AI and robot labor as a topic for a potential future column.

## What to Watch

The "AI double whammy" is a hypothetical, not a reported event, and its author is explicit that the historical pattern may not repeat. But the underlying tensions are real: concentrated market gains, debt-financed infrastructure, and a labor market that has historically shed automation-vulnerable jobs fastest during downturns. Whether those threads converge — or unravel harmlessly — is the question the next several quarters may answer.
