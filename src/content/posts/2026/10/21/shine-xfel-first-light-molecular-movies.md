# China's SHINE X-ray Laser Achieves First Light

Shanghai's flagship national science facility has achieved a pivotal milestone: the Shanghai High-repetition-rate XFEL and Extreme Light Facility (SHINE) generated its first free-electron laser (FEL) light on October 15, 2026, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261021/6fd43feca5174cafb283b417e49c4744/c.html). The result marks a critical step toward full operation targeted for the end of 2027 and positions China among an elite group of nations capable of producing ultrafast X-ray pulses to observe molecular processes in real time.

## A 'Super Camera' for the Microscopic World

The achievement was announced on October 21, 2026, by Xinhua and by the three institutions building the facility: ShanghaiTech University, the Shanghai Advanced Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS), and the CAS Shanghai Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics. The first-light milestone itself occurred six days earlier.

The facility is designed to capture what researchers describe as "molecular movies" — ultrafast, time-resolved images of atoms and molecules in motion. Xinhua offered a vivid comparison: "If traditional synchrotron radiation sources can take static 'molecular photographs,' then SHINE is like a 'super camera' that can film 'molecular movies' recording the rapid motion of the microscopic world."

That capability rests on extreme performance. According to [ShanghaiTech University](https://www.shanghaitech.edu.cn/2026/1020/c1001a1128256/page.htm), the first light was produced using a 4 GeV continuous-wave (CW) superconducting linear accelerator driving 18 undulator lines, each 4 meters long with a magnetic period of 55 millimeters. On completion, the accelerator's electron energy will reach 8 GeV, with the first two undulator lines producing X-ray pulses across a photon energy range of 0.2 to 15 kiloelectronvolts (keV). Pulse lengths will be as short as the femtosecond scale — quadrillionths of a second — at a repetition rate of up to 1 megahertz (a million pulses per second).

Combined with nanometer-scale spatial resolution and femtosecond time resolution, the device will let scientists "precisely capture the rapid dynamic changes of atoms and molecules," the university release stated, providing cutting-edge tools for structural analysis and ultrafast dynamics research in the life sciences, quantum materials, and future energy fields.

## One of Only Two in the World

SHINE's significance extends beyond national borders. Internationally, only the United States has built a comparable high-repetition-rate machine: the Linac Coherent Light Source II (LCLS-II) at SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory, which uses a 4 GeV CW superconducting accelerator and is being upgraded to a hard X-ray configuration known as LCLS-II-HE.

"SHINE and the upgraded LCLS-II-HE are currently the only two devices of this type in the world," ShanghaiTech and CAS officials stated in the joint announcement. The framing places China's facility alongside the foremost instrument in the field, underscoring how few countries can operate high-repetition-rate hard X-ray free-electron lasers.

Most XFELs worldwide fire tens to hundreds of pulses per second. By contrast, SHINE's continuous-wave superconducting design will ultimately deliver up to a million pulses per second — enabling far more data collection per experiment and opening new classes of scientific inquiry. As Xinhua reported, SHINE joins Shanghai's existing photon-science cluster, anchored by the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) and the soft X-ray free-electron laser (SXFEL), but represents the most advanced of the group.

## World-First Laser Station

The facility will also host a feature no other site possesses. According to the ShanghaiTech announcement, SHINE will build "the world's first experimental station based on a 100-petawatt (PW) ultra-intense, ultra-short laser," enabling the combination of ultra-intense laser light with the free-electron laser beam. This is expected to provide unprecedented conditions for research into extreme strong-field physics and high-energy-density physics — areas that probe matter under conditions approaching those found in stars and fusion reactions.

## Years in the Making

SHINE has been under development for most of a decade. Construction began in April 2018. ShanghaiTech University serves as the legal entity responsible for overall construction planning, engineering control, and quality and safety management, as well as future operation; it is building the civil engineering works, five experimental stations, and portions of the beamlines. The CAS Shanghai Advanced Research Institute is responsible for the accelerator, undulators, utilities, and parts of the beamlines, while the CAS Shanghai Institute of Optics and Fine Mechanics is building the extreme light physics station.

The project has advanced in stages. By 2024, the injector had been commissioned with a 100 MeV electron beam meeting major design specifications. At the SHINE Forum 2025 held at ShanghaiTech in July 2025, international FEL experts gathered to review progress, and more than 20 cryomodules were slated to be ready for beam commissioning by the end of 2025. The first-light lasing milestone, achieved in October 2026, is distinct from the delivery of first light to an experimental end station — the latter of which the 2025 forum had scheduled for early 2026.

## Strategic Framing

Official releases frame SHINE explicitly in national-strategy terms. "The construction of SHINE will greatly enhance China's basic research capacity and original innovation level, becoming an important scientific research platform supporting high-level science and technology self-reliance and the building of a science and technology powerhouse," the institutional announcement stated.

The phrase "high-level science and technology self-reliance" is a recurring priority in Chinese policy, reflecting a push to reduce dependence on foreign technology and to lead in frontier research. SHINE, as a home-grown national major science and technology infrastructure project, embodies that ambition in the domain of photon science.

## What to Watch

The first light is a beginning, not an endpoint. The critical question now is whether SHINE can convert its initial lasing into reliable, user-ready operations on the ambitious timeline of late 2027. The facility's value will ultimately be measured by the science it enables — the molecular movies it produces and the discoveries they yield in biology, materials science, and energy research.

For now, the achievement gives China a genuine claim to frontline capability in one of the most demanding and exclusive corners of modern physics: the ability to freeze and film the fastest processes in nature. The coming year will determine how quickly that capability translates into results.
