# De Wever: EU won't reopen Russian assets debate

Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever told parliament on Wednesday that he is "certain" the European Commission will not propose using frozen Russian assets to finance Ukraine in any new funding plan. Speaking at the Federal Advisory Committee on European Affairs about last week's EU summit, De Wever said the Commission had given him that assurance directly, [VRT NWS reports](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/21/premier-de-wever-zeker-dat-europese-commissie-debat-over-russi/).

"I am certain the European Commission will not make proposals to meet [Ukraine's] financing needs with a new operation tied to the Russian assets," De Wever said. "I am certain of that, because that is also what was said."

His confidence, however, is only partly about Brussels. The Belgian premier openly acknowledged that other member states may still try to revive the idea — and he aimed pointed criticism at newer European leaders whom he says are reopening a question he thought he had settled.

## The stakes: 185 billion euros in Brussels

The dispute centres on roughly 185 billion euros in immobilized Russian central bank assets held at Euroclear, the Brussels-based securities depository. The funds were frozen as part of the sanctions imposed after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. They are "immobilized" rather than confiscated: Russia theoretically retains ownership, but the assets cannot be moved.

Belgium has long been the most cautious EU member on tapping these funds, because Euroclear — and by extension the Belgian state — would bear the brunt of any Russian legal retaliation. De Wever fought hard against the move last year, and the bloc ultimately settled on an alternative: a 90 billion euro loan raised on financial markets to fund Ukraine through 2026 and 2027.

That alternative now looks insufficient. President Volodymyr Zelensky asked at last week's summit for a further 70 billion euros in support for next year — reopening the financial pressure that first put the Russian assets on the table.

## A 'flamethrower' and new leaders

The remarks were delivered during a formal debate in the Chamber of Representatives, where De Wever reported back on the European summit to the committee — [the full exchange is recorded in the Chamber's video archive](https://media.dekamer.be/meeting/56-020363-U2130).

De Wever's account was a candid description of how he believes he killed the assets proposal last year — and of his worry that the argument is not truly over. He singled out Dutch Prime Minister Rob Jetten, who he said "felt the need to say on the radio in the morning: 'I want to seize that money.'"

"Not everyone experienced last year's debate," De Wever said. "New leaders have emerged who are developing a discourse that I was able to nip in the bud last year."

He warned his European colleagues that even such language could carry legal consequences — and said the warning landed. "One colleague came to tell me in the corridor that it was as if I had gone over that debate with a flamethrower," he recounted. "There was no longer any great enthusiasm around the table to continue with it."

## The legal barrier — and the legal risk

De Wever also pointed to a procedural obstacle: since last year, unfreezing the assets would require a qualified majority of member states, rather than the unanimity that once applied. "That majority does not exist, not at all," he insisted.

His confidence rests on more than arithmetic. The legal risk Belgium faces is concrete, not theoretical: Russian investors have filed international arbitration claims against Belgium over the frozen assets, invoking old bilateral investment treaties. Those cases underpin De Wever's long-standing argument that leveraging the assets is "something that can shock the financial world order" and must not be treated lightly.

That concern has also made Belgium the pivotal player in any future proposal. Proponents of using the assets — including a group of experts who have floated a plan to shift the funds into EU custody and take on the liability at the European level — concede it cannot be done without Belgian consent.

## Analysis: assurance vs. pressure

De Wever's statement is best read as domestic reassurance wrapped around an admission of fragility. By declaring himself "certain" the Commission will stand down, he is telling the Belgian parliament and public that last year's hard-won settlement holds. But by criticising Jetten and warning that "new leaders" are reopening the debate, he concedes that he does not control the rhetoric in other capitals — or the fiscal arithmetic that keeps pushing the question back onto the agenda.

The central tension remains unresolved. Belgium's core demand in 2025 was an unlimited guarantee from fellow member states covering its exposure if the assets were ever used. That guarantee was never granted. Until the question of who bears the legal and financial risk is settled, the underlying dispute is dormant rather than dead.

## What to watch

The next test is financial, not rhetorical. If the 90 billion euro loan proves insufficient — as Zelensky's 70 billion euro request suggests it may — the pressure to revisit the Russian assets will only grow, regardless of De Wever's certainty. Also worth watching: whether the Commission puts its assurance to Belgium in writing, how Belgium's arbitration cases evolve, and whether the qualified majority De Wever says "does not exist" holds together if a formal proposal ever returns to the table.

For now, the Belgian premier has bought time — and made clear he intends to spend it reminding his colleagues of what he believes is at stake.
