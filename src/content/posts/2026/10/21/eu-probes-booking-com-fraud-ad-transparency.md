# EU Probes Booking.com Over Fraud and Ad Transparency

The European Commission has opened formal proceedings against Booking.com to determine whether the online travel giant has breached the Digital Services Act (DSA) by failing to adequately assess and mitigate risks arising from fraudulent listings and phishing scams. The investigation, [announced](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_2223) on 21 October 2026, also examines whether the platform clearly labels paid "boosted listings" as advertisements.

"Europeans should be able to book their holidays without fear of being scammed," said Henna Virkkunen, Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy. "The Digital Services Act sets clear responsibilities on platforms to address these risks, and we must ensure that the leading booking platform in Europe takes those responsibilities seriously."

The action is not a fine — it marks the opening of an in-depth inquiry that could lead to interim measures, a non-compliance decision, or a commitment deal with the company.

## Three Focus Areas

The Commission's investigation centres on three distinct concerns, all rooted in the DSA's obligations for Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs).

First, regulators will examine whether Booking.com properly identified and mitigated the systemic risks linked to **fraudulent and non-compliant listings**. This includes fake properties that do not exist, impersonation fraud, and non-compliant accommodation offers — and whether the company took effective measures to remove them.

Second, the probe will assess how reservation and guest data is shared with accommodation providers, and how Booking.com's messaging service is designed and functions. The Commission is examining whether the company put in place sufficient safeguards to prevent data from being misused to carry out **phishing and solicit fraudulent payments**.

Third, investigators will look at **advertising transparency**. Accommodation providers can make their listings more visible in search results by paying a higher commission. The Commission will examine whether users can clearly recognise these "boosted listings" as advertisements, and whether they can see who is behind an ad, who paid for it, why they are seeing it, and how to change their ad settings.

If proven, these failures would constitute infringements of Articles 34 and 35 of the DSA — which require platform and search engines to identify, analyse, assess and mitigate systemic risks — and of Article 26(1), which obliges platforms to ensure users can clearly identify advertisements.

## Consumer Complaints Behind the Case

The investigation follows mounting evidence of real-world harm. The Belgian consumer organisation Testaankoop has documented over 100 complaints of sophisticated fraud tied to Booking.com, as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/21/europese-commissie-opent-onderzoek-naar-booking-com/) reported.

"On the one hand you have people who booked a hotel and on arrival discover the hotel doesn't exist," Testaankoop spokesperson Laura Clays told VRT NWS. "What happens even more often is a very realistic phishing attempt. People receive an email or message from a hotel they actually booked, but that data turns out to be stolen from Booking.com. That's how criminals get customers' money."

According to Testaankoop, that pattern points to a security problem at the platform. "We believe there is a data leak and that they have not sufficiently protected the hotels' accounts," Clays said.

The Belgian consumer body had already pressed the company in mid-2025, when it publicly urged Booking.com to tighten controls and secure the booking process — framing fraud prevention as the platform's responsibility since the scams occur on its own infrastructure.

## Background

Booking.com was designated a Very Large Online Platform under the DSA on 25 April 2023, subjecting it to the regulation's strictest obligations. The Commission's decision to open proceedings follows a preliminary investigation that analysed the company's 2026 risk assessment report and those of the preceding three years, information in its transparency reports, and its replies to formal requests for information sent on 23 September 2025 and 26 May 2026.

The Commission is cooperating with the Dutch Authority for Consumers and Markets (ACM), the Digital Services Coordinator of the member state where Booking.com is established. Opening formal proceedings also relieves national authorities of their powers to supervise the suspected Article 26(1) infringement as regards the conduct covered by the case.

## Analysis: A Broadening Enforcement Footprint

The proceedings signal that the Commission is applying the DSA to a services-market platform in the travel and e-commerce sector, not just to social media and search. The EU has already taken on TikTok over minors' safety standards and Meta over addictive design and its failure to keep children off its platforms — a case that carries the threat of a multibillion-euro fine. Booking.com extends that enforcement push into online travel.

The "boosted listings" issue is the most structurally significant thread. Because prominence on the platform is sold through commission, the investigation strikes at the heart of Booking.com's commercial model. If the Commission finds an infringement, the company could be required to clearly label paid placements and provide ad provenance information — changes that would reshape how visibility is bought and sold on the platform.

The focus on data-sharing and messaging design is equally pointed. By treating platform design choices as a source of systemic risk, the Commission is signalling that it expects structural safeguards — hardened account security, data minimisation and a more secure messaging service — rather than piecemeal fixes.

Potential outcomes include interim measures, a formal non-compliance decision carrying DSA fines of up to 6% of global annual turnover, or acceptance of commitments offered by Booking.com to remedy the issues.

## What's Next

The Commission said it will carry out the investigation as a matter of priority, continuing to gather evidence through additional requests for information, interviews or inspections. It gave no deadline for a conclusion. Booking.com has not publicly responded to the opening of proceedings.

For European travellers, the case could ultimately mean safer booking — clearer labels on paid placements and stronger protections against the phishing scams that have cost consumers money. For the online-travel industry more broadly, it raises a question that platforms from Airbnb to Expedia will be watching: how far the DSA's transparency and risk-mitigation duties extend into the way bookings are sold, secured and ranked.
