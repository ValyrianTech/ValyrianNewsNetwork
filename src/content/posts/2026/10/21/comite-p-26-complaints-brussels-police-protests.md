# Comité P Logs 26 Complaints Over Brussels Police Conduct

Belgium's independent police oversight body has received 26 complaints about how officers handled a wave of youth protests and riots in Brussels this month, opening a fresh test of how the country scrutinises its police force. The Vast Comité van Toezicht op de Politiediensten (Comité P) said 20 complaints relate to the events of 9 October and six to a protest on 17 October, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/21/comite-p-kreeg-26-klachten-binnen-over-politieoptreden-bij-jonge/), which produced the report with the BRUZZ editorial team. No complaints had yet been filed over a student protest on 12 October.

The watchdog does not disclose the nature or content of the complaints, and a spokesperson cautioned that the tally could grow. "Of course it is always possible that we will receive more complaints in the coming period," the spokesperson said.

## What Happened

The complaints stem from three days of demonstrations rooted in federal austerity politics. On Friday 9 October, the Christian (ACV) and socialist (ABVV) trade unions staged a national day of action against the federal government's austerity measures, joined by student movements and antifa groups voicing discontent over cuts to Francophone education. The union action was relatively peaceful but was disrupted by rioters — estimated by police at around 1,000 — who caused extensive damage to public transport buses, shared cars and street furniture, and threw bricks and pyrotechnic material at officers. Police made 110 arrests that day, 30 of them judicial, with 21 people — all but four of them minors — brought before the prosecutor's office on charges including weapons possession, violence and vandalism, [BRUZZ reported](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/samenleving/21-personen-gerechtelijk-aangehouden-na-rellen-17-minderjarigen-2026-10-10).

A student protest on 12 October passed without complaints being filed. Then on Saturday 17 October, youths returned to the streets and the protest again degenerated into riots, with police pelted and shared cars and street furniture damaged. Police arrested 150 people — later revised to 152 — of whom 19 were judicial arrests and eight were minors, according to [BRUZZ](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/veiligheid/uiteindelijk-152-mensen-opgepakt-tijdens-jongerenprotesten-2026-10-18). Five officers from the Brussels-Capital/Ixelles police zone were lightly injured that day.

## The Civil-Society Case

While much of the coverage has focused on the destruction and arrests, legal aid groups say they are documenting a parallel pattern of alleged excessive force. Pierre-Yves Rosset, director of Service Droit des Jeunes (SDJ), which provides free legal advice to Brussels youth, said his organisation had received testimony of serious injuries. "We received testimonies from a minor who was hit on the head with a baton," he said. "Another minor had to go to hospital with a broken collarbone from a baton blow."

SDJ and the Francophone human rights league, the Ligue des Droits Humains (LDH), point to several recurring concerns: the use of tear gas when young people or even children are present, officers who are not clearly identifiable in footage, and the continued use of the "insluitingstechniek" — a containment or kettling technique in which demonstrators are encircled without distinguishing troublemakers from peaceful protesters. "The Brussels police have already been convicted for the use of the containment technique, but continue to apply it," Rosset said. "In addition, we have seen several images in which officers are not identifiable, which is also problematic."

That conviction is the backdrop to the current scrutiny. In a March 2025 ruling, the Francophone court of first instance in Brussels held the Belgian state, the Brussels-Capital/Ixelles police zone and Mayor Philippe Close (PS) liable for a violent police operation during a January 2021 protest against "class justice," [BRUZZ reported](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/justitie/brusselse-politie-en-close-veroordeeld-tot-schadevergoedingen-voor-politiegeweld). During that operation, police encircled demonstrators and arrested more than 232 people, 91 of them minors; the League said the ruling established that the containment technique is not permitted under Belgian law. That the practice is again being raised makes the new complaints legally and politically sensitive.

Rosset stressed that his organisation does not condone the rioting. "We condemn the looting and any form of violence," he said. "We are also not saying the police may not use force, but we do sometimes question its proportionality." He noted that in June, when similar protests took place, SDJ received around thirty testimonies about possible police violence, suggesting the current wave is part of a pattern rather than an isolated episode.

## A Watchdog Under Strain

Comité P is an external, independent body operating under the federal parliament, created in the aftermath of the Bende van Nijvel affair and celebrating its 35th anniversary this year. It does not punish; it investigates, analyses and issues recommendations. Its chair, Kathleen Stinckens, has said young people are more assertive than ever and file complaints more frequently, often filming police actions with smartphones — a development she views as positive. She has also strongly backed bodycams for their de-escalating effect and value as objective evidence.

The body's reach, however, may be limited. A January 2026 report found that Comité P has for years received incomplete information because of an outdated digital data-exchange system built in 2008, meaning thousands of complaints filed directly with police services are not always forwarded — though Stinckens stressed that every complaint filed is still handled. The 26 complaints reported this week may therefore not capture the full picture of grievances.

## What to Watch

The central question is what Comité P's examination concludes. Because the body does not publish the nature of complaints and cannot sanction, its leverage lies in recommendations — and in whether its findings identify systemic issues around tear gas, officer identifiability and the containment technique. Those findings could revive calls for bodycams and broader reform, and may sharpen a political debate already charged by federal austerity and linguistic-community tensions. For now, the complaints sit at the intersection of two narratives — large-scale public disorder and alleged disproportionate force — both of which the oversight process is meant to resolve.
