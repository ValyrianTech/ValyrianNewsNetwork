# Judge Rebukes Trump Plan to Overhaul D.C. Golf Course

A federal judge sharply rebuked the Trump administration during a tense hearing Monday over its apparent determination to push ahead with a sweeping renovation of the historic East Potomac Golf Links in Washington, D.C. — including the removal of hundreds of trees — just days after the court had moved to block the project.

The hearing ended without a decision, leaving unresolved a deepening legal confrontation over the future of a century-old, federally owned public course that sits on a man-made island in the Potomac River.

## A Contradiction at the Center

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/19/nx-s1-6007746/golf-course-potomac-white-house-tree-removal), U.S. District Judge Ana C. Reyes, a Biden appointee, focused on a glaring contradiction: President Trump had posted detailed renovation plans on social media on Sunday, while Department of Justice lawyers had told the court the plan remained "conceptual" with "no final agency action."

"Do I believe the president of the United States or do I believe you?" Reyes asked the government's attorneys.

She pressed further: "I have, on the one hand, the president of the United States saying to the American public three times now, I think, in the last two days, there is a final plan to redesign the golf course … Then I have you all telling me … that there's no final plan. Which is it?"

The hearing capped a dramatic sequence of events. Late on Friday, Oct. 9, ahead of a holiday weekend, government officials notified the court they planned to remove "up to 529 trees" in phases. Bulldozers began taking down hundreds of trees the next day. At a three-hour emergency hearing last week, Reyes said she was "beyond disappointed," rejected the administration's "routine maintenance" justification, and imposed a temporary restraining order halting further removals.

"I do not know how I could have made it any plainer that I did not want what was to happen, to happen," she said. "I have given the government every single benefit of the doubt that it would not do precisely what it has done."

On Friday, the White House filed a motion asking Reyes to recuse herself for alleged bias. She declined to step aside.

## Public Pressure From the White House

Ahead of Monday's hearing, Trump wrote on Truth Social that Reyes was a "crazed lunatic" and said renovation work did not require court approval, declaring "Work is scheduled to begin IMMEDIATELY!" That post appeared to contradict a DOJ filing submitted the previous day, which stated the plan "is not final because the Park Service has not completed its review."

Trump has described the course as "old, broken, dilapidated, and of no further use or effect." His proposed overhaul would reportedly convert three existing courses — 36 holes in total — into a single 18-hole "championship-style" course, and would expand the footprint into parkland now used for picnicking, fishing, and cycling.

## A Historic, Contested Landscape

The dispute centers on more than a golf course. As the plaintiffs argue, East Potomac Park was designated by Congress in 1897 as a public park "forever held and used as a park for the recreation and pleasure of the people." The links, designed by Walter J. Travis and patterned on Scotland's courses, were among the first public courses in the country to be desegregated, in 1941.

"This is not just some putt-putt course," Reyes said. The park holds "a deep amount of meaning for the people of the district, and is protected as a historic landmark."

The lawsuit, filed in February 2026 by the [DC Preservation League](https://dcpreservation.org/2026/02/13/east-potomac-links-lawsuit/) and two local residents, Dave Roberts and Alex Dickson, against the Interior Department and the National Park Service, argues the renovation would inflate tee-time costs at a facility meant to be "open to all people" and convert a public recreation space into an exclusive championship venue.

"East Potomac Golf Links is a testament to what's possible with public land and why public spaces matter," Roberts said. "It deserves better than becoming a dumping ground for waste and yet another private playground for the privileged and powerful."

Rebecca Miller, executive director of the DC Preservation League, said altering the site's historic character "would undermine a site meant to be accessible to the public."

The White House has said it is simply carrying out a 2019 preservation plan, but the plaintiffs counter that Trump's ambitions go far beyond it — the 2019 plan recommended removing invasive vegetation, not healthy native species. The suit also alleges that Interior dumped roughly 30,000 cubic yards of dirt and debris from the White House East Wing excavation onto the course in October 2025, material apparently untested for pollutants.

## Legal and Political Stakes

At its core, the case raises whether the renovation requires environmental review under federal law, whether the tree removal constitutes "substantial construction" triggering the court's notice requirement, and whether the plan violates the 1897 park mandate. In May 2026, Reyes ordered the administration to give notice of any substantial construction, including the removal of more than 10 trees.

At Monday's nearly seven-hour hearing, Reyes also appeared to withdraw a Rule 11 demand that DOJ lawyers sign the contested filing, according to reports of the DOJ's response filing, available [here](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.dcd.289401/gov.uscourts.dcd.289401.94.0.pdf).

The dispute fits a broader pattern in which the president has sought to reshape Washington's landmarks, NPR noted — including threats to tear down the Kennedy Center and a push to build a 250-foot arch near the National Mall.

## What's Next

With no ruling issued Monday, the temporary restraining order remains the key immediate safeguard against further tree removal, and Reyes must still decide whether to grant a longer-term block while the case proceeds. The administration's recusal request has been rebuffed, but the underlying question — whether a final plan exists at all — looms over the proceedings. For now, the fate of a course that has served District residents for more than a century rests with the court.
