# Why Pregnancy Costs So Often Exceed Insurers' Estimates

When Grace and Daniel Griffin began planning for their first child, they did what financial advisers recommend: they tried to budget. The 26-year-old couple from Calumet, Michigan, looked up their UnitedHealthcare student plan's estimate for a routine pregnancy and delivery and found a figure of about $2,700 — a number they felt they could save for. By early October, after their daughter was born in June, their out-of-pocket costs had surpassed $9,000, more than three times the insurer's published estimate, according to [NPR and KFF Health News](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/21/nx-s1-5996849/insurance-cost-baby-pregnancy).

## A Gap Between Estimates and Reality

The Griffins' experience exposes a persistent blind spot in American health care: the difficulty of predicting what a pregnancy will actually cost before the bills arrive. The plan's cost-estimator tool required medical billing codes for each likely procedure — information the couple did not have. They instead relied on the plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage, which projected the $2,700 figure.

"We thought, 'OK, that's doable,'" Grace said. "We should be able to save up that much and pay for that out of pocket."

The math shifted quickly. Early in the pregnancy Grace met her $500 deductible, after which she owed 20% coinsurance for in-network care. Routine lab work — checking iron levels or screening for gestational diabetes — ran "a couple hundred dollars every visit." The hospital ultimately charged about $18,000 for the delivery.

"I thought, 'Oh no, it's going to be a lot,'" she said. "Much more than we budgeted for."

"This whole experience has me wondering (keeping me up at night, actually): Can we ever afford to have another child?" Grace asked.

## A Federal Fix, Long Delayed

Federal lawmakers anticipated this problem. As part of the 2020 No Surprises Act, Congress required insurers to give consumers an individualized "advanced explanation of benefits" estimating the cost of scheduled care. The rule was set to take effect on Jan. 1, 2022 — but regulators have yet to implement it.

"So, it's basically 4 1/2 years since this was supposed to be available, and I think it's a source of a lot of frustration for a lot of folks," said [Sabrina Corlette](https://chir.georgetown.edu/bios/sabrina-corlette/), a research professor and co-director of Georgetown University's Center on Health Insurance Reforms. "People have a right under the law to know these things."

A further change looms. Beginning Jan. 1, 2027, the long-standing "global" maternity billing codes that bundle much of pregnancy care will be replaced with itemized coding for prenatal care, labor, delivery and postpartum care. The shift could make individual service costs clearer, but Corlette is skeptical it will help families stay on budget. "I could see where it might be advantageous to the provider," she said. "But I'm not sure I'm seeing benefits to the patient."

## Five Tips for Expectant Parents

Until costs become easier to predict, the report offers practical guidance for families preparing for a baby:

**1. Enroll your baby on time.** A birth triggers a special enrollment window. Many employer plans require parents to request enrollment within 30 days; Marketplace plans generally allow 60. Miss the deadline and a newborn may go uninsured until the next year. Michelle Critzer of Ventura, California, ran into exactly that problem after her son Hudson was born in February. "The way they set hard must-do's within 30 days of having a baby is actually insanity," she said.

**2. Know your MOOP.** The maximum out-of-pocket limit is the ceiling on what you pay each year for covered, in-network care. In 2026, ACA plans cap that amount at $10,600 for an individual and [$21,200 for a family](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/out-of-pocket-maximum-limit/). Be aware that a pregnancy spanning two plan years can reset the tally and force families to max out twice. The Griffins' family MOOP was $18,000, with Grace's individual maximum at $9,000.

**3. Stay in-network.** Don't assume the hospital, labs or imaging facilities are covered just because your obstetrician is. Ask the doctor's office to send tests to in-network providers. [Federal law](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/no-surprises-understand-your-rights-against-surprise-medical-bills) shields patients at in-network facilities from out-of-network surprise bills.

**4. Check every bill and appeal if needed.** The ACA requires certain [pregnancy-related preventive services](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/pregnancy-related-preventive-services-covered-by-the-aca/) — prenatal visits, folic acid, depression screening and lactation support — at no added cost. Those charges are sometimes billed to patients by mistake.

**5. Ask for help early.** Most hospitals offer financial assistance or payment plans, but families should confirm arrangements in writing and in advance so accounts aren't turned over to collections.

Support programs can also help. Grace Griffin qualified for Michigan's Rx Kids program, which provides eligible families $1,500 during pregnancy and $500 a month for the baby's first year — up to $7,500 in total, [Bridge Michigan reports](https://bridgemi.com/michigan-health-watch/michigans-55m-experiment-guaranteed-income-begins-flint-moms/). "It takes a lot of pressure off," she said.

## What to Watch

The Griffins' story is part of a broader national anxiety about medical debt and the distance between advertised insurance coverage and real out-of-pocket exposure. Two developments bear watching: whether federal regulators finally implement the advanced explanation of benefits, and how the 2027 coding overhaul affects total maternity costs. For now, expectant parents are left to plan around estimates that, as the Griffins discovered, can fall short by thousands of dollars.
