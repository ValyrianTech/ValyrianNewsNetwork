# Belgium Blocks Reimbursement of Obesity Drug Mounjaro

Belgian federal Health Minister Frank Vandenbroucke has decided not to reimburse Mounjaro, the obesity and diabetes drug made by Eli Lilly, under the national health insurance system (RIZIV/INAMI). The decision, confirmed by the minister's cabinet to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/21/minister-vandenbroucke-houdt-terugbetaling-obesitasmedicijn-moun/) and first reported by De Tijd and L'Echo, follows his earlier refusal in June to cover Novo Nordisk's Wegovy and thrusts Belgium into the centre of a widening debate over how to fund the fast-growing, expensive class of GLP-1 medications.

Mounjaro, whose active ingredient is tirzepatide, remains reimbursable for patients with type 2 diabetes under specific conditions. It is not covered for obesity. Without reimbursement, patients face out-of-pocket costs of roughly €179 a month for the lowest dose, rising to about €300 a month for higher doses — a recurring expense that must be sustained continuously for the treatment to keep working.

## A Precedent for an Entire Drug Class

The obesity decision mirrors Vandenbroucke's June stance on Wegovy. In a [cabinet press release](https://vandenbroucke.belgium.be/nl/nieuws/obesitas-vraagt-sterk-beleid-geen-terugbetaling-van-wegovy) announcing that refusal, the minister acknowledged that following the negative advice of RIZIV's drug reimbursement committee (CTG) "was not an easy decision," describing obesity as a chronic disease that "deserves the best and, above all, the right care."

At the heart of the government's reasoning is cost. According to the cabinet, spending on these diabetes drugs quadrupled from around €30 million in 2019 to about €122 million in 2025 — more than half a billion euros over the period. Nearly 130,000 people received a reimbursed diabetes drug last year, almost four times the number in 2019 when the first such products reached the Belgian market.

The minister's office argues that general reimbursement for obesity would set a precedent for the whole GLP-1 class and could, in a scenario where every eligible patient were treated, run to several billion euros a year — potentially more than half the total medicines budget. Such spending, on a treatment whose effect fades once it stops, would "displace other necessary care" for other patients.

## The Medical Counter-Argument

The decision has drawn sharp criticism from obesity specialists. Writing in De Morgen and [reported by Farma-Sfeer](https://www.farma-sfeer.be/nl/nieuws/endocrinologen-willen-ruimere-terugbetaling-voor-ozempic-en-mounjaro-nbsp.html), endocrinologists Luc Van Gaal of UZA and Bart Van Der Schueren of UZ Leuven argued that reimbursement criteria are too strict and medically outdated.

"As a diabetes patient, you already have to be very badly off before you get coverage for a treatment whose benefits are very clear," Van Gaal said. The doctors point to evidence that GLP-1 medication reduces heart attacks, strokes, dangerous liver fat and cirrhosis, sleep apnea and some cancers — complications whose avoided costs, Van Der Schueren argues, should be weighed against the price of the drugs.

Bart Vanderschueren, an endocrinologist and obesity specialist at UZ Leuven, warned that the refusal risks creating a two-tier system. "I fear this leads to a form of class medicine," he told VRT NWS, noting that wealthier patients sometimes buy the drug before they urgently need it, while those with smaller budgets — who are genuinely stuck and need the medication, for example because of arthrosis — see their chances of treatment shrink.

Eli Lilly, the manufacturer, said it understood the budget pressures but argued the decision understates the medical impact of obesity. "A well-considered reimbursement is not a cost but an investment: by preventing complications in time, we can actually manage health spending better in the long run," said Frédéric Clais, CEO of Eli Lilly Benelux. The company has proposed tying coverage to strict medical criteria and a structured care pathway.

## Why the Effect Depends on Staying on the Drug

The government's case leans heavily on the chronic nature of the medication. Citing a BMJ systematic review and meta-analysis, the cabinet notes that weight returns after treatment stops — about 0.8 kg a month faster for users of semaglutide and tirzepatide than for those on placebo. On average, it takes about 1.5 years for weight to return to its original level after stopping medication, versus nearly four years after a lifestyle programme, and the cardiovascular benefits also fade within months to about 18 months.

Vandenbroucke is working with doctors this autumn on a broader care pathway (zorgpad) for people who are overweight or obese, involving the RIZIV, the public health ministry and the Belgian Association for the Study of Obesity. That framework, the cabinet argues, is what the Wegovy and Mounjaro applications lacked.

## An International Pattern

Belgium is not alone in hesitating. The Netherlands' Zorginstituut advised against reimbursing Wegovy, and Germany, Norway and Denmark — despite hosting Novo Nordisk's headquarters — do not cover it. The United Kingdom reimburses semaglutide only within specialised weight-management services with mandatory supervision, and limits treatment to two years. The cabinet uses that time limit to underline the core dilemma: a time-limited reimbursement funds a temporary, reversible effect, while obesity is a chronic disease requiring a durable approach.

## What to Watch

The scale of the challenge is considerable. According to health institute Sciensano, 18% of Belgians have obesity and about 49% of the population over age three is overweight; The Lancet projects that nearly 70% of Belgians will be overweight by 2050 if trends continue. Yet only a fraction of those with diabetes currently qualify for coverage of these drugs.

The coming months will hinge on the design of the new care pathway and whether it can square two competing pressures: protecting a health budget strained by a quadrupling of spending, and ensuring that effective treatment does not become a privilege of the wealthy. For now, patients seeking Mounjaro for obesity in Belgium will have to pay for it themselves — and both the manufacturer and the specialists say the debate is far from over.
