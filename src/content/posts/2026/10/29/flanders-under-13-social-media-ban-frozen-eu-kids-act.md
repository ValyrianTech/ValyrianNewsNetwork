# Flanders' Under-13 Social Media Ban Frozen a Year by EU

A Flemish decree that would bar children under 13 from social media has been put on hold for twelve months, the European Commission confirmed on Thursday, as Brussels moves to impose harmonised rules across the bloc. The freeze, reported by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/interdiction-des-reseaux-sociaux-pour-les-moins-de-13-ans-le-projet-flamand-gele-pour-un-an-par-l-eu-kids-act-11807709), also affects similar national initiatives in Austria, France and Italy and delays one of the region's most concrete measures to protect minors online.

The decision was confirmed during the Commission's midday press briefing. It hands the European Parliament and the Council the time they need to negotiate the proposed EU Kids Act, the bloc's sweeping new framework for children's online safety.

## A standard EU procedure, not a rejection

The halt is the result of a routine mechanism of European law known as TRIS, the Technical Regulations Information System. Under the procedure, when the European executive proposes a text on a subject already covered by a national draft that has been notified to Brussels, that national draft is frozen for three months, extendable to one year.

The Commission has chosen the full twelve-month option to allow the EU's co-legislators to work through its proposal. A spokesperson framed the move as a matter of coherence. "All children in the EU deserve the same level of online safety," the spokesperson said, adding that the Commission had decided to postpone by twelve months any national legislative project overlapping with the future harmonised rules.

Without that coordination, the spokesperson warned, "you end up with different ages, different laws in the different Member States" — a patchwork that would make regulation "impossible to apply."

## What the EU Kids Act proposes

The EU Kids Act was adopted by the Commission on 17 September 2026. According to the [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/eu-kids-act-restrict-social-media-platforms-access-children-eu), the proposal prohibits social media platforms from giving access to children under 13 and sets an EU-wide minimum age of 15 for minors to open an account of their own. Between 13 and 15, accounts can only be created with parental consent, through a "mini-account" linked to a parent's, with limits on social contacts and screen time.

The proposal also reverses the burden of proof: platforms would have to demonstrate that their services are age-appropriate and safe by design. Obligations extend to online services offering social media, video sharing, online video games, AI companions and chatbots to users under 18. Platforms would be required to switch minor profiles to private by default, disable geolocation and turn off chatbots and AI for minors, while banning addictive and recommended content, unlimited scrolling, contact with strangers and night-time pushes.

"Our KIDS Act is reversing the burden of proof – it is for platforms to show they are safe by design," Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said. "And we put parents back in the driving seat, giving them the tools to help their children navigate a safer online world."

The proposal must still be approved by the European Parliament and the Council before entering into force. The Commission is pressing for speed, and says draft legislation is being prepared or negotiated in 17 Member States.

## A Flemish plan built on the same logic

Flanders had been advancing its own decree to anchor a legal minimum age of 13 for social media platforms deemed harmful or insufficiently safe for minors. In a committee debate on 28 April 2026, Flemish Media Minister Cieltje Van Achter (N-VA) argued that the age-13 threshold already existed in practice but was neither respected nor enforced. Children, she said, create social media accounts at an average age of about 10 years and 3 months, and under-13s often lack the maturity to understand addictive algorithms and the content they encounter.

As the [Flemish Parliament](https://www.vlaamsparlement.be/nl/actueel/nieuws-uit-het-vlaams-parlement/hoe-wil-vlaanderen-een-minimumleeftijd-van-13-jaar-voor) reported, responsibility under the Flemish approach rests first with the platforms to ensure no under-13 users are present on their services. Enforcement would run within the existing EU Digital Services Act, with sanctions reaching up to 6% of global turnover. The minister opposed a blanket age-verification requirement, arguing that forcing every Belgian to identify themselves to access social media would not be proportionate.

Van Achter reacted positively to the EU proposal when it was unveiled. "What is on the table largely goes in the right direction," she said at the time, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/17/eu-stelt-eu-kids-act-voor-toegang-jongeren-tot-sociale-media-wo/). "It is not young people who must adapt to harmful social media; social media must adapt to young people."

She did, however, call it "very far-reaching" that 13- and 14-year-olds would be allowed only one hour per day on their accounts under the proposal, questioning whether Europe should determine for every family how much time a young teenager may spend on a platform. She also warned against turning the internet into a permanent identity check, cautioning that regulators must avoid "Big Brother" outcomes.

## Delayed, not dead

The freeze does not cancel the Flemish decree; it suspends it while the EU framework takes shape. If the Kids Act is agreed and adopted, the Flemish regime may be superseded or need to be aligned with the European rules. Either way, the precise date on which a minimum age takes effect in Flanders remains unclear, and depends on how quickly the Parliament and Council can conclude negotiations.

The episode illustrates a broader dynamic in European digital policy: regional and national initiatives increasingly find themselves subordinated to bloc-wide harmonisation. Both Brussels and Flanders say they share the same goal, but the question of who sets the age — and how it is verified — is now firmly in European hands.

What to watch for next is the pace of the legislative process. The Commission wants a deal quickly; the Parliament and Council must weigh the proposal's strict screen-time limits and its age-verification requirements, the very provisions that have already drawn scrutiny from Flemish lawmakers and privacy advocates alike.
