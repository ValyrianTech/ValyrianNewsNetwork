# Verlinden Weathers i-Police Storm, for Now

Belgian Justice Minister Annelies Verlinden is back in the crosshairs after auditors revealed that the failed i-Police digitalisation project cost taxpayers 139.4 million euros — nearly double the 76 million previously acknowledged. Yet despite the mounting controversy, political analysts say her ministerial post is not yet under threat, even as opposition parties press for a full parliamentary investigation.

The trigger was the Court of Audit's report, [published on 28 October](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/28/audit-rekenhof-i-police/), which laid bare the scale of the fiasco. The federal police bought some 30 million euros in software licences, of which 21.6 million — about 70 percent — is now useless. Of that, 7.3 million euros in licences were never used at all, while 14.2 million became worthless after being deployed only in the development phase before the project was halted.

## A Difficult Position, But Not a Sealed Fate

Verlinden, of the Flemish Christian Democrat party CD&V, was interior minister when i-Police was launched at the end of 2021 to consolidate police data systems into a single intelligence platform. She signed a 299 million euro contract with the French IT consultancy Sopra Steria. In December 2025, her successor Bernard Quintin of the Reformist Movement (MR) tore up what he called the "monster contract" after roughly 76 million euros had been spent with, in his words, no tangible result.

According to political scientist Nicolas Bouteca of Ghent University, the minister is in a tight spot but her future is not decided. As long as no hard evidence of conflict of interest emerges, he argued, she should be taken at her word. Bouteca described the allegations surrounding her past legal work for Sopra Steria as insinuation rather than proof.

Separately, a [VRT NWS analysis of her position](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/29/positie-annelies-verlinden/) notes that a minister typically resigns for one of two reasons: out of a personal sense of political responsibility, or because the party chair has had enough. Neither condition currently applies. CD&V has not withdrawn its support.

## The Conflict-of-Interest Question

At the heart of the scrutiny is the so-called revolving door. Verlinden worked as a lawyer, serving as co-managing partner of the Belgian arm of DLA Piper, and in 2019 represented Sopra Steria in a case before the EU court in Luxembourg — before becoming interior minister in 2020.

When [VRT NWS examined the conflict-of-interest question](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/02/01/heeft-verlinden-zich-schuldig-gemaakt-aan-belangenvermenging-n/) earlier this year, professor Filip De Rynck of Ghent University said he did not believe irregularities had occurred in the contract award. He argued that procedural rules were followed and that the Finance Inspectorate scrutinised the contract strictly. His view was that Verlinden should have disclosed the earlier link not because of a legal conflict, but to avoid noise — an appearance problem rather than a proven impropriety.

## A Fresh Twist Keeps the Story Alive

The controversy intensified this week when Verlinden's spokesperson and a Brussels prosecutor's spokesperson were [placed under suspicion for breach of professional secrecy](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/27/woordvoerder-verlinden-in-verdenking-gesteld-na-lekken-in-zaak-r/). The prosecutor's spokesperson is alleged to have passed confidential investigation information to Verlinden's spokesperson; whether it reached the minister herself remains unclear. Verlinden has denied wrongdoing, saying she and her former cabinet staff always acted correctly and in accordance with the law, and that she has made herself available to authorities and has nothing to hide.

The charge against her inner circle has kept the affair in the headlines and prompted even coalition partner MR to demand answers — a sign that the political buffer around her is not unlimited.

## Why the Coalition Has Held the Line

Opposition parties, including Groen, the Francophone Socialists (PS) and Vlaams Belang, are demanding a parliamentary investigative commission. So far the governing majority has declined, pointing instead to the ongoing judicial probes — a Brussels prosecutor's investigation into possible embezzlement of public money and conflict of interest — and to next week's hearings in the Interior Committee.

The calculus is partly electoral. Bouteca noted that Verlinden is a popular politician and an asset for CD&V in the Antwerp constituency, where many seats are up for grabs. Antwerp is also home turf for N-VA heavyweights Bart De Wever and Jan Jambon, which complicates any pressure campaign against her. Analysts suggest the N-VA is content to let her wriggle, since the controversy weakens her position without requiring the coalition to move against a partner.

## The Bigger Picture: Reckless Spending?

The i-Police affair has also become a symbol of something larger. Dave Sinardet of the VUB and UCL said the episode paints a picture of how recklessly the government handles taxpayers' money, coming across particularly badly at a time when politicians are engaged in savings.

The Court of Audit found the federal government spent 2.5 billion euros on consultancy between 2020 and 2022, about 80 percent of it on IT services. It also concluded that the police — and by extension the state — have become structurally dependent on external consultants for critical IT functions. Sinardet argued that political oversight is likely insufficient, and that cutting civil servants to save money while losing in-house expertise, only to hire consultants, creates a problem.

## What to Watch

Verlinden's position thus rests on a set of interlocking factors: her party's continued backing, the absence of a smoking gun on conflict of interest, the majority's refusal so far to launch an investigative commission, and her electoral value in Antwerp. Each could shift.

The key tests lie ahead. Will she appear before parliament next week, as N-VA has demanded, and how will she account for the gap between 76 million and 139.4 million euros? Will the judicial investigation produce evidence that changes the political arithmetic? And if pressure from coalition partners grows, will CD&V's leadership keep standing behind her?

For now, the answer to the question hanging over Brussels is uncomfortable for her critics: the controversy has damaged Verlinden, but it has not yet sunk her.
