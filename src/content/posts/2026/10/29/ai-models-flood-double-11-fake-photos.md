# AI Models Flood Double 11 With Hard-to-Spot Fake Photos

Ahead of China's largest annual shopping festival, AI-generated model images are spreading rapidly across the country's biggest e-commerce platforms — frequently without clear disclosure, sometimes riddled with anatomical errors, and costing merchants less than a quarter of a real photo shoot. An investigation by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34352856) (澎湃新闻) published on October 29 documents how the technology has quietly reshaped the way goods are presented to millions of shoppers during Double 11.

## The economics driving adoption

The shift is fundamentally a story about cost. According to the investigation, a traditional photo shoot for a single garment — typically producing nine images — runs roughly RMB 200 to 300 and takes one to three days to complete. AI generation can deliver comparable images for as little as RMB 5 to 6 each, with a turnaround of around 30 minutes. That represents a saving of more than 75 percent and a dramatic compression of production time.

The barrier to entry has collapsed accordingly. The Paper found AI model-image "prompt packs" and tutorials selling in volume: a women's-fashion AI prompt collection priced at RMB 16.9 sold 866 copies, while a broader e-commerce AI workflow tutorial at RMB 9.9 sold 37,000 copies. An AI image vendor told the outlet it could supply high-resolution, watermark-free originals using mainstream models plus in-house tools, recommending overseas faces to avoid what the industry calls "face collision." The same vendor said a virtual model's height, weight, and measurements could be designed arbitrarily.

## What shoppers are actually seeing

The investigation catalogued a series of revealing errors. On Pinduoduo, one listing featured a "three-legged" model. A Taobao children's-clothing listing showed hands in anatomically impossible poses. Douyin shops displayed "headless" models, and a Taobao review photo showed a figure with "three feet."

Such artifacts are the visible tip of a larger transparency problem. Many merchants do not label their images as AI-generated at all, even as listing pages carry realistic-looking height, weight, and measurement data for models who do not exist. Some merchants go further, falsely claiming their images are real product photographs. When a customer service representative for a Taobao shop was asked about an image with obvious anatomical errors, the representative insisted the photos were genuine model shots — all real product photography — and that "some difference between the online pictures and the actual product is normal."

Consumers have noticed. One social media user captured the frustration succinctly: "Before, photos were heavily retouched, but at least you could see the fabric and texture. Now they just use AI — who are they selling to, the AI?"

## A legal grey zone

The technology itself is not the problem, legal experts say — the deception is. "What the law prohibits is misleading and confusion, not the technology itself," said Fang Caihong, a lawyer at Beijing Yingke (Hangzhou) Law Firm, as quoted by The Paper. "The law does not prohibit AI technology, but AI technology is not a defense either."

According to Fang, whether an AI image constitutes false advertising or consumer fraud turns on two questions: whether the displayed content matches the actual product, and whether the merchant makes false statements or conceals defects. A fraud finding becomes likely when a product shown differs significantly from the actual goods beyond reasonable beautification, combined with false customer-service statements, concealed defects, or the removal or hiding of AI labels — potentially triggering punitive damages under China's Consumer Rights Protection Law along with administrative liability.

Fang recommended that merchants proactively declare AI use, retain explicit labels and metadata, avoid watermark-removal tools, and prominently state on product pages that model images are AI-generated and that results are for reference only.

## Platforms tighten the rules

The investigation notes that major platforms have begun responding. Taobao launched AI fake-image governance in March 2025, targeting images used to deceive consumers or infringe original brand merchants. Douyin followed in September 2025 with upgraded AI content labeling, including a labeling tool and metadata read/write support to track content provenance. By August 2026, both Xiaohongshu and Pinduoduo had issued AI image governance rules; Pinduoduo's included penalties ranging from partial or full delisting to sales bans for images that violate physical or biological laws — precisely the kind of "three-legged" errors shoppers have spotted.

The oversight gap, however, remains real. Disclosure labels are often present but hard to spot, and many merchants simply do not use them.

## Real shoots, real stakes

The rise of AI has unsettled the commercial photography and modeling trades. Staff at a commercial model-photography firm told The Paper that "the modeling industry is quite resistant to AI; few models are willing to authorize use of their faces for AI." The firm noted the technology has hurt its business — though some former clients who experimented with AI later returned to reshoot.

Firms that insist on real white-background photography point to a practical limitation: AI struggles with fabric fidelity. "You can't see the fabric texture," one photography practitioner said. The same practitioner noted that merchants relying on AI tend to see higher return and complaint rates — a cost that may offset the savings at the point of sale.

## What to watch

The Double 11 season will serve as a large-scale test of whether new platform rules can keep pace with a technology that small merchants can now deploy for the price of a coffee. The Paper's reporting, paired with a companion video documenting the latest crop of AI modeling errors, suggests the answer is not yet settled: the tools are cheap, the labels are faint, and the difference between a real garment and a rendered one can be invisible to a shopper scrolling on a phone.

The larger question — whether China's emerging AI-labeling framework can build the consumer trust that e-commerce depends on — will be answered not in policy documents but in the return rates and complaints that follow the festival's biggest sales days.
