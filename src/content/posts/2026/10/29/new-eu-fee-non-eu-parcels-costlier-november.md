# New EU Fee Makes Non-EU Parcels Costlier from November

From 1 November, a new European handling fee of €2 per product category will apply to every parcel entering the EU from outside the Union, adding fresh costs for Belgian online shoppers who order from platforms such as Temu, Shein and AliExpress. The charge comes on top of the €3 "parcel tax" that has applied since July to shipments valued under €150, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/29/vanaf-1-november-nieuwe-pakjestaks-wat-betekent-dat-voor-jou/).

Unlike the earlier tax, the new fee applies to all parcels from outside the EU, regardless of value. "This measure comes on top of the €3 tax. It applies to all parcels, not only shipments under €150," said Francis Adyns, spokesperson for the FOD Financiën, Belgium's federal finance ministry. "Moreover, the measure is of a permanent nature."

## Two charges, one bill

The €3 tax, introduced on 1 July, is temporary and runs until 1 July 2028. The €2 handling fee, by contrast, is permanent. Philippe Heeren, a lawyer specialising in customs law, noted the legal distinction: the €3 charge is a genuine tax, while the €2 charge is a retribution intended to cover the costs of customs processing. For consumers, he acknowledged, the difference makes little practical difference.

Both charges are levied per product category rather than per parcel, which can multiply the cost. Two identical pairs of trousers worth €40 from China count as one category, adding €5 in total. A €100 order of trousers, jewellery and shoes counts as three categories, adding €15. An order worth €180 spanning three categories pays standard customs duties plus an extra €6.

## Who pays, and why

The platform or supplier pays the fee but passes the cost on to shoppers. "The platform or the supplier pays the levy, but will of course pass the cost on to the consumer," Adyns said. Products already held in an EU member state — for example in Amazon warehouses in France or Germany — are not subject to the fee, since nothing is imported from outside the Union.

The measure is intended to relieve pressure on customs services, which face far more work processing millions of small parcels than bulk freight. "The idea is to ease the pressure on customs services. They obviously have much more work with millions of small parcels than with goods sent in bulk to, say, France," said Maartje Wijffelaars, senior eurozone economist at Rabobank.

## A tax that is already working

The earlier €3 tax appears to have reshaped shopping habits. According to a separate [VRT NWS report](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/28/pakjestaks-resultaten-minder-goedkope-pakjes-meer-inkomsten/), the levy raised €364 million in EU taxes since July and cut cheap-parcel declarations by 65% between July and September compared with the same period last year. September's volume of around 29 million cheap parcels was 82% lower than June's 166 million, and the average declared value of cheap goods shipped to Belgium nearly doubled, from €6 to €12.

The scale of the problem is considerable. The EU took in 4.6 billion parcels in 2024 and 5.8 billion in 2025; Belgium alone handled 1.3 billion via Liège airport last year. Customs cannot inspect them all, and parcels frequently contain counterfeits or goods that fail European safety standards. In 2026, Belgian customs recorded 17,630 violations, with one in three cases involving a breach of public-health rules.

## Timing rules and loopholes

For consumers placing orders this week that will only arrive after 1 November, the fee is applied when the parcel is declared to customs. "If it enters the European Union on or after 1 November, it falls under the new regime and the extra €2 fee is charged," Heeren said. Crucially, though, shoppers should not expect to hand €2 directly to customs. "Once a purchase is completed, a webshop can in principle no longer charge additional costs," he said — though he cautioned that during July's rollout some situations did not fully follow the rules, and platforms may simply build the cost into their prices in advance.

Customs officials have also warned of attempts to game the system. Kristian Vanderwaeren of Belgian customs said some shippers try to declare parcels above €150 to reduce their tax bill, adding that his staff are working to detect such practices. Bart Buysse, CEO of the SME organisation Unizo, welcomed the levy's results as a win for fair competition but said it is "essential that those revenues are actually invested in extra customs officers, better technology and stricter controls."

## What to watch

The immediate question is how much of the €2 fee platforms choose to absorb to keep price-sensitive customers, and whether the sharp drop in cheap parcels proves lasting once the novelty of the tax fades. Enforcement capacity, the risk of value-declaration fraud, and the future shape of EU customs reform after the €3 tax sunsets in 2028 will all determine whether the levy delivers lasting change — or simply raises the price of a bargain.
