# Greenhouse Gas Levels Hit Record Highs, WMO Warns

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases reached unprecedented levels in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization said, warning that the continued rise locks in more long-term warming for the planet for decades and even centuries. In its [Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22](https://wmo.int/resources/publication-series/greenhouse-gas-bulletin/no-22), released on 27 October 2026, the UN agency reported that carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide all set new records — findings that land just ahead of the COP31 UN climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye.

## A New Year, Another Record

The globally averaged surface concentration of CO2 reached **426.8 parts per million (ppm)** in 2025 — 153% of the 1750 pre-industrial baseline. Methane (CH4) climbed to **1,948 parts per billion (ppb)**, or 267% of the 1750 level, while nitrous oxide (N2O) hit **339.1 ppb**, 126% above 1750. All three figures were confirmed in the agency's [press release](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high).

"Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies. The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue."

The Bulletin measures atmospheric *concentration* — the accumulated stock of gas in the air — rather than new annual *emissions*. Roughly half of the CO2 emitted each year remains in the atmosphere, while the remainder is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans. This distinction is central to the story: even as emissions-reduction efforts proceed, concentrations keep climbing because CO2 persists in the atmosphere for centuries.

## Growth Slows, But Only Slightly

From 2024 to 2025, the global average CO2 concentration rose by **2.8 ppm** — above the decadal average but below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024, when a strong El Niño weakened the planet's land carbon sinks through drought, drier vegetation and more wildfires. Reporting by [France 24](https://www.france24.com/en/live-news/20261027-greenhouse-gas-levels-hit-new-record-highs-in-2025-un) put that 2023/2024 surge at 3.6 ppm.

"The more modest growth rate from 2024 to 2025 reflects recovery of the land carbon sinks and reduced fire carbon emissions in 2025," said WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova. "However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning."

Tarasova warned that concentrations are likely to climb further. "We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026 because of fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño which will impact land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024," she said. "The risks of reduced CO2 uptake by the ocean are also increasing."

The long view is sobering. When the Bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 level was 377.5 ppm — nearly 50 ppm below today's figure. WMO notes that CO2 growth rates have roughly tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from about 0.8 ppm per year to some 2.4 ppm per year over the 2011–2020 decade.

## The Overlooked Gases

Beyond the headline figures, the Bulletin flags two potent but less abundant trace gases tied to the transition to clean energy: molecular hydrogen (H2) and sulphur hexafluoride (SF6).

SF6 — used chiefly as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution — has an atmospheric lifetime of more than 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO2. Its increase in 2025 was a record high. Hydrogen, which is increasingly used as an energy storage medium, is not itself a greenhouse gas but has a strong indirect warming effect; its concentration rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024. Both illustrate potential unintended consequences of decarbonization — SF6 leaking from switchgear and hydrogen escaping from pipelines and storage.

## Why It Matters Now

According to the WMO, the Bulletin is timed to give negotiators "authoritative scientific information" as they prepare for COP31, which runs from 9 to 20 November 2026 and is framed around turning climate commitments into concrete actions. The gap between pledges and the physical reality of rising concentrations will be a central tension at the talks.

Belgian coverage from [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779336/article/2026-10-27/une-nouvelle-annee-un-nouveau-record-la-concentration-gaz-effet-serre-atteint) captured the same core message for a European audience, reflecting the continent's mounting focus on adaptation. Its climate section in recent days has also featured an EU plan to prepare for 3°C of warming by 2100 and a call by 146 scientists to declare climate change a global health emergency before COP31.

## What to Watch

Three questions will shape the coming year. First, whether the developing 2026 El Niño pushes CO2 growth back toward the exceptional 2023/2024 levels, as the WMO warns. Second, whether COP31 delivers meaningful action on methane — fast-acting but comparatively under-addressed — and on curbing SF6 and hydrogen leakage from the energy transition. Third, whether the weakening of ocean carbon sinks accelerates, eroding the planet's capacity to absorb the emissions humanity continues to produce.

For now, the direction of travel is unambiguous. "Every additional part per million matters because it traps additional heat," Barrett told reporters. The data, she suggested, leaves little room for complacency.
