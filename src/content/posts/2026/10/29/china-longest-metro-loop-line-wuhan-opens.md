# China's Longest Metro Loop Line Opens in Wuhan

Wuhan Metro Line 12 entered full operation on 29 October 2026, closing a 59.9-kilometre circular route that China's national metro association confirms as the longest loop line in the country. The line runs through 37 underground stations, links the central Chinese city's two major railway hubs, and can interchange with 18 existing and planned metro routes, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261029/c38f88f99ff546eeb7e978cf94ed9c6d/c.html).

## A Ring That Completes the Network

The opening marks a structural shift for Wuhan's rail system. Line 12 is the city's first true circular line, completing a "ring-and-spoke" design in which orbital routes distribute passengers around the urban core rather than forcing every journey through it. That layout is intended to relieve pressure on the busiest central corridors and cut journey times for cross-city commuters.

Wuhan, a major hub on the Yangtze Economic Belt, is split by two large rivers. The Yangtze and the Han naturally divide the city into distinct zones, and bridging them has long shaped its transport planning. Line 12 is designed specifically to erode that barrier: it serves seven central urban districts, including Qiaokou, Jianghan, Jiang'an, Qingshan, Hongshan, Wuchang and Hanyang, weaving them into a single continuous loop.

A full circuit now takes roughly 96 minutes, and the network's total operating mileage has pushed past 581 kilometres with the new line in service.

## Built in Two Phases

The project opened in stages. The first phase, covering 35.32 kilometres and 23 stations, began operations on 1 May 2026. The second phase, adding 24.6 kilometres, opened on 29 October to close the loop. Delivering a circular line in halves is common practice for large urban rail projects, since the full benefit of an orbital route only materialises once the final segment connects the end of the arc back to its beginning.

Construction carried a heavy engineering burden. The line crosses under the Yangtze River twice and under the Han River once, navigating high water pressure, mixed and composite strata, and long underwater shield-tunnelling distances.

One of the most demanding segments, the Danshuichi-to-Kexue Park cross-river tunnel, stretches about four kilometres, with roughly 2.16 kilometres passing beneath the river. Its lowest point sits approximately 71.4 metres below the Yangtze's surface. The project is regarded domestically as a demonstration of clustered cross-river metro tunnelling, with multiple tunnel-boring machines advancing at once through water-rich sandy layers.

## How Long Is "Longest"?

The achievement is real, but the superlative deserves precision. Xinhua frames the line as China's longest metro loop, a designation confirmed by the China Association of Metros. Some regional outlets have gone further, describing it as Asia's longest loop and even, in promotional content, the world's longest.

The most defensible reading is that Line 12 is China's longest and Asia's longest metro loop, and among the longest in the world. Moscow's Big Circle Line, at roughly 70 kilometres, is generally considered the longest loop globally, which makes the "world's longest" framing an overstatement rather than an established fact. The distinction matters in a field where cities closely track bragging rights alongside engineering benchmarks.

## What It Means

Beyond the record-book framing, the practical consequences are straightforward. Circular lines tend to reduce the number of transfers a passenger needs, spread crowding more evenly across a network, and make peripheral districts more reachable without a detour through the centre. For a river-split city, that can change how residents move between banks for work, school and daily errands.

The line also illustrates how far Chinese underwater tunnelling has progressed. Large-diameter boring machines crossing major rivers at depth are now routine enough that a single line can cross the Yangtze twice, a capability that only a handful of countries can match at scale.

## What's Next

With the loop closed, attention turns to how riders adopt the new route and whether the promised relief to cross-river commuting materialises. Transport officials will be watching ridership patterns, transfer volumes and the effect on the busiest existing lines. The new interchanges with 18 routes also raise the stakes for timetable coordination across the network. For now, Wuhan has delivered a piece of infrastructure that reshapes its transit map, even if the title it carries is one best stated with care.
