# Banned Pesticides Linger in Flemish Soil 50 Years On

Traces of long-banned pesticides such as DDT are still present in the soil around Sint-Truiden, Hoogstraten and Roeselare, fifty years after they were outlawed, according to the first-ever regional mapping of pesticide residues in Flemish soil. The [OVAM](https://ovam.vlaanderen.be/w/pesticidenresiduen-vlaamse-bodem) study found at least one pesticide or breakdown product in 44% of 118 samples, but concluded the results are largely reassuring, with no general health risk to the public.

## What the study found

The exploratory study, carried out by the Flemish Public Waste Agency (OVAM) together with research institute VITO, took 118 soil samples across three regions known for intensive historical pesticide use: Haspengouw around Sint-Truiden (fruit growing), the Noorderkempen around Hoogstraten (soft fruit and horticulture), and central West Flanders around Roeselare (potatoes).

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/20/ovam-rapport-onderzoek-pesticiden-bodem-sint-truiden-hoogstrate/), traces of at least one pesticide or its breakdown product appeared in 44% of samples, while at least two substances turned up in 27%. Sampling spanned around 100 locations across 26 municipalities in the three regions, covering farmland, private vegetable and kitchen gardens, nature reserves, public green spaces and recreational areas. The samples were taken deliberately in winter to catch compounds that persist longest in the soil.

The dominant residues were historical: DDT and its breakdown products, alongside dieldrin. Belgium banned DDT in 1974, but the compound and similar organochlorine pesticides break down extremely slowly, explaining why they can still be detected roughly half a century later.

"Glyphosate itself was found in only four places," OVAM spokesperson Jan Verheyen said, noting that its breakdown product AMPA appeared in 13 samples, far less prevalent than the legacy substances.

## Gardens as a hotspot

Perhaps the most striking finding is where the highest concentrations turned up. Rather than on farmland, elevated DDT levels were concentrated in private gardens. "On agricultural plots the concentrations were generally much lower, but in some private gardens we found elevated concentrations," Verheyen explained.

In four individual cases, all private gardens, concentrations exceeded risk thresholds under one specific scenario: eating eggs from the owner's own backyard chickens. The pathway is well understood: DDT accumulates from soil into plants and worms, then into chickens, and finally into their eggs.

"The results are largely reassuring and do not point to a general health risk," said Werner Annaert, OVAM's director-general. "With targeted monitoring we can now follow up where additional research is most useful, including in private gardens."

## Why it matters

The findings, [reported](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/pesticiden-in-44-procent-van-bodemstalen-maar-ovam-ziet-geen-algemeen-gezondheidsrisico~a18d8f54/) by HLN, illustrate the long shadow that historical agrochemical use can cast. Pesticide regulation was far looser in the past, and substances once applied liberally can persist in soil long after they are banned, a legacy that existing monitoring of food, surface water and groundwater does not fully capture.

OVAM framed the study as a scientific baseline rather than a response to any known contamination requiring remediation. Its purpose, the agency said, was simply to establish the current state of the soil in these regions, complementing existing monitoring of food, surface water and groundwater.

The garden findings also underscore a lesser-known exposure route for households. While regulators tend to focus on commercial agriculture, the data suggest that small private plots, where soil may have absorbed decades of domestic pesticide use, warrant a closer look. For residents who keep chickens, the advice is practical rather than alarmist: if tests show elevated soil concentrations, caution over consuming home-produced eggs is reasonable.

More broadly, the results add to a growing body of evidence that the environmental footprint of agrochemicals outlasts their legal authorisation by generations. DDT's persistence, documented in soils from Belgium to the Arctic, is a case study in why regulators increasingly weigh a substance's long-term behaviour, not just its immediate efficacy, when approving or restricting its use.

## What's next

The agency is recommending continued targeted sampling and monitoring, particularly in private gardens, and has proposed organising a collection campaign for pesticides held by private citizens. The results suggest that while the broader public faces little risk, the legacy of banned chemicals is one that will require patient, place-specific attention for years to come, a reminder that environmental contamination rarely disappears on the timetable of the laws meant to stop it.
