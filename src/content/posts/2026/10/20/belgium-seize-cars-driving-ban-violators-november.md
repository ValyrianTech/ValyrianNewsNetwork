# Belgium to seize cars of driving-ban violators from November

From 1 November, motorists in Belgium who get behind the wheel despite a court-imposed driving ban will automatically lose their vehicle, after a legal reform that turns confiscation from a discretionary option into a mandatory rule, [VRT NWS reports](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/20/wie-rijverbod-negeert-is-voortaan-auto-kwijt/). Police judges will be obliged to declare the car forfeited, and the vehicle will become state property — typically sold at public auction, with the proceeds going to the national treasury.

## Closing a legal loophole

Previously, vehicle forfeiture was a discretionary instrument that judges applied selectively depending on the circumstances and severity of an offence. Under the new amendment — which [modifies Article 50, §2 of Belgium's Road Traffic Act of 16 March 1968](https://www.bpb-advocaten.be/nl/actualiteit/rijden-spijts-verval-zal-mogelijks-strenger-bestraft-worden-wetsvoorstel), according to a legal analysis by the Bruges law firm BPB Advocaten — forfeiture becomes the default outcome for anyone convicted of *rijden spijts verval*, meaning driving while one's licence is suspended or revoked. That category covers offences such as drink-driving, serious speeding, repeat violations, and medical unfitness.

The bill was introduced by Wouter Raskin, an MP for the Flemish nationalist party N-VA, who has argued that driving-ban violators form a particularly persistent group. According to [VRT NWS's coverage of the bill's passage through the Chamber's Mobility Committee in January](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/01/27/politierechters-moeten-wagen-van-bestuurders-zonder-rijbewijs-ve/), a 2019 study by Vias, the Belgian road safety institute, found that these drivers have the highest recidivism rates and appear frequently in accident statistics. At that committee stage Raskin framed the measure bluntly: "We hit traffic criminals where it hurts."

## "Take away their toy"

Raskin has been consistent about the intent behind the law. "You take the toy away from that type of profile when they refuse to change their behaviour," he said, framing the measure as a deterrent aimed squarely at repeat offenders who treat bans with contempt.

"To make clear to these people that this really cannot be tolerated, we are intervening strongly by obliging police judges to actually take that car and declare it forfeited," he added, in comments carried by VRT NWS.

In a [statement on his own website](https://www.wouterraskin.be/nieuws/neem-altijd-auto-af-van-wie-rijdt-zonder-rijbewijs), Raskin returned to the human stakes: "It could just be your child who gets knocked down by someone with a driving ban."

Judges retain a narrow escape route. They may still deviate from the mandatory forfeiture only in exceptional cases and with extensive, explicit justification — for instance where a medical emergency is involved, where the impact would be disproportionate, or where special personal circumstances apply.

## A significant gap remains

One limitation has drawn attention: forfeiture only applies when the vehicle is actually owned by the person who violated the ban. Drivers who violate a ban while using a company car, a rental, or a partner's or family member's vehicle are not caught by the measure.

Raskin acknowledges the gap and says he is quietly preparing a follow-up bill. "We know that a large group of people also drive around and commit offences without owning a vehicle — people who drive company cars, for example," he told VRT NWS. "We are therefore working in silence on a similar proposal to also catch drivers who drive vehicles that are not part of their own assets, that do not belong to them."

## Analysis: symbolic force versus practical reach

The reform is a clear signal of a broader political push in Belgium — driven notably by N-VA — for tougher treatment of traffic offenders. Its symbolic weight is considerable: obliging judges to seize cars sends a message that repeated disregard for driving bans carries a tangible, material cost.

Yet the measure's practical reach may be narrower than its headline impact. Because forfeiture hinges on ownership, the offenders most likely to be driving company or borrowed cars slip through — a loophole Raskin's promised second bill has yet to close, with no timeline announced. Legal practitioners have also flagged proportionality concerns, noting that confiscation can be particularly far-reaching for people who depend on a car for work or family life, which is why the motivated-exception route remains in the text.

Enforcement volumes remain another open question. The number of vehicles actually seized will depend on how effectively police detect people driving despite a ban — the so-called *pakkans* — which is difficult to predict from the legislation alone.

## What's next

The rule enters into force on 1 November, just under two weeks from now. The immediate test will be how prosecutors and police judges apply the new obligation in practice, and how quickly the anticipated follow-up legislation to capture non-owner drivers is tabled. Until then, the law gives driving bans a sharper edge — but one that, for now, has a clearly marked blind spot.
