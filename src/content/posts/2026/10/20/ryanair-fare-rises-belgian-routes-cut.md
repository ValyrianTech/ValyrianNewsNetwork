# Ryanair Warns of 20% Fare Rises, Cuts Belgian Routes

Ryanair expects European airfares to rise by as much as 20% in 2027 and is sharply scaling back its Belgian operations this winter, leaving Brussels Airport (Zaventem) with just a single destination. Group CEO Michael O'Leary announced the moves at a press conference in Brussels on 20 October 2026, tying the route cuts directly to the federal government's increase in the Belgian aviation tax.

According to [Het Laatste Nieuws](https://www.hln.be/zaventem/ryanair-verwacht-dramatisch-duurdere-vliegtickets-in-2027-en-zet-mes-in-belgisch-aanbod-nog-maar-een-verbinding-vanop-zaventem-deze-winter~af3838a8/), O'Leary said ticket prices "will rise dramatically in Europe in 2027," forecasting increases "of 10, 15 and possibly even 20 percent." He attributed the coming surge to high fuel prices and the expiration of airlines' fuel-hedging contracts, which will force carriers to pass higher costs on to passengers.

## A Winter of Retrenchment for Belgium

The Belgian cuts are severe. From Zaventem, only the Dublin route will remain for the winter 2026-2027 season, according to HLN — a loss of seven connections to Barcelona, Madrid, Malaga, Marrakech, Porto, Rome and Valencia. Ryanair has had no aircraft based at Brussels Airport since 2022, so the change largely removes point-to-point links. At [Charleroi](https://www.21news.be/nl/exclusief-ryanair-gebruikt-belgische-taks-als-bliksemafleider-de-problemen-in-charleroi-zitten-vooral-bij-boeing/), the airline's Belgian base will shrink from 19 aircraft to 13, cutting six planes, around 200 jobs and 13 routes — leaving 75 destinations for the winter.

O'Leary summarised the impact as "6 aircraft, 20 routes and more than a million seats from and to Belgium," and threatened to cut a further million seats in summer 2027 if the tax remains in place.

## The Tax at the Heart of the Dispute

O'Leary laid the blame squarely on the federal government of Prime Minister Bart De Wever, describing the route cuts as the result of "the stupid increase of the aviation tax." The Belgian boarding tax (vliegtaks) is set to rise to €7 per departing passenger on 1 January 2027, up from €2 in 2025 — which O'Leary characterised as "an increase of 250 percent compared with the €2 in 2025." An earlier plan to lift the levy to €10 was reportedly moderated to €7 after pushback.

The airline has warned repeatedly that it would move capacity out of Belgium. In a December 2025 press release quoted by [Redactie24](https://www.redactie24.be/overig/ryanair-het-kotsbeu-met-belgie-en-grijpt-drastisch-door-schuld-van-jullie-regering-186672), Ryanair called on the government to abolish the tax, "otherwise Belgian air traffic will collapse and fares will skyrocket." O'Leary now threatens to shift aircraft to countries without such levies — naming Italy, Slovakia and Sweden.

## A Convenient Lightning Rod?

The tax narrative, however, is contested. An analysis by 21News argues that Ryanair is using the Belgian levy as a "lightning rod," with the real driver being a shortage of aircraft and a need to redeploy a constrained fleet toward more competitive markets. Boeing 737 MAX 10 delivery delays — with first deliveries not expected until early 2027 — have left Ryanair with fewer planes than planned, the outlet reported. Meanwhile, Wizz Air is opening new bases in Spain, drawing capacity southward.

Wouter Dewulf, a professor of aviation economics at the University of Antwerp, has noted that Ryanair's ticket prices are shifting toward the €60-70 range, pressuring the ultra-low-cost model. Independent observers also point out that even at €7, the Belgian tax remains far below those of its neighbours — the Netherlands charges €30.25, Germany €15.53 and France €33.86.

Critics add that removing aircraft in winter is routine for Ryanair, that the airline holds roughly 85% dominance at Charleroi, and that the base remains profitable — benefiting from low airport fees subsidised by the Walloon Region. On that reading, the announced cuts may be partly a temporary, reversible capacity reshuffle wrapped in a political message rather than a permanent withdrawal.

## The Stakes for Travellers and the Region

The nearer-term concern for passengers is price. O'Leary advised travellers to "book their tickets as soon as possible, because prices will only go up." Airlines may introduce fuel surcharges as hedging protections expire, and Ryanair's most recent quarterly profit fell 34%, hit by Middle East tensions and rising kerosene prices. Fuel accounts for roughly 40% of total costs, and rising EU carbon costs under the Emissions Trading System add further pressure.

For Belgium, the dispute sharpens a political question about how to fund budget consolidation without undermining connectivity. Brussels Airlines has previously warned that a higher aviation tax "goes against the trend" in other countries, many of which have cut or scrapped levies to attract traffic. Ryanair, in turn, is holding out a growth plan: it says it could lift Belgian traffic by half to 16 million passengers a year, re-base aircraft at Brussels Airport and add hundreds of jobs — but only if the government scraps the tax and relaxes night-flight rules at Zaventem.

## What to Watch

Several variables will determine whether the cutbacks become permanent. The trajectory of Boeing deliveries, Ryanair's redeployment toward Spain, and the government's willingness to revisit the tax will all shape the winter and summer 2027 schedules. For now, Belgian travellers face a thinner route map and the prospect of steeper fares — with the airline and the government each betting the other will blink first.

For its part, Ryanair frames the fight as a test of whether Belgium wants low-cost connectivity at all. Whether the tax or the aircraft shortage is the true cause, the practical outcome is the same: fewer Ryanair planes over Belgium, and higher prices in the year ahead.
