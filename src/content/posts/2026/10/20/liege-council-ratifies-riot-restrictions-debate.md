# Liège council ratifies riot restrictions amid heated debate

Liège's communal council voted late on Monday 19 October to confirm the emergency ordinance that banned public gatherings of more than three people across the city during more than two weeks of student-led unrest, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/emeutes-a-liege-le-recit-d-une-longue-soiree-de-debats-entre-majorite-et-opposition-au-conseil-communal-11802729). The measure, taken by Mayor Willy Demeyer (PS) on 7 October and in force until midnight on 16 October, was ratified by 28 votes to 14 with one abstention out of 43 voters, after a marathon session that exposed deep divisions over how the unrest was handled.

## Context: from school blockades to a city under restriction

The unrest began on Thursday 1 October, when hundreds of secondary-school pupils mobilised outside schools in the Liège region in a spontaneous, decentralised movement coordinated over social media. Their grievances were broad: support for striking teachers opposing the Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles "décret-programme" reforms, a heavier teaching load, cuts to free supplies and school meals, a higher enrolment fee and the planned end of the cheap TEC bus pass for 18-to-24-year-olds. Pupils also cited overcrowded classes and degraded study conditions.

Most secondary schools in Liège's communal and free networks closed their doors on 2 October, and violent incidents, arrests and injured officers followed. On 7 October, with the situation escalating, Demeyer issued the emergency ordinance barring gatherings of more than three people. Under Belgian law, such an emergency measure must be confirmed by the next communal council, which is why the item was on Monday's agenda.

## The mayor's defence

Demeyer opened by acknowledging the gravity of the decision. "We are in a very particular moment, in which an exceptional decision that infringes on public freedoms is presented to the communal council," he said. He defended the ordinance as a means of preventing rioters from massing: "It must be noted that it worked, because we no longer experienced riots."

The mayor laid out the toll. Between 1 and 19 October there were 327 arrests, of whom 170 were minors and 156 adults; 143 of those arrested live in Liège, 144 elsewhere in the Liège region and 14 elsewhere in Belgium. Forty-four police officers were injured, while no injuries were recorded among young protesters, "subject to verification," he said. Forty-three businesses were affected, and €200,000 of street furniture was destroyed — equivalent to a full normal year's street-furniture budget, meaning the 2027 allocation is "already consumed."

Demeyer announced he would personally meet, one by one, anyone contesting their police control, and would open "spaces for dialogue" and a "forum for discussion and meetings" with young people, without giving a timetable. He also warned that money for shopkeepers and young people was not yet secured: "For the shopkeepers, we don't have the first euro. For the young people, we don't have the first euro. And if this continues, we will have fewer police officers tomorrow than today."

## The opposition's anger

The opposition did not hold back. Sophie Lecron, leader of the PTB group, accused the authorities of "docilely" applying the consequences of the political choices of the MR-Engagés parties with which the PS is in coalition, and of failing to listen to young people. Her colleague Sofia Touhami called the ordinance an unprecedented "precedent" and a "disproportionate" decision.

Caroline Saal of Vert Ardent described the measure as "the result of a failure in the management of public order" and criticised collateral damage from policing. "People who had nothing to do with it found themselves set upon without understanding what was happening," she said, describing tear gas thrown "in all directions" as families and elderly people evacuated the Saint-Lambert gallery.

Even the majority was not entirely unified. Michel de Lamotte of Les Engagés backed the ordinance as "founded," but with evident discomfort: "Mr. Mayor, I have a taste of ash in my mouth. Les Engagés also have this taste of ash in their mouth. I do not accept, and we do not accept, what happened." Diana Nikolic, leader of the MR group, insisted "the police are not our enemy" and accused the opposition of "blowing on the embers," while conceding the ordinance arrived "when the harm was already done." François Schreuer, a PS+ councillor, supported the mayor but said he was "not convinced at this stage that eight days were necessary," preferring a 24- or 48-hour ban.

## Analysis: a dispute over causes and consequences

The session crystallised two competing readings of the crisis. The majority framed police force — tear gas, water cannon — as a justified consequence of violence that had put the city "under siege," in Nikolic's words. The opposition framed it as the product of austerity and a failure to listen, with the ordinance itself as the symptom. The mayor said his delay in acting was a matter of resources, not will: "The situation had to become really serious for us to obtain the necessary staff. We obtained them on the Thursday. And to take an ordinance without having the means to apply it is the best way to burn your last cartridge and discredit yourself."

Demeyer also used the debate to attack a proposed federal "gendarmerie 2.0" backed by Interior Minister Bernard Quintin and his colleague, warning it would import a French-style model of policing. He defended Belgium's tradition of "negotiated management of public space." The exchange underlined how a local dispute over school reforms has become entangled with national arguments about policing, budgets and political responsibility.

## What's next

The ordinance is now formally confirmed, but the mayor's closing words made clear the matter is far from settled: "I think the sequence is not over." Several questions remain open. Will the promised forum for dialogue with young people actually convene and yield concrete policy responses? Will funding materialise for the shopkeepers who reported an average 50 percent drop in turnover, and for youth programmes? And with a weekend of fresh disturbances on 17 October in both Liège and Brussels — including attacks on property and the Brussels PS federation headquarters — the pressure on both the city and federal authorities is unlikely to ease soon.
