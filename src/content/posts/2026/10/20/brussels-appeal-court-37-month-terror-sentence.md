# Brussels Appeal Court Upholds 37-Month Terror Sentence

The Brussels Court of Appeal on Tuesday sentenced a 21-year-old man from Opwijk, in Flemish Brabant, to 37 months in prison for inciting others to commit an attack while simultaneously planning a double attack in Belgium, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-cour-d-appel-de-bruxelles-condamne-un-jeune-homme-a-37-mois-de-prison-pour-la-planification-d-un-double-attentat-11802909). The ruling slightly raises the three-year sentence handed down by a lower court in April 2025, against which the defendant had appealed.

The case closes one of Belgium's most closely watched far-right terrorism prosecutions, exposing how encrypted messaging channels and a manifesto-based "lone actor" model have become central concerns for Belgian intelligence and judicial authorities.

## Context: From Telegram to the Courtroom

The young man was flagged first by intelligence services and then by the justice system for his activity on far-right channels on the Telegram messaging service, according to RTBF. There, he displayed deep sympathy for a range of violent far-right figures and was accused of inciting other participants to commit attacks.

His case fits a broader pattern European counterterrorism officials have tracked for more than a decade: online radicalization through loosely moderated chat platforms, followed by the publication of a written manifesto. Like Anders Breivik in Norway and Brenton Tarrant in New Zealand, the defendant wrote a manifesto in which he called for the creation of a white ethnic state.

## The Planned Attacks

According to the RTBF account of the ruling, the defendant planned two attacks: setting fire to a mosque and killing as many people as possible with an automatic rifle. The federal prosecutor's office deployed an undercover infiltrator to determine whether he intended to act on his intentions. The young man made contact with the agent while searching for weapons—a detail that proved pivotal in establishing the link between ideology and concrete criminal planning.

The defense contested that link. According to the defendant's lawyers, even though he holds far-right views, he never incited anyone to commit an attack and never personally harbored such plans. The Court of Appeal nevertheless sided with the prosecution, upholding the conviction and extending the sentence by one month.

## Immediate Arrest Request Denied

In a notable procedural development, the federal prosecutor's office requested that the defendant be taken into immediate custody following the pronouncement of the sentence on Tuesday. The Court of Appeal did not grant that request, suggesting the court did not view him as an immediate flight or safety risk despite the conviction.

## Analysis: A Test Case for Intent

The Opwijk case illustrates a legal tension that has defined European counterterrorism prosecutions in recent years: the boundary between extremist ideology and punishable criminal intent. Belgium's use of an undercover infiltrator was significant precisely because it allowed investigators to test whether the suspect would move from rhetoric to action—the threshold that distinguishes thought from attempt.

The case also reflects a sustained focus by Belgian authorities on far-right violence, which has increasingly shared the counterterrorism spotlight with jihadist threats. Telegram's encrypted channels have become a known organizing vector for extremist groups across the continent, complicating detection and raising questions about platform accountability.

At 37 months, the sentence is relatively modest for a case involving plans for a mass-casualty attack. The court's decision not to order immediate detention will likely renew debate in Belgium over whether the justice system adequately protects the public when dealing with radicalized individuals who have not yet carried out an attack.

## What's Next

The ruling resolves the appellate phase of the case, though its implications extend well beyond one defendant. Belgian policymakers and prosecutors continue to grapple with how to treat manifesto-writing extremists who plot violence online—and how much weight to give undercover evidence in proving intent. For now, the Court of Appeal's decision signals that the prosecution's approach—combining online evidence, manifesto content, and infiltrator testimony—can secure convictions, while leaving open the question of whether the resulting sentences are proportionate to the threat.
