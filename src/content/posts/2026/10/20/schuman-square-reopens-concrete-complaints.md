# Schuman Square Reopens to a Chorus of Concrete Complaints

The redevelopment of Brussels' Schuman roundabout into a pedestrian-focused public square is officially complete after roughly three years of works, but the result has left many local residents and shopkeepers unimpressed. Critics describe the new "Place Schuman" as a large, empty, concrete circle that falls well short of the project's original ambition, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/rond-point-schuman-un-grand-cercle-vide-betonne-les-riverains-decus-par-la-nouvelle-place-publique-11802982).

## Context: A Symbolic Site Transformed

Schuman is the symbolic heart of Brussels' European district, home to the Berlaymont headquarters of the European Commission and the Council, and a major transport hub served by a train station, a metro station and the Loi tunnel. The project, led by federal construction agency Beliris and designed by Bruxelles Mobilité, was intended to turn a car-dominated roundabout into a shared public space favouring pedestrians and cyclists, opening a sightline from Rue de la Loi to the Cinquantenaire park.

The works were financed largely through the European recovery plan, with RTBF citing a total cost of €29 million, of which €21.5 million came from EU funds. Other outlets have reported figures above €40 million following construction-sector inflation and additional works.

## Disappointment Among Residents and Traders

Daniel Taton, a resident of 40 years and member of the residents' association Schuman Square, was blunt in his assessment. "We now have a big empty, concrete circle that doesn't match the project," he said. "An incredible amount of vegetation was removed. There was a ring of trees around the roundabout. All that was removed and replaced by a few flower pots."

Taton also highlighted mobility problems, arguing that the new layout makes it harder for locals to cross the square and forces detours that clog surrounding streets. A snack-shop owner on a corner of the former roundabout, identified only as Raphaël, echoed the sentiment, saying a more symbolic tree or more greenery would have improved the result.

Serge, manager of the nearby bistrot "Au coin du Diable," said car access to the other side of the neighbourhood had become "a nightmare," adding that traders now see fewer people from across the district. Local shopkeepers have gone further in recent weeks, with some telling [La DH](https://www.dhnet.be/regions/bruxelles/bruxelles-mobilite/2026/10/13/le-quartier-est-mort-les-gens-ne-savent-plus-passer-des-commerces-pleurent-la-disparition-du-rond-point-a-schuman-P3MFMML6DFBBTFRWDWOCBJR4RA/) that "the neighbourhood is dead" following the roundabout's disappearance.

Not everyone is dissatisfied. One cyclist interviewed by RTBF said the square was "nicer" and easier to reach by bike, with fewer cars around.

## Beliris Defends the Design

Beliris has pushed back against the criticism, pointing to factors tied to the site itself. "Here we are above a train and metro station. There are only 35 centimetres of earth under our feet. And there's also a limit on the weight: 500 kilograms per square metre," spokesperson Elien de Swaef said, explaining why trees cannot be planted in open ground.

In a statement, the agency emphasised that developing Schuman means building on the roof of the Schuman station, the metro station and the Loi tunnel, and that these underground infrastructures constrain what can be done at the surface. Beliris says the project includes 49 trees and more than 1,100 square metres of plantings within the project perimeter, placed in specially adapted containers — though residents maintain that the mature heritage ring of trees was removed.

A key element of the original design, a circular canopy that would have given the square shelter and a stronger identity, was never built. Its cost rose from an estimated €3–5 million to around €13 million, and [Beliris declined to finance it](https://www.dhnet.be/regions/bruxelles/bruxelles-mobilite/2025/08/29/cest-officiel-beliris-ne-financera-pas-lauvent-du-rond-point-schuman-I7XWBP4XBBAKFDX7GDTZDICL2Y/), leaving the space looking unfinished and heavily mineral.

## A Troubled Inauguration

The dissatisfaction has extended even to the official proceedings. An inauguration planned with federal and regional authorities was [cancelled at short notice](https://www.dhnet.be/regions/bruxelles/bruxelles-mobilite/2026/10/15/le-malaise-continue-au-rond-point-schuman-linauguration-officielle-en-presence-de-ministres-est-finalement-annulee-NRXXSOH4LVDODAGLOWVJEW5FKU/) in mid-October amid what La DH described as malaise and scheduling problems. The outlet has documented a string of oddities around the project, including the installation of a [bus lane on a square where buses will no longer run](https://www.dhnet.be/regions/bruxelles/bruxelles-mobilite/2026/10/15/encore-plus-de-surrealisme-au-rond-point-schuman-une-bande-bus-a-ete-creee-alors-que-les-bus-ne-rouleront-plus-sur-la-place-CHX44M6UAZG7DGO5AKXENZX6ZA/), and ran an editorial calling the square ["the symbol of what doesn't turn right"](https://www.dhnet.be/actu/edito/2026/10/16/rond-point-schuman-le-symbole-de-ce-qui-ne-tourne-pas-rond-QNDUUG4RMBCATKHNSLE6XZJ53Y/).

Criticism had been building for months. In July, [The Brussels Times](https://www.brusselstimes.com/brussels-2/2209235/near-completion-schuman-square-raises-questions-about-image-of-europes-capital) reported that the project's own architect had called the result "uninhabitable" and "a symbol for the failure of the city." A change.org petition has called for an "ambitious, climate-adapted" square with the central canopy.

## Analysis and What's Next

The Schuman episode illustrates the difficulty of applying modern mobility principles — fewer cars, more room for pedestrians and cyclists — to a highly constrained urban site. The underground infrastructure left engineers with little leeway, but the abandonment of the canopy stripped the square of a design anchor. Beliris presents the redevelopment as a step rather than a final point, and the central part of the square remains under review, with the Brussels Region reportedly considering further improvements in response to the criticism.

A Limited Access Zone with automatic bollards and ANPR cameras is due to regulate traffic around the square. For now, the site that was meant to showcase Europe's capital to the continent — and the world — stands as a test case in how ambition, engineering limits and public expectations collide.
