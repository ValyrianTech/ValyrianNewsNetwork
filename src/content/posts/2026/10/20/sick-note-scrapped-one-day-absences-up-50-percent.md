# Sick note scrapped: one-day absences up more than 50%

Allowing Belgian workers at large companies to take a sick day without a doctor's note has driven a 54% increase in single-day sick reports, according to a new impact study that is sharpening a political battle over absenteeism and labour-market reform.

The analysis, by HR-services provider [Securex](https://press.securex.be/versoepeling-ziektebriefje-zorgde-voor-ruim-de-helft-meer-eendaagse-ziekmeldingen) together with Prof. Philip Verwimp of HIVA-KU Leuven and ULB, found that employees at firms with 50 to 499 staff took on average 0.12 more one-day sick reports per person as a result of the policy — the equivalent of one extra short absence per eight employees.

## How the measure works

Since late November 2022, workers at Belgian companies with at least 50 employees have been allowed to take a limited number of single days off sick each year without a medical certificate — a measure introduced by the De Croo federal government, partly to ease pressure on family doctors and to discourage employees from coming to work ill.

The scheme originally allowed up to three such days a year. Since 1 January 2026, that maximum has been cut to two.

To isolate the effect of the policy, Securex compared companies just below the 50-employee threshold — where the note requirement remained in force — with those just above it, using anonymised sick-leave records covering more than 24,000 employers in 2021. This difference-in-differences design allowed researchers to separate the policy's impact from broader trends in absenteeism.

## What the numbers show

At small firms (5 to 49 employees), average one-day sick reports per employee rose modestly from 0.17 in 2021 to 0.25 in 2024 — a gain of 0.08. At large firms, where the certificate requirement was lifted, the figure climbed from 0.22 to 0.42, an increase of 0.20. The 0.12 gap between the two trends is attributed to the policy change, amounting to a 54% rise.

Notably, the increase in one-day absences did not come with any decline in longer absences. The number of two- to five-day sick reports remained statistically stable, undermining the argument that short recovery days are preventing more serious illness.

"The relaxation of the sick note has visibly lowered the threshold for a one-day sick report. But we see no indication that two-to-five-day sick reports decrease as a result," said Heidi Verlinden, a senior research project manager at Securex and co-author of the study. She noted that earlier Securex research suggests possible long-term benefits, since "now and then one day of recovery time can prevent long-term absence."

The study also found that overall short-term absenteeism — absences of up to one month, the period of guaranteed pay — did not change in a statistically significant way. There is no indication, in other words, that the measure raised direct wage costs for employers.

## A split among experts

Labour economists are divided on how to read the findings. Prof. Stijn Baert of Ghent University told [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/afschaffing-doktersbriefje-leidt-tot-meer-eendagszieken-ik-denk-dat-we-vooral-met-extra-valsspelers-te-maken-hebben~a4430eb2/) that the result was expected. "That abolishing the sick note would lower the threshold for absence was indeed expected. That is precisely why many warned against this system," he said.

Baert argued the data point to misuse rather than genuine recovery. "If it were about preventing something worse, you would also expect more two- and three-day illnesses. That is not the case," he said, adding that he would personally abolish the system altogether while doing more to reduce presenteeism.

Securex, however, cautions against drawing conclusions about motive. A rise "does not by definition mean abuse," Verlinden said. "Employees may now more quickly take a day of genuine recovery, whereas before they dragged themselves to work while ill."

The effect was found to be stronger among blue-collar workers than among white-collar staff — a group that typically has less flexibility over leave, working hours and work location.

## The political stakes

Employers' organisations are seizing on the data to press for change. Voka, the Flemish employers' group, wants each employer to be free to set its own sick-note policy. "The relaxation has had a reverse effect: more people now take a day off sick than before. A good absence policy requires customisation, not an imposed uniform system," said Voka chief executive Frank Beckx.

Unizo, which represents the self-employed and small businesses, called the figures "yet another signal that the 2022 reform, despite the adjustment this year, does not work," and wants employers to decide for themselves whether to require a certificate.

The debate has already reached parliament. N-VA chamber faction leader Axel Ronse has tabled a bill to let employers again require a doctor's note from day one, a proposal the party frames as a way to detect recurring short absences and intervene early rather than to punish workers — but one [coalition partners have received coolly](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/n-va-wil-af-van-de-baaldag-baas-moet-opnieuw-ziektebriefje-kunnen-eisen-vanaf-dag-1~aaac15ad4/), with numerous advisory opinions requested and the measure pushed down the agenda.

Verwimp, the academic co-author, urged caution about using company size as the sole yardstick for future policy. "Small companies with little absenteeism can equally abolish the sick note, while some large companies prefer to keep it," he said. "The company-specific approach to illness, prevention and HR policy is certainly as important as company size."

## What to watch

The result lands amid a broader decline in overall absenteeism. Securex reported in late September that absence rates fell further in the first half of 2026, linked to federal measures on earlier follow-up of long-term sickness and faster reintegration.

That broader backdrop is likely to shape the political response. The central question is whether the 54% figure will be read as evidence that self-certified sick days invite abuse — justifying a return to the doctor's note — or as a manageable shift in behaviour that does not raise direct costs and may reduce presenteeism over time. With a contested N-VA bill on the table and employers pressing for discretion, the answer will be decided as much in the coalition as in the data.
