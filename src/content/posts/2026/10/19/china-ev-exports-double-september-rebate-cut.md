# China EV Exports Double in September Before Rebate Cut

China's electric vehicle exports doubled in both volume and value in September, as automakers rushed shipments out the door ahead of an expected cut to export tax rebates, according to customs data released Sunday and [reported](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-10-19/chinas-ev-exports-double-in-september-ahead-of-tax-rebate-cut-102494622.html) by Caixin Global. EV exports reached roughly 705,000 units worth $13.2 billion, the highest growth rate this year.

The surge is widely read as front-loading — manufacturers pushing product abroad to lock in the current 13% export VAT rebate before it falls to 9% on April 1, 2027. The Ministry of Finance and the State Taxation Administration announced the cut on October 12, covering 67 categories of new-energy vehicle goods.

## The Broader Green-Tech Picture

The EV jump was part of a wider boom. Exports of the so-called "new three" green technologies — EVs, lithium batteries and solar cells — jumped nearly 70% year-on-year to $28 billion in September, the same customs data showed.

Within the EV category, plug-in hybrids led the charge: 229,000 units worth $4.73 billion, up 132.7% in volume and 143.5% in value year-on-year. Pure electric passenger cars reached 365,000 units worth $6.24 billion, with both volume and value up roughly 90%.

The numbers landed against a backdrop of robust overall trade. Total exports climbed 36.3% year-on-year to $447.18 billion, the second-highest monthly growth of the year, while mechanical and electrical products hit a record $298.4 billion, driven partly by AI-related hardware and integrated circuits.

## Why Cut the Rebate Now?

The rebate reduction serves two intertwined goals. Domestically, Beijing is trying to curb disorderly low-price export competition, in which firms effectively pass rebate benefits to foreign buyers. The China Photovoltaic Industry Association has argued that such rebates were "in substance converted into a subsidy for overseas end markets," raising the risk of anti-subsidy and anti-dumping actions.

Externally, the timing is diplomatic. The cut came days after China-EU trade talks in Beijing on October 8-9, during which the two sides reached a WTO-compliant understanding on hybrid-vehicle trade. With Brussels' anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese EVs ongoing, trimming home rebates signals goodwill and lowers the risk of being found to be effectively subsidizing exports.

That EU understanding could prove consequential. According to [Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-10-12/eu-china-deal-could-halve-chinese-hybrid-car-exports-102492440.html), the deal could push Chinese hybrid exports to Europe more than half below the bloc's earlier forecast over the next four years. Chinese brands held just 2% of the European hybrid market in 2024 but roughly 14% in the first half of 2026 — growth that the 2024 EU tariffs, which exempted most hybrids, inadvertently encouraged.

## A Policy Unwinding

The rebate cut is part of a broader retreat from the subsidies that built China's NEV industry. Purchase tax relief has already been halved, vehicle-vessel tax preferences are set to end in 2027, and automotive power lithium batteries began facing a consumption tax in September.

The rationale is maturity: NEV penetration exceeded 60% for three consecutive months in the first half of 2026, and the industry no longer needs cradle protection. Over-subsidization, officials and analysts argue, was feeding overcapacity and destructive price wars.

## Company Exposure and What's Next

The impact will vary by firm. BYD, with the highest NEV share of exports, is most exposed; Chery's larger fuel and hybrid mix dilutes the effect; SAIC's balanced structure offers more resilience; and Geely has been the fastest-growing exporter. For many vehicles, the rebate step-down amounts to a few thousand yuan less per unit — a meaningful bite given thin margins.

The strategic answer to the coming buffer period is localization. BYD's Hungary plant is set to begin large-scale production in the fourth quarter of 2026, while Xpeng and Changan are also building European capacity.

Analysts caution that the September surge overstates underlying momentum. Front-loading borrows demand from later periods, and a pull-forward spike in early 2027 is widely expected before the April cut takes effect. China International Capital Corp. expects AI-related export growth to slow from the fourth quarter of 2026, while Guotai Haitong warns that rising US and EU trade barriers could gradually weigh on exports.

For now, the headline is unambiguous: China's clean-energy export machine is running at full tilt — and racing a deadline of its own making.
