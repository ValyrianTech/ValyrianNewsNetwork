# Belgium's Budget Talks Stall as Tuesday Deadline Nears

Belgium's federal government resumed negotiations on the 2027 budget at 3 p.m. on Saturday, with few observers still believing that Prime Minister Bart De Wever and his coalition partners can reach an agreement before a self-imposed Tuesday deadline. The "Arizona" coalition—a five-party alliance spanning Flemish nationalists to French-speaking liberals—remains deadlocked over how to find at least €10 billion in savings, and senior sources now warn the country is heading toward "total paralysis."

The stalemate carries high stakes. If no deal is reached by the end of October, Parliament will be unable to scrutinise and approve the budget before year-end, forcing Belgium to govern for a second consecutive year on *voorlopige twaalfden*—monthly emergency budgets based on the previous year's figures.

## Where the Talks Stand

Negotiations have narrowed to bilateral meetings between De Wever and each coalition party since a joint "kern" session on Wednesday evening, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-gouvernement-se-reunit-a-15-heures-pour-reprendre-ses-discussions-budgetaires-11798017). On Friday, the Prime Minister presented a revised budget table to his partners and again called on each coalition component to demonstrate a sense of responsibility.

That appeal appears to have done little to break the impasse. As [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/09/federale-regering-begrotingsonderhandelingen-deadline-stand-van/) reported, the building blocks De Wever put on the table to find €10 billion ran into the same objections that have stalled the process for weeks. The coalition parties "keep repeating their positions around the table, as if they want to win a staring contest," the broadcaster noted.

## A Coalition of Red Lines

The obstacles are structural, not personal. The plan to reform value-added tax—raising the standard rate from 21% to 22% and merging the reduced 6% and 12% rates into a single 9% rate—has become the sharpest point of contention, pitting De Wever's search for revenue against the identity of the French-speaking liberal MR party.

"We have existed for 180 years and have been against taxes for 180 years," MR chairman Georges-Louis Bouchez said. "After 180 years I will not suddenly change our identity, so no one should be surprised about that." Vice-Premier David Clarinval framed the deadlock more broadly: "It is blocked on all sides."

But the MR is not the only holdout. Vooruit, the Flemish socialists, and Les Engagés, the French-speaking centrists, argue the effort is unfairly distributed—demanding too little from large fortunes and too much from the working middle class. Vice-Premier Frank Vandenbroucke of Vooruit has made the case pointedly, warning that he could not remain part of a government that "targets all vulnerable people," according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/budget-federal-je-ne-peux-pas-faire-partie-d-un-gouvernement-qui-s-en-prend-a-toutes-les-personnes-vulnerables-declare-vandenbroucke-11795677).

Other sensitive items on the table include cuts to the healthcare budget through a lower growth norm, possible indexation restraint on wages, reform of the BIM status for healthcare benefit recipients, and an increase in the patient co-payment known as the *ticket modérateur*.

## The Clock Is Running

Two deadlines structure the standoff. The political deadline falls on Tuesday, when De Wever is expected in the Chamber of Representatives to deliver his State of the Union-style general policy statement—a speech he can technically give without a budget in hand. The formal deadline for submitting a draft budget to the European Commission arrives on 15 October, though past practice suggests some flexibility.

The harder deadline is the end of October. Miss it, and the government of De Wever would have to operate on provisional twelfths for a second year running—a development VRT NWS described as a potential "blamage" for the N-VA, a party that promised to put public finances in order.

## A Warning From the Bond Market

The urgency reflects more than political optics. Earlier this week, the National Bank of Belgium and the Debt Agency briefed ministers on the so-called *rentesneeuwbal*—the interest snowball, in which the government must borrow money to pay interest on existing debt. That dynamic, previously expected to begin around 2031, could now start as early as 2029, or even 2028 if rates keep climbing.

The warning landed alongside a fragile vote of confidence from the ratings agencies. On Friday, [Moody's maintained Belgium's credit rating at A1](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/10/moody-s-verlaagt-rating-van-belgie-niet/), having downgraded it to that level only in April. The agency projects a budget deficit of about 5.3% of GDP this year, GDP growth of just 0.7%—slowed by higher energy costs and weaker foreign demand tied to the conflict in the Middle East—and inflation of 3.5%. Standard & Poor's rates Belgium AA- and Fitch rates it A+, both with stable outlooks.

Belgium paid an average 4.35% interest on 10-year bonds at the end of September, 85 basis points higher than at the start of the year. De Wever has used such figures to press his case. "Our sense of responsibility must transcend our different political views," he told the Chamber on Thursday. "Those who today lack the courage to overcome their own taboos contribute to a higher debt, which we cannot solve without a significant loss of prosperity."

## Analysis: A Staring Contest With No Winner

What makes this standoff distinctive is the absence of drama. No party appears willing to provoke a genuine political crisis, yet none is moving toward compromise either. Some participants, according to VRT NWS, quietly hope for a cathartic crisis—raised voices, slamming doors, a minister storming out—that could jolt the talks back to life. So far, none has materialised.

De Wever is pushing for multi-year agreements running to the end of the legislature rather than a single-year fix, arguing that only a longer horizon can stabilise the debt trajectory. But the coalition's early struggle has raised doubts about its durability. "If we cannot even agree at the start of the government, how are we going to get through this legislature?" an unnamed source within Les Engagés told Redactie24.

A high-ranking source put it more bluntly: if no deal emerges before the end of the month, "this government is de facto finished."

## What to Watch

Tuesday's Chamber session will reveal whether De Wever delivers his policy statement with or without a budget settlement—and how he frames the stalemate if he must proceed empty-handed. Beyond that, the end of October is the real cliff edge. If Belgium drifts into provisional twelfths a second time, the political cost will fall hardest on the N-VA, while the economic cost will be measured in an interest bill that grows larger with every month of delay. The question now is not whether the coalition can find €10 billion, but whether it can find the political will to agree on where it comes from.
