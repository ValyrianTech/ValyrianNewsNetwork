# China Detains 3 for Abusing Table Tennis Coach

Beijing police have administratively detained three people for repeatedly insulting the head coach of China's national men's table tennis team during a major tournament, the latest escalation in the country's widening crackdown on "fan circle" abuses in sports.

The Shijingshan District Public Security Sub-bureau announced on October 10 that the three — identified as Ms. Yang (19), Ms. Li (19), and Ms. Tao (16) — vented personal emotions by directing repeated abuse at the coach as he passed through a venue corridor, publishing insulting statements online, and spreading video of the on-site confrontation, [Xinhua News reported](https://www.news.cn/legal/20261010/51ae43e12c174471bcf134081bd83b3c/c.html).

The coach was identified in coverage as Wang Hao, head coach of the Chinese men's national team and a former Olympic and world champion.

## The Incident

The confrontation occurred on October 6 during the WTT China Grand Slam, held at Shougang Park in Beijing. According to the police notice, a group of 20 people gathered and participated in the public insult of the coach.

Of those, the three detainees were found to have committed the more serious violations and were placed in administrative detention, while the other 17 received "criticism and education," a non-criminal administrative measure.

Reports cited fans shouting "Start Lin Shidong in the first singles position" and "Wang Hao, step down," reflecting discontent over the team's lineup and tactical decisions. Wang Hao reportedly took out his phone to film the fans, responded "curse again, curse again," and called security and police, [according to Zhibo8](https://m.zhibo8.com/news/web/other/2026-10-10/6ac9a366e698enative.htm).

The abuse followed a turbulent period for Chinese table tennis. At the 2026 Asian Games in Nagoya, star player Lin Shidong won men's singles, doubles, and mixed doubles gold, while team captain Wang Chuqin took silver — but China's men's team lost the team event, fueling fan anger directed at coaching decisions.

## Legal Basis and Police Warning

Police applied provisions of China's Public Security Administration Punishments Law covering the disruption of large public events and the public insult of others. Under the law, disrupting sports or cultural events can draw a warning or a fine of up to 500 yuan, while serious cases can result in detention of five to 10 days and a fine of up to 1,000 yuan.

One detainee is 16 years old. Under the revised law, 16- to 18-year-olds who commit serious first-time violations can still be subject to administrative detention.

"For illegal acts such as provoking group antagonism, insult and abuse, causing disturbances, and spreading rumors to incite conflict, public security organs will strictly investigate and handle them in accordance with the law," the police said, [Phoenix News reported](https://news.ifeng.com/c/8x6XgDj5lg5).

## From the Venue to the Internet — and Back

The detentions mark the most high-profile escalation yet in an ongoing official campaign against fandom behavior imported from the entertainment industry — coordinated online support and attack, harassment of athletes' families, and increasingly direct confrontation at events.

The China Table Tennis Association had already signalled a harder line. In a statement issued October 7, the association condemned what it called extreme fans' boundary-crossing behavior, saying abuse had moved "from the venue to the internet, and from the internet back to the venue."

It described coaches and athletes receiving abusive text messages, harassing phone calls, and hostile social media messages, and said some coaches had been met with jeers and insults from the sidelines.

The association said it had helped affected athletes and coaches report to police and announced plans to establish a venue no-entry list system to improve on-site order, [China News Service reported](https://www.chinanews.com/ty/2026/10-07/10708820.shtml).

"Let us jointly build a rational, civilized, and inclusive table tennis culture — let passion have warmth, let expression have limits, and let the arena have order," the association said.

The case echoes a broader state effort dating back to 2024, when the Ministry of Public Security published typical cases of sports-domain fandom crimes and both the table tennis and gymnastics associations issued statements vowing legal action.

## What's Next

The detention of three fans — including a minor — underscores Beijing's willingness to treat sports-related online harassment as a public-order offense rather than merely a matter of etiquette. It also tests the limits of the planned no-entry list, which the sport's governing body has yet to detail.

Wang Hao's wife, Yan Xiaoya, publicly shared video of the confrontation on social media, writing that she did not "understand how competitive sports has become like this."

For China's table tennis authorities, the challenge is twofold: restoring order in the stands while managing the intense fan passion that has made the sport one of the country's most-followed — and most combustible.
