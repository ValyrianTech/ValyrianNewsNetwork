# Treasury Yields Hit 24-Year Highs as Bond Rout Deepens

A fresh global bond sell-off pushed U.S. Treasury yields to 24-year highs on Wednesday, with the 10-year Treasury yield rising as high as 5.35% and the 30-year yield hitting 5.73% — the highest levels for each since 2002, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/markets/treasury-yields-hit-24-year-highs-global-sell-accelerates-rcna602045). The surge is rippling through the economy, driving up consumer borrowing costs for mortgages, car loans and student loans while sending stocks lower.

## A Global Sell-Off

The move was not confined to the United States. Benchmark government bonds in France and Italy also rose sharply and were on pace for their biggest one-day jump since March 2026, when the launch of the Iran war rattled global markets. The yield on the U.K. 30-year government bond hit its highest level since 1998.

The bond rout sent equities tumbling. Europe's broad Stoxx 600 index fell 1%, benchmark indexes in France and Germany were off by more than 1%, and Italy's FTSE MIB slid nearly 2.5%. U.S. stocks also dipped, with the S&P 500 down 0.6% and the Nasdaq Composite off 0.8% in early trading — a day after both closed at record highs. As stocks fell, Brent crude rose 1% to more than $102 per barrel, adding further pressure to bond yields.

The Treasury Department, meanwhile, was scheduled to sell a $39 billion tranche of 10-year notes on Wednesday afternoon, part of a heavy week of issuance. Over the Oct. 6–8 window, the government is auctioning $119 billion in debt at the highest yields since 2002, according to [24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/10/06/the-treasury-has-to-sell-119-billion-of-bonds-this-week-at-the-highest-yields-since-2002/) That total comprises $58 billion of 3-year notes, $39 billion of 10-year notes and $22 billion of 30-year bonds, the last two of which are reopenings.

Analysts are watching demand closely. A Sept. 24 seven-year Treasury sale drew a bid-to-cover ratio of 2.42 against a 2.49 average, with indirect bidders — which include foreign central banks and overseas buyers — taking 57.2% compared with a 64.6% average.

## Why Yields Are Climbing

The rise reflects a confluence of pressures: concerns over government debt levels, elevated oil prices, little visibility on an end to the Iran war, and strong U.S. economic data. The war, which began in March 2026, stoked inflation worries that spooked the bond market and helped push oil above $100 a barrel.

The Federal Reserve added to the upward pressure. On Sept. 16, the central bank [raised its benchmark rate](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/16/fed-rate-decision-september-2026.html) by 25 basis points to a target range of 3.75%–4% — its first hike in more than three years — voting 12-0 and signaling another increase could come this year. Officials see headline personal consumption expenditures inflation at 3.7% and core inflation at 3.4% for the year, and do not expect to reach their 2% target until 2029.

"We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed," Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh said. "Today, the FOMC decided that this standard has not been satisfied."

Adding to the mix, some economists warn that vast borrowing by AI-linked companies could pile further pressure on government bond yields. Apollo economic strategist Huw van Steenis noted that hyperscalers have raised $48 billion in bonds in European currencies this year — more than triple the entire amount in 2025.

## Sticker Shock for Households

The pain is landing squarely on consumers. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/07/nx-s1-5991910/interest-rates-economy-inflation-bonds), the average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage has reached 7.28%, meaning a buyer could pay about $900 more a month than they would have at the 3% pandemic-era rates for a median-priced home. A four-year loan for a used car now carries an interest rate roughly three percentage points higher than at the start of 2022.

The housing market is already feeling the squeeze: existing home sales in August were down about 1.2% from a year earlier, according to the National Association of Realtors.

"If you need to borrow, boy, it's really not a good time," said John Diamond, senior director of the Center for Tax and Budget Policy at the Baker Institute. "It's added to higher mortgage payments. Higher student loan payments. Higher prices because of inflation. That's what makes it really painful."

For prospective buyers like Carrie Goldstein, the math simply stopped working. After dreaming of moving to a walkable suburb near Cleveland, she unsubscribed from real estate updates. "It just became more and more disheartening when I did the math," she said. "It's just not in the cards."

## A Warning on Global Debt

The surge in yields is also straining government finances. Speaking in Singapore, IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva [warned](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/oct/07/imf-chief-kristalina-georgieva-urges-governments-tighten-belts-global-debt-levels-soar) that global debt-to-GDP ratios are at their highest level since World War II and on course to hit 100% in the coming years.

"Policymakers had a relatively easy ride over the last 17 years, as for all that time interest rates were stuck below GDP growth rates," Georgieva said. "Higher interest rates now put an end to that."

She called on governments to act. "My message to the world's economic policymakers will be this: we cannot keep delaying necessary policy action — you have the tools, now have the wisdom to use them," she said. She also suggested central banks might want a "prudently hawkish bias," and singled out France, where Yardeni Research president Ed Yardeni wrote that the country "may be on the verge of a full-blown debt crisis."

## What to Watch

The coming days could prove pivotal. The Treasury's 30-year bond auction on Thursday will test whether buyers return at these yields. Meanwhile, minutes from the Fed's September meeting are due during the same period and could move markets further.

At 5.73%, the 30-year Treasury now offers little cushion for investors who fear rates could climb higher still. Whether the sell-off stabilizes or accelerates will depend on oil prices, the trajectory of the Iran conflict and the market's confidence in policymakers' willingness to confront the debt that has grown so large.
