# ACA Enrollees Face 2027 Crisis as Insurers Flee Market

Millions of Americans who buy their own health coverage are heading into the 2027 Affordable Care Act open enrollment period facing a double squeeze: at least 11 insurers are pulling out of state marketplaces, and the companies that remain are proposing a median premium increase of about 15%. Open enrollment begins Sunday, Nov. 1, in most states, giving consumers roughly two months to shop for coverage.

According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-care/many-aca-coverage-face-new-crisis-2027-insurers-fleeing-market-rcna606211), the combination of departing carriers and rising rates is sowing widespread uncertainty among enrollees who rely on the individual marketplace. For many, the changes mean not only higher bills but also the loss of a plan—or a doctor—they have come to depend on.

## A Second Year of Double-Digit Increases

The financial pressure is not new. Insurers are proposing the median 15% increase for 2027, the second consecutive year of double-digit hikes, according to [KFF's analysis of rate filings](https://www.kff.org/quick-insights/aca-marketplace-insurers-are-proposing-a-median-premium-increase-of-about-15-in-2027/) from insurers across all 50 states and Washington, D.C. Last year, the median finalized premium increase reached 20%. If the proposed rates are approved, typical ACA premiums will have climbed by more than a third over just two years.

The squeeze is compounded by the shrinking number of choices. [KFF reports](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/tracking-insurer-participation-changes-in-the-aca-marketplaces-in-2027/) that 11 carriers have announced they will exit the ACA marketplaces in 2027 in at least some of the states where they currently operate, while just seven carriers plan to enter new state marketplaces.

"The main takeaway is that more insurance companies are leaving the marketplace than coming in," Cynthia Cox, director of KFF's program on the ACA, told NBC News.

## Why Insurers Are Walking Away

The departures follow the expiration of enhanced premium tax credits at the end of 2025. When those subsidies lapsed, net premiums rose sharply, and healthier enrollees either dropped coverage or migrated to cheaper but thinner plans. Those who remained tended to have greater health needs and higher medical costs, making the marketplace less profitable for insurers even as underlying health care costs continued to rise.

Cigna's decision illustrates the trend. The company will exit all 11 states where it offers ACA plans, affecting more than 350,000 enrollees. In April, CEO Brian Evanko said the marketplace is "small business for us today, and it's been shrinking in recent years," adding that the exit would allow Cigna to focus on its "core growth platforms." PacificSource, which is leaving Oregon and neighboring markets, said the decision was not "made lightly" and was necessary to keep serving members amid "growing pressures across the healthcare industry."

## Human Costs Behind the Numbers

For enrollees, the shifts translate into difficult and sometimes wrenching choices. Courtney Mroch, a 56-year-old Nashville resident with diabetes, asthma and a history of cancer, pays $1,250 a month for a silver plan—up from $850 in 2025 and more than her family's mortgage. With Cigna exiting Tennessee, she is unsure what comes next. "Who knows who will cover me in 2027 or how much more it'll cost," she said.

In Colorado, Denise O'Connor and her husband saw their monthly premiums more than double this year, from just over $900 to about $2,300, forcing them to tap retirement savings. Her husband is considering going without insurance for roughly six weeks until he qualifies for Medicare. "It's a worry," she said. "He's healthy, but anything can happen at any time."

And in Idaho, winery owner Mark Lathrop and his wife are searching for new coverage after PacificSource announced its exit. A comparable plan for 2027 would cost about $17,600, up from roughly $16,000, and Lathrop needs an annual $2,400 MRI to monitor a spinal cord injury. "We don't know what we are going to do yet," he said.

## The Erosion Without Repeal

Analysts warn that the departures and rate hikes risk a self-reinforcing cycle: as healthier people leave and premiums rise, the risk pool worsens, pushing costs up further and driving still more exits—all without any formal repeal of the law.

"This is going to create a lot of discontinuities in care and coverage for people," said Miranda Yaver, an assistant professor of health policy and management at the University of Pittsburgh. "If you have a doctor who doesn't accept the fewer number of options that are available to you, that can be very disruptive, especially if you're someone who has a chronic illness."

Yaver framed the dynamic as attrition rather than repeal. "We don't see the repeal and replace of 2017, but we're making it much harder for patients to remain on these plans," she said. Public sentiment appears to be shifting with the market: a [recent KFF tracking poll](https://www.kff.org/public-opinion/kff-health-tracking-poll-health-care-costs-expiring-aca-tax-credits-and-the-2026-midterms/) found overall favorability toward the ACA fell to 58% from 64%.

## A Political Stalemate

Congress remains deadlocked on restoring the enhanced subsidies. A House-passed three-year extension has stalled in the Senate, while a bipartisan group of lawmakers has floated a one-year extension with modifications, and a broader proposal would make the credits permanent from 2027 while introducing a public option. The dispute has become a live issue in the 2026 midterm elections, set for Nov. 3. The Trump administration has taken a different tack, sending $500 refund checks to more than 950,000 enrollees while arguing the ACA limits competition and consumer choice.

## What to Watch

The immediate advice from brokers is simple: don't auto-renew. Jennifer Chumbley Hogue, a Texas insurance broker, urged enrollees to review their options when open enrollment begins rather than rely on mailed notices, which may list outdated premiums and subsidies. "It's worth the five minutes to take a look," she said.

Longer term, the trajectory hinges on whether Washington restores the enhanced subsidies or pursues a public option. Without intervention, analysts say, the marketplace risks a deepening adverse-selection spiral—fewer, sicker enrollees; fewer insurers; higher premiums—hollowing out the law without ever repealing it. For millions of Americans, the 2027 plan year may be the moment that calculus becomes impossible to ignore.
