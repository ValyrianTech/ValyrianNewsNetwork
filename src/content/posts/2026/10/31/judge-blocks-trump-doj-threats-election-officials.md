# Judge Blocks Trump DOJ Threats to Election Officials

A federal judge late Friday issued an emergency order barring the Trump administration from threatening, intimidating, or coercing state and local election officials, just four days before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. U.S. District Judge Trina Thompson found that the Justice Department and Department of Homeland Security had "likely" violated the Voting Rights Act's prohibition on voter intimidation, according to the [eight-page restraining order](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.482447/gov.uscourts.cand.482447.26.0.pdf) issued in the Northern District of California.

The ruling landed the same week the Justice Department escalated a nationwide pressure campaign against election workers, and it aligns the courts decisively against the administration in the final, decisive days of the campaign.

## The Pressure Campaign

The order caps a weeks-long escalation in which the Justice Department pressed states for voter data, demanded that election workers segregate ballots, and warned officials of possible prosecution. On Thursday, the DOJ sent letters to election workers in all 50 states alluding to criminal consequences if they did not preserve election records, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/judge-orders-trump-administration-stop-threatening-election-officials-rcna606659).

The tactics were especially aggressive in California, where federal prosecutors sent letters to officials in 57 of 58 counties demanding cooperation in investigating alleged noncitizen voting. In one emblematic letter, First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bilal Essayli told San Bernardino County officials on Oct. 22 that they had 48 hours to identify which of 1,133 people on an attached list were citizens, to hand over nonpublic data, and to segregate the vote-by-mail ballots of unconfirmed individuals.

"The legal consequences for either facilitating or ignoring federal crimes are significant," Essayli wrote, according to case materials reviewed by reporters.

## What the Order Does

Judge Thompson's temporary restraining order, which runs through Nov. 16, nearly two weeks past Election Day, bars federal officials from demanding that election workers halt ballot processing, segregate ballots, refuse to issue ballots, or remove voters from rolls based solely on the administration's claims that those voters are ineligible. It also requires the Justice Department to contact officials who received warning letters and inform them that the demands and threats of prosecution have been enjoined.

"In the lead-up to the election, high-ranking DOJ and DHS officials have engaged in a campaign to intimidate, threaten, and coerce state and local officials and employees who are engaged in administering the ongoing midterm 2026 election," Thompson wrote.

She added that although the administration claims its actions are meant to halt unlawful voting by noncitizens, "their actions are likely to have the effect of interfering with election administration and disenfranchising lawful voters."

"Ballots have already been issued," she noted. "Americans have already begun voting."

## A Pattern of Judicial Pushback

The order is the broadest in a string of courtroom defeats for the administration in the run-up to the election. On Thursday, Judge David Carter threw out the DOJ's effort to compel California to set aside up to 20,000 ballots federal officials identified as likely noncitizens, writing bluntly: "These threats must stop."

Separately, Chief Judge James Boasberg of the federal district court in Washington ordered the administration to hand the list of roughly 20,000 purported noncitizens to an immigrant advocacy group.

## The Lawsuit Behind the Order

The case was brought by the Service Employees International Union and other labor and civil rights groups, including the NAACP, which argued the administration's tactics violated the Voting Rights Act and interfered with states' constitutional control over their own elections, according to NBC News.

## What's Next

The administration has maintained that its lists identify "likely" noncitizens through what DHS describes as a multilayered, multiagency investigative methodology. But judges have repeatedly questioned the reliability of that identification, and no evidence has been publicly substantiated that large numbers of noncitizens are voting.

With the order set to expire Nov. 16, further litigation is all but certain. For now, election officials nationwide have a court-backed shield through Election Day, ensuring that the ballots already being cast can be processed without federal pressure in the final days of the campaign.
