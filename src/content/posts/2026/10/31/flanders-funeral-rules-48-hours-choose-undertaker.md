# Flanders Approves Funeral Rules With 48 Hours to Choose

The Flemish government on Friday approved a sweeping new funeral decree that, for the first time, enshrines the rights of the bereaved in law—giving families at least 48 hours to choose their own undertaker, mandating clear pricing, and introducing fines of up to €200,000 for abuses. The reform, championed by Flemish Minister of Home Affairs Hilde Crevits (CD&V), still requires a vote in the Flemish Parliament before it takes effect.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/30/nieuw-uitvaartbeleid-menselijkheid-crevits/), the decree replaces legislation dating from 2004 and is built on three pillars: legally protected rights for the bereaved, mandatory price transparency, and enforcement through financial penalties.

## A response to scandal

The overhaul follows a September 2025 [exposé by the VRT consumer programme *WinWin*](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/09/16/uitvaartondernemers-kopen-lichamen-van-woonzorgcentra-en-zieke/), which revealed that staff at nursing homes and at least one hospital, ZAS Middelheim in Antwerp, had accepted money from undertakers in exchange for handing over the deceased—payments reported at times to reach €250 per body.

"We were really taken aback by the incidents of the past weeks and months," Crevits said at the time. "People are vulnerable when a loved one dies. It is the first time that we truly enshrine the rights of the bereaved and say: you have the absolute right to choose for yourself."

## What the decree changes

**The right to choose.** Families will have a legally protected minimum period—at least 48 hours, per VRT—to select a funeral director, shielding them from "unreasonable time pressure" and "commercial influence." Crevits said the core flaw of the 2004 decree was that the rights of the bereaved were never described, leaving them dependent on arrangements between other parties.

**Transparent pricing.** Undertakers and crematoria will be required to display their tariffs clearly on their websites and to provide detailed quotes and final invoices. "Right now it is often very opaque, and you only find out how much it will cost after you have chosen a service," Crevits said. "People must know they are not overpaying and must be able to compare the services offered."

The average funeral in Belgium costs around €6,500, according to [VRT NWS background reporting](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/04/03/strengere-regels-uitvaartsector/).

**No profit on bodies, no volume discounts.** The decree bans making a profit on the cremation of a body and prohibits volume discounts—so-called "whisper deals"—in which crematoria rewarded undertakers for delivering multiple bodies. "You do not book profit on the bodies of the deceased, so you should not give discounts either," Crevits said. "You charge the cost price."

**Enforcement and fines.** Violations can draw fines ranging from €100 to €200,000, depending on severity. Enforcement officials will carry out inspections, though the government has yet to specify which amounts attach to which breaches.

## Broader reforms in the pipeline

The decree builds on measures Crevits announced in April 2026, including a requirement that bodies be transported only in clearly identifiable hearses and stricter rules on mortuary storage and home preservation. Those moves followed the discovery of an unlicensed, illegal mortuary in Zaventem earlier this year.

## A sector that asked for clarity

The reform was also requested by parts of the funeral industry itself, which grew frustrated that a few bad actors were tarnishing the majority. Johan Dexters, chairman of Funebra, the royal federation of the Belgian funeral sector, called the overhaul "a necessary and positive step forward," stressing that "the body of a deceased person is not a product and may never be the object of commercial practices."

Crevits echoed that balance. "This decree also comes at the request of the sector, which finds it annoying that a few individuals make things much harder for them. Because the vast majority of undertakers, of course, do excellent work."

## A note on the details

Some reporting on the decree has stated that families would have a minimum of 24 hours rather than 48 to choose an undertaker. VRT NWS, whose reporter Jani Lambrechts covered the announcement in detail, states "at least 48 hours" repeatedly. The confirmed figure should be checked against the official decree text once the Flemish Parliament takes up the bill. The entry-into-force date has not yet been set.

## What to watch

The Flemish Parliament must still vote on the decree, and several specifics remain unresolved: the exact mapping of fines to violations, the final wording on the time limit for choosing an undertaker, and the date the rules take effect. For families navigating one of life's hardest moments, the stakes are concrete—and for a sector long criticised for opacity, the reform marks the most significant attempt in two decades to put dignity ahead of dealing.
