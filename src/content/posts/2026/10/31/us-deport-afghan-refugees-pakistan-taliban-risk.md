# U.S. to Deport Afghan Refugees to Pakistan, Advocates Warn

The Trump administration is preparing to deport more than 150 Afghan immigrants and asylum-seekers to Pakistan on a first-of-its-kind charter flight that could depart as early as Monday, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/trump-administration-set-deport-afghan-refugees-pakistan-rcna606059). Because Washington has no diplomatic relations with the Taliban, the removals cannot be arranged directly to Afghanistan—leaving Pakistan, which has aggressively expelled Afghans since 2023, as the expected waypoint. Advocates warn that those deported, among them relatives of Afghans who served alongside U.S. forces, could face persecution, imprisonment or death at the hands of the Taliban.

The flight, which has not been publicly announced, would rank among the largest single removals of Afghans from the United States.

## How the Plan Took Shape

The deportation preparations cap a dramatic reversal in U.S. policy toward Afghans. After the chaotic 2021 withdrawal from Kabul, the United States evacuated tens of thousands of Afghans and, in 2022, established the "Enduring Welcome" relocation program. But President Donald Trump halted Afghan and refugee admissions upon returning to office in January 2025, part of a broader immigration crackdown.

His administration has since deported thousands of people to countries that are not their own—many in Africa—in what immigration lawyers say is an effort to skirt international laws against returning asylum-seekers to the countries they fled. The legality of those so-called third-country deportation agreements is set to be debated by the Supreme Court in December.

The plan for the Afghan flight was first reported by Bloomberg and The New York Times. According to [Reuters, via The Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/international/article-913377), more than 150 Afghans are expected to be deported in what would be one of the largest single expulsions of Afghans from the U.S., with the administration already detaining dozens in Louisiana.

## A Route Through Pakistan

A U.S. official said the deportations would proceed through Pakistan because Washington lacks diplomatic relations with the Taliban and cannot arrange removals directly to Afghanistan. A senior administration official told NBC News that a U.S. charter flight would not be able to land in Pakistan without the Pakistani government's knowledge and approval, though it remains unclear what formal agreement, if any, the two countries have reached.

Pakistan's role is central to the arrangement—and to the danger advocates describe. The country has hosted millions of Afghan refugees for decades, but in 2023 it launched a sweeping crackdown, pressuring hundreds of thousands to leave and forcibly deporting others. Since then, more than two million Afghans have been deported from Pakistan back into Afghanistan. Deportees flown from the U.S. to Pakistan are therefore not expected to stay long before being pushed across the border.

Those returning arrive in an Afghanistan battered by overlapping crises: widespread malnutrition amid aid cuts, an ongoing military conflict with neighboring Pakistan that has killed civilians in airstrikes, and a dramatic rollback of women's rights under Taliban rule.

## Relatives of U.S. Allies Among Those Facing Removal

Among those facing deportation are relatives—described by AfghanEvac president Shawn VanDiver as "brothers and sons"—of Afghans who worked with the U.S. military during its 20-year war against the Taliban. VanDiver said at least 80 Afghans have recently been moved to the Central Louisiana ICE Processing Center in Jena from 34 other ICE facilities, 19 of them without legal representation. He estimated that hundreds could be held at the facility.

The group may include people with final deportation orders, people denied asylum, and people whose Temporary Protected Status was terminated by the administration. Many requested asylum after crossing into the U.S. from Mexico, and some of their cases remain pending.

VanDiver argued that the administration is well aware of the risk. "You don't have to be that smart to figure out that they're sending them to Pakistan so that Pakistan will finish the job," he said. Regardless of whether they worked with U.S. forces, he added, any Afghan flown from the U.S. would be viewed with suspicion by the Taliban.

That prospect does not appear to weigh heavily on the White House, according to the senior administration official. "The administration's position is, 'These individuals do not have status in our country, therefore we don't really care what happens to them,'" the official said. Asked whether the administration was concerned about the long-term national security consequences of abandoning Afghan allies, the official replied: "I don't think they care."

The Department of Homeland Security declined to confirm removal operations "for operational security reasons," and a State Department spokesperson said the agency had "no plans to announce." Pakistan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and its Washington embassy did not respond to requests for comment.

## A Family's Wait

Behind the policy sits a human cost that advocates say the secrecy obscures. A U.S. citizen identified only as Charlotte told NBC News that her Afghan husband, held in ICE custody since early 2025, was moved to the Louisiana facility this month and told he would be deported in early November. His asylum case has been pending for nine years.

Over 20 months of prison visits in Pennsylvania, she said, her son was never allowed to hug his father. "They never let him hug him one time," she said. "We just sat there and cried every time." Now that he has been moved more than 1,200 miles away, she said, "we can't even visit or even say goodbye."

With her husband potentially bound for Pakistan or Afghanistan—a country he has not seen since 2016—Charlotte said he has begun growing his beard to comply with the Taliban's strict rules for men. "I'm just terrified that he's not going to be safe," she said, "or he's going to be held in the jail and there's nothing I can do."

## A Test Case—and a Broader Warning

VanDiver suggested the Pakistan flight may be a test case for Camp As Sayliyah, a U.S.-run facility in Qatar where more than 1,000 Afghans slated for resettlement have been stranded since Trump took office. According to AfghanEvac, the camp housed 287 men, 319 women and 433 children as of July—most already approved for U.S. resettlement after extensive vetting, and about 150 of them relatives of U.S. service members. The administration has weighed sending those residents to the Democratic Republic of the Congo, which is experiencing the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak in history.

The State Department had said everyone would leave the camp by March 31, a deadline later extended to Sept. 30; in September, the administration delayed its closure by another nine months, the Associated Press reported.

In a statement, AfghanEvac accused the administration of planning the flight while the country is distracted by next week's midterm election, "hoping no one is watching." "The government won't say where these flights land, because it knows what happens to the people on them when they do," the group said. "A government confident in what it is doing does not hide it."

## What to Watch

Several questions remain unresolved. It is not confirmed that the flight will depart Monday, only that it "could." The precise legal authority underpinning any U.S.-Pakistan arrangement is unclear, and the Supreme Court's December review of third-country deportations could reshape the landscape. VanDiver has urged Congress to pass legislation granting Afghans in the U.S. Temporary Protected Status to shield them from removal.

For now, the episode crystallizes a broader debate: whether a state may send people to a third country knowing they will likely be expelled onward to the very danger they fled. For the families involved—and for a military that may one day ask foreign allies to trust American promises—the stakes extend well beyond a single charter flight.
