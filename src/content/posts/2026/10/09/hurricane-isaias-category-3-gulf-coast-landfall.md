# Hurricane Isaias Hits Category 3 Ahead of Gulf Landfall

Hurricane Isaias intensified into a major Category 3 storm on Friday morning and was bearing down on the northern Gulf Coast, where it is forecast to make landfall late Friday between 8 p.m. and 10 p.m. near the Alabama-Florida line, according to [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/live-updates/hurricane-isaias-updates-isaias-strengthens-category-3/?id=137128637). The storm's rapid strengthening — from a Category 2 hurricane at daybreak to a Category 3 by 8:20 a.m. ET — triggered evacuations, emergency declarations and a sweeping shutdown of offshore oil production.

The National Hurricane Center said Isaias was moving north-northeast at roughly 15 mph with maximum sustained winds near 120 mph, prompting warnings that stretched from just east of Biloxi, Mississippi, through Mobile and Pensacola to Panama City and Mexico Beach, Florida. Communities along the Florida Panhandle and coastal Alabama were told to complete preparations and be in safe spaces by midday.

"We are expecting Isaias to be a dangerous hurricane when it makes landfall," Michael Brennan, director of the National Hurricane Center in Miami, said in a warning echoed across the region, as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/09/nx-s1-5996396/hurricane-isaias-updates). "Today is the day to prepare and get ready and be in your safe place by tomorrow as conditions will rapidly deteriorate along the northern Gulf Coast as Isaias approaches."

## Evacuations and emergency declarations

Alabama Gov. Kay Ivey declared a state of emergency covering 40 counties as the storm approached, a move that also activated the state's price-gouging protections and positioned the Alabama National Guard to respond, [WSFA reported](https://www.wsfa.com/2026/10/07/state-emergency-declared-40-alabama-counties-ahead-isaias/). Florida declared emergencies in 27 counties, and several south Mississippi cities and counties followed suit. Gov. Ron DeSantis said roughly 5,000 National Guard troops were expected to mobilize by Friday.

Ivey ordered non-residents to evacuate Gulf Shores, Orange Beach and Dauphin Island, while officials on Dauphin Island — a 15-mile barrier island particularly vulnerable to storm surge — encouraged voluntary evacuations of its western section. At least four Florida school districts canceled late-week classes, and the Pensacola airport closed to commercial flights Friday morning.

Forecasters warned that storm surge could reach 5 to 7 feet in parts of Alabama and Mississippi, and up to 9 feet from the Santa Rosa/Okaloosa County line to Grayton Beach, Florida. Isaias could also dump up to 15 inches of rain on coastal areas, spawn tornadoes overnight, and cause inland flooding as far north as Tennessee and North Carolina. The National Weather Service cautioned that "long-duration power outages" were possible.

"Your time for prep is coming to an end very fast," Escambia County Public Safety Director Eric Gilmore said in a Friday morning briefing. "Make sure by this afternoon, by noon today, you're in your safe space."

## A record-late storm in a quiet season

Isaias became the first hurricane of the 2026 Atlantic season only after developing more than four months into the season — one day short of tying a 121-year-old record for the region's latest-forming hurricane, which dates to 1905. The Atlantic has been unusually quiet this year because of a strong, so-called Super El Niño that disrupts storm formation over the Atlantic while boosting activity in the Pacific, where Hurricane Simon was forecast to strengthen into a Category 4 storm.

The storm is bearing down on a stretch of coastline all too familiar with disaster. Hurricane Camille killed more than 250 people near Waveland, Mississippi, in 1969; Hurricane Katrina razed Mississippi neighborhoods, flooded New Orleans and killed nearly 1,400 people in 2005; and Hurricane Michael leveled homes near Panama City in 2018.

## The stakes for energy and the economy

The Gulf accounts for almost 15 percent of the crude oil pumped annually in the United States, and producers moved quickly to shut in operations ahead of the storm. Roughly 63 percent of regional oil production — about 1.3 million barrels a day — was temporarily halted, and 121 of the Gulf's 371 manned platforms were evacuated, according to the Marine Minerals Administration.

Jim Burkhard, global head of crude oil market research at S&P Global Energy, said the cuts were steep enough to move markets, though larger disruptions have followed other Gulf storms. The greater concern, he said, is whether Isaias strikes as a powerful storm near refineries.

Utilities along the Mississippi coast said they were prepared for extended outages. Mississippi Power mobilized more than 1,000 employees and contractors and secured nearly 500 additional resources, while C Spire activated its Severe Weather Protocol, staging generators and fuel, as [Magnolia Tribune reported](https://magnoliatribune.com/2026/10/09/isaias-churns-toward-u-s-gulf-coast-on-verge-of-being-a-category-3-hurricane/). "Our storm plan is active, our storm center is staffed and we've secured the additional resources needed to support restoration efforts across southeast Mississippi," said Fran Forehand, Mississippi Power's vice president of power delivery and division operations.

## A major test for a restructured FEMA

Beyond the immediate danger, Isaias is shaping up to be a significant test of the Federal Emergency Management Agency's ability to mount a large-scale response after the Trump administration's sweeping cuts and restructuring, experts warned.

More than 4,300 employees — about 17 percent of the agency's workforce — left FEMA in fiscal year 2025, according to a Government Accountability Office report cited by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/09/hurricane-isaias-fema-trump-cuts). From January 2025 to January 2026, 58 senior executive service employees departed, compared with an average of about 13 per year in the prior three fiscal years. The Region 4 administrator, who oversees eight states including Florida and Alabama, left the agency last year.

"It's not whether FEMA is going to respond. They're going to respond. It's more about the depth," said Chris Currie, director of homeland security and justice at the GAO. Deanne Criswell, who led FEMA under the Biden administration, said the concern is less about headcount than lost experience. "You lose the people who had been through these big disasters in the past," she said.

A federal judge ruled in September that the administration's plan to cut FEMA staff by 50 percent was unlawful. The administration has also been slow to approve major disaster funding this year, according to an analysis by the Texas Tribune, with most major declarations in 2026 arriving about a month after the initial request — roughly three times longer than average.

Ginny Goldman, founder of the disaster-survivor group Rebuild, called Isaias the "biggest test of FEMA" since the cuts, adding that it is "coming at a challenging time because there are so many other compounding factors that are making life hard for people in America." FEMA said it is deploying incident management assistance teams to Alabama and Florida, embedding personnel in state emergency operations centers, and prepositioning supplies at Maxwell Air Force Base in Montgomery.

## What to watch

Landfall is expected Friday night, with Isaias forecast to weaken quickly after moving ashore. The coming days will reveal how the region's infrastructure and emergency systems hold up — and whether a restructured FEMA can marshal the depth of response that a major hurricane demands. With a busy fall and winter disaster season still ahead, that question may not be answered by this storm alone.
