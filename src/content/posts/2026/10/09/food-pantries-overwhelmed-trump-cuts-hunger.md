# Food Pantries Overwhelmed as Trump Cuts Deepen Hunger

Food pantries across the United States are being stretched to their breaking point, caught between surging demand and shrinking federal support, according to an [NBC News survey of 48 of the largest food banks](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/overwhelmed-food-banks-heartbreaking-choices-trump-rising-hunger-rcna601639) across 35 states and Washington, D.C. Nearly three-quarters of the food banks reported more people arriving over the past year, while more than 80% said the food and funding they receive through the U.S. Department of Agriculture had declined.

The strain reflects the compounding effects of several Trump administration policies: cuts to a Covid-era farm-to-food-bank program, a pause on hundreds of millions of dollars in USDA food purchases, and stricter work requirements for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). Rising gas prices, tied in part to the Iran war, have added further pressure on both household and food bank budgets.

## A Policy Squeeze on Two Fronts

Food banks' difficulties began early in President Donald Trump's second term, when officials eliminated a $400 million Covid-era program that supplied food banks with food from local farmers and paused an estimated $500 million in planned USDA food purchases.

At the same time, the Republicans' "One Big Beautiful Bill Act," signed into law in July 2025, imposed new SNAP restrictions. Under the law, nearly all able-bodied adults not caring for young children must work, volunteer, or attend school at least 80 hours a month. The law also raised the upper age limit for work requirements from 54 to 64.

According to [MARCA](https://www.marca.com/en/lifestyle/us-news/personal-finance/2026/10/02/snap-benefits-millions-lose-food-assistance-here-s-what-to-check-before-your-aid-ends.html), roughly 5.2 million fewer people received food assistance in June 2026 than in July 2025, based on a Food Research & Action Center analysis of USDA data. The law also expanded work requirements to include parents whose youngest child is 14 through 17, removing an exemption that previously shielded those families. A further change took effect on October 1, 2026, requiring states to cover 75% of SNAP's administrative costs, up from 50%.

In West Virginia, the effects have been acute. Since last summer, about 24,000 people, including 8,000 children, have lost food stamps, according to the West Virginia Department of Human Services. The [Allegheny Advocate](https://alleghenyadvocate.com/how-snap-work-requirements-are-hurting-a-west-virginia-woman/) reported that the state's Department of Human Services had earlier estimated 36,000 residents would be affected, and chronicled the case of Helen Comer, a 62-year-old who quit her bank job to care for her infirm parents full-time and saw her benefits cut to $24 a month because she is under 65 and not working.

## On the Front Lines in Coal Country

Nowhere is the crisis more visible than in West Virginia's coal country, where an [NBC News feature](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/west-virginia-hunger-trump-cuts-food-stamps-gas-prices-iran-rcna601637) documented the toll on pantries and the people they serve.

In Dunlow, Bill Likens runs a food pantry serving 700 to 800 households. He has received 25% less food from the USDA over recent years. In August, his pantry distributed just 2.5 pounds of food per household, compared with 20 pounds monthly several years ago.

"There's a decent chance we could run out," Likens said. "I don't want them to sit in that line and not get anything. But I don't have a lot of extra food these days."

He described the situation as the worst he has seen in more than two decades. "This is the leanest we've been in the 23 years I've been doing this," he said.

Facing Hunger Foodbank, which supplies Likens' pantry and others across Appalachia, has seen a 25% increase in people seeking help over the past year, while the amount of food the federal government supplies has fallen by a third, according to CEO Cyndi Kirkhart.

"It's like the pandemic on steroids without funds," Kirkhart said. "I don't believe the government needs to provide everything, but if you're going to ask us to, please help."

## A Widening Circle of Need

The crisis is not confined to Appalachia. In Maine, Good Shepherd Food Bank reported that the USDA cut the amount of food it received from 13 million pounds to 9 million pounds in a single year. In Michigan, Forgotten Harvest is handing out less food to stretch supply, distributing one bag of potatoes instead of two and two tubs of yogurt rather than six.

Poonam Gupta, a senior research associate at the Urban Institute, said households are absorbing repeated financial shocks. "People are facing multiple hits to their budgets," she said. "It is really difficult to find a way to continue affording a healthy and nutritious and balanced diet when you just have these constant hits."

In Matewan, about an hour from Dunlow, Donna Paterino runs the Widow's Mite Food Pantry. It now serves more than 200 households a month, twice as many as a year ago, while the quantity and quality of USDA food has declined. The deliveries are often less filling and harder for seniors to cook with, she said.

Paterino said the profile of those she serves has shifted. "My focus used to be just the seniors and grandparents raising grandchildren," she said. "Now, I have just as many middle-class families who reach out and say: 'Do you have anything left that I can use? Do you have any juice I can give the kids until Monday?'"

In Mingo County, where she operates, 1 in 3 children live in poverty, and the population has declined 9% since 2020. One in 7 working-age adults receives Social Security disability benefits, a figure driven up by coal mine injuries such as crushed spines and black lung disease.

## Disappointment in a Deep Red State

The frustration is keen in a state Trump won in 2024 with 70% of the vote. Several residents interviewed by NBC News said the president they supported has not delivered the relief they expected.

Bonnie Newsome, a retired school bus driver living on a fixed income, has been hit by rising electric, grocery, and gas costs. With winter approaching, she worries about affording propane. "I really thought President Trump would move right on in and take care of everything," she said. "He's disappointed me, he has."

Joanne Girvin, 73, said she has lost 20 pounds over the past year because she has been eating less. "Sometimes I just sit down and cry when I'm trying to figure out what you're going to eat out of a can or something because you don't have no meat," she said. "It's just really bad, this economy. It's horrible."

The White House disputes that its policies are harming vulnerable Americans. Spokesperson Anna Kelly said Trump is "uplifting every American's financial situation" through tax cuts and efforts to increase jobs and business investments, and is "strengthening SNAP for the Americans who need it by ensuring these programs are sustainable for future generations," including measures to "crack down on waste, fraud and abuse."

The USDA likewise maintains it has not reduced the amount of food it provides, noting it invests $340 million daily in low-income communities. A spokesperson said the department has prioritized increased purchases of fruits, vegetables, seafood, and other proteins. Pantry operators dispute that characterization, citing their own reduced supplies.

## Community Networks Fill the Gap

As federal support has contracted, local institutions have stepped in. In West Virginia, food pantries have taken on an expanded role. During an earlier round of SNAP suspensions amid a government shutdown, [WCHS reported](https://wchstv.com/news/local/wv-food-pantries-rise-to-meet-demand-amid-snap-suspension-vow-no-one-will-be-turned-away) that pantries across the state pledged not to turn anyone away.

"We're not going to let a little government shutdown and SNAP benefits stop us from feeding people," said John Roberts, executive director of Mountain Mission. "We have not turned anybody away. We don't intend to turn anybody away."

The interconnection between SNAP and food banks is often overlooked. Bill Fortune of the Nitro Community Food Pantry noted that food banks benefit from SNAP dollars too. "If you don't get SNAP benefits, we don't get the money through the organizations that get our food," he said.

In Dunlow, Likens has built mutual-aid networks of his own. Weeks after the September distribution, he and his volunteers arranged a final outreach for a family that arrived late from a doctor's appointment. He went back inside to round up what he could.

"We make sure everyone gets something," he said.

## What to Watch

The coming months will test whether the current patchwork of charities can absorb a crisis that federal programs were designed to handle. The October 1 rule shifting more administrative costs to states could strain state budgets already stretched thin, and the full effects of the expanded work requirements are still working their way through the system.

For families in West Virginia and beyond, the choices are immediate: pay for gas, pay for heat, or pay for food. As Paterino put it, "We stick together here." But as demand outpaces supply across the country, the question of how long that solidarity alone can hold is becoming harder to answer.
