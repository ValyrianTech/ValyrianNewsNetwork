# China Marks 70 Years of Space Ambition

China commemorated the 70th anniversary of its space program on October 8, with state media framing the milestone as vindication of General Secretary Xi Jinping's leadership and a springboard toward becoming a leading space power. The anniversary falls in the opening year of the 15th Five-Year Plan, which for the first time lists "space power" among 16 national strength-building goals.

In a feature published by [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/leaders/20261008/0e13f43277e9476bb21d645602595b65/c.html) and republished by [People's Daily](http://politics.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1008/c461001-40810906.html), Xi is quoted as saying: "Exploring the vast universe, developing the aerospace industry, and building a space power is the space dream we tirelessly pursue." The article traces the program from its 1956 founding to its current deep-space ambitions.

## Context: From Isolation to Orbit

China's space industry formally began on October 8, 1956, with the establishment of the Fifth Research Institute of the Ministry of National Defense, led by Qian Xuesen, who returned from the United States the previous year. At the time, the country faced severe industrial weakness and external technology blockades.

According to [CCTV News](https://www.thecover.cn/news/jT7Y6LU7vTqH90qSdq8Jkw==), the program's self-reliance was forged by necessity. In 1960, Soviet experts withdrew and took their documentation with them. "That was a huge blow to us," recalled Qi Faren, 93, the first chief designer of the Shenzhou spacecraft. "Our generation understood clearly: to develop and become a major power, we could only rely on ourselves. That is our space spirit — self-reliance and hard work."

That ethos produced a series of firsts. The Dongfanghong-1 satellite reached orbit in 1970, carrying Chinese-made components and no foreign parts. In 2003, Shenzhou-5 carried Yang Liwei into space, making China the third country to independently launch humans into orbit. In 2021, Tianwen-1 achieved Mars orbiting, landing, and roving in a single mission, and in 2024, Chang'e-6 returned the world's first samples from the lunar far side.

## Key Developments and Scale

The anniversary coverage highlighted the program's growing scale. According to Xinhua, China's crewed space engineering program has completed 40 flight missions, with the longest single spacewalk reaching nine hours. The BeiDou-3 navigation system achieved 100 percent domestic content in core components, overcoming more than 160 key technologies. The Long March rocket family has logged 670 launches with a success rate above 97 percent.

The economic footprint is expanding as well. BeiDou's industrial output exceeds 600 billion yuan, the high-resolution Earth observation system serves more than 20 sectors, and satellite broadcasting reaches 155 million households. More than 4,000 space station research results have been applied across industries.

A further marker came in July 2026, when the Long March 10B rocket performed China's first controllable recovery of a first stage and the world's first sea-based net-capture recovery of a launch vehicle, launched from the Hainan Commercial Space Launch Site. Long Lehao, a launch vehicle expert with the Chinese Academy of Engineering, said the Long March family is now "capable of launching payloads to any Earth orbit."

China has also deepened international cooperation. It has signed agreements with 17 countries and international organizations for the International Lunar Research Station and more than 200 space cooperation pacts with over 50 countries and organizations. In September 2026, China donated Chang'e-6 lunar samples to the United Nations for permanent display in Vienna.

"I hope every visitor who pauses before the lunar soil will feel the awe and marvel at what humanity can achieve individually and united," said Aarti Holla-Maini, director of the UN Office for Outer Space Affairs.

Human spaceflight is broadening geographically. In May 2026, Lai Ka-ying became the first female astronaut from Hong Kong to fly, aboard Shenzhou-23 to the Tiangong space station. Two Pakistani astronauts are training in China, with one expected to join a Chinese crew. Yang Liwei noted the station has grown from Shenzhou-5's six cubic meters to more than 120.

## Analysis: A National Priority, With Caveats

The anniversary framing reflects how central space has become to Beijing's claim of technological self-sufficiency. The elevation of "space power" to the 15th Five-Year Plan's list of 16 national goals is, in effect, institutional signaling — a commitment embedded across ministries, state-owned enterprises, and research institutes.

That signaling is visible in the machinery of government. The Communist Party's 20th Central Committee's Fourth Plenary Session explicitly proposed "accelerating the building of a space power," and the State Administration for Science, Technology and Industry for National Defense has established a Commercial Space Department, a nod to the country's fast-growing private launch sector.

The coverage, however, is inseparable from its source. Xinhua, People's Daily, and CCTV are official state media whose framing centers Xi's guidance. Independent verification of specific figures is difficult, and the anniversary narrative emphasizes continuity and achievement over the program's setbacks and costs. Beijing's space diplomacy also serves strategic ends: the International Lunar Research Station is widely viewed as a counterpart to the US-led Artemis Accords, and export of complete satellites and ground systems along Belt and Road routes extends influence.

## What's Next

China's near-term pipeline is dense. Chang'e-7 is slated to travel to the lunar south pole in search of water ice, the crewed lunar landing program aims to put a Chinese astronaut on the Moon before 2030, and Tianwen-3 is in prototype development with a Mars sample-return goal of around 2028 launch and 2031 return. Development of a heavy-lift rocket is accelerating, while in-orbit servicing, space computing, and space manufacturing are positioned as emerging growth areas.

Whether Chinese industry can sustain the cadence — and whether the political emphasis translates into durable commercial and scientific leadership — remains the central question as the program enters its eighth decade.
