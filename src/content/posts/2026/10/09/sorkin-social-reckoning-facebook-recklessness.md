# Sorkin Warns of Facebook Recklessness in New Film

Aaron Sorkin is returning to the story that made him an Oscar winner—and, this time, he is sounding an alarm. The writer-director's new film, *The Social Reckoning*, opens in theaters on Oct. 9, 2026, as a companion piece to his 2010 drama *The Social Network*. Drawn from the 2021 "Facebook Files" whistleblower saga, the film argues that Facebook has placed growth ahead of the safety of its users, and Sorkin has used its press tour to call for new regulation of social media platforms.

Speaking to NPR's *Morning Edition*, Sorkin framed the company's priorities bluntly. "There's supposed to be some kind of tension between growth and safety. He's abandoned the safety part, and it's just about growth," he said, referring to the fictionalized Facebook co-founder Mark Zuckerberg at the center of the film. Meta declined to comment to NPR on the movie, noting that it "remains a work of biographical fiction," as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/08/nx-s1-5591394/the-social-reckoning-film-aaron-sorkin).

## A Companion Piece, Not a Sequel

Sony Pictures, which distributes the film through Columbia Pictures, describes *The Social Reckoning* as a companion piece rather than a sequel. Where *The Social Network* dramatized Facebook's founding in a Harvard dorm room, the new film focuses on the fallout years—specifically the leak of internal documents that became known as the Facebook Files.

That material was gathered by then-Wall Street Journal reporter Jeff Horwitz, whose investigative series exposed how the platform handled hate speech, violence and misinformation. Horwitz also wrote the book *Broken Code*, which helped inspire the film. Whistleblower Frances Haugen, who took the unusual step of going public in 2021, is portrayed by Mikey Madison; Jeremy Allen White plays Horwitz. Following the outcry, Facebook's parent company rebranded itself as Meta in October 2021.

The cast represents a deliberate break from the original. Jeremy Strong takes over the role of Zuckerberg from Jesse Eisenberg, who played the part to wide acclaim in 2010. Sorkin said he reached out to Eisenberg first. "It turns out Jesse Eisenberg no longer wants to be conflated with Mark Zuckerberg. He doesn't like the guy," Sorkin told NPR. He added that Strong, whom he called "the best American actor under 50 years old," had asked to be considered for the part during the 2025 Academy Awards.

## The Algorithm at the Center of the Story

Much of the film's argument rests on how Facebook retuned its news feed. According to the research dramatized in the movie, Facebook changed its algorithm in 2018 so that resharing or replying to a post was weighted up to 30 times as much as an ordinary "like," while comments counted 15 times as much and an emoji response five times as much.

Sorkin argues those changes amounted to a deliberate amplification of outrage. "Facebook did this knowing that the thing you're most likely to re-share is anything that makes you angry—rage bait," he said. "So in addition to the enormous amount of misinformation they are pumping into the air supply, they are also purposely giving you content that they hope will make you angry."

The director tested the platform himself, creating a dummy account while preparing the film. After the feed surfaced a picture of a dachshund, he kept clicking on similar content and watched the algorithm adapt—a harmless illustration of the same mechanism he considers dangerous at scale. "But if, like 43% of adults, you use Facebook as your source of news, you're in deep trouble," he said. Pew Research pegs the share of U.S. adults who get news from Facebook closer to 38%, so the precise figure he cites should be treated with some caution.

## A Push for Regulation

The film arrives amid continuing debate over how to oversee large technology platforms, and its stars have joined the call for outside oversight. Jeremy Allen White told the Press Association that social media companies need more safeguards. "For air travel, there's third-party regulators. For pharmaceuticals, there's third-party regulators," he said, arguing that tech firms should face the same scrutiny.

Sorkin has been candid that he does not believe the industry can simply be unwound. "I don't think we can put the toothpaste back in the tube. But what we can do is pass laws," he said. In a separate interview, he suggested a line can be drawn from rage-driven amplification to the Jan. 6 Capitol attack—though, by his own design, the film does not name former President Donald Trump.

## Mixed Reviews and a Modest Opening

Early reception has been mixed. Variety's Guy Lodge wrote that the procedural "already feels behind the curve," even if it is "diverting enough, clever enough and true enough," while The Hollywood Reporter's David Rooney called it a "sharp whistleblower thriller." Box-office projections point to a $7 million to $8 million opening weekend in North America—well short of the roughly $22 million debut of *The Social Network* in 2010, but modest enough relative to the film's reported $30 million budget that it is unlikely to be a financial disaster.

## What to Watch Next

The coming weeks will test whether the film finds an audience beyond the tech-policy crowd. Its opening weekend returns, word of mouth and any awards-season momentum will determine whether *The Social Reckoning* becomes a cultural touchstone like its predecessor—or a well-intentioned footnote. What seems certain is that the debate it dramatizes, over who is responsible when an algorithm rewards anger, is far from settled.
