# Mike Ditka, Legendary Chicago Bears Coach, Dies at 86

Mike Ditka, the Hall of Fame tight end and Super Bowl-winning coach whose fierce competitiveness and outsized personality made him the enduring face of the Chicago Bears, died Friday, October 9, 2026, his family announced. He was 86.

The family released a statement through Jarrett Payton, son of Bears great Walter Payton, and Chicago's 104.3 The Score. "It is with profound sadness that we announce the passing of our beloved husband, father, grandfather, and friend, Mike Ditka," the statement read. "While the world knew Mike as a Hall of Fame football player, legendary coach, and leader of the Chicago Bears' Super Bowl XX championship team, we knew him as the heart of our family. Mike will be missed beyond measure and remembered forever."

Born Michael Dyczko on October 18, 1939, in Carnegie, Pennsylvania, he was later known as Michael Keller Ditka. The oldest of four children, he was the son of a welder of Polish and Ukrainian descent who anglicized the family surname to make it easier to pronounce. Raised in the steel town of Aliquippa, Ditka became one of the most recognizable figures in American sports. He was one of only two men ever to win Super Bowls as a player, an assistant coach, and a head coach, per the [Pro Football Hall of Fame](https://www.profootballhof.com/news/pro-football-hall-of-famer-mike-ditka-86-dies).

## A Tight End Who Changed the Position

Ditka starred at the University of Pittsburgh, where he was a consensus All-American as a senior and briefly considered a career in dentistry. The Bears selected him with the No. 5 overall pick in the 1961 NFL Draft, and under the guidance of founder-coach George Halas, he redefined the tight end position.

"Tight ends when I came into the game were kind of an extension of the offensive tackle. They blocked most of the time," Ditka recalled. Halas, he said, "had the vision of what a tight end could do if he could get down the field."

Ditka caught 58 passes for 1,076 yards and 12 touchdowns as a rookie — the first 1,000-yard receiving season by a tight end in NFL history — and was named Rookie of the Year. That record stood for 63 years, until Brock Bowers surpassed it in 2024. Ditka won an NFL championship with the Bears in 1963 before being traded to Philadelphia, and he later revived his career with the Dallas Cowboys, winning Super Bowl VI in 1972.

"He blocked like a madman," Hall of Fame running back Gale Sayers told NFL Films. "I liked that in him, because I knew that I could depend on Mike."

## 'Iron Mike' and the 1985 Bears

Ditka's greatest triumph came as a coach. After eight seasons as an assistant under Tom Landry in Dallas, he was hired by Halas in 1982 to lead the Bears. Upon arriving, he bluntly told his players that those who endured the coming years would be rewarded with a Super Bowl ring.

The 1985 Bears delivered on that promise in spectacular fashion. The team went 15-1 in the regular season, shut out two playoff opponents, and crushed the New England Patriots 46-10 in Super Bowl XX on January 26, 1986. Their defense is still widely considered one of the most dominant units in NFL history.

"It was more than a team. It was a team of characters that had character that played for a crazy man, who let him be crazy and had fun doing it," Ditka said of that squad. "I was what I was and they were what they were and, damn, it was fun."

Ditka's fiery temperament became part of his legend — calm one moment and hurling a clipboard the next. Hall of Fame linebacker Mike Singletary recalled Ditka's first meeting with the team, when he promised a championship within three years. "In '85, we were there and it was because of that vision," Singletary said.

Ditka was named NFL Coach of the Year in 1985 and again in 1988, the same year he was enshrined in the Pro Football Hall of Fame as the first tight end ever inducted. He also suffered a heart attack during the 1988 season and returned to the sideline within days.

## Beyond the Sidelines

Ditka coached the Bears through 1992, then spent three seasons (1997-1999) with the New Orleans Saints, a tenure remembered for his decision to trade the team's entire 1999 draft class — plus a 2000 first-round pick — to select running back Ricky Williams. His overall record across 14 NFL seasons was 121-95.

After coaching, Ditka became a beloved broadcaster on NBC, CBS, and ESPN's "NFL Countdown." He founded the Gridiron Greats Assistance Fund to support struggling retired players and was an outspoken advocate for improved health and pension benefits. He also built a career as a restaurateur, pitchman, and occasional actor.

His cultural reach extended well beyond football. His mustache, trademark sweater vest, and sideline intensity inspired the "Bill Swerski's Superfans" sketches on "Saturday Night Live," which cemented his status as the ultimate embodiment of Chicago's blue-collar identity. NBC News noted that the sketches featured fans singing his praises "to the point of absurdity" in thick Chicago accents.

Jim Porter, the Hall of Fame's president and CEO, described Ditka as a figure who "brought an unrivaled level of toughness to an era already defined by physicality" and whose "captivating personality and leadership qualities will be preserved and celebrated forever in Canton."

## A Complicated Legacy

Ditka was also a man of contradictions. In a 2015 HBO interview, he acknowledged the widespread use of pain pills during his era and said that, given what is now known about the health risks of the sport, he would not let an 8-year-old son play football — even as he said he owed his life to the game. He later suffered a mild stroke in 2012 and coped with the long-term toll of a brutal playing career.

He is survived by his wife of nearly five decades, Diana; his first wife, Margery Dougherty; and their four children, Mark, Matt, Mike, and Megan.

His own words perhaps best captured his approach to life and the game he dominated. "Success isn't permanent and failure isn't fatal," he once said. "Living in the past is for cowards. If you live in the past, you die in the past."

For the city of Chicago and the league he helped shape, Ditka's influence is unlikely to fade. What remains to be seen is how a new generation of players and fans — many of whom know him only through highlight reels and his larger-than-life reputation — will carry forward the standard he set, both for the tight end position he revolutionized and for the demanding, uncompromising style of leadership he embodied.
