# Community Colleges Train Workers for Data Center Jobs

Community colleges are emerging as a primary training ground for the data center industry, as institutions rush to supply workers for a construction boom that shows little sign of slowing. In northeast Louisiana, Louisiana Delta Community College (LDCC) has built two short-term programs to prepare local residents for jobs at Meta's Hyperion data center in rural Richland Parish, roughly 30 minutes away — a facility the company now describes as its largest in the world.

Since January, more than 100 students have graduated from LDCC's new four-week Data Center Technician and Construction Craft Laborer courses, according to [WWNO](https://www.wwno.org/education/2026-09-04/this-community-college-is-training-louisiana-residents-for-jobs-at-metas-data-center). Dan McCarty, the college's director of trade programs, said all of the graduates are working.

## A Labor Market Transformed

The programs mark a sharp turn for a region long defined by out-migration. "It was typical for me to see friends of mine that would have to leave the state in order to find any kind of meaningful economic means," McCarty said. "Now it's 30 minutes away."

Meta broke ground on the Richland Parish campus in December 2024 with an initial $10 billion commitment. In July 2026, the company [announced](https://datacenters.atmeta.com/2026/07/deepening-our-investment-in-richland-parish-louisiana/) a further $40 billion expansion, bringing total investment to $50 billion and delivering a planned 5 gigawatts of computing capacity for AI training. Meta estimates about 7,500 workers will be needed at peak construction, with roughly 1,000 permanent jobs — including hundreds of data technicians — projected once the site is fully online by 2036.

In response, LDCC developed courses tailored to the specific skills Meta and its contractors required. "When a business comes to us and says, 'We need people to work here,' we will turn around and say, 'Well, what skills do they need?'" McCarty said. Both programs cost $3,698, though scholarships from the college and the state, along with corporate sponsorships, cover many students' costs. Entry-level fiber technicians can earn between $18 and $24 an hour.

Meta has pledged $5 million to LDCC — the largest gift in the college's history — creating scholarships for any graduate of a Richland Parish high school to take data center-related courses. The company also launched its own free [America's Workforce Academy](https://www.meta.com/actions/americas-workforce-academy/) this year, with a planned Baton Rouge site that provides housing, transportation and a daily stipend.

## National Pattern, Local Questions

The shift mirrors a broader national pattern in which community colleges train workers for jobs their regions need most. But it also unfolds against rising public skepticism about data centers themselves. A [Gallup poll](https://news.gallup.com/poll/709772/americans-oppose-data-centers-area.aspx) earlier in 2026 found that 7 in 10 Americans do not want data centers built near their homes, citing environmental impact, quality of life and higher utility bills. Supporters most often point to job opportunities as the rationale.

That tension is visible in Louisiana, where the Hyperion project has drawn scrutiny on several fronts. Construction traffic produced a 600% spike in crashes near the site, according to a Gulf States Newsroom investigation. Dozens of elected officials signed nondisclosure agreements that critics say cloaked major developments from public view. And [Entergy](https://www.wwno.org/local-regional-news/2026-04-14/entergy-wants-to-fast-track-21b-meta-deal-as-psc-commissioner-advocates-sound-alarm) sought to fast-track a $21.37 billion power deal to serve the campus, prompting alarm from a state utility commissioner.

The Gulf States Newsroom also launched a [monitoring project](https://www.wwno.org/public-health/2026-04-13/were-monitoring-the-air-and-water-around-metas-data-center-in-louisiana-heres-why) to track air and water quality around the facility. In August 2026, the Louisiana Public Service Commission [declined](https://www.wwno.org/politics/2026-08-12/louisiana-psc-kills-ruling-that-would-force-meta-to-turn-over-key-data-center-information) to force Meta to turn over additional information about the project's energy footprint.

## Opportunities at Home

For LDCC students, the debate is secondary to the paycheck. Shanae Williams, a 35-year-old certified nursing assistant and mother of four, earns $16.25 an hour at a nursing home after more than a decade on the night shift. She hopes that installing fiber will start her at $20 an hour or more. "I'm taking a risk right now," she said. "I'm just getting to the point where I want to try something else."

Marcus Jackson, 20, is finishing an associate's degree in information technology and expected to leave Louisiana for work. Now, he said, the data center "makes me kind of wanna stay at home, knowing that I'm going to be set."

Fletch Batchelor, the fiber instructor and a former Mississippi River boat captain, sees the change in generational terms. "Our area's never gonna be the same, y'all," he told his students. "Our community is about to change forever. We're gonna have a lot of people moving back."

He also dismissed fears that automation will erase the jobs he teaches, arguing that maintenance and repair will always require human hands. "Somebody's gotta understand the inner workings to maintain it," he said — adding that upkeep is "where you really make your money."

## What to Watch

McCarty acknowledged the controversy surrounding the project but argued the region should make the most of its new reality. "We're not gonna go tear it down," he said. "So, as long as it's here, let's make the best of it."

Whether the model holds depends on forces beyond Louisiana. Data centers have become a [political issue crossing party lines](https://www.wwno.org/npr-news/2026-08-08/data-centers-are-a-political-issue-crossing-party-lines-driving-voters-to-candidates) nationally, and the durability of the AI construction boom — along with the power costs and water demands that accompany it — will shape whether programs like LDCC's become enduring economic engines or a fleeting bet on a single industry.
