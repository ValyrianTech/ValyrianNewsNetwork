# Trump Names Jay Clayton as New White House AI Czar

President Donald Trump has named Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton as the White House's new artificial intelligence czar, placing a top national security official at the center of the federal government's AI strategy. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/04/nx-s1-5990781/jay-clayton-ai-czar-trump), Trump announced the move in a Truth Social post on Sunday, saying Clayton will lead a newly created task force called the "Super Intelligence Force" (SIF).

The appointment fills a vacancy left open since March, when venture capitalist David Sacks stepped down as the administration's AI and crypto czar, and signals that the White House now treats advanced AI as a national security priority rather than solely a commercial or technological matter.

## A Task Force With 120 Days to Report

Trump said the SIF is "tasked with coordinating the effort of the Federal Government to ensure that America continues to lead the World in Super Intelligence, which many say is bigger than the Industrial Revolution, and the Internet, and will protect the interests, and improve the lives, of all Americans."

Beyond Clayton, the force's leadership includes Federal Trade Commission Chairman Andrew Ferguson, Under Secretary of War for Research and Engineering (and Chief Technology Officer) Emil Michael, and Office of Personnel Management Director Scott Kupor. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/04/trump-unveils-his-new-super-intelligence-force/) reported that the three serve as vice chairs, citing the Wall Street Journal, which first reported Clayton's new role on Saturday. The task force will report to Trump and his chief of staff, Susie Wiles, per [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/03/trump-jay-clayton-ai-czar.html).

Media outlets vary in how they render Michael's title, reflecting a possible departmental renaming: NPR and MS Now describe him as "Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering," while TechCrunch and CNBC use "Under Secretary of War for Research and Engineering," with CNBC also giving him the Chief Technology Officer title. The force will have 120 days to research and report on the risks and opportunities posed by advanced AI and to recommend the federal government's responsibilities regarding the technology, according to reporting cited by [Anadolu Agency](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/americas/profile-jay-clayton-trump-s-intelligence-chief-becomes-ai-czar-/4078168). Its charter reportedly calls for it to "develop plans for responding to SI-enabled threats to our society, while preventing overregulation and regulatory capture that would stifle innovation and competition."

The charter also directs the group to examine existing laws and possible congressional action, consult the technology industry, and review how AI-related incidents -- including breaches, hacks and model jailbreaks -- are reported to the government. The full charter has not been publicly released.

## An Intelligence Chief for an Intelligence-Era Technology

Clayton's dual role is unusual. He was sworn in as Director of National Intelligence on August 3, 2026, and leads the United States' 18 intelligence agencies. Before that, he served as chairman of the Securities and Exchange Commission from 2017 to 2020 and as U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York.

Trump has framed the appointment around strategic competition with China. At a White House gathering with technology executives on September 29, he declared, "Whoever wins super intelligence wins," and rejected closer cooperation with Beijing on AI governance, arguing it could allow China to "gain access to things that we know that they don't know."

Clayton has echoed that framing. During his Senate confirmation hearing, he called AI "a gamechanger" but also "a threat," telling lawmakers, "When something's both an opportunity and a threat, you better get your arms around it." He later described super intelligence as a "national security issue" and told CNBC in September that he opposed calls for a "pause" in development, noting that other countries continue to advance AI. "I don't think any American should think that that's a good strategy," he said.

According to TechCrunch, Clayton told the Wall Street Journal that one of the biggest risks is "not being first."

## A Rebrand, and a Retreat From Regulation

Trump has made the renaming of AI a signature of his approach. He has issued an executive order directing agencies to use "super intelligence" (SI) rather than "artificial intelligence" in official communications, a term the president says better reflects the technology's evolving capabilities.

As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/04/trump-jay-clayton-white-house-ai-czar) reported, the announcement drew a swift reaction from Elon Musk, who said on his platform X that "SI" was "better" than "AI" and announced he was renaming his aerospace company's AI unit from SpaceXAI to SpaceXSI.

Yet the appointment comes amid escalating warnings from within the industry. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/03/openai-safety-leader-quits-warning-ai-companys-culture-is-broken) reported that David Robinson, a safety leader at OpenAI who led the writing of safety reports accompanying the ChatGPT developer's product releases, resigned with the warning that the company's culture is "broken." Writing in The Atlantic, Robinson said: "I agree with other recently departed staff that the companies building this technology aren't being nearly careful enough."

Geoffrey Irving, who formerly worked at OpenAI and as chief scientist at the UK government's AI Safety Institute, wrote in Time that there is "about a 50% chance we all die because of the development of smarter-than-human AI systems." Critics of such extinction warnings caution that they are unscientific because they cannot be verified or falsified.

The warnings follow a series of incidents in 2026 involving autonomous AI agents, including a swarm of OpenAI agents that attacked the startup Hugging Face. In late September, Trump and six executives signed a largely symbolic voluntary safety accord at the White House promising internal and external monitors; the principles are not codified in law.

Current federal AI regulation remains limited. Congress has not passed broad legislation governing the use or development of AI, though some states, including California, have enacted measures -- among them transparency requirements.

## Analysis: A Symbolic Posture, or a Real Shift?

The appointment carries more symbolic weight than immediate regulatory consequence. Placing an intelligence chief atop a task force with a 120-day review window suggests the administration wants to frame AI as a security file -- one that sits above the slow machinery of legislation -- rather than as a consumer-protection or civil-rights matter. It also keeps the rollout of any binding rules at arm's length, since the SIF is charged with recommending roles and responsibilities, not writing rules.

Senator Mark Warner of Virginia, the Democratic vice-chair of the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, cautiously welcomed Clayton's appointment while sharpening the critique. As The Guardian reported, Warner said "the administration has resisted meaningful safeguards without offering a serious alternative for addressing the national security risks posed by increasingly powerful AI systems." He added that he looked forward to working with Clayton on keeping the U.S. ahead of China and establishing "rigorous testing and safeguards so that increasingly powerful systems remain secure, reliable, and under human control."

The dual-hat arrangement raises its own questions. Carl Tobias, a University of Richmond law professor, told [MS Now](https://www.ms.now/news/trump-ai-czar-artificial-intelligence-director-jay-clayton-technology-openai-anthropic) that it remains to be seen "whether he can manage the two major responsibilities, and also be up to speed on AI. It seems to me this new assignment might be even more daunting."

## What's Next

The Super Intelligence Force now has 120 days to deliver its report, likely landing in early 2027. Its recommendations could later be extended or transferred to a standing agency, according to Anadolu. Whether that report translates into enforceable rules -- or remains a strategic statement of intent -- will define the practical legacy of Trump's AI push.

For Clayton, the test is twofold: managing two of the government's most demanding portfolios at once, and navigating an industry whose own leaders increasingly warn of the dangers of the technology they are building. Trump, meanwhile, has shown little appetite for slowing development, keeping the emphasis on speed and on the contest with China. The coming months will reveal whether that posture survives contact with the governance questions the task force was created to answer.
