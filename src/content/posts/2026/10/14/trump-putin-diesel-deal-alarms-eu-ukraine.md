# Trump-Putin Diesel Deal Alarms EU, Ukraine

A bilateral diesel agreement between US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin has rattled Europe and Ukraine, with analysts describing it as a potential stab in the back for Kyiv and its Western allies. Announced on 9 October 2026 after a roughly 90-minute phone call, the deal would see Russia ship large volumes of diesel to the United States and global markets, facilitated by a US Treasury exemption that suspends sanctions on Russian-origin diesel until April 2027.

Trump framed the arrangement as a pre-election measure to bring down fuel costs. "Diesel prices for Americans and, in reality, for the world, WILL FALL RAPIDLY BY RECORD PROPORTIONS," he wrote on Truth Social, adding that it was "my biggest priority." The announcement lands less than a month before US midterm elections on 3 November, with US average diesel at $6.28 a gallon on 8 October — roughly 70 percent higher than a year earlier.

## The Mechanics of the Deal

Under the terms announced by Trump, Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel to the US and world market, 500,000 more tonnes in November, and 1,000,000 tonnes shortly after, with possibly 3,000,000 additional tonnes depending on the state of Russia's refineries. If all commitments are met, the total would exceed 4.8 million tonnes.

To enable the shipments, the US Treasury published General License No. 135 on the same day, a sectoral exemption authorizing transactions on Russian-origin diesel — not a broad lifting of sanctions. According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/trump-negocie-avec-poutine-des-livraisons-massives-de-diesel-russe-11797925), Moscow moved quickly to reciprocate. On 11 October, Russia authorized the export of 500,000 tonnes of diesel with immediate effect, instructing its oil companies to conclude supply contracts with foreign partners, as [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/diesel-la-russie-leve-partiellement-son-interdiction-d-exporter-apres-un-accord-avec-trump-11798327). The first cargo left the port of St. Petersburg on 12 October, bound for the US East Coast, per the Russian program Vesti.

The deal reverses, at least partially, Russia's own restrictions: in early July 2026, facing fuel shortages caused by Ukrainian strikes on its refineries and depots, Moscow had imposed a total ban on diesel exports, repeatedly extended since.

## Europe's Sharp Recoil

The European Union reacted with unusual bluntness. "Suspending sanctions on Russian diesel gives Moscow more revenue to wage war," EU High Representative for Foreign Affairs Kaja Kallas wrote on social media on 10 October. "It is not the time to ease pressure on Russia, and Europe will not do so." She added that Russian strikes had killed more than 80 Ukrainian civilians in two days and that Moscow has intensified hybrid attacks against Europe. The European Commission, in response to the Washington-Moscow arrangement, called for a ceasefire protecting infrastructure in both Ukraine and Russia, warning that "Europe knows too well the hidden costs of excessive dependence on Russian hydrocarbons." Both responses were carried by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/guerre-en-ukraine-pour-l-ue-lever-les-sanctions-sur-le-diesel-aide-l-effort-de-guerre-russe-11798142).

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky was equally scathing, calling the decision "unworthy." On X, he wrote that "giving gifts to Putin will not bring peace nor any benefit to the civilised world," and that Russia would "repay" the diesel with "more terror and perfidy."

## A Strike That Overshadowed the Deal

The agreement was announced just as news broke of a deadly Russian strike on the Ukrainian city of Zaporizhzhia. According to [Tunisie Numérique](https://www.tunisienumerique.com/ukraine-zaporijjia-17-morts-trump-poutine-diesel-russe-sanctions/), five guided glide bombs hit residential buildings in the early hours of 10 October, killing at least 17 people, including three children, and damaging 26 residential buildings. A day of mourning was declared for the following day.

Zelensky drew a direct link, saying: "Today Russia expressed its thanks for the lifting of sanctions on diesel exports by dropping five glide bombs on residential buildings in Zaporizhzhia." Yet the same outlet cautioned that no proven causal link exists between the strike and the deal, noting that Russian bombardments had already reached highly lethal levels before Friday's announcement.

The announcement coincided with US-Ukraine talks in Miami, where envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Ukrainian officials for roughly eight hours. The Financial Times reported the meeting turned short, and no ceasefire was announced. Witkoff described the discussions as "productive."

## Splits in Washington

Opposition to the deal has emerged even within Trump's own party. Twelve US senators — six Democrats and six Republicans — sent a letter urging its immediate cancellation. As reported by [Zamin](https://zamin.uz/fr/monde/224469-12-senateurs-exigent-de-trump-lannulation-de-laccord-sur-le-diesel-russe.html), the letter, led by Senator Richard Blumenthal, argues that the agreement "is a gift to our adversary and an open betrayal of our allies. It must be cancelled immediately." Signatories include Republicans Mitch McConnell, Susan Collins, and Lisa Murkowski. Copies went to Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. Republican Representative Don Bacon separately argued that Washington should tighten sanctions rather than offer new revenue to the Kremlin.

Trump, for his part, escalated his criticism of Zelensky on 10 October, telling reporters: "I suggest they get a new leader who can reach a deal," and warning Kyiv to stop strikes on Russian refineries, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/guerre-en-ukraine-furieux-contre-zelensky-trump-veut-un-nouveau-president-en-ukraine-11798233).

## Limited Strategic Impact?

With a roughly 90-minute phone call behind the announcement and no ceasefire on the table, the practical effect may be smaller than the political shockwave suggests. Russia's export ban remains linked to the state of its refineries, still under Ukrainian attack, and implementation of the announced volumes remains a promise rather than a delivery.

Analysts interviewed by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/accord-sur-le-diesel-entre-trump-et-poutine-un-coup-de-poignard-dans-le-dos-de-l-ue-et-de-l-ukraine-11799796) were careful to temper expectations. Federico Santopinto, research director at the French think tank IRIS, described the move as "a new hard blow for Europeans, but without doubt not a decisive one," arguing that "this initiative is not capable of truly changing the balance of the Russia-Ukraine conflict." He also stressed the split between Trump's declarations and the positions of US military and diplomatic institutions, which are "a bit more reliable," noting that NATO's top commander, General Grynkewich, warned from Warsaw on 12 October that any attack on an alliance member would be "totally futile."

## Europe's Structural Vulnerabilities

The episode has laid bare Europe's dependence on American capabilities even as Washington's commitments waver. According to Santopinto, the US continues to sell arms to Europeans, provides essential intelligence — including targeting and ISR systems — and Starlink remains a key Ukrainian communications tool. Without European support, he said, "Ukraine would probably have caved, because it did not have the financial and structural means to face the conflict."

Looking ahead, Santopinto warned that major elections in 2027 — in France, Poland, Italy, and Spain — could empower populist, Russia-friendly parties and shift EU policy on Ukraine. "We will have to see if populist parties, which have a much more conciliatory policy with Russia, won't have more influence and be able to sway the policies of the European Union," he said.

The EU, meanwhile, has just adopted a new sanctions package against Russia, which Santopinto called "indispensable" while acknowledging its limits: "Russia is capable of living with European sanctions."

## What's Next

The diesel agreement's durability depends on quantities actually delivered, prices, and beneficiaries — and on whether Russia's refinery capacity can sustain the promised flows. What Washington has obtained on the diplomatic front so far is less clear: no ceasefire, no halt to Russian bombardment, and no concrete advance toward peace have been announced. As European capitals weigh their next steps and Kyiv presses for firmer Western resolve, the story will be defined less by the volume of diesel at sea than by the trust it has eroded on land.
