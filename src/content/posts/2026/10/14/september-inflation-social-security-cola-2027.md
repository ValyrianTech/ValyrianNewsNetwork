# September Inflation Hot; Social Security COLA Set at 3.6%

U.S. consumer prices rose sharply in September, keeping inflation well above the Federal Reserve's 2% target and triggering an automatic 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) for Social Security benefits in 2027. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Wednesday that the headline Consumer Price Index (CPI) climbed 0.6% for the month and 3.7% over the past 12 months, while the Social Security Administration (SSA) [announced](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/press/releases/2026-10-14.html) the benefit increase within hours of the inflation release.

The twin developments underscore how persistently elevated prices are reshaping household finances — eroding workers' buying power while delivering a modest cushion to retirees.

## Energy Drives the Headline

Surging energy costs again powered the increase. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/14/nx-s1-6000593/inflation-consumer-prices-cpi-social-security-benefits), the energy index jumped 5.2% for the month, with gasoline soaring 9.1% and accounting for about half of the gain in headline CPI. Over the past year, gas prices are up 34.2% while fuel oil has climbed 74.2%.

"It's not looking good," economist Brian Bethune of Boston College told NPR. "The main problem, of course, that everybody knows is gasoline prices. But also heating oil."

The energy shock traces to the U.S. war with Iran launched in late February, which has constrained Middle East oil supply and pushed diesel prices above $6 a gallon for the first time in late September. Because diesel powers trucking, rail, shipping, and manufacturing, those costs feed into consumer goods over time.

Even excluding volatile food and energy, so-called core inflation ran at 2.6% annually and 0.3% for the month — both above Dow Jones consensus estimates of 2.5% and 0.2%. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/14/cpi-inflation-report-september-2026.html) noted that price pressures were broad-based, with gains in shelter (+0.4%), used cars and trucks (+0.7%), and apparel (+0.3% monthly, +3.4% annually), the latter sensitive to tariffs. Food prices were flat for the month.

"CPI came in hot and it wasn't just fuel prices, as core inflation ran at a 3.7% annualized pace in September and 3.2% over three years," said Sonu Varghese, chief macro strategist at Carson Group. "The only surprise is that investors keep getting surprised by broad-based inflation. And we've yet to see the full impact of higher diesel prices and the AI boom."

## A Raise for Retirees

Hours after the CPI release, SSA Commissioner Frank J. Bisignano announced the 3.6% COLA, describing it as support for "76 million Americans, giving them greater financial stability."

The adjustment means the average Social Security retirement benefit will rise by about $75 per month. Roughly 72 million Social Security beneficiaries will see higher payments beginning in January 2027, while about 7.3 million Supplemental Security Income recipients will receive increases starting December 31, 2026.

The 3.6% increase is the largest since 2023's 8.7% boost — the highest in four decades — and tops the 2.8% COLA for 2026. The COLA is calculated by statute using the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W), which rose 3.9% over the past 12 months.

Advocates for seniors welcomed the increase but argued it falls short. "It helps millions of Americans keep up with rising costs for groceries, routine bills, healthcare and other everyday expenses," AARP CEO Myechia Minter-Jordan said. But Max Richtman, president of the National Committee to Preserve Social Security and Medicare, was blunter: "It won't be enough for most seniors to even tread water financially."

[CNBC's personal finance coverage](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/14/social-security-cola-2027.html) highlighted that about 40% of beneficiaries aged 65 and over rely on Social Security for at least half of their income, and an AARP survey found 76% of respondents said a 3.5% COLA would not be enough. Advocates have long pushed to switch the COLA formula to the CPI-E, which they say better reflects seniors' healthcare costs.

## Wages Lag Behind Prices

For workers, the picture is bleaker. Average wages rose just 3% in September, below the 3.7% headline inflation rate, meaning the typical worker's real buying power eroded over the past year.

"People are kind of saying, 'Something isn't right with that,'" Bethune said. "I'm working harder. I'm more productive. And this is not a square deal."

## The Fed's Next Move

Policymakers at the Federal Reserve will weigh the new data when they meet October 27–28. The Fed raised its benchmark rate in September — its first hike in more than three years — to a range of 3.75%–4%, and futures traders now price roughly a 54% chance of another increase this month, with better than 96% odds of at least one hike by year-end.

Beth Hammack, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, warned that repeated supply shocks risk entrenching expectations of higher prices. "This could be a sign that an inflationary mindset is taking hold," she said. "When people expect higher prices, they make decisions with this in mind. It can become a self-fulfilling prophecy that makes it harder for monetary policy to bring inflation back down."

Fed officials follow the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index as their primary gauge; September PCE data is due later this month, along with the September Producer Price Index on Thursday.

## What to Watch

The coming weeks will test whether inflation cools or hardens. The Fed's October decision is the immediate focus, but the broader question is whether the energy shock from the Iran war fades or feeds into a durable wage-price spiral. For retirees, COLA notices will be available online in late November — beneficiaries who want paperless notices must enroll by November 18 — and the size of the annual adjustment is already emerging as an election-season issue, with advocates warning that Social Security's future, including how COLAs are calculated, is on the ballot in November.
