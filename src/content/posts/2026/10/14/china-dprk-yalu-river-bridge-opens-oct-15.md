# China-DPRK Yalu River Bridge to Open Oct 15

A new bridge connecting China and North Korea across the Yalu River will officially open on October 15, both governments announced, ending more than a decade of delays that turned the structure into a symbol of stalled bilateral ties. According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261014/d1c1cd4886284838a547167975bda93d/c.html), the two sides agreed by mutual consent to open the China-DPRK Yalu River Boundary River Highway Bridge — commonly known as the New Yalu River Bridge — and will jointly hold an opening ceremony that day.

The bridge spans the Yalu River between Dandong in China's Liaoning Province and Sinuiju in North Korea's North Pyongan Province, linking the two countries at the DPRK's most important land trade hub. Its opening is expected to help ease a long-standing bottleneck in cross-border freight and signals that improving China-DPRK relations are translating into concrete economic cooperation.

## A Decade-Long Delay

The project is far older than its opening date suggests. Plans for the new crossing date to a 2009 agreement reached during talks between then-Chinese Premier Wen Jiabao and senior DPRK officials, as [DW](https://www.dw.com/zh/搁置十年的新鸭绿江大桥即将投入使用/a-79491970) reported. Construction began in late 2010 and was mostly funded by China; the main bridge body was largely finished by 2014. The opening, however, never came.

The delay was driven by a confluence of factors: a deterioration in China-DPRK relations, DPRK nuclear and missile tests, international sanctions, and the COVID-19 border closure. The most basic problem was on the ground — the DPRK side did not build connecting approach roads, customs, or immigration facilities for years.

The new bridge is a four-lane, cable-stayed highway span. Its body runs more than 3,000 meters, and it sits roughly 12 kilometers downstream of the older China-DPRK Friendship Bridge, near the Yellow Sea estuary. The older crossing, built in 1943, carries both road and rail traffic over single lanes and tracks, with freight vehicles passing in alternating one-way time slots — a structure that sharply limits how much cargo can move.

## A Modern Crossing for Growing Trade

China accounts for 98.2% of the DPRK's publicly reported foreign trade, according to the Korea Trade-Investment Promotion Agency's "2025 DPRK Foreign Trade Trends" report cited by [AFPBB News](https://www.afpbb.com/articles/-/3651661), and more than 70% of that traffic uses the aging Friendship Bridge. Once the new span opens, road freight is expected to shift onto it, freeing the old bridge to focus on rail transport.

Recent months brought steady signs of preparation. China-side customs registered roughly 300 cargo trucks that had previously used the Friendship Bridge, and truck test runs took place in late September. Satellite imagery taken on August 30 and analyzed by the U.S.-based North Korea monitoring project 38 North showed significant progress at the trade and immigration facilities at the Sinuiju entrance — roads within the facility were paved, landscaping was underway, and large warehouses had roofs. July imagery, by contrast, showed many customs yards and access roads still unpaved.

A recent official visit added momentum. After the June 2026 China-DPRK summit, during which President Xi Jinping signaled a policy of fully opening border trade points and expanding trade and construction cooperation, preparations accelerated, AFPBB reported.

## Pyongyang's Balancing Act

Analysts caution that the bridge's opening reflects a careful strategy rather than a wholesale opening by Pyongyang. North Korea has been deepening ties with Russia — supplying arms and troops for the war in Ukraine since 2023 — while also bringing the Yalu crossing online. On September 7, a separate DPRK-Russia road bridge over the Tumen River opened, creating the first road link between Rason and Russia's Khasan region, where previously only rail connected them.

"North Korea left this bridge connecting to China idle for over ten years, but in the pace of pushing forward the other bridge, North Korea is more eager than Russia," Han Ki-bum, a former deputy director of South Korea's National Intelligence Service, told DW. He said Pyongyang appears to be deliberately pacing its relations with both China and Russia to avoid repeating what he called "the mistake of over-dependence on Beijing."

Ahn Chan-il, a former North Korean soldier who heads the World Institute for North Korea Studies in Seoul, told DW that the bridge is "effectively the first modern bridge between North Korea and China." He suggested Pyongyang could use its deepening ties with China and Russia, combined with a willingness by the United States to engage with Kim Jong-un, to gain diplomatic leverage and take a firmer line toward South Korea.

An anonymous former senior South Korean Unification Ministry official offered a more transactional reading. "The fact that it is about to open now means North Korea has completed its customs, clearance facilities and logistics warehouses," the official told DW. "From this angle, North Korea's need is more urgent. It now needs to bring goods in from China."

## Economic Hopes and Cautions

The reopening of the crossing is expected to benefit China's northeastern provinces — Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang — as well as the DPRK, which has historically imported construction materials, industrial machinery, and foodstuffs while exporting seafood and agricultural products. Han predicted the new route would accelerate the recovery of China-DPRK trade toward pre-pandemic levels, telling DW that it "will further boost DPRK-China trade."

Even so, analysts temper expectations. Pyongyang's trade system has grown increasingly centralized, with the state tightening control over imports and restricting the autonomy of individual factories and enterprises. Foreign-currency shortages — reflected in sharply rising unofficial U.S. dollar exchange rates inside the DPRK — could also constrain how much trade flows once the bridge opens.

## What to Watch

The official opening date confirms a timeline that had remained uncertain for months. South Korean media and DW had earlier reported expectations of an October 6 opening, timed to the 77th anniversary of China-DPRK diplomatic relations, while the South Korean government had anticipated the bridge would open by the end of 2025. Neither came to pass.

Ahead of the October 15 ceremony, key questions remain: the precise scope of the opening event and which officials will attend from each side, and whether the bridge will move freight at the volumes its four lanes allow. Also unclear is how the region's recent flooding may affect early operations, though satellite imagery after the waters receded showed continued paving work and vehicle movement.

For now, the bridge's long-delayed opening stands as the clearest sign yet that ties between Beijing and Pyongyang are moving from symbolism toward substance — with both sides watching how far that opening will go.
