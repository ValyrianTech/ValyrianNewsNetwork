# Limburg Drug Trial Collapses as All 31 Acquitted

A sprawling drugs trial in the Belgian province of Limburg ended on 23 October 2026 with every one of its 31 defendants acquitted — not because the charges were disproven, but because the proceedings took so long that the court ruled they could no longer be justified. The Criminal Court of Hasselt declared a lapse of criminal prosecution, a rare and drastic sanction for a breach of the right to a trial within a reasonable time, and the Federal Prosecutor's Office has announced it will appeal.

The case, one of the largest drug prosecutions in the region's history, revolved around the Lommel soap factory Polysan, where criminal networks obtained the chemical precursors needed to manufacture synthetic drugs. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/23/verval-strafvordering-drugsproces/), the federal prosecutor had sought more than 160 years of prison time in total and €14 million in confiscations. None of it will now be imposed.

## A Case That Ran Too Long

The dossier opened in 2016 with a report of suspicious chemical orders. The first judicial intervention followed in January 2018, and the federal judicial police completed their active investigation that same year. From there, the file stalled.

"The active investigation of the federal judicial police was already completed in 2018. The court then identifies multiple periods of inexplicable standstill, together accounting for nearly five years," the court said in its press release. The court found no explanation for the delay in the complexity of the case or in the conduct of the defendants themselves.

In total, almost nine years elapsed between the first judicial intervention and the verdict. The charges were serious: unlawful trade in drug precursors, production of and trade in MDMA, amphetamine and cocaine, narcotics transport, participation in and leadership of criminal organizations, and weapons offenses. At the September 2026 trial, the federal prosecutor had already applied a discount for the overrun — seeking 18 years rather than the maximum 20 for the alleged ringleader, the Dutch drug criminal John H., nicknamed "De Onderwijzer" (The Teacher).

## The Legal Ground: Lapse, Not Limitation

The ruling rests on a specific and relatively new legal mechanism. As the court explained, the case represents a "serious breach of the reasonable term," triggering what is known in Belgian law as *verval van de strafvordering* — the lapse of prosecution.

"That means no convictions follow," the court stated. "Not because the facts would be insufficiently proven. The merits were not judged. The court rules that continuing the procedure can no longer be justified in any respect."

Court spokesperson Luc De Cleir framed the decision as a matter of principle: "The court found that the reasonable term was exceeded. That is a fundamental principle. It is a serious infringement of the rights of defense and a fair trial."

The court was explicit that the verdict carries no judgment on the gravity of the charges — although that gravity was weighed in the balance. "It is a ruling on what a state under the rule of law owes defendants: treatment within a reasonable term and the real possibility to defend themselves," the court said.

The legal basis is Article 27 of the Preliminary Title of the Code of Criminal Procedure, introduced by the law of 9 April 2024. As [legal analysis published by Jubel](https://www.jubel.be/het-verval-van-de-strafvordering-als-sanctie-voor-een-overschrijding-van-de-redelijke-termijn/) explains, that reform shifted Belgium from a "completion limitation" regime to an "adjudication limitation" regime, and created the lapse sanction as an explicit remedy for a "very serious breach" of the reasonable term. The preparatory works specifically cite a case that has lain dormant for years without justification as a typical example — precisely the scenario the Hasselt court identified.

## Evidence the Court Could Not Search

The excessive duration was not the only problem the court confronted. Roughly 2.7 terabytes of tapped telephone conversations, spread across hundreds of folders, were "practically inaccessible."

"The computers available to the court and the defense are not powerful enough to search the data efficiently within an acceptable timeframe," the court's press release stated. The detail points to a technical capacity deficit as well as a scheduling one — a system unable to process the volume of evidence its own investigations generate.

Defense lawyers welcomed the outcome. "Legally speaking this is the most correct decision," said Manon Gutwirth. "One could even question how a good defense was still possible, because it concerns facts from 10 years ago." Asked how the case had drifted, she pointed to the file itself: "The only thing we can establish is that there are large periods of standstill. That means nothing happened during those periods. We suspect the file was left lying somewhere on a desk gathering dust."

## A Test of a Young Legal Remedy

The acquittal is striking for its scale. The court also weighed the personal circumstances of the defendants: nine are now over 65, some face serious health problems, many appear to have resumed their lives without new convictions, and nearly all had already served time in pretrial detention. The public prosecution service pointed to a shortage of people and resources at the Justice Department, but the court held that this was not a valid argument in this case.

The decision is an early and severe application of the April 2024 lapse sanction. Because the mechanism is young, the appeal will be closely watched. The Federal Prosecutor's Office is challenging the ruling, and an appellate court will have to decide whether the delay truly rose to the level of a "very serious" breach — or whether a lesser sanction, such as a reduced sentence, would have sufficed.

## What to Watch

The appeal, expected before the Antwerp Court of Appeal, is the immediate next step. Beyond this single case, the ruling raises broader questions. If upheld, it could encourage defendants in other long-running, document-heavy prosecutions to seek the lapse of their own cases, intensifying pressure on a justice system already strained by backlog and resources. It also spotlights the technical shortcomings — the millions of files a court cannot efficiently search — that can quietly undermine even the most ambitious investigations.

For now, the message from Hasselt is unambiguous: in a state under the rule of law, the clock is part of the case. When it runs too long without explanation, even a prosecution seeking 160 years can run out of time.
