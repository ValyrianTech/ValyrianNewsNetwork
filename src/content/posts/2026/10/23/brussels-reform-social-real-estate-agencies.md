# Brussels Approves Overhaul of Social Real Estate Agencies

The Brussels regional government on Friday approved the broad outlines of a reform of its Social Real Estate Agencies (Agences Immobilières Sociales, or AIS), the bodies that lease private housing to low-income tenants at controlled rents. Announced by Secretary of State for Housing Karine Lalieux of the Socialist Party (PS), the reform is expected to take effect in early 2028 and introduces a new monitoring framework for the sector, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-gouvernement-bruxellois-approuve-la-reforme-des-agences-immobilieres-sociales-11804793).

## A Sector Under Pressure

Brussels' 22 AIS agencies act as intermediaries between private property owners and households on modest incomes. Together they manage more than 8,100 homes owned by private landlords, which they rent out at prices below the open market — a mechanism designed to make part of the capital's private rental stock accessible to lower-income residents.

That model came under strain in 2025, when two agencies — Saint-Josse and Anderlecht-Cureghem — collapsed into bankruptcy. The failures exposed structural weaknesses in how the agencies are monitored. "The bankruptcies of the AIS of Saint-Josse and Anderlecht-Cureghem in 2025 demonstrated the shortcomings of the current model, which does not allow difficulties to be detected in time," Lalieux said.

The Saint-Josse case proved especially damaging. An audit later found that, over more than two decades, employees had allocated social housing to themselves and their close associates and falsified the agency's accounts. The agency, founded in 2000, managed some 230 private homes and was declared bankrupt in late 2025 under more than half a million euros of debt, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/un-audit-accuse-l-agence-immobiliere-sociale-de-saint-josse-d-avoir-favorise-des-proches-et-amis-11737723). A judicial investigation is ongoing, and the Brussels Region has joined the case as a civil party.

## The Eight Pillars

The reform rests on eight pillars, which officials describe as designed to detect struggling agencies earlier, support their recovery, and use sanctions only as a last resort. The measures include:

- A **financial barometer** — an annual assessment of each agency's financial health
- A **graduated surveillance procedure** to flag and escalate concerns in stages
- A **common governance framework** for all agencies
- **Ex-post control** of compliance with housing allocation rules
- **Strengthened oversight** of housing quality
- A **single registration list** shared across all participating agencies
- A **progressive reorganization** of the sector, setting a minimum number of housing units per agency
- A **growth roadmap** for the housing stock

Under the plan, each AIS would be evaluated annually on its financial health. Where problems emerge, the agency would be guided step by step, with a recovery plan required before any withdrawal of accreditation. Oversight of governance, housing allocation, and housing quality would all be tightened.

## Why It Matters

The stakes extend well beyond the agencies themselves. Brussels faces a severe social housing shortage: tens of thousands of households sit on waiting lists, while private-market rents have climbed sharply, widening the gap the AIS were created to bridge. When an agency fails, the consequences fall hardest on tenants and on the private owners who entrusted their properties to it.

The reform's emphasis on early detection and graduated support is a direct response to the Saint-Josse collapse, which critics say went unnoticed for years despite warning signs. By pairing a financial early-warning mechanism with stricter allocation and governance checks, the government is attempting to close the gaps that allowed a failing agency to operate until it went bankrupt.

At the same time, the push for a minimum size per agency and a sector-wide reorganization reflects a longer-running effort to rationalize a model that expanded rapidly in the 2010s, partly driven by private developers delivering large new housing projects. Consolidating smaller, fragile agencies into more robust structures — while preserving the affordable-rent function — is the reform's central balancing act.

## What's Next

With the orientations now approved, the government must translate the eight pillars into formal legislation and detailed operating rules ahead of the planned early-2028 start. Key questions remain: how the minimum-size threshold will be applied to smaller agencies without disrupting service, how the financial barometer's criteria will be set, and whether consolidation can proceed quickly enough to prevent further failures.

For the thousands of tenants and owners who depend on the AIS network, the reform signals a shift from a model that reacted to collapse toward one intended to anticipate it.
