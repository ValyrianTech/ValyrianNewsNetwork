# Meta Lets Zuckerberg Stay in California to Blunt Wealth Tax

Meta has struck a deal allowing CEO Mark Zuckerberg to remain based in California amid the state's proposed billionaire wealth tax, the company confirmed — a maneuver that creates a new class of non-voting stock Zuckerberg could sell to cover a looming tax bill while keeping ironclad control of the company he founded.

Zuckerberg had told Meta's board he was considering moving his primary residence outside California in response to Proposition 40, a ballot initiative that would impose a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of billionaires who were California residents as of Jan. 1, 2026. According to [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/business-leaders/meta-agrees-let-mark-zuckerberg-remain-california-amid-proposed-billionaire-wealth-tax), the arrangement commits Zuckerberg to remain a California resident — or a resident of whichever state hosts Meta's headquarters — for the next eight years.

## The Mechanics of the Deal

At the center of the agreement is a financial instrument engineered to solve a specific problem: how to let a billionaire pay a wealth tax on concentrated stock holdings without ceding voting power.

Meta said it plans to issue a new class of non-voting Class C common stock in January 2027, funded through a two-for-one stock split for all existing shareholders. The company framed the move as a routine measure to raise capital, compensate employees and fund acquisitions — all while preserving the voting power of existing Class A and B shareholders.

"We created this new class of shares with the purpose of creating a publicly traded non-voting stock usable for capital raising, employee compensation, acquisitions, other general corporate purposes while preserving the voting power of existing Class A and B shareholders," Meta Chief Financial Officer Susan Li said during the company's third-quarter earnings call, according to Fox Business.

But the timing and structure carry a second, personal benefit. Zuckerberg can immediately sell 20% of the Class C shares he receives — representing roughly 10% of his overall economic stake — potentially generating enough liquidity to pay the tax without surrendering control. Zuckerberg, the world's sixth-richest person with a net worth approaching $250 billion according to Forbes, would otherwise likely have had to sell voting shares to settle a bill that could run into the billions.

The decision followed a unanimous recommendation from a special committee of independent directors, which concluded that the new stock class would both finance Meta's growth and preserve Zuckerberg's leadership. Company filings emphasized the importance of Zuckerberg remaining in California to maintain close coordination with management and proximity to Meta's Superintelligence Labs.

## What Proposition 40 Would Do

Proposition 40 would impose a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of billionaires who were California residents on Jan. 1, 2026, if approved by voters in November. The tax would be due in 2027, though payments could be spread over five years at an additional cost. Real estate, pensions and retirement accounts generally would be excluded from the calculation.

California's nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office has offered a mixed assessment. It estimates that "some billionaires" may decide to leave the state in response, potentially trimming the income tax revenue they generate, according to the outlet. Yet the office also projects the wealth tax could generate tens of billions of dollars over several years — far outweighing the estimated revenue loss of less than $1 billion annually from behavioral changes.

## A Dividing Line Among Democrats

The measure has split California's political establishment. The California Democratic Party has endorsed Proposition 40, but some prominent Democrats — including Gov. Gavin Newsom — have opposed it. The intra-party tension has spilled into public view, with actor Mark Ruffalo sharply criticizing Newsom's stance as "disqualifying" ahead of the vote.

Critics argue the tax would accelerate an exodus of the state's wealthiest residents, draining tax revenue and entrepreneurial energy. Economist Art Laffer, a former adviser to President Ronald Reagan, warned that the measure targets exactly the people who are least sensitive to ordinary income taxes.

"No one would want to go there to build their wealth only to be faced with this type of tax," Laffer told Fox Business. "It would cause an exodus of billionaires, which have not left California because the income taxes do not affect the billionaires much at all. This tax would really affect them."

California entrepreneur Eric Schiffer, chairman of the family office Patriarch, offered a similarly bleak forecast. "I think the impact of this passing in California is a giant sucking sound of all of these entrepreneurs being sucked out of California because they're just not going to want to stay," he said.

## Why the Deal Matters

The Meta arrangement is notable not simply because of its size, but because it aligns a corporate governance change with a single individual's tax exposure. The two-for-one split and the new Class C shares are, on their face, standard capital-markets tools. Yet the deal's residency commitment and the share-selling provision together function as a bespoke hedge against Proposition 40.

The structure also signals how tech billionaires may respond if wealth taxes spread. Rather than leave California, Zuckerberg — and by extension Meta — found a way to stay, while insulating his control of the company from the financial hit. The arrangement was reported in detail by outlets including [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/22/meta-mark-zuckerberg-california-wealth-tax) and [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/22/mark-zuckerbergs-creative-plan-to-pay-californias-proposed-billionaire-tax-without-losing-control-of-meta/), which described it as a creative way to "pay California's proposed billionaire tax without losing control of Meta."

## What to Watch

Voters will decide Proposition 40's fate in November. If it passes, the first tax payments would fall due in 2027 — the same month Meta plans to issue the new Class C shares, setting up a closely watched test of how the wealthiest Americans adapt to direct taxes on net worth.

Also worth watching: whether other California-based billionaires pursue similar corporate workarounds, and whether courts or regulators scrutinize a stock structure whose stated purpose and practical effect are so neatly intertwined.

For now, Zuckerberg is staying — and Meta has engineered a way to keep him.
