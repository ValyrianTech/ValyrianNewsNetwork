# CD&V Warns Middle Class Cannot Bear Budget Pain Alone

As Belgium's five-party coalition enters a critical final stretch of federal budget negotiations, a senior CD&V lawmaker has warned that the country's hard-working middle class cannot be the only group made to shoulder the pain of roughly €10 billion in savings and new revenue.

Nawal Farih, the CD&V faction leader in the Chamber, told VRT NWS's morning program "De Ochtend" that any deal must be distributed fairly. "The hard-working middle class cannot be the only group bearing the consequences," she said. "When 10 billion has to be found, there will be measures that hurt everyone."

The intervention, [reported by VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/nawal-farih-cdv-hardwerkende-middenklasse-kan-niet-enige-gr~1790599460224/), comes as the governing "Arizona coalition" struggles to close a budget gap that has repeatedly pushed back its own deadlines.

## A Coalition Under Pressure

Prime Minister Bart De Wever's government — an unusual five-party alliance of Flemish nationalists (N-VA), French-speaking liberals (MR), the centrist Les Engagés, Flemish Christian democrats (CD&V), and Flemish socialists (Vooruit) — has been negotiating for weeks over how to find the money. The talks stalled most recently over Vooruit's objection that the proposals on the table were neither just nor credible enough, with Vice Premier Frank Vandenbroucke publicly lowering expectations before a key meeting.

CD&V chairman Sammy Mahdi reacted sharply to that move, calling it "mind-boggling" that a partner would say the talks would fail before even entering the room. Farih, for her part, struck a more conciliatory note on Friday, insisting the ingredients for a balanced package are present — but that the partners must "step over their own shadow."

## The Fiscal Stakes

The pressure to reach a deal is not abstract. Belgium's government debt stood at 110.2% of GDP — €719.07 billion — at the end of the second quarter, the fourth-highest in the eurozone behind Greece, Italy, and France. The budget deficit has climbed to 5.3% of GDP, and Belgium now borrows on international markets at higher rates than Greece, a politically potent symbol of the country's fiscal fragility.

De Wever has repeatedly invoked the scrutiny of financial markets, warning lawmakers this week that "you don't play with matches on a powder keg" and describing the budget problem as "an infected wound that must be disinfected as quickly as possible." Vice Premier Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés put it bluntly: "Belgium is meanwhile borrowing at higher interest rates than Greece."

## A New Deadline — and a Fallback Risk

Political journalist Bart Verhulst reported that the coalition's new target is November 3, when De Wever could deliver a policy statement to parliament. If that timeline holds, the full budget accord could be approved before Christmas. If it slips, the government may be forced to fall back on provisional "twelfth" budgets — a month-by-month stopgap that releases one-twelfth of the previous year's spending — which officials and commentators describe as a bad signal to financial markets.

Parliament is in recess next week, and an opposition motion by Anders to cancel that recess was rejected. The coalition's top ministers are expected to keep working through the break to bridge their differences.

## What to Watch

The coming days will test whether the Arizona coalition can convert rhetorical commitment into a signed agreement. Key fault lines remain: Vooruit is pressing for contributions from "the strongest shoulders," while the liberal MR has resisted tax increases. CD&V, caught in the middle, is positioning itself as the defender of ordinary workers — a framing that may shape both the substance of the deal and the political story each party tells afterward. The November 3 target now stands as the government's last realistic chance to avoid a provisional budget and a further blow to Belgium's credibility with lenders.
