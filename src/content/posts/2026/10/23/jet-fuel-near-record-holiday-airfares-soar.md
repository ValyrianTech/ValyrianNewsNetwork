# Jet Fuel Near Record, Holiday Airfares Soar

Holiday travelers should brace for the most expensive flights in years. The average price of U.S. jet fuel reached $4.90 a gallon on Thursday, the highest single-day average this year and more than twice its level a year ago, according to the Argus US Jet Fuel Index tracked by [Airlines for America](https://www.airlines.org/dataset/argus-us-jet-fuel-index/). Airlines are passing much of that cost onto passengers, driving Thanksgiving and Christmas airfares sharply higher.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/23/nx-s1-6009506/jet-fuel-price-holiday-airfare), airfares for Thanksgiving are up about 30% compared with last year, while tickets for the Christmas holiday have risen more than 20%. The outlet reports that Hopper, a travel tech company, characterizes current fares as a 10-year high.

## Why Fares Are Climbing

Jet fuel is one of an airline's largest expenses, so when its price spikes, carriers have limited room to absorb the cost. "Airfares right now are at what we see as a 10-year high because of the high cost of jet fuel," Hayley Berg, lead economist at Hopper, told NPR. "Airlines have to pass on these super high jet fuel prices in the form of higher fares to consumers."

The surge is rooted in a global fuel-supply shock. Tensions involving Iran have severely disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical artery for oil and fuel exports, while Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries have further strained global supplies, as NPR detailed in its [coverage of record diesel prices](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/04/nx-s1-5948016/diesel-price-record-high) in September.

Chris Sununu, head of the trade group Airlines for America, said carriers are absorbing most of the increase but cannot shoulder all of it. "The airlines themselves are eating most of that cost," he told CNBC in an [interview](https://www.cnbc.com/video/2026/10/20/airlines-are-eating-most-of-the-cost-of-jet-fuel-prices-says-airlines-for-americas-chris-sununu.html) this week. "And some of it unfortunately has to be passed on to the customer and so you see that 20 to 30 percent increase as we go into the holidays."

## Travelers Are Paying — For Now

Despite the higher prices, demand appears to be holding steady. Delta Air Lines CEO Ed Bastian said on an earnings call this month that consumers have shown a "limited amount of resistance" and still view airfare as "reasonably affordable" after the increases — a signal, he said, that bodes well for the industry.

Berg noted that holiday travel is "really sticky," meaning many travelers will keep their plans even at elevated prices. "Even with higher prices, travelers are unlikely to make major changes to their traditional holiday home for the holiday trips," she said.

Some consumers are adapting rather than abandoning their trips. Berg said an unusual number of travelers booked holiday flights in September, earlier than usual, in an effort to lock in better prices. Others are being priced out entirely. "I'm gonna have to drive," Rachel Stern of Frederick, Maryland, told NPR. "For me and my kids, just too much to get tickets."

## A Global Squeeze

The pain is not confined to the United States. Michael O'Leary, CEO of the European low-cost carrier Ryanair, warned this week that European fares could jump sharply next year. "You could see airfares rise next year in Europe. I think by 10, 15, maybe 20 percent," he said, adding that it would be "the biggest increase in airfares ... in history in Europe in the next 12 months."

The U.S. may be somewhat more insulated because it has greater domestic oil-producing and refining capacity than Europe. But the same forces — constrained global supply and elevated crude prices — are weighing on carriers on both sides of the Atlantic.

## What to Watch

The trajectory of holiday fares will depend largely on whether the global fuel-supply crunch eases. With the Strait of Hormuz still disrupted and refinery capacity under strain, jet fuel prices show little sign of a quick retreat. For now, the advice from industry watchers is consistent: book early, shop around, and expect to pay more than last year. For some households, the math may simply mean staying closer to home.

"I feel bad for people who don't have the means to pay the extra money," traveler Mary Haller of Bethany Beach, Delaware, told NPR.
