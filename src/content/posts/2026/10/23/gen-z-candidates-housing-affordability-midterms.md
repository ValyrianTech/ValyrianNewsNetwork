# Gen Z Candidates Put Housing at the Heart of the Midterms

A new wave of Generation Z candidates is running for office in the 2026 midterms with housing affordability as a defining issue, arguing that their generation is uniquely squeezed by the highest housing costs in decades. As [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/23/nx-s1-5983813/gen-z-candidates-midterms-housing-affordability), the campaign season has become "laser focused on affordability," and these young candidates are betting that a shared generational grievance can translate into bipartisan policy — and votes.

The story is not confined to one party. NPR profiled two Democrats running for Connecticut's state legislature and a Republican running for reelection in Montana, all centering housing in their platforms. Their common ground reflects a broader reality: a chronic national housing shortage, record renter cost burdens and a widening wealth gap between renters and homeowners have made housing a cross-partisan, cross-generational campaign theme.

## The Squeeze on Young Americans

The numbers behind the anxiety are stark. Average 30-year fixed mortgage rates have topped 7.5%, according to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/06/housing-affordability-election.html), making the leap from renting to buying increasingly difficult for first-time purchasers. Meanwhile, [Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/six-takeaways-americas-rental-housing-2026) found in its *America's Rental Housing 2026* report that renter cost burdens are at record highs, with household growth down for the fourth consecutive year.

The result is a generation renting with roommates into their 30s, struggling to save for down payments, and questioning whether buying even makes sense at current prices. "We're in the thick of it," said Olaleye Onikuyide, 28, a Democrat running for Connecticut state representative in the 19th District. "We might be the generation that is more educated and yet worse off than our parents."

## Three Candidates, One Theme

Onikuyide, who works professionally in real estate, still lives with his parents to save money and rides public transit. He is campaigning on mixed-income housing, walkable cities and added density. He acknowledged that housing has suddenly become a crowded political stage — but argued the real action is closer to home. "All of a sudden, it's blown up and everybody's at the concerts," he said. "I keep one eye on the national conversation about it, but the action is happening at the state level."

In New Haven, 26-year-old Democrat Eli Sabin is a renter who served on the city's Board of Alders since his college sophomore year and worked on zoning changes to allow denser, more affordable development. He won a three-way primary against a democratic socialist and a longtime incumbent. "I think it's the experience of being a renter and just the insecurity and instability that you feel," Sabin said of his motivation. "I think people feel like we need a fight right now... We need energy, we need new ideas and we can't accept that the status quo is all right."

On the Republican side, 21-year-old Lukas Schubert represents District 8 in Kalispell, Montana. Elected in 2024, he is running for reelection with housing at the top of his issues page. He proposed a 2025 tax break, HB895, for developers selling starter homes below 85% of the local median value, though the measure did not pass. He wants to rein in large foreign corporate homebuyers and non-resident large landowners. "It is a top priority among Republicans as well," Schubert said. "It's not just, you know, a Democrat issue."

## A Bipartisan Playbook in Montana

Schubert's state offers a model that both parties have cited. Montana's 2023 "Montana Miracle" — a package of zoning and land-use reforms legalizing duplexes, reforming parking rules and overhauling planning — has been held up as a bipartisan template for tackling the housing crisis, as the [American Planning Association](https://www.planning.org/planning/2023/fall/how-the-bipartisan-montana-miracle-confronts-the-housing-crisis-head-on/) documented. Gen Z and millennial lawmakers helped drive the overhaul, and reforms continued in 2025.

Schubert argued the problem is partly about what gets built. "Seemingly all of the new development is apartment buildings or big, you know, 5, 10 million-dollar mansions," he said. "That's what we need to be really getting rid of government barriers to producing." Homeownership, he added, "has been one of the biggest factors in wealth retention and development for the middle class in this country."

## Why Representation Matters

The candidates' personal stakes are notable given who typically holds office. A study by the [National Low Income Housing Coalition](https://nlihc.org/resource/study-finds-renters-are-highly-underrepresented-all-levels-government) found that renters are highly underrepresented at all levels of government, where elected officials are overwhelmingly homeowners. Candidates like Sabin, who rents, are an exception to that pattern — a point that underscores how directly housing policy affects the people writing it.

## Analysis: A Generational Grievance Meets a Crowded Field

The Gen Z housing push reflects a convergence of forces. Housing affordability has climbed the national agenda as mortgage rates stay elevated and the renter-owner wealth gap widens. For young candidates, the issue offers a rare opportunity to speak to voters across party lines — housing touches renters, would-be buyers, downsizing empty-nesters and rate-locked homeowners alike.

But translating a shared grievance into policy is harder. Housing is governed largely at the state and local level, where zoning fights are slow, technical and often opposed by entrenched interests. The candidates' emphasis on state-level action is telling: even as housing dominates the national conversation, the levers of change sit in city halls and statehouses.

The broader electoral backdrop adds uncertainty. Young voters' support for Democrats fell to a two-decade low in the 2024 presidential election, though it has been returning in 2026 polling, according to [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/22/upshot/young-voters-polls-midterms.html). Whether housing affordability becomes a decisive force — or merely a shared talking point — will depend on whether these candidates can convert anger over costs into concrete results.

## What to Watch

The midterms are set for November 2026, and several signals bear watching. Massachusetts voters will weigh Question 7, a ballot measure that would relax single-family zoning to allow homes on lots as small as 5,000 square feet — a parallel affordability test case. And in Connecticut and Montana, the fortunes of these young candidates will offer an early read on whether a generation priced out of housing can begin to write its own solutions.

For now, the message from the campaign trail is that housing has moved from the margins to the center of American politics — and that the generation most burdened by high costs intends to be heard.
