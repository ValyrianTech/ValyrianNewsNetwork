# China's Snow Leopard Population Doubles to About 5,000

China's wild snow leopard population has roughly doubled over the past 13 years, growing from about 2,500 in 2013 to an estimated 5,000 today, the National Forestry and Grassland Administration announced on World Snow Leopard Day, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261023/5766f612fd334b6993658e3a89d548ed/c.html). The milestone accompanies a landmark breeding achievement in the Helan Mountains, where conservationists have completed what officials describe as the world's first full snow leopard reintroduction cycle.

## A Global Stronghold for the 'King of Snow Mountains'

China now claims the widest snow leopard distribution, the largest population, and the largest habitat area of any country, the agency said. Its snow leopard habitat spans roughly 2.08 million square kilometers—more than 60 percent of the global total.

The species is found across eight Chinese provinces and regions: Xinjiang, Tibet, Qinghai, Gansu, Sichuan, Yunnan, Inner Mongolia, and Ningxia. Snow leopards typically inhabit alpine bare rock, alpine meadow, and desert mountains at altitudes of 3,000 to 5,000 meters.

Classified as a national first-class protected wild animal, the snow leopard is regarded as a flagship and umbrella species of global alpine ecosystems and a core indicator of high-altitude mountain ecosystem health.

## Building Back Habitat and Prey

Officials attributed the recovery to a broad set of ecological measures. Since 2013, when China began systematic, targeted snow leopard protection, the government has pursued integrated management of what it describes as "mountains, rivers, forests, farmland, lakes, grassland and sand." Major programs such as Natural Forest Protection and Returning Grazing Land to Grassland have helped restore alpine vegetation and rebound prey species including blue sheep, ibex, and argali—rebuilding the food chain on which snow leopards depend.

In the species' core range, authorities have established two national parks and 78 important protected areas. More than 800,000 square kilometers of habitat now fall under strict protection, and nearly 40 percent of potential snow leopard habitat is under standardized management, according to Xinhua.

Monitoring has also been modernized. Infrared camera networks across key areas are combined with satellite collar tracking, fecal DNA identification, and large-scale population assessments to track populations, home ranges, and breeding behavior.

## A World-First Breeding Cycle in the Helan Mountains

The most striking development involves the Helan Mountains, historically important snow leopard habitat where the species went locally extinct for nearly 70 years due to human disturbance and habitat degradation.

Since 2021, the Helan Mountain National Nature Reserve has reintroduced snow leopards from Inner Mongolia, Gansu, Qinghai, Tibet, and Xinjiang, reaching eight introduced individuals cumulatively. Combined with natural dispersal, the local population has now reached nine, according to [China News Service](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/sh/2026/05-22/10626611.shtml).

In 2026, the project achieved a complete "wild release and settlement—natural pairing—wild birth—successful rearing" cycle. A female released in April 2024 gave birth between April and May 2025 and completed a full year of rearing; she and her one-year-old cub were captured together on infrared camera. China News Service reported the result as the first snow leopard reintroduction project anywhere to fully achieve wild natural reproduction.

Officials framed the achievement as a shift from "population recovery" to "historical distribution area reconstruction," and held it up as a Chinese model for restoring endangered alpine felids worldwide.

## Community Co-Governance and Legal Backing

Beyond protected areas, China has emphasized community participation and law enforcement. Herders in pastoral regions help with patrols, monitoring, and habitat management, backed by government compensation and insurance mechanisms for wildlife-related losses. Authorities have also enforced the Wildlife Protection Law through special campaigns against poaching, illegal trade, and habitat destruction.

Progress has appeared elsewhere as well. In Xinjiang, a snow leopard was fitted with a satellite tracking device for the first time in January 2026, and in Sichuan a mother snow leopard with two cubs was caught on camera in the Giant Panda National Park in June 2026—signs of stable breeding.

## What's Next

China's forestry authority said it will continue optimizing the protection framework, with a focus on restoring habitat connectivity, preserving population genetic diversity, and managing human-wildlife conflict more precisely. The task ahead is not only to sustain the recovery but to secure the high-altitude ecological barrier that scientists consider central to Asia's mountain biodiversity.
