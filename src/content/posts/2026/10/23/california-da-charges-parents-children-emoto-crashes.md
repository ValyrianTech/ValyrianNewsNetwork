# California DA Charges Parents Over Kids' E-Moto Crashes

Orange County prosecutors have charged at least a dozen parents this year for allowing their children to illegally ride electric motorcycles, an aggressive legal strategy that seeks to hold adults criminally responsible for injuries and deaths tied to the fast-growing devices. The district attorney's office announced six additional cases on Wednesday, bringing the total to 12 since January, according to [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/california-parents-charged-childrens-accidents-motos/story?id=137473876).

## A Rising Toll on Young Riders

The charges follow a series of serious accidents involving children on e-motos — two-wheeled, off-highway electric motorcycles with no pedals, distinct from pedal-equipped e-bicycles that carry motors under 750 watts. Under California rules, children under 16 are prohibited from riding e-motos, yet the devices have proliferated on public streets where they are illegal and unregistered.

According to the California Statewide Integrated Traffic Record System cited by the district attorney's office, children ages 11 to 14 account for 61.7% of e-moto crashes — a striking statistic given the under-16 prohibition. Injuries attributed to e-bikes and e-motorcycles have spiked more than 400% in Southern California over the past four years, the office said, a figure reported by [ABC7](https://abc7.com/post/orange-county-cracking-down-illegal-bikes-motorcycles-parents-face-charges/19099804/).

"We don't want to have to prosecute parents — we want to save kids' lives," Orange County District Attorney Todd Spitzer said in a statement. "When parents refuse to parent and tell their kids 'no' when it comes to riding something that is dangerous and illegal, then I have no choice but to hold parents accountable and file criminal charges."

## The Cases Behind the Crackdown

The six newly announced cases illustrate how the devices — some capable of nearly 50 miles per hour — have outpaced both parental oversight and law enforcement:

- **Alex Li** faces a misdemeanor child endangerment charge after prosecutors say his 11-year-old son collided with a pickup truck in Yorba Linda while riding an e-moto capable of exceeding 33 mph. The boy suffered broken bones and an arterial bleed.
- **Maria Roehling** was charged with felony child endangerment and an infraction for allowing an unlicensed minor to drive. Her 9-year-old son was allegedly speeding on an e-moto capable of more than 47 mph when a pickup truck struck him in Yorba Linda.
- **Dan Rezmives** was charged with misdemeanor child endangerment, contributing to the delinquency of a minor, and an infraction after a police officer allegedly saw his 12-year-old son riding an e-moto on a city street — with Rezmives nearby on a bicycle. The family had been previously warned.
- **Hulin Guo and Xiaomin Wang** each face misdemeanor endangerment and delinquency charges plus an infraction after their 17-year-old son allegedly led Newport Beach police on a pursuit exceeding 40 mph, following two prior police run-ins.
- **Tuan Nguyen** faces misdemeanor child endangerment after his 13-year-old son allegedly led police on a 40 mph pursuit and crashed a Surron capable of 49 mph into a police vehicle.
- **Jose De Jesus Ramirezbarragan** was charged with misdemeanor contributing to the delinquency of a minor after allegedly allowing his 15-year-old brother to ride his e-motorcycle, who police say ran red lights and crashed into bushes during a pursuit.

ABC News reported that it had not yet identified attorneys for any of those charged. All accused are presumed innocent unless proven otherwise.

## A Novel Legal Approach

The prosecutions represent a notable choice to target parents rather than manufacturers or retailers of the high-powered devices. The charges rest on three legal theories: child endangerment for exposing minors to illegal and dangerous vehicles, contributing to the delinquency of a minor, and an infraction for allowing an unlicensed minor to drive.

Law enforcement officials have voiced support for the approach. "Todd Spitzer's office did a phenomenal job holding his parents accountable because when the parents are starting to be held accountable, who are really buying these devices for the kids that can't afford to buy them in the first place, really the accountability lies with the parents," Sgt. Mikes Thomas of the Huntington Beach police told ABC7.

The strategy builds on a case that drew widespread attention. In April, prosecutors [announced](https://abcnews.com/GMA/News/mother-charged-after-teen-son-allegedly-hits-injures/story?id=132283010) that a mother was arrested and charged after her 14-year-old allegedly struck an 81-year-old veteran while riding an e-motorcycle. The charges were later [upgraded](https://abcnews.com/US/mother-charged-involuntary-manslaughter-teen-sons-alleged-motorcycle/story?id=132586911) to involuntary manslaughter after the man died. The mother has not yet entered a plea.

In May, Spitzer launched a dedicated prosecution unit called "RIDE SAFELY" to pursue accountability for illegal e-bike and e-moto riders and their parents — the same week Huntington Beach police hosted an e-bike safety symposium.

## Broader Pressure on the Devices

The crackdown coincides with growing scrutiny of how high-powered e-motos are sold. Amazon announced it would stop selling certain e-bikes in California that exceed state speed limits, amid rising crashes, according to [ABC7](https://abc7.com/post/amazon-says-sell-bikes-california-exceed-state-speed-limits-amid-rise-deadly-crashes/19078566/). The devices at the center of the Orange County cases — models such as the Surron, frequently marketed as off-road dirt bikes — are increasingly used on public streets by minors.

The stakes were underscored by one of the county's most closely watched tragedies. In May, 13-year-old Benson Nguyen died in Garden Grove after losing control of an e-motorcycle at more than 40 mph and slamming into a light pole, [ABC7 reported](https://abc7.com/post/oc-parents-teen-killed-riding-motorcycle-charged-child-endangerment/19965722/). Prosecutors later charged his parents — his father, Vu Thanh Nguyen, with child endangerment and contributing to the delinquency of a minor, and his mother, Hong Cuc Thi Nguyen, with misdemeanor child endangerment — alleging the father bought his son a faster, more powerful e-motorcycle despite repeated warnings.

## What to Watch

The Orange County cases will test how far criminal law can stretch to address a product-safety and parenting problem that regulators and retailers have struggled to contain. Key questions remain: whether courts will uphold endangerment charges built on parents' failure to supervise, and whether the prosecutions will deter purchases of the fastest devices or simply push sales further underground.

For now, Spitzer's office shows no sign of slowing. With 12 parents charged in ten months and injuries still climbing, the county has effectively declared that when children ride illegal machines, the adults who enable them will answer for it.
