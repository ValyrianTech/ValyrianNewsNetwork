# US Consumer Sentiment Nears Record Low Before Midterms

US consumer sentiment has fallen to near its lowest level on record, according to the University of Michigan's long-running monthly survey, as persistent inflation, elevated gasoline prices and a weakening job market fuel deep economic pessimism just weeks before the November midterm elections.

The final reading of the university's consumer sentiment index registered at 48.0 in October, the second-lowest final reading in the survey's 74-year history, [ABC News reported](https://abcnews.com/Business/consumer-sentiment-nears-time-low-ahead-midterms/story?id=137507935). The figure was essentially flat from September's 48.1, and well above the all-time low of 44.8 set in May, in the months after the outbreak of the Iran war.

## A Squeeze on Household Budgets

The pessimism is rooted in prices. The average cost of a gallon of gas in the US stood at $4.29, according to AAA, a roughly 43% jump since the Iran war began in late February. On Friday, global oil prices crossed $116 a barrel, hitting a six-month high.

Fuel costs have become a visible, daily reminder of the war's economic reach. The conflict, now in its eighth month, has disrupted global oil supplies and kept upward pressure on energy prices, feeding directly into the household budgets that the sentiment survey tracks.

The broader price picture offers little relief. Federal data showed inflation rose at an annual rate of 3.7% in September, more than a percentage point above the Federal Reserve's 2% target. Consumers surveyed by the university said they expect inflation to climb even higher over the next year.

Those expectations have begun to ripple through financial markets. A key long-term Treasury rate has jumped in recent months as investors feared that rising prices would erode their returns. The resulting climb in bond yields has pushed up borrowing costs on credit cards and mortgages, threatening to deepen the financial crunch for households.

"Democrats and Republicans each registered improvement in consumer sentiment in October from the previous month, but it was outweighed by a decline in sentiment among independents," Joanne Hsu, director of the university's Surveys of Consumers, said in a statement.

## An Economy Sending Mixed Signals

The gloomy reading sits in tension with other measures of economic health. Gross domestic product grew over the three months ending in June, defying fears of a downturn triggered by the Iran war, and consumer spending surged 0.9% in August, according to government data.

Yet the labor market has wobbled. Employers added just 29,000 jobs in September, a sharp slowdown from 162,000 in August and well below economists' expectations, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported. Combined with accelerating prices, the weak hiring figures help explain why Americans feel worse about the economy than headline growth would suggest.

Affordability — from grocery bills to rent, utilities and gasoline — has emerged as a central concern for voters heading into the early-November elections, giving the sentiment data clear political weight.

## What's Next

The October survey underscores how quickly household confidence can sour even when the broader economy remains resilient on paper. With oil prices climbing and inflation running well above target, the pressure on budgets — and on voters' moods — is unlikely to ease before ballots are cast.

Economists and policymakers will be watching whether the sentiment slump proves a temporary reaction to fuel prices or a more durable shift in how Americans view their financial prospects. For candidates in both parties, the answer may shape the midterm outcome.

The University of Michigan index has long been treated as an early warning signal, one that can diverge from official statistics when households feel the pinch of prices more keenly than they celebrate growth. That gap — between a resilient economy and a pessimistic public — is likely to define the economic debate through Election Day and beyond.
