# Supreme Court Upholds Pause on Trump Trans Prison Policy

The Supreme Court on Wednesday declined to let the Trump administration end gender-affirming care for transgender people in federal prisons, denying an emergency request to lift a lower-court injunction while the case proceeds. In an unsigned order issued without explanation, the justices left intact a ruling that requires the federal Bureau of Prisons to continue providing medically necessary treatment, including hormone therapy, to inmates diagnosed with gender dysphoria.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/21/nx-s1-5993878/supreme-court-trump-trans-prison-policy), the order read simply that "the application for stay presented to The Chief Justice and by him referred to the Court is denied." Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito indicated they would have granted the administration's request.

The decision is a temporary but significant setback for a policy that has been contested in the courts since President Donald Trump returned to office.

## Context: An Executive Order and a Legal Fight

The dispute traces to January 2025, when Trump signed Executive Order 14168, titled *Defending Women from Gender Ideology Extremism and Restoring Biological Truth to the Federal Government*, just hours after taking office. The order barred federal funds from being used to "promote" gender ideology and directed the Bureau of Prisons to wind down gender-affirming treatments.

The BOP moved to implement the directive through internal memoranda and, in February 2026, issued Program Statement 5260.01, which continued to prohibit gender-affirming care. In response, three incarcerated transgender people filed a class action — *Kingdom v. Trump* — in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia in March 2025, representing roughly 1,000 to 2,000 transgender people in federal custody, according to [reporting](https://www.aclu.org/press-releases/supreme-court-denies-trump-administration-request-to-withhold-health-care-from-incarcerated-transgender-people). The plaintiffs argue the policy violates the Eighth Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment.

## Key Developments

U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth first granted a preliminary injunction in June 2025 requiring continued care. After the BOP issued its new program statement, the court issued a second injunction on June 17, 2026, blocking the policy and ordering care under the pre-executive-order rules.

The administration appealed, and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit rejected its request to pause the injunction. Solicitor General D. John Sauer then turned to the Supreme Court, filing an emergency application on September 28, 2026. The Court's denial on October 21 leaves the injunction in force as litigation continues on the merits.

## The Care at Issue

The ACLU estimates that about 800 of the roughly 1,000 class members with a gender dysphoria diagnosis have been prescribed hormone therapy, and many also receive social transition accommodations. Advocates stress that such care has been part of federal prison practice for decades.

"This is a critical ruling for our clients and all transgender people in Bureau of Prisons custody," said Li Nowlin-Sohl, senior staff attorney with the ACLU's LGBTQ and HIV Project. "The denial of medically necessary health care, including gender-affirming health care, to people in prison is a violation of their fundamental constitutional rights."

Shawn Thomas Meerkamper, managing attorney at the Transgender Law Center, said the ruling "affirms that everyone, trans or not, deserves access to medically necessary health care and to be treated with dignity in federal custody."

## The Government's Argument

In its emergency filings, the administration contended that providing the care posed risks, arguing that recipients could become "targets for attacks" and that the treatment raised "fairness concerns" by breeding resentment among other inmates. It also questioned the widely used standards of care developed by the World Professional Association for Transgender Health.

The ACLU countered that the government's security and safety rationales were absent from the administrative record. "Had BOP's doctors found this treatment unhelpful or harmful to patients or had the agency found that the treatment compromised security, surely BOP would have mentioned it in the administrative record," the group wrote in its opposition, calling the omission a "glaring" sign that the arguments were "contrived."

An unsigned order from the Court offers no reasoning, leaving the underlying Eighth Amendment questions to be resolved by lower courts.

## Analysis: A Ruling Against the Grain

The decision is notable because it cuts against a broader pattern of Supreme Court rulings that have favored the administration's restrictive transgender policies. In June 2025, the Court upheld state laws barring gender-affirming care for minors, and in June 2026 it ruled that states may bar transgender girls from competing in sports at publicly funded schools.

Legal observers see the emergency ruling less as a signal of the Court's leanings on the merits and more as a reflection of the narrow standard for emergency intervention: the administration failed to show it was entitled to immediate relief while appeals play out. The underlying constitutional dispute over prison health care remains unresolved.

A separate data issue complicates the picture. The BOP stopped publishing statistics reflecting transgender inmates after Trump took office, recording only male or female individuals — a gap that makes the full scope of the affected population difficult to measure.

## What's Next

The case returns to the D.C. Circuit and the district court for adjudication of the Eighth Amendment claim. Whether the administration will pursue new policy mechanisms to restrict care — or seek further review — remains uncertain.

Advocacy groups have also raised concerns about real-world compliance, noting that some facilities have been slow to honor earlier injunctions. Meanwhile, the Supreme Court is set to hear several more transgender-rights cases in the months ahead, including a dispute over a religious preschool's access to state funding while excluding LGBTQ families and a question of parental rights over teens' access to care.

For now, the ruling means that transgender inmates with a gender dysphoria diagnosis can continue to receive the care their doctors deem medically necessary — a pause, not a final resolution, in a legal battle that shows no sign of ending.
