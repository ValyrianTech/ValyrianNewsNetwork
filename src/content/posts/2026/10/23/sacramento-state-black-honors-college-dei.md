# How One University Elevates Black Students Amid DEI Cuts

As the Trump administration wages a broad campaign against diversity, equity and inclusion programs in higher education, Sacramento State is pressing ahead with a first-of-its-kind effort to elevate Black students — and officials say it complies fully with federal and state law.

The university's Black Honors College (BHC), a school-within-a-school focused on Black history, life and culture, opened in August 2024, just months before the administration began its nationwide push against DEI. NPR's [Elissa Nadworny reports](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/23/nx-s1-5797959/one-university-elevating-black-students) that the program now enrolls more than 800 students and offers a case study in how institutions are responding to the shifting legal landscape.

## Designed to be race-neutral

The BHC is open to students of any race. White, Asian and Latino students may apply, and some do. Admission is based on a minimum GPA of 3.5, not race — a deliberate design that President Dr. J. Luke Wood says keeps the college within legal bounds.

"It's nondiscriminatory because you can be a white student, an Asian student or Latino and be in the Black Honors College," Wood told NPR. "...and we have non-Black students who apply who want to have an elite, specialized experience."

All courses are taught from a Black studies perspective — a math class, for example, includes the stories of famous Black mathematicians. Faculty are chosen for their track record of educating Black students, including low withdrawal and failure rates among those students.

"Historically, the campus struggled to create an environment of success [for Black students]," Wood said. "So we created an intervention that was designed to meet their needs while also serving any student who was interested in being part of it."

## A response to federal pressure

The program launched as colleges nationwide were scrambling to respond to a series of policy moves. After the Supreme Court's [2023 decision banning affirmative action](https://www.npr.org/2023/06/29/1181138066/affirmative-action-supreme-court-decision) in admissions, the Trump administration opened civil rights investigations into programs it said singled out specific races. In September, the Treasury and IRS [proposed a rule](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/03/g-s1-141882/trump-tax-exempt-status-schools) that would strip tax-exempt status from private institutions engaging in what it calls racial discrimination — a measure that could affect thousands of schools if finalized.

Many universities preemptively rolled back DEI offices and programs to avoid losing federal funding. At the University of Michigan, senior Hayden O'Neill lost a scholarship program that was shut down last year.

"I was pretty sad just at the fact that our university would comply with those federal guidelines," O'Neill told NPR. "It definitely feels disheartening. I don't feel as comfortable as I thought I would at a school like this."

The administration has framed its push as an end to discriminatory practices. "We ended D-E-I in America!" President Donald Trump declared in his February 2026 State of the Union address. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent argued in September that rebranding race-based preferences "as equitable, inclusive, or diversity-enhancing does not change their discriminatory nature."

## Measurable results

Sacramento State says the approach is working. Black student enrollment has risen 22% over three years, according to [Amsterdam News](https://amsterdamnews.com/news/2026/03/05/how-sac-states-black-honors-college-centers-black-students/), and the university says more Black students are re-enrolling and graduating. The college, now in its third year, has grown from about 80 students at launch to more than 800 today.

The BHC is housed on the first floor of the university library — described as a "hub, a home within a home." It offers smaller classes, mentorship, paid internships, scholarships and a dedicated residential community.

For students like Naomi Thrower, a junior majoring in Deaf Studies, the appeal is personal. "The kids used to bully me in middle school and say I was whitewashed," she told NPR. "So when it came time for college, I wanted to be surrounded by students who looked like me and also had that same academic drive."

## What to watch

Whether the model can scale — and survive — remains an open question. The Treasury's proposed rule technically targets private institutions, and Sacramento State is a public university, leaving the college's direct exposure to the rule unclear. But the broader investigative climate applies to any institution that receives federal funds.

Wood believes other schools could replicate his approach. "I think that more institutions could be easily doing the same," he said.

As the legal fight over race and education continues, Sacramento State's experiment will be closely watched as a potential template — one that elevates Black students not by excluding others, but by building a space open to all.
