# Trump Merch Stores Close as 'He Has Peaked'

A wave of brick-and-mortar stores selling Trump and MAGA merchandise is closing across the United States, with owners and observers pointing to a mix of causes: a term-limited president who can no longer run again, falling approval ratings, a strained economy weighing on discretionary spending, and anxiety about the coming midterms. At least four Trump merchandise stores have shut their doors in 2026 alone, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/22/nx-s1-6002970/trump-merchandise-stores-closing-maga-midterms).

The closures have become a barometer of something harder to measure than sales figures: the durability of a political brand built on enthusiasm, identity and spectacle.

## From Boom to Bust

The trajectory of Bill Hays' Knoxville-area superstore illustrates the shift. Hays opened the store in April 2024, and it boomed to roughly $200,000 a month in sales, with lines of up to 80 people waiting to buy caps and shirts. By the time he closed it in September 2026, revenue had collapsed to about $20,000 a month.

"People were not wearing their colors, so to speak, like they did before the (2024) election," Hays told NPR. "After the inauguration, I think a lot of people felt that their job was done. They didn't have to promote MAGA or Trump."

Hays also cited unpopular policies, including mass government cuts under the Department of Government Efficiency, as a drag on business. He still operates a store in Gatlinburg, Tennessee, and is watching Christmas sales closely.

Other sellers describe a similar arc. Steven Slaton, who runs the Trumped Store and Coffee Shop in Show Low, Arizona, said September 2026 sales were down 20 to 25 percent compared with September 2024, and he has marked down $30 hats and shirts to $10. MAGA Mall, an online retailer and wholesaler, reports MAGA gear sales down about 30 percent compared with a presidential election year. Richard Kligman, who owns two Myrtle Beach, South Carolina stores with his brother, told Fortune that Trump merchandise sales have fallen 39 percent from 2024 - a figure reflective of the broader pullback in election-year demand described across sellers interviewed by NPR and local outlets.

## The 'He Has Peaked' Argument

For some longtime supporters, the decline is about more than economics. Wes Alker, a Trump superfan in Bensalem, Pennsylvania - where a Trump store closed in early 2026 - offered a blunt assessment.

"He's no longer a viable business model for entrepreneurs because he has peaked," Alker said.

The numbers behind that claim are suggestive. Trump's approval stood at about 50 percent at the start of his term and had fallen to 44 percent around the time the Bensalem store closed; nine months later, around the Hays store closure, it had slipped five more points, according to RealClearPolling figures cited by NPR.

## Economy or Enthusiasm?

Not every owner agrees that fading enthusiasm is the culprit. Whitey Taylor, who runs Trump Town USA in Boones Mill, Virginia, said his daily sales have fallen from a peak of about $15,000 a day to roughly $1,200 - but he blames the economy, not voters' feelings about the president.

"Businesses are off, but it's been off since the election," Taylor said. "What's going on is, of course, the economy. Nobody's buying anything, but the essentials. They're buying gasoline and groceries."

The macro picture supports at least part of that view: consumer prices rose roughly 9 to 10 percent from early 2024 through September 2026, squeezing household budgets and discretionary purchases alike. The two explanations - a genuine cooling of the Trump brand and a broad retail slowdown - likely interact rather than compete.

Loyalty, meanwhile, has not vanished. Customer Paul Orr of Franklin County, Virginia, remained emphatic. "President Trump, he's an awesome president," he said. The decline, in other words, may be concentrated among casual buyers rather than the core base.

## What's Next

With the midterms approaching, retailers are hedging. Kligman is bundling $40 worth of merchandise into $9.99 grab bags to clear inventory and hopes to be largely out of the Trump goods business by the end of 2027. Ron Solomon, president of MAGA Mall, estimates about 20 Trump-specialty stores remain nationwide and expects the Trump merchandise business to "fade away" unless a family member takes up the mantle. Many sellers are diversifying into broader patriotic merchandise, such as "We the People" hats.

A Democratic sweep in November could further dampen casual demand; a strong Republican showing could revive it. Either way, the stores' struggles capture a transition: a movement that once sold itself through its symbols now faces the harder task of sustaining a brand beyond the ballot box. As Kligman put it, "It's absolutely gone down, but it had to, because he can't run again."
