# Quarter of Belgian Firms Breach EU Pay Gap Ceiling

One in four Belgian companies pays women at least 5 percent less than men of the same age performing the same job, a new Randstad study has found -- a gap that exceeds the maximum permitted under the European Union's pay transparency rules, which Belgium has still not written into national law. The share of firms breaching the threshold has risen from 20 percent a year ago to 25 percent now, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/23/loonkloof-te-groot/), which reported the findings on 23 October 2026.

The 33rd annual Hudson Reward Survey analysed more than 375,000 pay packages across more than 400 salaried job functions at 1,121 organisations, offering one of the most detailed pictures yet of the "equal pay for equal work" component of Belgium's gender pay gap.

## Wider Gaps at the Top

The clearest discrepancies appear in management roles. According to Randstad's Wouter Beuckels, the gap widens as responsibility increases -- a pattern the study attributes largely to representation and negotiation. Only a quarter to a third of management positions are held by women, Beuckels noted, meaning fewer women sit at the table when pay and promotions are decided. Women also negotiate their salaries less frequently, he added, so they "don't pull the longest straw."

At the senior and middle-management level just below the top tier, the gap rose from 6.95 percent to 8.27 percent year-on-year. At lower levels of the organisational ladder, Beuckels said, women often earn the same as -- or even more than -- their male colleagues, keeping the difference within the 5 percent margin. Company size also matters: large firms with formalised pay scales largely already comply, while organisations with 100 to 250 employees are less likely to do so and show wider gaps.

## A Directive Belgium Has Not Yet Adopted

The 5 percent ceiling stems from the EU Pay Transparency Directive, which gives employees the right to request average pay data for colleagues in the same function, requires job applicants to be told a pay range in advance, and obliges large companies to report regularly on their gender pay gap. Member states were given until 7 June 2026 to transpose it into national law. Belgium missed the deadline and, as [HLN](https://www.hln.be/mijn-geld/loonkloof-wordt-nog-groter-kwart-bedrijven-betaalt-mannen-en-vrouwen-niet-evenveel~a18ada74/) reported, requested a six-month extension in a letter to the European Commission.

Federal Work Minister David Clarinval, of the French-speaking liberal MR party, cited 30 open questions to the Commission -- including how the rules interact with EU privacy law and how broadly "pay" should be defined, since Belgium traditionally counts bonuses, meal vouchers and company cars alongside base salary. "As long as we have no answers to those questions, it is impossible to introduce this directive," Clarinval said. He stressed that the government remains "in favour of equal pay, regardless of gender."

The delay has not been politically neutral. The N-VA party, a coalition partner, has argued that Belgium already has one of the smallest gender pay gaps in the EU and warned of added administrative burden; N-VA faction leader Axel Ronse has called the transparency rules "welfare destruction." Belgium's socialist Vooruit party, by contrast, strongly defends the directive.

## A Paradox of Numbers

The debate is complicated by competing measurements. By Eurostat's method, which compares average gross hourly wages across the whole economy, Belgium has one of the smallest gender pay gaps in the EU: in 2022, the latest harmonised figures available, only Luxembourg, Italy and Romania recorded a narrower gap, according to [Statbel](https://statbel.fgov.be/nl/nieuws/vrouwen-verdienden-nog-5-minder-dan-mannen-2022). Across all jobs without controlling for sector or working hours, Randstad found women earn 11 percent less than men -- a figure driven largely by the over-representation of women in lower-paid sectors.

Controlling for age and function, the overall gap was 2.21 percent in 2026, up from 1.79 percent the year before. That same-function figure and the 25 percent company breach rate are, in Randstad's view, the most relevant measures of equal pay for equal work.

## What's Next

How the gap evolves now depends on two unknowns. The first is whether Brussels grants Belgium its extension, pushing transposition toward roughly December 2026. The second is whether the rising share of non-compliant firms -- from 20 percent to 25 percent in a single year -- reflects genuine slippage or merely better measurement ahead of the law's arrival. Employers remain sceptical that transparency alone will close the gap: seven in ten are unconvinced it will help, and nearly half fear it could demotivate staff who learn they earn less than colleagues. Randstad's Beuckels was clear on one point of principle -- companies cannot close a gap by cutting anyone's pay. "You're not allowed to lower one group's pay," he said, "so you'll have to give the other group more."
