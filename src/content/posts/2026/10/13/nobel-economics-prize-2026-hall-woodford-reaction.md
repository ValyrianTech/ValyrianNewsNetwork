# Hall and Woodford Nobel: Economists Debate the Award

A day after Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford were named winners of the 2026 Nobel Economics Prize, economists are converging on a verdict that is admiring but notably measured: the award honours an entire intellectual tradition rather than a single breakthrough — one that quietly rewired how central banks and governments around the world try to steady their economies.

Hall, of Stanford University, and Woodford, of Columbia University, received the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel on 12 October for what the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences called ["microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation"](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2026/press-release/). The prize, worth 12 million Swedish kronor and shared equally, recognises work that rebuilt the theoretical scaffolding behind modern monetary and fiscal policy.

The citation is technical, but its practical meaning is straightforward: the two laureates helped explain how the decisions of individual households and firms shape the wider economy — and why the expectations people hold about the future can matter as much as the interest rates set today.

## Assessing a "Safe Choice"

Reaction from the University of Warsaw, [published by CEO Magazine Poland](https://ceo.com.pl/en/robert-hall-michael-woodford-nobel-economics-2026/), framed the prize as recognition of a broad research tradition founded on New Keynesian models. Paweł Kaczmarczyk, of the Faculty of Economic Sciences, called it "a very safe choice," reflecting the laureates' affiliations with leading American universities and their position firmly within an established tradition of contemporary economics.

He was candid about what the award does and does not settle. Woodford's work on expectations, he said, "has profoundly affected not only economic theory but also the everyday practice of institutions responsible for economic stability." Yet he added a note of caution: "We live in very uncertain times," and he remains "sceptical about whether we are actually succeeding" in finding stability.

Joanna Tyrowicz, a professor at the university's Faculty of Management, stressed that the two laureates represent distinct strands of macroeconomics. "The two laureates probably never co-authored a research paper," she noted, describing Woodford's approach as one of exceptional scientific precision — building complex models step by step from simple mechanisms.

## A Foundation Built from the Bottom Up

For most of the twentieth century, the dominant Keynesian models relied on stable statistical relationships between big economic variables such as inflation, unemployment and consumption. When the oil crises of the 1970s produced stagflation — rising prices alongside rising joblessness — those relationships broke down and stabilisation policy appeared to fail.

As the Academy recounts, Hall and Woodford responded by rebuilding macroeconomic models on a microeconomic foundation, tracing aggregate outcomes back to the choices of individual households and businesses. A central feature of their models is that these actors are forward-looking: what people expect governments and central banks to do changes their behaviour now.

"The laureates' work has provided us with a stable theoretical foundation for the models that are now used by central banks and governments around the world," said John Hassler, Chair of the Committee for the Prize in Economic Sciences. "With them, we are better equipped to deal with shocks in the economy, such as energy crises or sudden changes in how much households consume."

## Woodford and the Power of Expectations

Woodford's contribution centres on monetary policy. His 2003 book *Interest and Prices: Foundations of a Theory of Monetary Policy* laid out a complete New Keynesian model — built around three equations linking the output gap, inflation and the policy interest rate — that became the prototype for central-bank models worldwide.

His central insight, as Belgian public broadcaster [RTBF explained](https://www.rtbf.be/article/nobel-d-economie-pourquoi-les-americains-woodford-et-hall-ont-provoque-un-changement-de-paradigme-11799185), is that central banks do not fight inflation solely by moving interest rates. They also act by influencing the expectations of households, firms and investors. "What economic actors think the Fed or the ECB will do tomorrow can matter as much as the decisions taken today," the broadcaster noted, which helps explain why a single central-bank announcement can move markets before any rate actually changes.

The announcement itself produced a memorable moment. Woodford was woken by a call from Stockholm at 4:30 a.m., hours before he was due to set an economics exam for his Columbia students. "They're probably hoping I'll cancel the exam," [he joked](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2026/woodford/interview/), praising co-laureate Hall and expressing his admiration for the Swedish economist Knut Wicksell, whose concept of a natural rate of interest helped inspire his work.

## Hall and the Puzzle of Consumption

Hall's contributions are broader, spanning labour markets, technology and growth. But his most cited result, dating to 1978, transformed the study of household consumption. He argued that if consumers smooth spending over their lifetimes based on expected future income, then changes in consumption should be largely unpredictable — a random walk.

Follow-up work with Frederic Mishkin in 1982 using microdata found that roughly 80% of households behaved in this forward-looking way, while the remainder lived "hand-to-mouth," spending what they earned. The distinction helped explain why a temporary income boost does not necessarily translate into an equal rise in spending — a puzzle with direct implications for how stimulus measures are designed.

## Managing Expectations, Not Just Rates

Łukasz Goczek, a professor at the Faculty of Economic Sciences, put the core idea plainly: "Stabilising the economy largely involves managing public expectations." Because people form rational expectations about the future, he explained, any change in the economy also changes those expectations — which is precisely why economic forecasting remains so difficult.

He cautioned against reading the award as a promise that crises can be foreseen or prevented. "It is worth remembering that crises have not disappeared," he said, noting that recessions carry social costs that go beyond unemployment. The value of the laureates' work, in his telling, lies less in prediction than in illumination: it clarifies the mechanisms through which policy transmits to households and firms.

## Limits and Lingering Questions

Not every economist treats the New Keynesian framework as settled. Critics have argued that representative-agent models struggle to capture liquidity-constrained or risk-exposed households, and that they underrepresented banking, leverage and financial instability in the run-up to the 2008 crisis. Forward guidance, in the strongest formulations, proved less omnipotent than once hoped.

RTBF was careful to set expectations: the laureates' work "does not mean that crises can be avoided, nor that central banks and governments always know how to respond." It helps illuminate mechanisms and inform decisions, but cannot predict with certainty how households, firms or markets will react to a shock.

## What to Watch

The award lands at a moment when central banks from the Federal Reserve to the European Central Bank are again trying to steer expectations to contain energy-driven inflation without tipping their economies into recession — essentially the problem the laureates' models were built to address.

The prize also carries a quieter story. Unlike physics, chemistry or literature, the economics award was established by Sweden's central bank in 1969, not by Alfred Nobel's will. Its 2026 edition goes to two researchers whose influence is now woven into the daily practice of the institutions that keep modern economies on an even keel — an influence that, as the reaction from Warsaw suggests, is both deeply embedded and still being debated.
