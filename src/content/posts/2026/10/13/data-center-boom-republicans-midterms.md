# Data Center Boom Puts Republicans in a Bind

The rapid buildout of AI data centers across the United States has become an unexpected political flashpoint ahead of November's midterm elections, forcing incumbents in both parties to recalibrate as public opposition to the facilities intensifies. While the issue was initially framed as a problem for Republicans, new polling and grassroots organizing suggest the backlash now cuts across party lines — and could shape the outcome of several marquee races.

An [NBC News Decision Desk Poll](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/trump-data-centers-voters-key-midterm-races-rcna594914), powered by SurveyMonkey and conducted from Aug. 20 through Sept. 1, found that 69% of respondents oppose the construction of AI data centers in their local area, with 45% saying they "strongly oppose" the idea. Opposition is broad: 57% of Republicans, 81% of Democrats and 71% of independents are against new facilities in their communities.

## Why the Issue Cuts Across Party Lines

The data center debate is no longer a simple partisan story. As [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/21/nx-s1-5927288/data-center-election-voters-ai), the issue has become a fault line that is "cutting across political parties, splitting constituencies and forging unexpected alliances."

That dynamic is visible at the local level. Dawn Vogel, a Republican county supervisor in rural Buchanan County, Iowa, voted for a moratorium on data centers in her heavily Republican county even as she acknowledged her unlikely alignment with the leading democratic socialists in Congress.

"I don't think this is a Republican or Democratic issue," Vogel told NBC News, adding that she hasn't talked to a single voter at home who wants data centers. "It's an issue of what's the best use for the land, what's the best use for our natural resources."

At the grassroots level, NPR found that the [Data Center Opposition Report](https://datacenteropposition.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/August-2026-DCOR1.pdf) tracks some 640,000 people active in the anti-data-center movement, with 220 new groups forming in just the three months from June through August. The movement has produced a rare coalition: Nusheen Farahani, a Loudoun County, Virginia, resident who describes herself as a liberal Democrat, noted that "not all of them are liberals or Democrats" in the group with which she organizes.

## The Scale of the Buildout

The political stakes are heightened by the sheer scale of construction. According to [POLITICO's analysis](https://www.businessinsider.com/the-data-center-boom-is-colliding-with-the-midterms-2026-6), 40 of the 69 competitive House districts have data centers either planned or under construction. Nationwide, more than 1,500 facilities are planned or being built across 232 congressional districts, with a nearly even partisan split.

More than 2,500 U.S. data center facilities already operate across 373 congressional districts. More than one in three Americans live within 5 miles of an operating data center, and in five states, most residents do.

That footprint is driving a bipartisan scramble. POLITICO found that even incumbents most supportive of data centers now caveat their support with the need to protect consumers — a reflection, strategists say, of voters' unwillingness to shoulder any cost increases.

"They're between a rock and a hard place," Texas-based GOP consultant Brendan Steinhauser told POLITICO. "Politically, it's not a very smart move to come out and be seen as too close to big tech or doing the bidding of Big Tech, but a lot of the money is flying to them through that."

## The Ohio Senate Race as a Test Case

The clearest test of the issue's political potency is in Ohio, where Republican Sen. Jon Husted — a former champion of the industry — is locked in a competitive race against Democratic former Sen. Sherrod Brown. A Marist Poll found Husted trailing by eight points.

The [Daily Caller News Foundation reported](https://dailycaller.com/2026/10/05/data-centers-2026-midterms/) that the National Republican Senatorial Committee warned AI companies in an August memo that "data centers are the anchor hanging around Husted's neck." Husted has sought to respond by authoring the Ratepayer Protection Act, which would require data center developers to cover the energy costs associated with their facilities. The bill passed the House 417-3 in mid-September but was blocked in the Senate by Democrats 57-43.

Brown has branded Husted "the face of data centers," while his campaign argues that state residents have been forced to subsidize the electricity costs of large corporations. Husted counters that his legislation is a commonsense solution. "I am responding to what the voters want: They want the data centers to be held accountable for the costs of new electricity and infrastructure," he told POLITICO.

Similar recalibrations are underway elsewhere. POLITICO reported that Michigan Republican Senate candidate Mike Rogers called for a one-year moratorium on new data centers, while Democratic candidate Abdul El-Sayed has proposed a "Terms of Engagement" platform. In Pennsylvania, Gov. Josh Shapiro — once a booster of the industry, who touted a $20 billion Amazon commitment — signed an executive order his office described as the "strictest guardrails in the nation" on data center development.

## The Economics: Boon and Burden

The tension reflects competing realities. Data centers have generated billions of dollars in private investment, construction jobs and tax revenue. Loudoun County, Virginia, alone generated about $1.2 billion in property tax revenue in fiscal year 2026, roughly 39% of its overall budget, and has reduced its real property tax rate by about 30% over a decade.

"We've seen property taxes go down two years in a row," Dan Diorio of the Data Center Coalition told NPR. "That's money directly back into people's pockets made possible by the revenue from the data center industry."

But critics point to the environmental and infrastructure toll. NPR noted that variable issues — including substantial electricity demand and water consumption for cooling — are particularly sensitive in areas facing population growth or drought. A New Jersey data center spilled roughly a tanker truck's worth of diesel into a creek in September, and a Georgia facility consumed more than 29 million gallons of water.

## Secrecy Fuels Distrust

Researchers and advocates say a key driver of the backlash is opacity. A study led by sociologist Eric Bonds at the University of Mary Washington found that nondisclosure agreements had been signed in 25 of 31 Virginia localities with an existing, approved or proposed data center.

"Fundamentally, I believe this comes down to an issue of people feeling a loss of control over their future, over their community, to Big Tech," Chris MacKenzie of Americans for Responsible Innovation told the DCNF.

Vantage Data Centers' John Stephenson acknowledged the industry's missteps to NPR, saying that in hindsight it would have been better to engage "earlier and more proactively" with residents on issues such as noise and air quality.

## What to Watch

The issue's trajectory will depend in part on whether the message resonates beyond the most affected communities. A September New York Times/Siena poll found voters nearly split on whether they trust Republicans (42%) or Democrats (40%) more on AI, and the DCNF reported that an internal GOP poll found more than 60% of key voter blocs would support data centers if developers met local requirements — paying for their own energy, avoiding rate increases and funding infrastructure.

But all of the congressional and gubernatorial ads mentioning data centers this cycle, as tracked by AdImpact, are critical of the facilities. As Lehigh University political scientist Chris Borick told NPR, "If you're in power right now and you are dealing with this issue, you have a target on your back."

With just weeks until the midterms, the data center boom has become a referendum on who benefits from the AI economy — and who pays for it.
