# Chinese Language Fever Sweeps Central Asia

A new feature report from Xinhua correspondents documents a surge in Mandarin Chinese learning across Central Asia, where the language is moving from the classroom into daily life. According to the [People's Daily Overseas Edition](http://world.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1013/c1002-40813576.html), bilingual ticket machines, ride-hailing drivers greeting passengers in Chinese, and Chinese-language restaurant menus are becoming common sights from Almaty to Tashkent — signs, the report argues, of deepening cultural and economic ties between China and the region.

Chinese is "visibly embedding itself into Central Asian society and entering the daily lives of local people," the report states, describing a "Chinese fever" driven by a mix of trade connectivity, civilizational exchange, and policy facilitation.

## Confucius Institutes Report Overflowing Demand

State-supported Confucius Institutes remain the primary channel for Chinese instruction, and several report demand outstripping capacity.

At the Confucius Institute of Al-Farabi Kazakh National University in Almaty — co-established with China's Lanzhou University — Chinese director Deng Hong said the local learning atmosphere "has kept heating up, with students ranging from age 7 to 70." The institute serves as a global test site for the HSK Chinese Proficiency Test, handling more than 2,000 test-takers annually, with applications rising year on year.

The picture is similar in Tajikistan. Sun Jing, Chinese director of the Confucius Institute at Tajik National University, said the institute enrolls two cohorts a year of more than 1,000 students each — but every intake receives more than 1,500 applicants. "Some parents travel long distances to bring their children to learn Chinese," she said.

## Private Tutoring Fills the Gap

Demand is spilling well beyond state-run institutes into a fast-growing private market. In Astana, a search for "Chinese courses" on a lifestyle services platform returned 136 Chinese-language training institutions, while other large cities host dozens to more than a hundred each.

At the "Teacher" Education Center in Almaty, a private school run by Tatyana Kokenova, children, university students, and working professionals fill the same classrooms. "As Kazakhstan–China trade, logistics, and technology cooperation keeps deepening, the number of locals proactively learning Chinese grows year by year," Kokenova said.

Private tutoring is emerging as a trend in its own right. Roza, 27, a graduate of the Al-Farabi Confucius Institute, now teaches 15 teenagers. "Chinese culture is fascinating; Chinese has opened a window for us to observe China," she said.

## Economic Demand Drives the Trend

Analysts tie the boom directly to labor-market realities. Gulnar Shaimergenova, director of the Kazakhstan China Research Center, said China's lead in high-tech sectors such as the digital economy has made bilingual talent in fields like artificial intelligence and fintech increasingly sought-after — fueling demand for Chinese study.

Individual stories reflect the same calculation. Ayim Noryzbayeva, a 26-year-old logistics worker in Almaty who enrolled in September 2026, told the reporters she hopes to study in China next year and return to work in the Kazakhstan–China cooperation field.

## Visa Liberalization as a Catalyst

The report also points to recent policy changes as a spur. Since August 20, 2026, Kyrgyz citizens can enter China under the 240-hour transit visa-free policy and the Hainan 30-day visa-free policy. According to the [International Travel Health Consultation Network](https://m.ithc.cn/article/475730), the same date added Kyrgyzstan and Vietnam to both programs, bringing the transit visa-free total to 57 countries and the Hainan policy to 61.

Baikazak Sultanbekov, a Kyrgyz student at Jalal-Abad State University who has studied Chinese for more than two years, welcomed the news. "I was especially happy to hear it; my wish to see the sea in Hainan is getting closer," he said.

Among younger learners, 17-year-old Muslima, studying at the Confucius Institute of Tashkent State University of Oriental Studies in Uzbekistan, said a 2025 study trip to Xi'an sparked her deep interest in Chinese culture. "I really want to see more of China," she said.

## What to Watch

The trend aligns with a broader diplomatic push. The China–Central Asia summit mechanism and the designation of 2025–2026 as the China–Central Asia years of cooperation provide the framework within which language learning is expanding. Whether the surge sustains itself will likely depend on whether the economic promise driving it — bilingual jobs in trade, logistics, and technology — continues to materialize. For now, the report concludes, language is carrying "the warmth of civilizations meeting," making the China–Central Asia partnership "vivid and tangible" in every recitation and conversation.
