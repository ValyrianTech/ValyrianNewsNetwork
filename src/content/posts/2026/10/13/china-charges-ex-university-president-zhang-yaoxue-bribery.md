# China Charges Ex-University President Zhang Yaoxue

Chinese prosecutors have formally indicted Zhang Yaoxue, the former president of Central South University and a onetime academician of the Chinese Academy of Engineering, on charges of accepting bribes—the latest high-profile case in the country's campaign against corruption in its academic and higher-education institutions.

The Supreme People's Procuratorate announced on 13 October that the case had been investigated by the National Supervisory Commission, transferred to prosecutors, and that public charges had now been filed with the Anqing Intermediate People's Court in Anhui Province, according to [Xinhua, carried by People's Daily](http://society.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1013/c1008-40813709.html).

## The Charges

Prosecutors allege that Zhang exploited a succession of senior posts to seek benefits for others while illegally accepting property "in especially huge amounts," the indictment states. The positions cited span his two careers: deputy director and then director of the Ministry of Education's Department of Science and Technology, director of its Department of Higher Education, director of its Department of Degree Management and Graduate Education, and finally Party Standing Committee member and president of Central South University.

The Supreme People's Procuratorate issued a bribery arrest decision and designated the Anqing Municipal People's Procuratorate to review the case, which it has now forwarded for trial. The [Procuratorial Daily / Justice Network](https://www.jcrb.com/livenews/2026/202610/t20261013_1029955.html) reported the indictment in full, noting that prosecutors informed the defendant of his procedural rights, questioned him, and heard his defense counsel.

## A Case Rooted in Academic Honors

What distinguishes Zhang's case from a routine graft prosecution is the specific conduct investigators highlighted. When the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection and National Supervisory Commission announced in May 2026 that he had been expelled from the Communist Party and dismissed from public office, their findings focused on alleged abuses connected to the conferral of academic standing.

According to the disciplinary notice, Zhang "sought benefits for others and accepted property" in cadre selection and appointment, the conferral of academic titles, and honorary commendations—conduct the notice said "abetted unhealthy tendencies and corruption in the academic field." The notice also accused him of violating the Party's Eight-Point Regulation by improperly accepting banquets, failing to truthfully answer organizational inquiries, and "treating public power as a tool for private gain," including by helping others with business operations and job promotions. It stated that his conduct "did not stop after the 18th Party Congress"—a reference to the 2012 leadership transition that has become a benchmark for judging whether officials reformed after the anti-corruption campaign intensified.

Zhang, born in 1956 in Hunan Province, built a prominent career as a computer network scientist. He studied in Japan at Tohoku University, taught at Tsinghua University in the 1990s, and rose through the Ministry of Education before being appointed president of Central South University in 2011. He was elected an academician of the Chinese Academy of Engineering in 2007. On 25 May 2026, the academy [announced](https://www.cae.cn/cae/html/main/col4/2026-05/25/20260525140403381526806_1.html) that it had revoked his academician title effective 21 May—the date of his expulsion.

## Timeline of a Fall

The case unfolded over roughly a year. On 17 October 2025, the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection's website [announced](http://www.news.cn/politics/20251017/55f8e39046384b0394ce18fb3457b21f/c.html) that Zhang was under disciplinary review and supervisory investigation for suspected serious violations of discipline and law. Two months later, in late December 2025, Central South University vice president Guo Xueyi was also placed under investigation. In May 2026, Zhang was expelled from the Party and stripped of public office. By July 2026, [Caixin](https://china.caixin.com/2026-07-23/102467277.html) reported that Guo had been transferred to judicial authorities on suspicion of three crimes—suggesting a broader sweep touching the university's leadership.

## Why It Matters

Zhang is among the most senior figures in Chinese academia to face criminal prosecution in recent years, and his case underscores a deliberate turn in the anti-corruption campaign toward the education and research sectors. The disciplinary findings treated the exchange of money for academic titles and honors as a distinctive harm—one that corrodes the credibility of a system meant to reward merit.

That theme resonates in China's scientific community, where questions about the integrity of prestigious awards have simmered for years. Zhang's own record was already contentious: his "transparent computing" project won the 2014 National Natural Science Award, First Prize, a decision that drew widespread criticism from computing researchers. As [Chinese Wikipedia's](https://zh.wikipedia.org/zh-hans/张尧学) account of the episode notes, the China Computer Federation published—then deleted—a proposal urging the government to withdraw from reviewing national science and technology awards, and in 2015 an IT engineer alleged on GitHub that the award-winning demonstration was based on existing open-source remote-desktop code. Zhang denied plagiarism, saying the demonstration was a separately developed system, and his team said open-source code had been used lawfully under its license. The controversy left lasting public skepticism about how academic honors are awarded.

There is also a procedural signal in where the case is being heard. Caixin observed that three recent prosecutions—those of Chen Weijun, Pan Liang, and now Zhang Yaoxue—were all assigned to courts in Anhui, outside the defendants' home jurisdictions, a pattern that suggests a deliberate effort to insulate sensitive cases from local influence.

## What to Watch

The Anqing Intermediate People's Court has yet to schedule a trial date, and no sentencing has been announced. Beyond Zhang's own fate, observers will be watching whether the case yields further prosecutions at Central South University, following Guo Xueyi's transfer to judicial authorities. More broadly, the case will test whether the anti-corruption drive in academia produces durable changes in how universities and national bodies confer titles, honors, and senior appointments—or remains, for now, a matter of individual accountability.
