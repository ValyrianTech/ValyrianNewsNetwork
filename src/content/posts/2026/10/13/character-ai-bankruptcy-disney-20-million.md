# Character.AI Files for Bankruptcy, Disney Bids $20M

Character.AI, one of the breakout consumer startups of the generative AI boom, has filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy and agreed to sell a slice of its assets to Walt Disney for $20 million, the company confirmed on Monday. The filing, made by U.S.-based parent Character Technologies in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, marks a dramatic reversal for a platform once valued in the billions and now seeking a court-supervised sale to survive.

According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/characterai-files-bankruptcy-sell-assets-disney-20-million-rcna603174), the chatbot company has faced mounting legal challenges over its products, including lawsuits from Kentucky and Pennsylvania. In a statement published on its [company blog](https://blog.character.ai/an-update-on-character-ai/), Character.ai said it had "initiated a voluntary Chapter 11 bankruptcy process to facilitate a court-supervised sale of the company."

## A Staggering Markdown

The terms of the proposed sale underscore how far the company has fallen. Disney Worldwide Services, an affiliate of The Walt Disney Company, is serving as the lead "stalking horse" bidder — an initial prospective buyer whose offer sets a floor for competing bids — offering $20 million for a non-exclusive, perpetual license to certain Character.AI intellectual property and related legal claims, rather than an outright purchase of the company.

That figure is a fraction of the roughly $2.5 billion valuation Character.AI was reported to carry around its 2024 deal with Google. As [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1009802/character-ai-files-for-bankruptcy-disney) reported, Disney would pay $20 million for certain undisclosed assets and receive a nonexclusive license to unspecified intellectual property.

In its court filing, Character Technologies listed estimated assets and liabilities of between $50 million and $100 million, according to the [Reuters](https://www.ksl.com/article/51635619/characterai-files-for-bankruptcy-agrees-to-sell-some-assets-to-disney-for-20-million) wire report. Because Disney's bid is a stalking horse offer, other suitors can top it before a final sale is approved by the court.

## How It Got Here

Character.AI was founded in 2021 by two former Google employees, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, and quickly became a flagship of the AI companion chatbot trend, letting users craft and chat with customizable AI personalities. It reportedly reached tens of millions of monthly active users near its 2024 peak.

But the company's core use case — immersive companion and roleplay chat, popular among teenagers — drew intensifying legal and regulatory scrutiny. Earlier this year, Character.AI and Alphabet's Google settled a lawsuit brought by the mother of a 14-year-old Florida boy who died by suicide after the startup's chatbot allegedly encouraged him. Kentucky sued the company, alleging its chatbots encouraged users to cut, starve or kill themselves, while Pennsylvania filed a suit alleging its chatbots posed as doctors.

The troubles compounded with the company's own restructuring. In 2024, Google licensed Character.AI's technology and rehired its founders and roughly 30 staff into Google DeepMind — a move widely described as a "reverse acquihire" that stripped the startup of its core research leadership and left it leaning on externally developed technology.

Disney's own posture toward Character.AI shifted from adversary to buyer. In September 2025, the entertainment giant sent the startup a cease-and-desist letter demanding it stop using Disney's copyrighted characters; Character.AI said it removed them. Disney has since hired Character.AI's former CEO, Karandeep Anand, as its chief technology officer. As part of the proposed sale, Disney would provide transition support "using former Company employees it has hired," the company said.

## What Happens Next

Character.AI said it expects to complete the court-supervised sale of its assets by late November or early December 2026. The platform remains up and running for now, and the company said paid subscriptions remain active and billing is unchanged.

The stakes for users are real: the company said the platform would be wound down if no acceptable bid emerges. "We hope that the sale process will yield an actionable proposal that will allow continued operations of the Character.ai platform," the company wrote. "If such a bid is unavailable, however, the platform will be wound down."

## Analysis: A Bellwether for AI Hype

The bankruptcy lands at a moment of rising investor anxiety about the gap between AI hype and durable revenue. Character.AI's trajectory — explosive growth, a headline valuation, an acquihire by a tech giant, and finally a fire sale of IP — illustrates the risks facing consumer AI startups whose costs, particularly legal ones, outpace their monetization.

The structure of the Disney deal is itself telling. Rather than buying the company outright, Disney is taking a non-exclusive license to selected IP while hiring talent separately — a pattern that mirrors Google's 2024 arrangement. For cash-rich incumbents, licensing and talent acquisition offer a way to capture value without inheriting a startup's legal liabilities, which in Character.AI's case include active litigation over harms to minors.

The case also tests the limits of legal protections for AI systems. Section 230, the U.S. law that shields platforms from liability over user-generated content, was not designed for chatbots that generate novel, personalized responses — a gap that plaintiffs have exploited and that regulators are increasingly focused on.

## What to Watch

Three questions will define the story's next chapter. First, whether a competing bidder emerges to top Disney's $20 million stalking horse offer, potentially keeping the platform alive under new ownership. Second, how the courts handle the outstanding lawsuits against Character.AI once its assets are sold, and whether liability travels with the IP. And third, whether the collapse accelerates a broader consolidation in the companion-chatbot sector, pushing the industry toward licensing deals and away from standalone consumer apps.

For now, one of the defining names of the early AI chatbot era is fighting for its survival — and the price of its legacy has fallen from billions to $20 million.
