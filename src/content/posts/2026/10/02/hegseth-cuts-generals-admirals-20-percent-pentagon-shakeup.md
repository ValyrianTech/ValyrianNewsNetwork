# Hegseth Cuts Generals, Admirals by 20% in Pentagon Shake-Up

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced Wednesday that the Pentagon is eliminating roughly one in five of its generals and admirals, removing senior officers he accused of clinging to what he calls a "woke department," in one of the most sweeping shake-ups of the U.S. military's leadership in recent memory. Speaking at Marine Corps Base Quantico, Virginia, Hegseth framed the restructuring as "accountability, and long overdue," while critics labeled it a political purge.

## The 20% Reduction

According to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/hegseth-20-cut-generals-and-admirals-military-pentagon/), Hegseth said his goal was for the department to be "unrelenting, unapologetic, and uncompromising," adding that generals who "clung to the woke department or seem to think civilian control of the military is simply a suggestion, well, they no longer work here."

He detailed the math behind the figure: roughly 10 percent of generals and admirals have been fired, and an additional 10 percent of general officer positions have been eliminated. "So yes, 20% reduction across the board," he said. "Now the media calls that a purge. I call it accountability, and long overdue."

The reductions follow a May 2025 directive in which Hegseth ordered a minimum 10 percent cut to all flag and general officers. As of July, there were 851 active-duty generals and admirals across the Army, Navy, Marine Corps, Air Force, and Space Force, according to a [Defense Department tally](https://dwp.dmdc.osd.mil/dwp/app/dod-data-reports/workforce-reports) — up slightly from 848 a year earlier, and near the federal legal cap of 857.

## What the Cuts Actually Change

An important nuance separates the headline number from what will happen on the ground. As [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/exclusive-hegseth-double-cuts-generals-admirals-major-pentagon-shakeup) reported, the reductions apply to billets — positions designated for generals and admirals — rather than an across-the-board removal of 20 percent of the officers currently serving in those ranks, according to a department source. Some positions will be downgraded rather than eliminated, allowing colonels and Navy captains to fill roles that previously required a star, and services have been directed to complete the reductions by Jan. 1, 2027.

Hegseth has described the effort as "less generals, more GIs," arguing that trimming senior headquarters positions pushes resources and authority down the chain of command. When he first announced the earlier reductions, he insisted the process was not punitive: "This is not a slash-and-burn exercise meant to punish high-ranking officers," he said in May 2025.

## New Commands and Initiatives

The speech, billed as a "State of the Force Address," was not limited to personnel cuts. As the [Associated Press](https://www.wral.com/news/ap/67a20-hegseth-confirms-that-the-us-military-will-cut-20-of-its-generals-and-admirals/) reported, Hegseth announced the creation of a new four-star combatant command focused on autonomous and robotic warfare — what he called "the fastest peacetime shift in modern military history."

The Pentagon chief acknowledged that "the pace of war is changing faster than the process to support it," and pointed to Ukraine's pioneering use of drones as a model. "Ukraine's lesson is not that one type of drone wins wars, but rather the need for a purpose-built system that maximizes the ability to adapt," he said. The announcement comes as the U.S. remains in a stalemate with Iran over the Strait of Hormuz, seven months into a war in which Tehran has repeatedly used cheap drones to strike at American and allied forces.

Hegseth also unveiled "Project Meridian," a 120-day study of the future of war that will be led by SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, former House Speaker Newt Gingrich, and Palmer Luckey, founder of the defense technology firm Anduril. He described the work as focused on "discovering, developing and fielding the weapons and systems that our children and our grandchildren will need," and called for dominating "any future battlefield, from under the Earth to beyond the moon."

Beyond those plans, Hegseth said the Pentagon will build a new military base for the first time in 40 years, inviting all 50 states to compete to host between 15,000 and 25,000 service members, and said every military base would be made self-powered.

## A Culture War at the Top

The staffing changes sit atop a broader ideological project. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/sep/30/hegseth-military-personnel-cuts) reported, Hegseth used the address to escalate his attacks on diversity programs, declaring, "We are no longer the woke department or the weak department. Simple translation of that: no fatties, no trannies, no beardos, no weirdos, no wimps, no radicals. Just warriors."

He also took aim at elite universities, branding Ivy League and liberal arts institutions "DEI-worshipping woke factories," and announced partnerships with five colleges — Louisiana State University, Liberty University, Tuskegee University, Mississippi State University, and Hillsdale College — to establish cadet corps that would give students priority in officer commissioning. Notably, Hegseth is himself a Harvard and Princeton graduate.

The defense secretary dismissed reports of low morale in the ranks as "disingenuous headlines" and "patently false claims." His remarks come as eight Navy personnel assigned to the aircraft carrier USS Abraham Lincoln attempted suicide during a deployment that stretched to 286 days before the crew disembarked in Thailand in September. Hegseth said about "80% of the force" aboard was retained.

At least 20 generals, admirals, and civilian military leaders have been fired or resigned since Hegseth took the Pentagon's helm, including the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the Army, Navy, and Air Force's top uniformed officers.

## Mounting Questions

Critics say the scale of the turnover risks hollowing out the military's most experienced leadership. "He's purging a generation of great leaders of the military, and it's going to take a generation to recover from it," said Sen. Mark Kelly, D-Ariz., a former Navy combat pilot.

Some Republicans have signaled they want more evidence before endorsing the cuts. Sen. Mike Rounds of South Dakota, a member of the Senate Armed Services Committee, said: "If it's simply a matter of eliminating a very heavy top, there may be some validity to it, but I want to see it first."

The debate is not without merit: a Congressional Research Service report notes disagreement over whether the military has too many senior officers, with critics arguing that bloated command structures slow decision-making, and supporters countering that senior leaders are essential for managing coalitions and an increasingly complex department.

## What to Watch

The reductions are expected to be completed by Jan. 1, 2027, and their practical effect will depend on how many billets are downgraded versus eliminated. Key questions remain: whether Congress will push back on the pace of the drawdown, how the new drone command will be funded and staffed amid heavy weapons-stockpile demands from the Iran war, and whether the turnover at the top affects readiness and retention. For a military already stretched across multiple theaters, the coming year will test whether fewer generals means a leaner force — or a more brittle one.
