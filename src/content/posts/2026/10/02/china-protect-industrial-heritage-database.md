# China Moves to Digitize, Protect Industrial Heritage

China will intensify the protection and reuse of its industrial heritage, establishing a national archive database and applying technologies such as 5G, the BeiDou satellite navigation system, virtual reality, and artificial intelligence to the task, a senior official from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) said this week.

The announcement, [reported by Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261002/8ba9d67b3de940ea84eb831988e36777/c.html) on Oct. 2, was made by Gao Dongsheng, MIIT's chief economist, at the 2026 Industrial Culture Development Conference in Shanghai. The measures form part of China's broader push to tie cultural heritage preservation to advanced manufacturing and high-quality urban renewal as the country enters its 15th Five-Year Plan period.

## Context: From Rust to Renewal

"Industrial culture" is a relatively recent concept in China, emerging as cities that grew up around factories, mines, and warehouses confront what to do with aging infrastructure. Rather than demolish shuttered plants, policymakers have increasingly sought to convert them into creative hotels, shopping districts, and cultural landmarks.

A [March 2026 Xinhua commentary](https://www.news.cn/comments/20260327/7ade2fa9fea146eea0e0f72364cce0b5/c.html) framed the trend as a shift away from "large-scale demolition and construction" toward finer-grained urban stewardship. It cited a former sugar mill in Yangshuo, Guangxi, converted into a creative hotel; a disused grain depot in Hefei turned into a shopping mall; and an old oil and chemical plant in Zhengzhou reimagined as a trendy lifestyle district.

The scale is significant. As of the end of 2025, China had recognized 264 national-level industrial heritage sites and about 500 provincial-level sites, according to the commentary. By the time of this week's conference, that provincial figure had grown to more than 600, Xinhua reported — evidence that recognition efforts are accelerating.

## Key Developments

Speaking at the conference, Gao said industrial culture's "innovative spirit, craftsmanship, and aesthetic concepts" are the "deep soil" that nourishes advanced manufacturing.

According to Xinhua, the ministry's plans include building a high-quality industrial heritage protection system, creating the National Industrial Heritage Archive Database, and advancing an "industrial culture digital engineering" initiative. The latter would deepen applications of 5G, BeiDou, virtual reality, and artificial intelligence in how heritage is documented, presented, and used.

Gao added that MIIT would weave "cultural empowerment" through every link of the manufacturing chain, vigorously develop the industrial design industry, and explore new scenarios for political education, science popularization, and cultural-tourism consumption. The ministry also intends to launch a campaign to shape and communicate China's modern industrial image, supporting industrial-themed film, television, and cultural productions.

The broader policy framework is multi-ministerial. On May 25, 2026, seven central ministries and agencies — including the Ministry of Culture and Tourism, the Central Propaganda Department, MIIT, the Ministry of Education, the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission, the National Cultural Heritage Administration, and the All-China Federation of Trade Unions — issued a [joint notice](https://www.wlmq.gov.cn/wlmqs/c119292/202606/1d5a084c70304af0865a48a235a1c0ba.shtml) on promoting industrial culture, protecting industrial heritage, and developing industrial tourism.

That notice calls for registering and archiving heritage sites, building a tiered and categorized resource database, systematically protecting sites, incorporating qualifying heritage into the cultural relic protection system, and conducting dynamic monitoring and benefit evaluation. It also encourages converting former industrial sites into tourism destinations and developing "factory tours" and immersive, technology-enabled experiences.

At the Shanghai conference, officials released a first batch of "Industrial Culture Starry Sky" placement sites, published a Guide to Brand Culture Construction for Industrial Enterprises, and launched an industrial research-study map, an AI-focused design competition, and a public lecture campaign, the Economic Daily reported. China News Service noted that the event drew more than 400 participants and ran in parallel with the 2026 World Design Capital Conference.

## Data and Scale

Fresh figures shared at the conference illustrate how far the recognition effort has progressed. As [China News Service](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/cj/2026/09-29/10705824.shtml) reported, 817 industrial heritage resources had been designated as heritage sites at various levels by the end of 2025, and 537 had been incorporated into cultural relic protection systems. A heritage protection network spanning 31 provinces, regions, and municipalities is taking shape.

Shanghai has positioned itself at the center of the movement. Zhang Ying, a deputy secretary-general of the Shanghai municipal government, described the city as the birthplace of China's modern industry and said it treats industrial culture construction as a lever for "carrying forward the industrial lineage and empowering industrial upgrading," according to the [Economic Daily](https://www.jingjiribao.cn/static/detail.jsp?id=686777). Shanghai issued an "Industrial Museum Coordinated Development Initiative" in 2025 and this year embedded the conference into the World Design Capital program through ministry-city cooperation.

## Analysis and Implications

The announcement is best understood as policy signaling rather than a binding new regulation. Its concrete deliverables are institutional: a centralized archive database, standardized protection criteria, and a slate of promotional and educational campaigns.

Three themes stand out. First, the emphasis on a national archive database reflects a shift toward centralized, systematic record-keeping — a move that could bring consistency to a scattered landscape of local designations. Second, the marriage of heritage policy with advanced technologies such as BeiDou, 5G, and VR aligns cultural preservation with China's broader industrial and technological self-reliance agenda, turning heritage into a proving ground for domestic tech. Third, the multi-ministry framework — spanning culture, education, industry, and labor — signals that industrial heritage is now treated as a cross-cutting concern rather than a niche cultural matter.

The economic rationale is equally clear. Reused industrial sites generate tangible returns through tourism, creative industries, and commercial real estate, while officials argue that the craftsmanship and innovation embedded in industrial culture can strengthen manufacturing brands. The March commentary described the shift as one from "incremental expansion" to "stock renewal," positioning heritage reuse as an engine of high-quality urban development.

## What's Next

Much will depend on implementation. The archive database, once operational, will reveal how many sites exist, their condition, and their protection status — data that could sharpen future policy. The digital engineering initiative will test whether technologies like VR and AI can meaningfully deepen public engagement with industrial history rather than serve as novelty overlays.

Key questions remain open: how funding will be divided among central and local governments, how aggressively sites will be protected versus developed, and whether the tension between preservation and commercialization can be managed. For now, the signal from Shanghai is unambiguous — China intends to treat its industrial past as an asset in its industrial future.
