# Flemish Authors Demand Labeling as AI Books Flood Market

The Flemish Authors Association (Vlaamse Auteursvereniging) is calling for mandatory labeling of AI-generated books, warning that thousands of machine-written titles are reaching readers through Belgian webshops without any disclosure. The appeal follows a Bruges bookseller's alarm over an AI publisher that registered hundreds of titles at once on a distribution platform, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/16/ai-boeken-steeds-meer-echte-auteurs-markt-andries-waries-standaa/).

## Context: A Growing Flood of Machine-Written Titles

The concern is not new. Earlier this year, VRT NWS [reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/05/15/duizenden-ai-boeken-te-koop-zonder-waarschuwing-mensen-staan-e/) that thousands of AI-generated books were on sale on Belgian webshops — including Bol.com, Standaard Boekhandel and Amazon — without clear warnings to buyers. Dutch newspaper Trouw had exposed that Andries B.V., the company behind much of the output, was producing roughly 10 books a day, each selling for about €17.

The trigger for the latest appeal came in late August 2026, when Thomas Barbier, a bookseller at De Reyghere in Bruges, noticed an AI-book company registering hundreds of titles at once across both fiction and non-fiction. In a single day, the company published 200 fiction titles, with more than 500 scheduled for the following week.

"That means an enormous deregulation … and thus a concrete 'drowning' of the book market," Barbier said, according to VRT NWS.

The company in question, Andries B.V., now publishes through a new entity, Waries B.V., led by Andries Herremans. Its books are sold via Centraal Boekhuis — the platform handling contact between publishers and booksellers — and delivered through print-on-demand. By early September, Bol.com listed 2,804 AI books from Waries B.V. and 1,048 from Andries B.V.

## The Transparency Gap

The core problem is structural. While some Waries titles carry a small disclosure note under the barcode and inside the cover stating that AI was used for content and design, that information does not travel through the distribution chain. Centraal Boekhuis has no field to attach an AI label to a book.

"I often don't even know myself whether it's AI," Barbier said. "How can I then guarantee its quality?"

Matthijs de Ridder, chairman of the Flemish Authors Association, framed the demand as a matter of informed choice rather than opposition to the technology. "Everyone is of course free to choose whether or not to read AI," he said. "We only ask that they know *that* they are reading an AI book."

He warned of a dual threat: unverified quality and the crowding out of human-authored work. "The danger is that the quality of AI books is not guaranteed at the moment. And the problem is that quality books will have a harder time if thousands of books end up on webshops."

## Industry Divided, but Seeking Solutions

Booksellers and publishers are responding in different ways. Standaard Boekhandel's marketing director Karen Vanstaen acknowledged that the sector is still finding its footing: "Various parties are looking for solutions for possible AI labels. Because at the moment everyone is sailing somewhat blind."

Some have drawn firmer lines. Brussels bookshop PassaPorta refuses to stock fully AI-made books, with manager Ludovic Bekaert noting the shop has spent 20 years curating its selection and can only grow more important in that role. Publisher Lannoo likewise does not publish AI-generated books. "Authors are the engine of their work, through their craftsmanship, authority and the soul they put into it," said publishing director Maarten Van Steenbergen.

At the other end, Waries B.V. defends its model, arguing that AI "makes books possible that would otherwise not appear because the target group is often too small for the work an author must put in" — framing its output as serving niche audiences rather than displacing established authors.

The scale of ambition behind the trend was underscored by Olivier Fuchs of Meta4Books, which assigns ISBN numbers for Flanders and Brussels. He said a start-up focused on AI books recently approached the agency about buying hundreds of thousands to more than a million ISBN numbers. "That was not taken up," he said.

## Regulatory Vacuum

The push for labeling runs into a legal gap. A new phase of the EU AI Act took effect on 2 August 2026, introducing transparency obligations, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/07/29/geen-toezichthouder-wel-al-strengere-regels-hoe-ver-staat-belig/). But Belgium — like the Netherlands — had not yet appointed an official AI supervisor. The BIPT is earmarked for the role but requires a parliamentary law, leaving a legal vacuum in which complaints can only be pursued through the courts.

In practice, that means mandatory labeling of AI books is not currently enforceable. No reliable figures exist on how many AI books circulate versus human-written ones; even the Royal Library (KBR), which archives all Belgian publications, cannot provide a number, partly because of ambiguous in-between categories where authors are only partly assisted by AI.

The BoekenOverleg, a sector consultation body, has pushed for an EU-wide label alongside a European levy on AI firms' use of copyrighted texts and a ban on copyright for fully or partly AI-generated works.

## What to Watch

The debate over AI books mirrors a broader struggle across creative industries to keep pace with generative tools. The immediate question is whether Belgium will formally empower the BIPT as AI supervisor — and how quickly a labeling standard, whether national or European, can move from consultation to enforceable rule.

Until then, the burden falls on booksellers and readers to detect what the distribution chain will not disclose. For the Flemish Authors Association, that is precisely the problem: a market where the distinction between human craft and machine output is invisible at the point of sale.
