# High Fens Restoration Begins with €20M Walloon Investment

Work to restore the High Fens (Hoge Venen / Hautes-Fagnes), the peatland and heathland reserve scarred by the largest wildfire in Belgian history, has officially begun, with the Walloon Region committing €20 million over four years to the effort. The plan, detailed at a ministerial working meeting on Friday, splits the funding between on-the-ground restoration, region-wide fire prevention and tourism support — but officials and scientists warn that full ecological recovery will take far longer than the money's four-year horizon.

## How the money is spent

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/16/restauratie-van-hoge-venen-kan-van-start-gaan/), roughly €3 million a year for four years — €12 million in total — will go directly to restoration works in the High Fens. A further €1.5 million annually (€6 million over the period) is earmarked for fire-prevention measures in nature areas and forests across the entire Walloon Region, not just the Fens. A final €0.5 million is set aside to support tourism. Together, the three strands make up the €20 million invested over four years.

The structure reveals a deliberate shift in emphasis. By directing nearly a third of the total budget to prevention across all of Wallonia, the regional government is signalling that the August disaster has prompted a broader rethink of how it manages fire risk in its natural landscapes — moving from reactive firefighting toward anticipation.

## A fire unlike 2011

The August blaze broke out around 14:00 on Friday 14 August 2026 in the High Fens and spread rapidly through dry, windy conditions, forcing the partial evacuation of the villages of Sourbrodt and Bütgenbach. By 19 August, roughly 3,000 to 3,300 hectares had burned, according to varying estimates — making it the largest wildfire in Belgium since 1911, when some 3,900 hectares burned, part of it on what is now German territory.

What set this fire apart was not only its scale but its depth. Writing as the fire still smouldered, VRT NWS [reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/15/hoge-venen-herstel-lang-duren-biodiversiteit-natuur/) that the flames had penetrated the dried surface peat itself — a far more damaging outcome than in 2011. Prof. Kris Verheyen, a forest ecology and management expert at Ghent University, explained the difference: the 2011 fire struck in spring, when the soil was still wet and the flames could not bite into the ground. In August, during exceptional drought, the peat itself became fuel.

That matters because peat is extraordinarily slow to form. Verheyen noted that living high peat grows at an average of about one millimetre per year. "The rebuilding of that peat can take decades and perhaps even centuries," he said — a sobering counterpoint to any expectation of a quick green recovery.

## Six workstreams, one long road

The restoration is organised around six working groups. The first handles operational management and coordination. The second, under a scientific committee, is tasked with mapping the state of habitats, species, soil and water both before and after the fire — establishing the baseline against which recovery will be measured. The remaining four cover restoration works including fire prevention, cartography, infrastructure design and dams, alongside economic development, tourism and heritage — including the management of archaeological remains discovered in the wake of the fire.

The discovery of archaeological material is an unexpected dimension of the project, adding a heritage-management workstream to what is fundamentally an ecological mission.

The working meeting near the High Fens brought together Walloon Nature Minister Anne-Catherine Dalcq, representatives of the Service Public de Wallonie (SPW), local authorities, scientists, businesses and tourism actors. Dalcq was candid about the timeline: "We will not be able to restore the peat plateau in a few months, but we are starting work today."

## Why the High Fens matter

The High Fens is a rare habitat in Western Europe: an ombrotrophic — rain-fed — high peat bog and heathland that hosts species such as the black grouse and functions as a "water battery," a sponge that holds rainfall and regulates the flow and chemistry of surrounding streams. It also stores substantial quantities of carbon; when it burns, the reserve flips from a carbon sink to a carbon source.

The fire occurred amid a Europe-wide pattern of severe wildfires during a hot, dry summer in 2026, a context that has sharpened attention on climate adaptation. According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/incendie-des-hautes-fagnes-quel-est-le-vrai-cout-de-l-impreparation-climatique-de-la-belgique-11784753), this summer's fires cost France, Greece and Spain alone an estimated €16–19 billion, and only about a quarter of EU climate-disaster losses are insured. The Belgian Federal Planning Bureau estimates that unmitigated climate change could cut Belgian GDP by 3 to 5 percent by 2050.

Wallonia has already begun translating those warnings into policy. When the regional government presented its first restoration roadmap on 27 August, it also adopted in principle a Walloon Climate Adaptation Strategy with a 2060 horizon, nine goals for 2040 and a first 2026–2030 plan of around 20 priority measures, as [QU4TRE](https://www.qu4tre.be/infos/politique/incendie-dans-les-fagnes-la-wallonie-tire-les-premiers-enseignements-de-la-crise/2016272) reported. Minister-President Adrien Dolimont framed the goal as being to "better anticipate risks so we can act more effectively when we face a new crisis."

## Open questions

Two threads remain unresolved. The first is accountability. The Liège prosecutor's office opened a judicial inquiry, and early findings reported by RTBF point to a possible agricultural machine as the cause.

The second concerns crisis communication: the regional government has itself flagged improvements needed in the 112 emergency and BE-Alert warning systems.

There is also a wider institutional backdrop. Belgium missed the 1 September 2026 deadline to submit its national nature-restoration plan to the EU, partly because of the country's complex division of competences between regions and the federal state — a reminder that in Belgium, environmental action often lives or dies at the regional level. It is the Walloon Region, not the federal government, that is the locus of this restoration.

## What to watch

The €20 million buys a process, a scientific baseline, prevention infrastructure and a coordinated plan — but not a healed landscape. The peat will keep growing at roughly a millimetre a year regardless of the budget line. The measure of success will be whether the investment can slow the damage, protect what remains, and prevent the next fire from reaching the peat at all. As Dalcq put it, the work starts now; the recovery will be measured in decades.
