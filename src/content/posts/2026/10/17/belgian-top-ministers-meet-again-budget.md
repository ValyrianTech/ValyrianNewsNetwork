# Belgian Top Ministers Meet Again on Stalled Budget

Belgium's federal top ministers reconvened in Brussels on Saturday morning in a fresh attempt to break a deadlock over the national budget, but none was willing to speak to the press on arrival. Prime Minister Bart De Wever arrived by car and, like his deputy premiers, kept silent as the so-called *kernkabinet* gathered for a meeting scheduled to begin at 10:30 a.m., [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/federale-topministers-zitten-weer-samen-over-begroting-en-hullen~1790599460224/).

The talks resume after negotiations collapsed the previous weekend following a session of roughly three hours. The government's central fiscal challenge is to improve Belgium's deficit by at least €10 billion by 2029 — a target anchored in EU fiscal obligations and the need to keep one of the eurozone's most heavily indebted states from sliding further.

## A Deadlock Over VAT

The main obstacle remains a proposed VAT reform, which would raise the standard rate from 21% to 22% and merge the reduced 6% and 12% rates into a single 9% rate. MR leader Georges-Louis Bouchez has refused to accept the change, arguing that tax increases are no substitute for structural reform. According to the VRT NWS live blog, it is chiefly the MR's resistance that makes the €10 billion goal difficult to reach.

On the other side of the table, Vooruit and Les Engagés have complained that De Wever's proposals are unbalanced — demanding too much from working and middle-class households while asking too little of large wealth and management companies. Proposed interventions in healthcare growth norms and the possibility of an index jump have proven sensitive across party lines.

## The "Debt Brake" Contingency

With the multi-year deal stalled, De Wever has floated a *schuldenrem* — a "debt brake" — under which the government could activate an emergency package worth €3–4 billion for 2027 alone, freezing public spending on pensions, civil-servant pay, benefits and fiscal brackets while adding money within healthcare. As [VRT NWS explained](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/15/begroting-schuldenrem-voorstel-n-va-premier-bart-de-wever/), the term is somewhat misleading: unlike the German or Swiss constitutional debt brakes, De Wever's proposal is not a constitutional amendment — which would require a two-thirds parliamentary majority — but a contingency mechanism meant chiefly to reassure financial markets and to avoid a stopgap budget.

The alternative the government is desperate to avoid is a budget of "provisional twelfths," a stopgap system under which each month the state may spend one-twelfth of the previous year's budget. Belgium already relied on that mechanism for three months last year — a political embarrassment for a government that came to office promising fiscal order. The concern is a *rentesneeuwbal*, or "interest snowball," in which rising borrowing costs compound the debt burden and force the state to borrow simply to service existing obligations.

Finance Minister Jan Jambon (N-VA) has insisted the ambition to improve the deficit by at least €10 billion by 2029 "remains fully intact." Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem (CD&V) warned that "our country cannot afford immobilism," adding that the responsibility is "too great to push ahead of us and look only to tomorrow."

## Political Fallout

The impasse has already spilled into the wider political arena. De Wever cancelled his planned State of the Union policy declaration — scheduled for Tuesday — for the second consecutive year, leaving the government benches empty at the opening of the parliamentary year and drawing sharp criticism from opposition parties. N-VA faction leader Axel Ronse has insisted the debt brake is only an "emergency button" and that nothing, including the VAT reform, has been taken off the table, warning that "if it fails, we get elections — and that is the worst thing we can imagine."

## What to Watch

A full budget must clear parliament before the end of the year if the government is to start 2027 on a normal footing, which in practice means an agreement is needed by late October or early November. Whether Vooruit and CD&V can be persuaded to accept the debt-brake package — or whether talks pivot back to the harder multi-year choices for 2028 and 2029 — will determine whether Belgium's fifth coalition in a decade can hold together, or whether the country heads toward yet another period of fiscal uncertainty.
