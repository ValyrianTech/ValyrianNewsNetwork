# Belgium Doubles Fines for Speeding While Using Phone

Drivers in Belgium caught speeding or running a red light by roadside ANPR cameras may now face an additional fine if the images also show them holding a smartphone. The practice, which has existed for several years but is unevenly applied, effectively doubles the penalty for a driver committing both offenses at once, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/17/hardrijders-met-smartphone-achter-het-stuur-riskeren-dubbele-boe/), citing Het Nieuwsblad.

According to the federal police, the key condition is that a primary offense — speeding or running a red light — must be established first. Only then can images be scrutinized for other violations such as phone use.

## How the Enforcement Works

Automatic ANPR (Automatic Number Plate Recognition) cameras are authorized to register only a limited list of traffic violations. However, police are permitted to manually review the photographs they capture to identify other offenses, including handheld phone use.

"Currently the cameras register speeding or driving through a red light. A photo is taken, including from the front of your car," An Berger of the federal police told VRT NWS. "Based on those photos, a police officer can establish that you were also using your phone behind the wheel and that you can be fined for it as well."

The federal police press office explained that whether images are checked for phone use depends on local arrangements: "Depending on the agreements with the local prosecutor's office, the staff at the regional processing centers then check the images for phone use."

That discretion means the practice has run for years without being uniformly enforced, since not all prosecutor's offices prioritize the additional checks.

## No Dedicated Phone Cameras — Yet

Berger stressed that Belgium does not yet operate cameras specifically designed to detect phone use — a capability the country is still working toward.

"It is not the case that there are special cameras that will detect phone use behind the wheel. That is a project in preparation, but certainly not for this year," she said.

The Netherlands, by contrast, already deploys dedicated camera systems combined with artificial intelligence to spot drivers using handheld devices. Mobility Minister Jean-Luc Crucke (Les Engagés) and Interior Minister Bernard Quintin (MR) want to bring that model to Belgium. A working group has been formed with the FPS Mobility, police and justice authorities, and study visits to the Netherlands have already taken place.

Crucke also wants to amend Belgium's camera surveillance law so that phone use behind the wheel can be detected automatically, rather than relying on manual review.

## Court Ruling Settles the Legal Question

A recent ruling by the Court of Cassation — Belgium's highest court — confirmed that the practice is lawful. A driver's lawyer had argued that police may only establish a limited list of offenses using "automatically operating devices," and that phone-use findings based on ANPR images exceeded that authority.

The court disagreed, ruling the additional finding was "regular and lawful." Once police have observed an offense from the permitted list, they may also establish other violations, such as phone use, on the basis of the original ANPR photograph.

## What It Means for Drivers

The combined financial impact is significant. A fine for calling behind the wheel amounts to slightly more than 200 euros including administrative costs, on top of whatever penalty applies to the original speeding or red-light offense.

It is important to note this is not a single, doubled penalty but two separate fines issued for two distinct violations. Drivers photographed only for speeding, with both hands visible and no phone in sight, face the standard penalty alone.

## The Bigger Picture

The development fits a broader trend of automated traffic enforcement in Belgium. ANPR cameras have been in use since around 2013, and since July 2019 they have been linked to an automatic fine system. The same infrastructure is also used to identify uninsured or uninspected vehicles, and officials have floated extending camera-based enforcement to seatbelt violations.

The push toward AI-assisted phone detection also intersects with privacy concerns. Belgium's ANPR fine system has faced challenges before the Council of State, and drivers have contested fines on privacy grounds — tensions likely to intensify as detection becomes more automated.

## What to Watch

Several questions remain open. It is unclear when — or whether — the amendment to the camera surveillance law will pass, and whether all prosecutor's offices will adopt the phone-use checks uniformly. The cost and timeline of a Dutch-style AI system beyond 2026, and how privacy and GDPR challenges will shape automatic detection, are also unresolved.

For now, the message from authorities is clear: a smartphone in hand behind the wheel is an expensive habit — and increasingly likely to be caught on camera.
