# Court Weighs If Americans Own the President's Papers

A three-judge federal appeals panel on Friday signaled deep skepticism toward the Trump administration's claim that papers created inside the White House belong to the president personally rather than to the American people, in a case that could reshape how the government preserves — and hides — its own history.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit heard roughly two hours of argument in *American Historical Association v. Donald Trump* (No. 26-5185), an appeal of a lower-court ruling that blocked a Justice Department opinion declaring the Presidential Records Act unconstitutional. The panel's questioning left little doubt that the administration faces an uphill fight, and the dispute is widely expected to reach the Supreme Court regardless of the outcome, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/16/nx-s1-5996836/trump-who-owns-presidential-records-act-appeals-court).

## A 1978 Law Under Attack

The Presidential Records Act, passed in 1978 in the shadow of Watergate, declares that presidential records are the property of the United States — not of the president — and requires their preservation and eventual transfer to the National Archives. For nearly five decades, presidents of both parties complied with it. That changed this year.

In April, the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel, headed by T. Elliot Gaiser, concluded the law is unconstitutional, writing that it "unconstitutionally intrudes on the independence and autonomy of the President guaranteed by Article II" and establishes "a permanent and burdensome regime of congressional regulation of the Presidency untethered from any valid and identifiable legislative purpose," [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/20/g-s1-117887/trump-justice-department-presidential-records-lawsuit).

The White House has defended the president's approach. White House spokeswoman Abigail Jackson said in a written statement that "President Trump is committed to preserving records from his historic Administration and he will maintain a rigorous records retention program," adding that staff members would undergo training on document preservation. Lawyers for the historians and the watchdog group American Oversight noted that the training did not appear to apply to Trump or Vice President Vance.

Historians and watchdog groups — including the American Historical Association, American Oversight, the Freedom of the Press Foundation and Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington — sued to challenge the memo. Their case gained traction in May, when Senior U.S. District Judge John Bates, a George W. Bush appointee, issued a 54-page ruling finding the law likely constitutional and preliminarily blocking the administration's position. Bates held that the Act was "validly enacted by Congress under the Property Clause because Congress may prospectively designate presidential records as federal property and then regulate that property," and said the OLC opinion rested on a "stark misreading" of Supreme Court precedent. He opened his decision with a line from Orwell's *1984*: "Who controls the past controls the future; who controls the present controls the past."

## Skeptical Judges, Blunt Hypotheticals

The administration's position ran into trouble at oral argument. According to a UPI wire account of the proceedings — [republished by Breitbart](https://www.breitbart.com/news/appeals-court-appears-to-reject-trump-claims-of-records-ownership/) — Judge Patricia Millett pressed the government's lawyer, John Bailey, on the logical endpoint of his theory. "Can the president then offer on eBay to sell them, all of our national security secrets, to the highest bidder?" she asked.

Bailey's answer was striking: "The Presidential Records Act, if unconstitutional, would not be a bar to that. ... Consistent with history and tradition ... the president's papers belong to the president, yes."

Judge Cornelia Pillard posed a variation, imagining a deceased president's "bad boy nephew" who "decides they're going to sell the presidential records to the highest bidder, and that happens to be, you know, the Chinese Communist Party." The exchange underscored the practical stakes the panel is weighing: if official papers are personal property, almost nothing in current law would stop a president from destroying or selling them.

## Why the Fight Matters

The case reaches beyond Trump. For roughly 200 years, presidents treated their papers as personal property, until the struggle over Richard Nixon's records prompted Congress to intervene. The Supreme Court upheld an earlier related records statute in *Nixon v. Administrator of General Services* (1977), a 7–2 decision requiring Nixon to turn over his White House records — precedent the administration's memo now asks courts to depart from. Daniel Jacobson, a lawyer for the historians, attacked that move, saying the executive branch was taking "for itself the authority to declare the Supreme Court got it wrong and they can ignore a law based on that disagreement."

Former officials warn the consequences are concrete. "Access to the records on national security issues of former presidents is really vital for the continuity of our government," said John Bellinger, who served as legal adviser to the National Security Council under George W. Bush, noting that the only record of what a president said to a foreign leader is often a written document.

Historian Matthew Connelly of Columbia University framed the dispute as a basic question of democratic ownership. "In America, I think most of us have now come to understand that the president works for us," he said. "The papers, the records of the decisions they make on our behalf, those are our papers, that's our history."

The case is also entangled with Trump's Mar-a-Lago classified-documents dispute. The administration reinterpreted the records law after Trump was indicted on obstruction-of-justice charges for allegedly stockpiling classified documents at the resort; the case was later dismissed on technical grounds and dropped after Trump won reelection. Timothy Naftali, a former director of the Nixon Presidential Library, called the records-law attack "an attempt at post facto vindication for having taken public property to Mar-a-Lago."

## What's Next

The D.C. Circuit is not expected to rule immediately. Should the panel affirm the lower-court injunction, the administration is widely anticipated to seek Supreme Court review, where a conservative majority could weigh a broad theory of executive autonomy. Conservative lawyer Michael Edney predicted as much, telling NPR the administration "want[s] this resolved at a time of their choosing, with arguments of their choosing, and a court of their choosing."

For now, the May injunction remains in effect, requiring White House personnel to comply with the records law — though it does not directly bind the president or vice president, whom the courts generally cannot enjoin in the performance of official duties. The question the litigation ultimately poses is deceptively simple, and as consequential as any before the federal courts this year: whether the record of a presidency belongs to the nation, or to the person who occupied the office.
