# Trump Adds Some Back After Cutting 760K From ACA

Late last month, the Trump administration announced it had cut about 760,000 people from Affordable Care Act (ACA) marketplace plans, citing fraud and improper enrollments and touting billions of dollars in potential taxpayer savings. Now, some of those people are quietly being let back in — and the government is saying little about how many, or why.

Insurance industry sources told [KFF Health News](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/15/nx-s1-6002957/aca-fraud-trump-health-insurance) that the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) has reinstated a small number of people whose coverage should not have been canceled. More reinstatements could follow in coming weeks, as enrollees discover they are uninsured only when they try to use their care.

## A crackdown built on warning signs

The August 31 cancellations covered roughly 315,000 enrollments representing about 760,000 individuals, and CMS estimated the move would recoup about $2.2 billion in advance premium tax credits. The sweep applied only to the roughly 30 states that use Healthcare.gov, the federal marketplace; the 21 states running their own exchanges generally reported few or none of the unauthorized sign-ups that plagued the federal system.

According to the account CMS gave KFF Health News, the agency in June flagged nearly 1.2 million enrollments that carried all four of what it deemed fraud warning signs: no Social Security number or immigration document number on the application, a broker involved in the sign-up, no monthly premium payment, and no contact with the insurer or claims filed. CMS sent those lists to insurers, instructing them to reach policyholders by at least two methods — such as a phone call and a letter — and give them 30 days to respond. Those who did not were disenrolled.

## The people behind the numbers

Critics say the warning signs are imperfect proxies that can just as easily flag legitimate enrollees. Healthy people often file no claims in a year; families may not answer calls from unknown numbers; and a Social Security number is not always required, such as for a child when the adult applying is not seeking coverage for that child.

"It may be true that some of those enrollments were not authorized, but it can be equally true that people got lost in the shuffle and have a valid enrollment. Without more information, it's almost impossible to tell," said Katie Keith, director of the Center for Health Policy and the Law at Georgetown Law. "That they are not being very transparent is telling too," she said of the administration.

Supporters of the crackdown, including Brian Blase of the Paragon Health Institute, argue the sweep was overdue. "This was clearly phantom enrollees," he said, estimating that more than 99% of the cancellations were zero-premium coverage people were signed up for by rogue call centers without their knowledge, or for people who do not exist.

For anyone wrongly caught up, Blase and administration officials say reinstatement is as simple as calling the marketplace and supplying the missing information. "Everyone has the political incentive for that process to be that easy," Blase said. "We're a month before the election."

## A second phase, and a transparency gap

Even as the reversal unfolds, a second phase of the anti-fraud effort is underway, scrutinizing an additional 419,000 sign-ups. Insurers must tell CMS how many of those enrollees don't respond to outreach by "around mid-to-late November, after the midterm elections," said David Merritt, senior vice president of external affairs at the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association. He confirmed that some BCBS members are among those reinstated and noted that CMS has an expedited review process for people improperly disenrolled.

Yet the administration has released few details about the cancellations themselves: how robust the outreach was, how long reinstatement takes, or how many reversals have occurred. CMS did not respond to requests for a breakdown of why enrollments were canceled — whether for incorrect income reporting, other coverage, or missing documents. "Currently, there is no additional data to share," the agency's press office said.

## Why it matters

The timing carries political weight. Healthcare costs are among voters' top concerns going into the 2026 midterms, and many of those removed are likely concentrated in red states such as Texas, Florida, Louisiana, and Mississippi — areas that saw rapid ACA enrollment growth from 2020 to 2025. Overall marketplace enrollment has fallen by nearly 3 million in the first months of 2026, driven largely by the expiration of enhanced subsidies at the end of last year, though the administration also credits its anti-fraud push.

Experts remain divided on whether the dragnet will hit its target. "There will be legitimate people who don't have a claim or don't respond to an inquiry from an insurer," said Matthew Fiedler, a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution. "If the goal is to target fraudulent enrollment, what CMS did is better than tossing people at random, but is likely to miss the mark."

## What to watch

The next milestone is the November reporting deadline on the 419,000 sign-ups under review — set, notably, for after voters go to the polls. Whether more wrongly disenrolled people surface as they seek care, and whether the administration releases data on how many cancellations it has reversed, will shape both the program's credibility and the political fallout. As Harvard's Adrianna McIntyre put it, any public reaction "depends on whether the government got it wrong, whether people try to get care and then find out their coverage is no longer active."
