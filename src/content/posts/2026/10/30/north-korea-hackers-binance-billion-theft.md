# North Korean Hackers Likely Behind $6B Binance Theft

Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, said Friday that hackers stole more than $6 billion in what blockchain intelligence firms describe as the largest cryptocurrency heist on record — an attack they attribute with "high confidence" to an elite North Korean state-linked hacking unit. The company has reserved more than $6.34 billion of its own funds to cover the stolen assets and says it is already recovering money for users, even as investigators race to stop the hackers from laundering the unrecovered balance.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/binance-cryptocurrency-security-hack-north-korea-550e9b876550f71f07098494fe735beb), Binance said it worked with blockchain partners to freeze roughly $3 billion in affected assets — about half the total. The exchange has pledged to cover all losses and has been posting regular updates on social media as its investigation unfolds.

## A heist measured in minutes

The scale of the operation has stunned analysts. U.S.-based blockchain intelligence firm TRM attributed the attack to TraderTraitor, which it describes as North Korea's elite hacking unit, noting that the attackers moved approximately $6.36 billion out of Binance wallets across ten blockchains — most of it in about 20 minutes.

"We make this judgment with high confidence," TRM said, adding that several features of the attack matched earlier North Korea-linked heists. Blockchain analysis firm Chainalysis reached a similar conclusion, with its co-founder and CEO Jonathan Levin writing on X that the Binance hack was likely carried out by TraderTraitor, according to AP.

The attribution carries a notable caveat. Binance itself has not confirmed the identity of the attackers. Asked about potential ties to North Korea, a Binance spokesperson said only that the company is "working closely with our law enforcement partners to confirm the identity of the criminals and bring them to justice." The North Korea link therefore rests on private-sector blockchain analysis rather than a formal government statement.

## How the breach happened

Binance's initial investigation suggested the incident may have involved a compromised laptop, but the company said a subsequent review uncovered additional "points of breach." Cybersecurity specialists pointed to a deeper structural weakness.

"This looks like one signature that gave the attacker a never-expiring, full-access token to a Binance hot wallet," said Kok Tin Gan, co-founder and CEO of cybersecurity firm NyxLab, referring to internet-connected wallets that hold users' private keys. The remark raises questions about why stronger approval controls were not in place.

Binance co-CEO Richard Teng confirmed in a video posted to X that the exchange's own hot wallet was affected, while stressing that most user assets sit in offline cold wallets. "User assets are held under Binance's established security architecture," he said. "The vast majority sits in cold wallets and are only moved into a hot wallet when a user makes withdrawal." Teng added that the attack was not carried out by a single person and involved complex tactics, including a "self-destruct mechanism" designed to frustrate investigators.

## The race to recover — and to launder

With roughly half the funds frozen, attention has shifted to a fast-moving contest between investigators and the hackers. Stablecoin issuers moved quickly: Circle froze more than $800 million and Tether froze more than $1.5 billion tied to the incident. Those frozen tokens could eventually be burned and reissued, potentially returning value to Binance.

But the attackers were, in some respects, a step ahead — quickly selling USDT to market makers and borrowing ether against the stablecoin, according to TRM. Ether is harder to freeze, leaving the lenders holding stolen stablecoins.

As [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/30/binance-hack-stolen-crypto-recovery.html) reported, investigators describe a "cat and mouse game" in which hackers use obfuscation techniques and professional money-laundering networks to convert digital coins into fiat currency. Chainalysis said it has observed "laundering patterns we have not seen before," with hackers using automation to circulate funds repeatedly among addresses rather than simply moving them onward. No use of crypto mixers has been observed so far.

"Ultimately, they're trying to off-ramp it to fiat. And that becomes the challenge, that's sort of this race against time," Ari Redbord, global head of policy at TRM Labs, told CNBC. "As you get further and further away, the amount of seizure opportunities go down," Levin added.

Experts say hackers increasingly turn to professional, often Chinese, money-laundering organizations that buy stolen crypto over the counter for a fee and return it to the hackers in a form that can be exchanged for cash. "So we are not necessarily only chasing North Korean on-chain, often we're chasing these professional money laundering networks," Redbord said.

## A hacker economy built on sanctions

The attribution fits a longer pattern. Experts say North Korea has turned to crypto hacking and other illicit cyber activities as a source of badly needed foreign currency to support its troubled economy and fund its nuclear program in the wake of tough U.N. sanctions. In December 2022, South Korea's spy agency said North Korean hackers had stolen an estimated 1.5 trillion won ($1.1 billion) in cryptocurrency and other virtual assets over the previous five years, more than half of it in 2022 alone.

The February 2025 hack of the exchange Bybit, a $1.5 billion loss the FBI attributed to North Korea and TraderTraitor, offered a clear precedent — one TRM cited when drawing parallels to the Binance attack.

## A victim with a complicated history

The heist has also thrust Binance's own record back into the spotlight. As [AP News detailed](https://apnews.com/article/binance-changpeng-zhao-hack-north-korea-trump-world-liberty-3255f83c8ca64131bd52ca9b4033c17c), Binance and its billionaire founder, Changpeng Zhao, have long been accused of profiting off crypto criminals — accused of turning a blind eye as the exchange was used by drug traffickers, terrorists and money launderers.

Zhao pleaded guilty in 2023 to Bank Secrecy Act violations, including allowing trades with Iran in violation of sanctions and failing to report suspicious transactions involving groups such as Hamas' Al-Qassam Brigades and al-Qaida. He stepped down as CEO and served four months in prison. President Trump pardoned Zhao in 2025; Binance and Zhao have been key supporters of Trump family crypto enterprises, including World Liberty Financial, though Binance denies any quid pro quo.

Zhao, who remains a major shareholder, posted on X that "User funds are safe!" and that he expected all user funds to be fully covered. He also noted that while the Binance theft may be the largest by dollar value, earlier attacks such as the Mt. Gox collapse had a more substantial impact in terms of the number of bitcoin affected.

## Market jolt and a warning

The disclosure rattled markets. The price of bitcoin dropped 3.2% on Thursday evening to under $79,000, according to AP. Binance suspended deposits and withdrawals temporarily, saying deposits were unaffected and withdrawals were expected to resume within a day or two.

Analysts warn the episode may be a sign of worse to come. "A cyber attack of this level is a matter of when, not if, particularly alongside the rise of AI," said Andy Ward, a senior vice president at Absolute Security. "Cyber threats are becoming more sophisticated and widespread as ever, and it is likely only a matter of time before an even larger hack, with even greater financial fallout, makes headlines."

## What to watch

The immediate focus is whether investigators can claw back more of the roughly $3 billion that remains unrecovered before it disappears into fiat. Teng said Binance is working with law enforcement, blockchain intelligence firms, token issuers and other exchanges to trace and recover stolen funds. "We will do everything within our power to make sure that these bad actors will be held accountable," he said.

Beyond this case, the incident sharpens two questions: whether major exchanges will face pressure to overhaul their hot-wallet access controls, and whether governments will move to better regulate an industry whose cross-border, largely anonymous plumbing remains a prime target for state-sponsored theft. For now, the hackers hold an edge measured in minutes — and the investigators are racing to close the gap.
