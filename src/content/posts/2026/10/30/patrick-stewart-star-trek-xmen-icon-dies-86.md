# Patrick Stewart, Star Trek and X-Men Icon, Dies at 86

Sir Patrick Stewart, the classically trained British actor who became a global icon as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in *Star Trek: The Next Generation* and Professor Charles Xavier in the *X-Men* films, has died at his home in Los Angeles. He was 86.

His spokesperson, Kelly Bush Novak, said Stewart died on Wednesday, 28 October 2026, following "a brief and poignant journey" with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy Body Dementia — diagnoses that had not previously been made public. According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/patrick-stewart-dies-8b38054ff74d4187a32158d78e345ad7), he died in the care of his wife, Sunny Ozell, and is survived by two adult children from his first marriage, Daniel and Sophie Stewart.

His death came only days after that of Walter Koenig, the original-series *Star Trek* actor who played Ensign Pavel Chekov — a painful double loss for one of the world's most devoted fanbases.

## A two-track career

Stewart was unusual among screen stars in that he built genuine classical-theatre credentials before, and independent of, his Hollywood fame. Born on 13 July 1940 in Mirfield, West Yorkshire, into a working-class household without indoor plumbing, he won a scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theatre School and joined the Royal Shakespeare Company in 1966, remaining a core member for roughly 14 years and appearing in more than 40 productions. As [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cye0l23lx15o) noted in its career profile, he moved effortlessly between the starship and the stage.

He treated *Star Trek* as something of a detour rather than a destination, later recalling that he had been assured the syndicated science-fiction series "would fail." Instead, *The Next Generation* ran seven years and 178 episodes, spawned four feature films, and made the phrase "Make it so" a cultural touchstone. His rich baritone and stiff-backed bearing gave Picard a Shakespearean weight that set the show apart. He would return to the role decades later in *Star Trek: Picard*.

As [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/patrick-stewart-shakespeare-theater-appreciation-9fe4ffe6024a270aad3a456ae26a11b7) recounted in its appreciation of his stage work, Stewart kept returning to Shakespeare even at the height of his screen fame, most memorably in a celebrated 2007-08 *Macbeth* and as Claudius opposite David Tennant's *Hamlet* — a performance that won him one of his three Olivier Awards.

## Tributes from across the industry

Tributes poured in from co-stars, friends and institutions. Ian McKellen, his close friend and *X-Men* co-star who officiated Stewart's 2013 wedding, led the mourning, calling him "the most respected and beloved actor of his generation" and describing a man shaped to the end by his Yorkshire roots. "He wore his heart on his sleeve, which made him a man to rely on, to admire and in my case, love," McKellen said, according to [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cm20v3029p5xo).

Hugh Jackman, his *X-Men* co-star, said: "We lost one of the greats of stage and screen today. And, in my book, a tremendous friend and man. I learned so much from him." William Shatner, the original Captain Kirk, called him "a magnificent actor" who "brought intelligence, grace, and a deep sense of humanity to everything he did," while George Takei said Stewart had "inspired a whole next generation of star-gazers."

Marvel Studios, whose cinematic universe Stewart helped launch as Professor Xavier in 2000, called him "a singular talent, a generous collaborator, and a true gentleman." The Royal Shakespeare Company described him as "a colossus of both stage and screen." Seth MacFarlane, who worked with Stewart on *Family Guy* and *American Dad!*, remembered "that rare actor who could take any bit of dialogue and elevate it."

## A voice beyond the screen

Stewart's public life extended well beyond acting. He became a patron of the domestic abuse charity Refuge in 2007, motivated by memories of his father's violence against his mother, and used his platform to break taboos around the subject. "Violence is always a choice a man makes and he alone is responsible for it," he wrote. A spokesman for the UK prime minister noted that Stewart "always stood up for what he believed in, including through his campaigning to help those affected by domestic violence."

He was knighted by Queen Elizabeth II in June 2010 and was also a Grammy winner, taking Best Spoken Word Album in 1996 for his recording of *Peter and the Wolf*. In later life he embraced social media with disarming humour, sharing Shakespeare sonnets during the COVID-19 pandemic alongside lobster-suit bath photos and apple-picking memes.

## A final performance to come

Stewart's Professor Xavier spanned the original *X-Men* trilogy, later films including *Logan* (2017), and *Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness* (2022). His final film appearance will be seen posthumously, as Professor X in the forthcoming *Avengers: Doomsday* — a fitting coda for an actor whose reach extended across two of the biggest franchises in popular culture.

His family said they would "forever cherish the peaceful ease and reflection on his face, an enduring reminder of his invincible grace." West End theatres are due to dim their lights for two minutes in his honour on 3 November.

## What's next

The disclosure of Stewart's Alzheimer's and Lewy Body Dementia diagnoses has already prompted wider public discussion of the conditions — the two most common forms of dementia. Michelle Dyson, chief executive of the Alzheimer's Society, said his death was "a heartbreaking reminder of the devastating impact dementia has on people and families across the globe."

For *Star Trek* fans still grieving Walter Koenig, Stewart's death closes a bruising week and marks the passing of a performer who bridged the classical stage and the multiplex. What remains is an unusually broad body of work — and, in a posthumous turn as Professor X, one last appearance still to come.
