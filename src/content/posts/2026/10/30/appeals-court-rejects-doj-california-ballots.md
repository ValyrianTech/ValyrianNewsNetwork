# Appeals Court Rejects DOJ Bid to Set Aside California Ballots

The U.S. 9th Circuit Court of Appeals on Friday rejected the Justice Department's request that California set aside disputed ballots received in the midterm election, delivering the Trump administration its latest legal setback in a widening effort to assert federal control over state-run elections.

The 2-1 decision comes just days before Tuesday's midterm vote and marks the second time this week the appellate court has rebuffed the administration on the matter — the first time on procedural grounds, and now on the merits. According to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/california-noncitizen-voting-trump-midterm-lawsuit-49cee0f4184b0ecb573f9f0274350642), the panel's ruling is "the latest setback for the Trump administration's efforts to assert control over state-run elections."

The administration has alleged that California sent ballots to roughly 20,000 people who may not be U.S. citizens, and it wants the state to hold those ballots aside and not count them until citizenship can be verified. California officials counter that the underlying list is highly unreliable and likely includes many people who are U.S. citizens.

## What the Court Decided

The majority opinion was authored by Judge Mark J. Bennett, a Trump appointee, and Judge Gabriel P. Sanchez, a Biden appointee. Writing for the panel, they affirmed the reasoning of the district court below, finding "no feasible way to identify and segregate the ballots of specific individuals without potentially catastrophic effects on the entire system."

The court framed the dispute in constitutional terms, holding that the Elections Clause gives the states and Congress — not the executive branch — the power to set the rules for federal elections. Allowing the administration to invoke federal statutes to dictate how California processes ballots, the panel suggested, would improperly override the Constitution's own allocation of authority and amount to a power grab that the courts could not endorse.

"If the United States believes different procedures are necessary for vote-by-mail ballots, we believe it should take that suggestion to Congress, not the courts," the majority wrote.

The panel also leaned on the federal 90-day "quiet period" that bars major changes to voter lists in the run-up to an election, noting that the district court had found the administration's data to be of questionable reliability. The court said that suspect reliability "reflects the concern Congress sought to mitigate with the 90-day quiet period."

Judge Patrick J. Bumatay, the other Trump appointee on the panel, dissented. He argued that the government sought only a "modest remedy — stopping California from mixing the ballots of citizens and suspected noncitizens" that "would not cancel any vote but would allow time to verify the eligibility of suspected noncitizens."

The Justice Department did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

## A Blistering District Court Record

The appeals court ruling builds on an unusually forceful opinion from U.S. District Judge David O. Carter, who on Wednesday denied the same request and warned that segregating ballots would "wreak havoc" on the election. As [AP reported](https://apnews.com/article/california-noncitizen-voting-trump-midterm-lawsuit-481bed64919ad193e80e051d28beb2c0), Carter wrote that "eroded trust in our democracy is irreparable" and that "if the Executive can discount votes and threaten an ongoing election due to unproven allegations, then our democracy is in danger."

Carter had earlier floated the idea of appointing a special master to independently vet the government's data — an option the administration itself had pursued, as [AP detailed](https://apnews.com/article/california-noncitizen-voting-trump-lawsuit-ballots-8f6e267a51c574716386f502b77f7ce0) — but his final order reversed course and did not establish one.

The practical problem the courts kept returning to is timing. California mails ballots to all of its roughly 23 million registered voters, and by the time of the district court ruling more than 3.7 million people had already voted. More than half a million ballots had already been processed — their envelopes verified and the ballots removed — which elections officials say renders them anonymous and impossible to match back to individual voters. LA County has testified that once ballots are separated from their envelopes, they cannot be traced to a specific person.

## The Reliability Question

At the heart of the case is the quality of the government's list. California officials have pointed to LA County's review of a small subset of names, finding that a substantial portion of those examined appeared to be U.S.-born — a finding Homeland Security has disputed. A federal agent's affidavit conceded that some citizens' names could have been mistakenly included, and the administration built the list in part by enriching purchased voter-roll data with a private third-party database, a method the state has challenged.

Earlier on Friday, a federal judge in Washington ordered the administration to share the disputed list with lawyers for the Coalition for Humane Immigrant Rights and the UCLA Voting Rights Project — the first time a nongovernment organization will be able to evaluate the merits of the roughly 20,000 names. The two groups sued the Homeland Security Department in early October to stop it from using federal immigration data to challenge state voter rolls, and they argue that such data-matching methods risk disenfranchising citizens.

## A Broader Federal Push

The California case is one front in a wider campaign. The administration has issued a [raft of subpoenas](https://apnews.com/article/alaska-peltola-senate-dan-sullivan-democrats-dunleavy-c391cc7b546384c7adde9aebc9973620) in Alaska amid a spoiler-candidate controversy in a Senate race, and it has provided a list of alleged noncitizen voters to officials in Washington state, part of what the AP describes as [broader federal scrutiny](https://apnews.com/article/justice-department-midterm-elections-push-fc53d33a4ada827be3089ae358a5d826) of local elections.

In Los Angeles, the pressure has taken the form of subpoenas. The AP [obtained subpoenas](https://apnews.com/article/california-noncitizen-voting-trump-midterm-lawsuit-20714065e59fe13db0e53c711a3ca390) issued to LA County election officials seeking documents and communications tied to the federal letters outlining the noncitizen-voting allegations. At least three county election officials have been asked to appear before a grand jury on Nov. 4 — the day after the election.

The administration has also pursued prosecution as an alternative. In October, federal authorities announced the identification and arrest of 28 people across California accused of illegally registering or voting.

## Election Workers Under Pressure

The threats have extended to the officials counting the vote. Bill Essayli, the top federal prosecutor in Los Angeles, has warned that "any election official who knowingly processes the ballot of a noncitizen is opening themselves to legal jeopardy," arguing that processing such ballots "disenfranchises American citizens through dilution." Judge Carter, in his ruling, said it "would be wise that any threats to state and county election employees cease immediately."

On Friday, a coalition of unions — including the A.F.L.-C.I.O. and the American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees — asked a federal judge to temporarily block the Justice Department from demanding that local officials stop counting ballots and to drop the criminal-prosecution threats. The lawsuit accuses the department of issuing threats that could interfere with officials' ability to process votes.

## What's Next

The immediate question is whether the Justice Department will seek emergency relief from the Supreme Court, given that Election Day is Tuesday and California must certify its results by Dec. 11. Legal analysts were blunt about the scale of the loss. "The federal government lost in every way it could possibly lose," said Rick Hasen of UCLA Law, as reported by the AP. "It lost on the law, and it lost on the facts, and it lost on the equities."

David Becker of the Center for Election Innovation and Research told the AP that the department's demands appeared "designed to cause chaos," but concluded that "the administration's efforts to interfere with the midterms are failing."

California officials, meanwhile, cast the ruling as a vindication. Governor Gavin Newsom said the legal system was "standing up to the President's authoritarian attempts to remain in power unchecked and unchallenged," while Attorney General Rob Bonta called it "yet another important victory for the rule of law and the right to vote — but this fight is not over."

With ballots already cast and counting underway, the fight may now move to the grand jury, to the Supreme Court, or to Congress — the venue the appellate panel itself suggested was the proper place to change the rules. For the millions of Californians who have already voted, the practical effect of Friday's ruling is that their ballots, for now, will be counted.
