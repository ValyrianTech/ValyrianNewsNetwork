# VA Ended a Lifeline. Thousands of Veterans Are Paying More

More than 15,000 veterans who modified their VA-backed mortgages after a financial hardship are now paying at least $150 more each month, and more than 700 are paying $1,000 or more extra, according to an NPR investigation drawing on previously unreported Department of Veterans Affairs data. The increases trace back to the Trump administration's decision to shut down a low-interest rescue program in May 2025 — a move that housing groups and mortgage industry leaders had warned would leave veterans with worse options than other homeowners.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/30/nx-s1-5995628/trump-va-home-loan-program-veterans-paying), 63% of veterans who received a loan modification were hit with a payment increase, compared with just 2% for loans backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which cover most of the U.S. mortgage market.

## What VASP Did

The Veterans Affairs Servicing Purchase (VASP) program allowed the VA to buy delinquent VA-backed loans and roll missed payments into a new mortgage at a fixed 2.5% interest rate. In a market where rates hovered near 7%, that made VASP a genuine lifeline: a veteran who fell behind could catch up without being forced into a costlier loan at current market rates.

As the VA's own [program FAQ](https://benefits.va.gov/HOMELOANS/vasp-process-faq.asp) confirms, the program ended on May 1, 2025. The administration terminated it administratively — with no vote from Congress — and House Republicans announced the move before the VA did.

## A Warning That Came True

The decision did not happen in a vacuum. The mortgage industry and housing advocates explicitly warned the administration not to kill VASP before a replacement was operational. The [National Consumer Law Center](https://www.nclc.org/abrupt-end-of-vasp-program-leaves-veterans-families-at-risk-of-home-loss/) called the abrupt termination a move that would leave veteran borrowers "with substantially worse options" than those with federally backed loans from the FHA, USDA, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac.

"This was a foreseeable outcome of abruptly ending a program without a replacement in place," Matthew Douglas of the Housing Policy Council, which represents the nation's largest mortgage companies, told NPR. "It's a mathematical certainty that borrowers are going to have those kind of shocking payment increases."

The consequences were not merely theoretical. By April 2026, more than 10,000 veterans had lost their homes to foreclosure since the program's shutdown, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/02/nx-s1-5750814/veterans-mortgages-foreclosure-va-rescue), the highest pace of VA-loan foreclosures in a decade, with another 90,000 veterans behind on payments or already in the foreclosure process.

## One Veteran's Math

For Scott Boyd, an Army veteran who saw combat in the infantry before serving in military intelligence, the change was personal and costly. Boyd bought a home near Columbus, Ohio, in 2021 with a VA loan at 2.75%. After a mass layoff and a PTSD crisis that led him to seek treatment at the Dayton VA hospital, he fell behind on his mortgage.

His lender repeatedly told him VASP would be there as a last resort. It wasn't. By the time he faced his options, the only path offered was a loan modification at 6.75% — raising his payment by $1,181 a month and stretching his term to 40 years. More than 90% of his monthly payment now covers interest rather than principal.

"This is outrageous," Boyd told NPR. "I remember just thinking to myself, 'This is just so broken.'"

Two weeks after Boyd signed the modification, the VA launched its replacement program — one that would have let him keep his original 2.75% rate.

## The Replacement Arrives — Slowly

After ending VASP, the VA took more than 13 months to design and announce its replacement. The VA Partial Claim Program, authorized by the VA Home Loan Reform Act that President Trump signed into law on July 30, 2025, formally launched on [June 15, 2026](https://news.va.gov/press-room/va-launches-partial-claim-program-to-help-veterans-avoid-home-foreclosure/). It lets veterans move missed payments to the back of their loan term, preserving their original low-rate mortgage.

But the industry was given another 180 days to implement it — a deadline of November 28, 2026 — and most mortgage companies still do not offer it. More critically, the new program does not retroactively help veterans like Boyd who already accepted higher-cost modifications during the gap between the two programs.

"This is the height of insanity," Rep. Mark Takano (D-CA), ranking member of the House Veterans' Affairs Committee, told NPR. "These are veterans that are already financially distressed — they were already in trouble." Takano said Congress could act: "We could do something for those veterans who saw a jump in their interest rates."

## A Question of Accountability

The VA defends its record. Press secretary Quinn Slaven told NPR that "many leading voices in Congress supported the termination of VASP," and said the VA home loan program "is based on the premise that while Veterans may need some assistance, they must generally be able to make their mortgage payments."

Rep. Mike Bost (R-IL), who chairs the House Veterans' Affairs Committee, and Rep. Derrick Van Orden (R-WI), who led the call to wind down VASP and sponsored the replacement bill, did not respond to NPR's requests for comment. Van Orden is in a tight reelection race.

## What to Watch

The immediate test is the November 28 deadline, when the mortgage industry is supposed to have the Partial Claim Program fully in place. Housing advocates are pressing the VA to pause foreclosures until then and to restructure its rules so that a higher payment is a last resort, not a default. Whether Congress chooses to help the veterans stranded in the gap — through legislation or administrative action — remains an open question.

For Boyd, the damage is already done. He is still making the higher payments, and he says he does not feel entitled to better treatment than any other American — only not to be treated worse.

"I really felt hurt more than anything," he said. "I wasn't angry. I was just like, I felt so defeated."
