# Belgium Electricity Output Hits Near 20-Year Low

Belgium's electricity production fell to 4,021 gigawatt-hours (GWh) in September 2026, the lowest monthly gross output since the federal statistical series began in January 2008, according to [official figures](https://economie.fgov.be/nl/themas/energie/energie-cijfers/maandelijkse) published by the FOD Economie's Directorate-General for Energy. The near-record low comes as the country's two remaining nuclear reactors have been offline for months and wholesale energy prices across Europe surge.

## Context: A Nuclear-Dependent Grid Runs Dry

The decline is not a sudden shock but the culmination of a decade-long shift in Belgium's energy mix. The country permanently closed Doel 3 and Tihange 2 by 2023, leaving only Doel 4 and Tihange 3—roughly 2 GW of the 5.9 GW of nuclear capacity Belgium once operated. Those two reactors were taken offline in early April 2026 for major maintenance and modernisation tied to their ten-year life extension to 2035 and their adaptation to new safety standards.

According to the FOD Economie, the situation is unprecedented: the reactors had never produced zero electricity since Belgium began industrial nuclear generation in the 1970s and 1980s. Previously, the two units supplied more than 1,500 GWh per month. Their output fell below 100 GWh in April and has been zero since.

## Key Developments: Record Imports Fill the Gap

With domestic nuclear generation at zero and wind and solar output weak in September, Belgium has become exceptionally dependent on imports. Net imports have been systematically above 2 TWh per month from April through September. In the second quarter of 2026, net imports reached 7.5 TWh—the highest since measurements began in 1998, and well above the previous record set in the third quarter of 2015, when nuclear availability was similarly low.

In the first half of 2026, Belgium imported 18.7 TWh and exported 6.2 TWh, yielding a net import balance of 12.5 TWh that the FOD Economie describes as "unseen." Electricity exchanges with Germany, previously a net export destination, have flipped to net imports since July, while exchanges with the UK turned to net import in September.

The reactor restarts are imminent. [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/12/opnieuw-elektriciteit-van-belgische-kerncentrale-volgende-week/) reported on 12 October that Tihange 3 was scheduled to restart on 19 October, with Doel 4 planned for 1 November, according to operator Engie. Both units were originally due to close in 2025 but were granted a ten-year extension. A new major maintenance of both reactors is already planned for April 2027.

Renewables have filled some of the gap, but their rising share is partly an artefact of falling total output. Renewables accounted for 51% of national production in the first half of 2026 and more than 60% since April—up from an average of 39% in 2025. Yet in July 2026, the last month with final data, renewable production was 2,634 GWh, or 60% of gross production, with solar contributing 1,701 GWh and wind 711 GWh.

## Analysis: Supply Security and the Energy Transition

The September low arrives amid a broader European energy crisis. Gas prices on the Dutch TTF benchmark rose above EUR100/MWh, the highest since December 2022, driven by conflict in the Middle East, disruption around the Strait of Hormuz, and low European gas reserves at the start of the heating season. Belgian inflation rose to 5.26% in October 2026—the highest in more than three years—driven mainly by gas and electricity price increases, while diesel reached a record maximum price of EUR2.51 per litre in late October.

The combination of near-zero nuclear output, weak renewables, and record import dependence has coincided with a period of exceptionally high and volatile wholesale prices, as [Business AM](https://businessam.be/belgie-breekt-record-met-elektriciteitsimport-na-stillegging-kernreactoren/) noted in July. That confluence raises questions about the cost and resilience of Belgium's supply model—particularly whether the grid can lean on imports from neighbours at moments when heating demand peaks.

Belgian electricity consumption has also fallen structurally since August 2022, with a historic low of 5,568 GWh in July 2023, and has remained below the 2015-2021 normal. The decline has been observed across industry and other economic sectors, suggesting that part of the demand adjustment is permanent rather than purely cyclical.

## What's Next

Attention now turns to whether Tihange 3 and Doel 4 restart on schedule and at full output. Their return would restore more than 1,500 GWh of monthly domestic supply and reduce Belgium's reliance on imports heading into winter. But the April 2027 maintenance window looms, and the structural questions—about nuclear's role in a renewable-heavy system, the cost of import dependence, and the policy response to household energy vulnerability—will outlast this particular shutdown.

For now, Belgium's grid is being held together by its neighbours, and the official data show just how much the country is leaning on them.
