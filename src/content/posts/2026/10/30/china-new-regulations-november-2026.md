# China Rolls Out Batch of New Rules From November

A batch of new national regulations and mandatory standards will take effect across China from November, targeting areas from classroom lighting to children's product safety and museum management, according to state media. The changes, announced in a roundup by the official [Xinhua News Agency](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261030/fe495ceff9914373b1086353a89343ae/c.html), span education, youth sports, consumer protection and cultural heritage.

The Xinhua report highlighted a new national standard clarifying classroom daylighting and lighting requirements, intended to better "light up" student classrooms, alongside strengthened management of museum collections to raise the standardization and precision of how artifacts are curated.

## A Focus on Child Health

The most detailed changes center on four standards tied to children's wellbeing that take effect on November 1, as outlined in a separate explainer carried by [China News](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/edu/2026/10-28/10722228.shtml) and sourced to the "Capital Education" WeChat channel.

The first is a compulsory national standard on the hygienic lighting of primary and secondary school classrooms. It sets requirements for natural daylighting—such as having single-sided light enter from students' left—and mandates artificial lighting levels: desks must maintain an average illuminance of at least 300 lux with uniformity of at least 0.7, while writing boards must reach 500 lux with uniformity of at least 0.8. A core feature is a requirement that classroom lighting meet the RG0 "exempt" level for blue-light hazard, a move aimed at easing eye strain among students. The standard responds to rising rates of near-sightedness among Chinese children, a trend that has become a public-health priority.

The second is a code of conduct for youth sports training, rooted in the government's "double reduction" policy to ease academic pressure. It sets space requirements (at least 5 square meters per participant for most sports), caps ordinary classes at 35 students, bars individuals with records of sexual or violent crimes from coaching, and limits prepaid fees to no more than 5,000 yuan for a period not exceeding three months or 60 class hours.

## Tighter Product and Toy Safety

The third standard is China's first all-category compulsory national standard for children's products, covering items used, played with or worn by children under 14. It imposes structural safeguards—such as preventing detachable small parts from easily falling off to avoid choking—and strict limits on seven categories of harmful chemicals, while banning the sale of products with strong pungent odors.

The fourth revises four compulsory standards on toy safety, adding limits on 10 categories of harmful substances. Formaldehyde in toys for children under 36 months is capped at 30 milligrams per kilogram, the number of regulated phthalate plasticizers rises from six to ten, and boron in "crystal mud"-style slime toys is limited to 300 milligrams per kilogram.

## Analysis and What's Next

The batch reflects a broader push by Chinese regulators to codify safety and quality standards in fast-growing consumer and education sectors, particularly those touching children. The youth sports rules, for instance, address a rapidly expanding private market that has drawn concern over fees, safety and unqualified coaches, while the toy standards respond to public worry over so-called "internet-famous" toys.

Separately, new rules on cable broadcasting and television operation services, issued by the National Radio and Television Administration, take effect on November 27, though details beyond the prohibition of power-on advertisements could not be independently confirmed.

With implementation staggered between November 1 and November 27, the coming weeks will test how effectively local regulators and businesses adapt. Schools will need to audit and upgrade lighting, training providers will need to verify coach credentials and fee structures, and manufacturers will need to demonstrate compliance with the new chemical limits. How thoroughly these rules are enforced—rather than merely announced—will determine whether they deliver on their promise to raise standards for children's health and consumer safety.
