# Wendy's Gets $1.4B Buyout Offer From Peltz's Trian

Wendy's shares jumped more than 14% on Wednesday after Nelson Peltz's Trian Fund Management and a group of partners offered to buy the struggling burger chain for $7.35 per share in cash, a deal that would take the company private at a valuation of roughly $1.4 billion, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/wendys-peltz-trian-buyout-8f9ab7443eee801608c8058e4a77e862).

The unsolicited proposal represents a premium of nearly 19% over Wendy's closing price of $6.14 on Tuesday. The stock rose 93 cents to close at $7.07 on Wednesday, though it remains down about 26% since the start of the year. Wendy's, based in Dublin, Ohio, confirmed receipt of the proposal and said its board has formed an independent committee to evaluate it.

The offer lands at a precarious moment for a 57-year-old brand once synonymous with square burgers, Frosty desserts and the "Where's the beef?" era. Rising beef prices, intensifying competition, leadership churn and a franchisee bankruptcy have all weighed on the company — and the bid is now drawing a sharp challenge from a fellow shareholder who argues the price is far too low.

## A Company at a "Critical Juncture"

In a letter to Wendy's board, Peltz framed the take-private as a rescue mission rather than an opportunistic grab. "Wendy's sits at a critical juncture," he wrote, citing "significant pressures on the U.S. consumer, economic uncertainty, persistent commodity inflation and intensifying competition, particularly within the QSR (quick service restaurant) burger category."

Peltz argued that going private "will deliver the best outcome for the broader stakeholder base." Trian said private ownership would provide the "long-duration capital needed to restore the business and the franchise system to a position of strength."

The bid consortium includes Trian, Peltz, his partner Peter May and his son Matthew Peltz, alongside restaurant operator Flynn Group and investment firms BlueFive Capital and Sixth Street Partners. Trian already owns about 31 million shares, or 16.3% of the company, according to the AP.

The offer follows a bruising stretch. Wendy's share of U.S. burger chain sales fell from 11.5% in 2019 to 10.4% last year, according to market researcher Technomic, as rivals such as In-N-Out and Five Guys expanded. The chain closed 240 restaurants in 2024 and said earlier this year it planned to shutter up to 358 locations in 2026, and last month one of its largest franchisees, Michigan-based Meritage Hospitality Group, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection after Wendy's tried to terminate its franchise agreement. In May, the company hired Bob Wright, formerly of Potbelly, as its fourth CEO since 2024.

## An Insider Deal Under Fire

The proposal's most sensitive feature is who is making it. Peltz has been a Wendy's director since 1993, served 17 years as chairman and now holds the title Chairman Emeritus, while May and Matthew Peltz also remain directors. That makes the bid an insider transaction — and one shareholder wasted little time attacking it.

In a sharply worded statement, Blackwells Capital said the proposal "dramatically undervalues" Wendy's and "lacks the basic protections of a fair insider transaction," vowing to "pursue every lawful avenue to ensure it does not succeed as proposed," according to the [full statement](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/blackwells-issues-statement-trian-7-200000261.html).

Blackwells laid the company's troubles directly at Trian's feet. "Under their direction, Wendy's has cycled through four chief executives in under three years, cut its dividend by 72%, posted six consecutive quarters of declining U.S. same-store sales, watched its largest franchisee enter bankruptcy, and surrendered the No. 2 position in American burgers," the statement read. "These are not conditions Trian discovered; they are conditions Trian presided over. This is an offer by the Company's longest-tenured fiduciary to purchase, at the bottom, the consequences of his own disastrous tenure."

Blackwells also questioned the timing, noting that Wendy's traded at $20 per share two years ago and that shares were above $9 in August before reports that no bid was coming sent the stock tumbling. "Shareholders are entitled to a process that tests whether $7.35 is what Wendy's is worth — not merely what Wendy's was trading at after its would-be buyer finished talking his book," the statement said. It posed the question at the heart of the dispute: "Was Wendy's being run to win, or being run to be bought?"

## The Market's Verdict: Not a Done Deal

The offer is far from assured. Wendy's stock is trading below the $7.35 bid price — a signal that investors do not treat a deal at that level as imminent and are anticipating either a higher offer, a rejection, or a competing bid.

The mechanics reinforce that caution. TD Cowen analyst Andrew Charles said an independent special committee is expected to evaluate the proposal and that both parties have entered a confidentiality arrangement. "In our view, a take-private makes sense as Wendy's turnaround is likely to be prolonged given the challenged state of the lower-income consumer and soft state of the quick service burger category," Charles wrote in a note to investors.

The move is also company-specific rather than a sector-wide signal. Rivals barely moved on the news, with McDonald's edging up about 0.7% and Yum! Brands up roughly 0.2%, according to [24/7 Wall St.](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/10/28/wendys-surges-14-on-trian-led-1-4b-take-private-offer-mcdonalds-edges-higher-yum-brands-stays-put/), underscoring that the surge is a Wendy's deal story, not a broad rally in fast food.

This is not Trian's first attempt. Peltz considered a take-private bid in 2022 but dropped the idea in January 2023, citing improved business momentum, according to Charles. More recently, reports of a revived bid in August sent shares up 5% before Trian shelved the plan on Aug. 27, a reversal that sent the stock down 13%.

## What to Watch

Wendy's is scheduled to report quarterly results next week, with analysts expecting earnings of 9 cents per share on sales of $531 million, according to FactSet. The company has promised a fuller strategic plan, with Wright insisting earlier this year that the chain's problems "are within our control."

The near-term catalysts are clear: whether the independent committee accepts, rejects or pushes for a higher price; whether a competing bid emerges; and how aggressively Blackwells or other shareholders pursue legal challenges over the insider-ownership conflict.

For Peltz, the stakes are both financial and reputational. The bid tests whether the investor who has shaped Wendy's for more than three decades can be trusted to buy the company he helped steer — or whether, as his critics charge, he is simply trying to own the bottom of a decline. For a brand built on a slogan about where the beef is, the more pressing question may now be who ends up holding the patty.
