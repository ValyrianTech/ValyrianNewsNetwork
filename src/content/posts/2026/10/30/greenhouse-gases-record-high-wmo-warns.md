# Greenhouse Gases Hit Record Highs, WMO Warns

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases all reached new record highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization warned this week, in a bulletin that commits the planet to decades — and possibly centuries — of continued warming. According to the [WMO's annual Greenhouse Gas Bulletin](https://public.wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high), the globally averaged surface concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2) reached 426.8 parts per million, or 153% of the pre-industrial baseline of 1750.

Methane and nitrous oxide, the second and third most important long-lived greenhouse gases tied to human activity, also hit new peaks, the agency reported on 27 October.

## A Year, Another Record

The headline figure carries a pointed message. "Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies."

Barrett's warning was echoed at a press briefing in Geneva, where she stressed that the accumulation of CO2 has consequences far beyond the immediate year. "The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue," she said. "CO2 has a very long lifetime and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries."

Speaking to journalists, Barrett drove the point home. "Every additional part per million matters because it traps additional heat," she said, according to [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/10/1168677). "In effect, we are supercharging the ocean and the atmosphere. That means that heat waves, tropical cyclones, and rainstorms are intensifying faster than our historical baselines prepared us for."

## Concentration, Not Emissions

The bulletin measures the amount of gas that has accumulated in the atmosphere, not the volume emitted in any given year — a distinction the WMO is keen to emphasize. About half of the CO2 emitted annually remains airborne, while the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans. Because CO2 lingers in the atmosphere for such a long time, past emissions will continue to drive warming even as new emissions fall.

The report shows that CO2 growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from an annual average increase of about 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year across the 2011–2020 decade. From 2024 to 2025, the global average rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average, though slightly below the exceptional surge recorded between 2023 and 2024.

That more modest increase partly reflects a recovery of the land carbon sinks in 2025 after the strong 2023/2024 El Niño, whose droughts, drier vegetation and wildfires reduced the biosphere's efficiency. But the respite looks unlikely to last. "We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026 because of fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño which will impact land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024," said WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova.

For context, when the bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 level was 377.5 ppm — nearly 50 ppm lower than today.

## Methane and Nitrous Oxide Join the Records

Methane concentration in 2025 stood at 1,948 parts per billion, or 267% of the 1750 level. Roughly 35% of methane comes from natural sources such as wetlands, with about 65% from human activities including cattle, rice farming, fossil fuel exploitation, landfills and biomass burning.

Nitrous oxide reached 339.1 ppb, 126% above pre-industrial levels, driven by biomass burning, fertilizer use and industrial processes.

The WMO also highlighted the accelerating warming effect that these accumulated gases exert. According to NOAA's Annual Greenhouse Gas Index, radiative forcing from long-lived greenhouse gases rose 56% between 1990 and 2025, with CO2 accounting for about 81% of that increase.

## The Energy Transition's Hidden Risk

One of the more distinctive themes of this year's bulletin is a warning that the clean-energy transition itself carries climate risks. Two potent but less abundant trace gases tied to electricity production and distribution are rising: molecular hydrogen (H2) and sulphur hexafluoride (SF6).

SF6, used mainly as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, has an atmospheric lifetime of more than 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO2. Its increase in 2025 was a record high. Hydrogen concentrations rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024; though not a greenhouse gas itself, its leakage influences atmospheric chemistry and has a strong indirect warming effect.

"Both H2 leakage and SF6 persistence represent potential threats that could undermine the climate benefits of the energy transition," the WMO cautioned, stressing that continued monitoring is essential to detect and prevent unintended consequences.

## What to Watch at COP31

The WMO releases its bulletin each year to give negotiators authoritative scientific data ahead of the UN climate conference. This year's edition arrives just ahead of COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, running from 9 to 20 November 2026 — a summit the agency frames as an opportunity to turn climate commitments into concrete action.

With a powerful El Niño developing and CO2 concentrations continuing to climb in 2026, the report leaves little room for optimism about the near-term trajectory. The longer-term outlook depends on how quickly emissions fall — and on the planet's sinks, whose ability to absorb carbon is itself increasingly at risk.
