# Arizona's Colorado River Lifeline Is Drying Up. Its Designers Warned Us Decades Ago.

Phoenix and Tucson as we know them would not exist without a gravity-defying, 336-mile system of canals, pipelines and pumping plants that cuts through the desert to carry Colorado River water to more than six million people. Now, that system — the Central Arizona Project, or CAP — faces federal cutbacks that could slash its allocated supply by as much as 47%, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/29/nx-s1-6012724/arizonas-lifeline-to-the-colorado-river-is-drying-up-its-designers-warned-us-decades-ago).

The crisis, reported this week by NPR's Alex Hager in partnership with KJZZ, is not a complete surprise. The warnings were explicit — and they were issued by the very people who designed the canal in the 1960s.

## An engineering marvel facing its limits

"It's an engineering marvel," said Rich Weissinger, who spent nearly 30 years working for the organization that operates the canal, most recently as director of field maintenance. Standing inside one of the pumping plants that keeps water moving, he described its purpose in plain terms: "We have lots of people, lots of industry, lots of municipalities that depend on a stable water source, and that's what CAP can bring — a stable, reliable water source."

That reliability is now in question. The 21st century has brought record-breaking drought supercharged by climate change, leaving the Colorado River — which supplies nearly 40 million people across seven states — pinched between supply and demand. In August 2026, the nation's two largest reservoirs, Lake Mead and Lake Powell, [dropped to record lows](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/20/nx-s1-5936459/drought-and-demand-leaves-water-levels-at-two-largest-u-s-reservoirs-at-record-lows), with levels not seen since 1957.

## A warning, largely forgotten

When President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Central Arizona Project into law on September 20, 1968, he promised it would "provide more water for growing cities, more water for expanding industries, for the farmer's crops, and for the rancher's cattle."

But the project's loudest proponents were candid about its limits. Floyd Dominy, then commissioner of the Bureau of Reclamation, forecast large declines in CAP water availability between 1990 and 2030, suggesting supplies would be more than halved in that period. He attributed the drop to growing upstream use — though drought and climate change turned out to be the primary driver.

The architects proposed "augmentation": adding outside water through a connection between the Colorado and Columbia River systems in the Pacific Northwest, or through large-scale Pacific Coast desalination. Neither was ever built.

Congressman Mo Udall, an Arizona Democrat, was certain the fix would come. "Let's assume there is no augmentation in the river — not a drop," he testified in 1968. "I think this is a very violent assumption, because I am as sure as anything in this life that there will be augmentation."

The idea felt so real that Pacific Northwest senators insisted on a provision barring the federal government from even studying cross-basin water transfers for ten years after the law passed.

"There was no willingness to spend money to address a future problem that wasn't here yet, and that I think people were not sure whether it was going to materialize or not," said Tom McCann, a former deputy general manager for the CAP who has extensively studied its history. "The warning that had been issued before, if you want to think of it that way, was largely forgotten."

## Kicking the can down the road

Today's water experts largely resist the temptation to blame the 1960s architects outright. Including one with a uniquely personal perspective.

"I think Stewart and Mo sort of blew it on some of this," said Brad Udall, Mo's son and the nephew of former Interior Secretary Stewart Udall. The younger Udall is a senior water and climate research scientist at Colorado State University.

But he argues his father and uncle deserve some forgiveness. The brothers grew up on a Depression-era farm in rural eastern Arizona, and by the time they reached the halls of power, an era of booming national optimism colored their thinking. The 1960s had brought the interstate highway system and massive dams; the nation was riding high.

"The 1968 idea was we could build our way out of anything," Brad Udall said. "We could engineer our way out of every crisis. We're smart. We're rich. The world's our oyster."

After a years-long legal battle with California that ended in a landmark Supreme Court decision, CAP proponents "just wanted to get this thing across the line," he said. "They figured, like many humans do, we'll do some of the hard work later... We're going to kick the can down the road and hope it all works out."

## The political convenience of augmentation

Chuck Podolak, former director of the Water Infrastructure Finance Authority of Arizona, argues the promise of augmentation served another purpose: it let policymakers dodge a harder conversation.

"I have a strong thesis that this is a way to avoid saying Phoenix is going to grow at the expense of agriculture," he said. Farms and ranches account for roughly three-quarters of all Colorado River water use, and water is far more valuable in cities. But reallocating it from farms to cities is politically fraught. If you can promise new water instead, Podolak said, you can avoid that fight entirely.

He cautioned against treating the 1960s ideas as "founding father documents," seeing them instead as the product of "fallible people" needing short-term political success. "I think we might be naive to assume complete honesty and wonderful intent by everyone back then, just because it's written in black and white on a piece of paper," he said.

## A structural problem, and a legal fight

The deeper issue, researchers say, is a "structural deficit": even before the current shortage era, Arizona was promised an unsustainably large volume of water. Most years, the Lower Basin pulls more from Lake Mead than flows in to refill it.

That imbalance has now landed in court. Nevada sued the federal government in August after the seven basin states failed to agree on a unified plan for managing the river. Arizona moved to join the suit in mid-October, and this week the Central Arizona Project itself [moved to intervene](https://www.kjzz.org/politics/2026-10-27/central-arizona-project-moves-to-join-colorado-river-water-lawsuit-against-the-federal-government).

"The actions by the federal government disregard the Colorado River Compact and other laws governing the Colorado River," CAP general manager Brenda Burman said in a news release. "Those of us who share this precious resource have no choice but to turn to the courts to correct those errors."

The federal plan, which took effect October 1, imposes mandatory cuts on the three Lower Basin states — Arizona, California and Nevada — but only voluntary reductions on the Upper Basin. Under the 2027–2028 rules, the downstream states must cut use by 1.25 million acre-feet per year, a roughly 20% reduction, with forced cuts potentially rising to 3 million acre-feet annually, [according to The Colorado Sun](https://coloradosun.com/2026/10/29/arizona-heavyweight-colorado-river-lawsuit/). Arizona officials say cuts of that scale could effectively dry up the CAP canal system.

## What's next

No quick court decision is expected, and litigation does not steer the states away from a more drastic possibility: suing one another and taking Colorado River matters to the Supreme Court.

Meanwhile, cities are preparing for a drier future. Some are investing hundreds of millions in wastewater recycling — what Phoenix's Max Wilson calls "advanced water purification" — while the CAP explores a transfer deal with San Diego County's Carlsbad desalination plant. Podolak believes ocean desalination deserves serious consideration, but the question, he said, is "at what cost? And are the people of Arizona, the ratepayers of Arizona, the elected leaders of Arizona, willing to pay or invest in that cost right now?"

John Berggren, regional policy manager for the nonprofit Western Resource Advocates, who researched the CAP's conception for his Ph.D., offers the most direct lesson from history.

"I wouldn't say it's helpful to look back at the architects and blame them, but it's helpful for today," he said. "We don't have an unlimited supply. We're probably not going to build the new big pipeline anytime soon, so let's find every way to tighten our belts here."

His conclusion is unavoidable: "Everyone across the Colorado River basin is going to have to help chip in and use less water because that's how the system was designed."
