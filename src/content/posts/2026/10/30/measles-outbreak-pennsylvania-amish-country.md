# Measles Outbreak Shakes Pennsylvania's Amish Country

Lancaster County, Pennsylvania — home to the largest Amish population in the United States — has become the epicenter of the nation's worst measles outbreak in 35 years, and the crisis is beginning to shift long-held attitudes toward vaccination among the region's "Plain communities." According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/measles-outbreak-vaccination-amish-mennonite-pennsylvania-8f4fcd6e089e09a94c61a41d0ea474f4), Pennsylvania has recorded more than 1,350 measles cases this year, more than a third of them in Lancaster County alone — figures community leaders say substantially undercount the true toll, since many infected people never see a doctor.

The outbreak is prompting some Amish and conservative Mennonite residents to accept vaccines for the first time, even as it hardens skepticism in others. The dynamic reflects a fundamental truth public health experts have stressed throughout the crisis: the Plain communities are not isolated, and their health decisions ripple across the entire region.

## A Historic Outbreak, an Undercounted Toll

Pennsylvania's caseload makes it the hardest-hit state in a national outbreak that has shattered records. The state has recorded three measles-associated deaths in Lancaster and two in other counties — five in total — according to the same AP accounting, with community leaders warning that many cases never reach official notice.

The national picture is equally sobering. As of Oct. 1, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention had confirmed 3,887 measles cases this year and the highest number of measles-associated deaths in decades, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/05/pennsylvania-measles-outbreak-hospitalizations-deaths). When Pennsylvania crossed the 1,000-case mark in early October, state health officials counted 1,004 confirmed cases and 198 hospitalizations; Lancaster County alone accounted for 391 cases, with the highest number of infections in the 24-49 age range, the newspaper said.

The outbreak began in April west of Philadelphia and spread steadily through the summer and fall. It now stands as the worst U.S. measles year since 1991.

## Why the Plain Communities Are at the Center

Roughly 45,000 Amish and 6,000 Old Order Mennonites live in and around Lancaster County, according to the Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies at Elizabethtown College. Vaccination rates among them have long lagged the general population — but experts caution that the gap is driven more by culture and habit than by religious doctrine.

"There's nothing in Amish theology or historic documents that you can find that specifically addresses or is opposed to immunization," Steve Nolt, director of the Young Center, told the AP.

Allen Hoover, an Old Order Mennonite and former administrator of the Parochial Medical Center in New Holland, offered a blunter explanation for why many Plain families skipped shots. "The overwhelming reason had nothing to do with being against vaccinations," he said. "It was simply, 'Well, we never did before, and we came through all right.'"

Attitudes have alternately warmed and cooled over the years, most recently cooling in reaction to strict COVID-19 public health measures, community leaders and scholars say. A 2022 Franklin & Marshall survey found that the vast majority of Mennonite families reported vaccinating their children, versus about three in ten Amish families.

## The Broader Vaccination Decline

The outbreak is unfolding against a national backdrop of eroding vaccine coverage. Just 92.4% of U.S. kindergarteners received the required MMR shots in 2025-26, down from 95.2% in 2019-20, according to the CDC. Pennsylvania's rate fell to 93.2% last year, and Lancaster County's stands at just 88% — below the 95% threshold that makes outbreaks unlikely.

Crucially, the decline is not confined to the Plain communities. Berwood Yost, director of the Center for Opinion Research at Franklin & Marshall College in Lancaster, cautioned against scapegoating the Amish. "Though Plain communities are an easy and recognizable target, declining childhood vaccination rates in our county are not limited to the Amish," he wrote in a recent commentary. "Kindergarten vaccination rates have declined in every central Pennsylvania county since 2020."

Dr. Anne-Marie Derrico, chief medical officer at the nonprofit Union Community Care, pointed to the pandemic's legacy of distrust. "The COVID vaccine just created a lot of distrust, a lot of conspiracy theories," she said. "There's still kind of an overall distrust of vaccines." As Derrico noted, the worlds are deeply intertwined: "We have Plain patients coming to the city, we have patients in the city who don't vaccinate, who are going to school, going to other programs with other children."

## Signs of a Shift

Despite the entrenched skepticism, there are signs the outbreak is changing minds. Lancaster County Commissioner Alice Yoder recalled that during Pennsylvania's last major measles outbreak in 1991, "there were lines of people in the Plain community that would come and get vaccinated." A similar dynamic appears to be taking hold now.

Dr. D. Holmes Morton, who founded the Central Pennsylvania Clinic in Mifflin County, said his team is "having more success at having vaccine days." And a Plain farmer whose adult siblings fell ill told the AP he decided to get vaccinated himself: "I thought, if I get it, I'm going to be done" — with vegetables to harvest and tobacco to pick.

Yet resistance persists. Ephraim Zook, an Amish farmer who opposes vaccines after his family contracted measles, told the AP: "We just felt that measles is a natural thing."

The stakes of closing that gap are high. "Measles is really the lion king of infectious diseases when it comes to transmission," said Michael Osterholm, who directs the University of Minnesota's Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy. "No one in the United States of America who is susceptible to the measles virus... should feel safe from measles. No one."

Dr. Diego Hijano of St. Jude Children's Research Hospital framed vaccination as a communal duty. Getting the shot, he said, is "an act of love to your community. You will get protected yourself. But in truth, you are actually protecting everyone around you."

## A Politicized Response

The outbreak has also become entangled in a bitter political dispute. Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro, a Democrat, has clashed publicly with U.S. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. over how measles deaths are counted, with Shapiro calling Kennedy "incompetent," the newspaper reported.

Kennedy has publicly questioned whether measles directly caused the state's recorded fatalities, and for a time the CDC's national dashboard — which Kennedy oversees — listed only two measles deaths in the U.S. this year, even as Pennsylvania reported five, the newspaper said. The discrepancy has fueled confusion over the true toll. Shapiro, meanwhile, has moved to expand access to the MMR vaccine in affected communities.

"Measles does not care about your county, your faith, or your social circle," Shapiro said in August. "I urge everyone to take this seriously and to discuss any concerns with their trusted health care provider."

## What's Next

The outbreak's trajectory remains uncertain. Case rates have declined from their September peak, but some doctors expect a resurgence as colder weather drives more people indoors. Neighboring states have also been hit hard — New York Gov. Kathy Hochul declared a state of emergency in early October, citing 108 cases as of Oct. 3, and Ohio, which also has a significant Amish population, has reported more than 200 cases this year.

Perhaps most consequentially, international health officials are set to meet in November to determine whether the United States and Mexico have lost their measles-free status — a designation the U.S. has held since eliminating domestic spread in 2000.

For experts like Hoover, the path forward runs through patient relationship-building rather than aggressive mandates. Asked how long it takes to earn the trust of Plain communities, he offered a sobering estimate: "Twenty years."

That timeline may be far longer than the outbreak can wait — a reality that makes the modest signs of new openness in Lancaster County both significant and fragile.
