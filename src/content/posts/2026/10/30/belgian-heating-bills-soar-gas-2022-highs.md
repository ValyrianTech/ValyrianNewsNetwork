# Belgian Heating Bills Soar as Gas Hits 2022 Highs

Belgian households are facing a sharp jump in heating costs as winter sets in, with European natural gas returning to crisis-era prices for the first time since 2022. An average household on a variable gas contract could pay more than €1,000 extra this winter compared with last year, according to estimates from a leading energy specialist.

The spike comes as temperatures drop and Belgium enters its heating season at a moment of historically low European gas reserves and mounting geopolitical tension. Energy prices are now feeding directly into headline inflation, which reached 5.26% in October 2026, even as the broader economy held up.

## Gas Back Above €100/MWh

On 28 October, natural gas traded at €100 per megawatt-hour on the Dutch TTF benchmark, the European reference market, a level not seen since December 2022, when the continent was reeling from the collapse of Russian pipeline supplies. It had already climbed to €97/MWh two days earlier and above €90/MWh the week before — a swift, sustained run upward.

The drivers are both geopolitical and structural. Markets are pricing in fears of a new escalation in the Middle East, while Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and tensions with Iran have pushed Brent crude above $115 a barrel. On the supply side, European reserves are unusually low as demand rises with the cold, and the end of Russian gas orders from January is set to deepen Europe's reliance on US and Qatari liquefied natural gas.

Damien Ernst, a professor at the University of Liège and a prominent energy commentator, said the situation amounts to an acute gas crisis reminiscent of 2022. He warned that Qatari volumes are unlikely to return before December, leaving heavy dependence on the United States heading into the coldest months.

## The €1,000 Winter

For households, the arithmetic is stark. Ernst estimates that a household with average consumption on a variable gas contract will pay more than €1,000 more this winter than last. The figure was calculated at around €90/MWh — meaning that if prices hold near €100, the final bill could be higher still.

Fixed-contract holders are protected until their deals renew, but variable-contract and heating-oil users face the sharpest exposure. Heating oil, or mazout, is common in rural Wallonia and Flanders, where households buy fuel in bulk and are directly exposed to spot-price swings.

The squeeze extends well beyond the boiler room. Energy is now the main engine of Belgian inflation, which reached 5.26% in October, [according to Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779801/article/2026-10-29/belgique-les-prix-de-lenergie-dopent-linflation-mais-lactivite-economique), citing the Institut des comptes nationaux and the National Bank of Belgium. Yet the broader economy has proven resilient: GDP rose 0.3% in the third quarter of 2026 after three quarters of near-stagnation, a sign that activity is holding up even as purchasing power erodes.

## A Modest Federal Response

Federal Energy Minister Mathieu Bihet has announced a €15 million support package, split equally between the *Fonds social chauffage*, which helps heating-oil users, and the *Fonds Gaz et Électricité*, which covers gas and electricity.

The headline measure is a temporary increase in heating-oil aid. Currently capped at €0.20 per litre — €300 for a 1,500-litre delivery — the intervention would rise progressively to €0.50 per litre when the price reaches or exceeds €1.775 per litre, or up to €750 for a standard delivery. The scheme applies to deliveries between 1 December 2026 and 28 February 2027.

Bihet framed the aid as a matter of dignity. "Heating should never become a luxury," he said. "When the price of heating oil rises sharply, it is the most vulnerable households who feel the consequences most harshly."

The help, however, is tightly means-tested. Eligibility is capped at a gross annual income of €25,817.27, plus €4,777.36 per dependent. People on reduced-contribution (BIM) status qualify, and no income check applies to an isolated BIM person or a fully BIM household; those in debt mediation may also be eligible. Applications go through the local CPAS within 60 days of delivery, with an invoice and supporting documents — the aid is not automatic.

## The Gap in the Safety Net

Critics note that the package is small relative to the scale of the bill increase. The €7.5 million earmarked for heating oil can only reach a limited number of households, and the scheme excludes most middle-income families who face higher bills but earn too much to qualify. Natural gas users are not covered through the heating-oil fund at all.

Households looking for alternatives face their own constraints. Demand for wood and pellet heating is rising, but supply is limited. "If everyone switched to wood, we'd run out of forests within two winters," warned Benjamin Wilkin, director of the energy group Energie Commune. Heat pumps, meanwhile, remain costly to install, and Quentin Jossen of the Climact consultancy pointed to electricity prices that stay above those of gas or wood, the risk of grid saturation, and a Belgian building stock that is insufficiently insulated for low-temperature heating.

Structural pressures are mounting on the electricity side as well. Belgian electricity production fell in September to 4,021 GWh, its lowest level since 2008, as maintenance on two nuclear reactors increased reliance on imports.

## What to Watch

The coming weeks hinge on whether gas prices hold near €100/MWh into December. If they do, pressure is likely to build on the federal government to expand support well beyond the €15 million envelope — a debate that will play out against the backdrop of ongoing federal budget talks.

Beyond this winter, the deeper questions remain open: how quickly Europe can rebuild its gas reserves and diversify supply, how many eligible households will actually claim the means-tested heating-oil aid, and what happens to the many families who fall outside it. For now, Belgium's households face a colder season with a thinner cushion than many had hoped. As Ernst put it, this is "an acute gas crisis worthy of 2022" — and the authorities, he argues, must confront it as such.
