# China's 12345 Hotlines Drop 'Zero Bad Review' Targets

Multiple cities and provinces across China are dismantling rigid "zero negative review" assessment standards for the 12345 government service hotline, a shift aimed at freeing grassroots officials from form-over-substance metrics and curbing the exploitation of complaint systems by a small minority, [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34316736) reported on October 30.

The reform, which took decisive shape in Ya'an, Sichuan, explicitly allows "reasonable existence of unsatisfactory work orders" and prohibits faking results or pressuring residents to be "satisfied," according to the report. Jiangsu province, Xiamen in Fujian, and Ningbo in Zhejiang have adopted similar measures, excluding genuinely hard-to-resolve cases from performance assessments.

## Why 'Zero Bad Reviews' Became a Problem

The 12345 hotline is China's unified government service line, consolidating numerous departmental complaint channels into a single entry point for the public to report problems and seek help. In many localities, satisfaction and resolution rates became key performance metrics for officials.

That pressure created perverse incentives. Some staff chased "good reviews" rather than solving problems; some departments focused on steering residents toward positive ratings instead of addressing grievances; and some fabricated "satisfied" work orders so that favorable data masked real complaints, noted a [People's Daily commentary](https://paper.people.com.cn/rmrb/pc/content/202610/27/content_30187825.html) published October 27.

## The Strain on the Frontlines

The costs fell disproportionately on frontline workers. Chen Feng, dean of the School of Sociology at Beijing University of Technology, whose team has tracked 12345-driven city governance since 2019, put the imbalance starkly: truly malicious or unreasonable demands may account for 5% to 10% of cases, "yet this tiny fraction consumes 50% to 60% of grassroots energy."

A street-level worker told China Newsweek that contesting a single negative review required preparing materials against 620 exclusion clauses and sometimes listening to two-hour call recordings to mark timestamps. The evaluation meant to drive service had begun consuming service capacity.

The exploitation was sometimes brazen. Wang Bo, a sociology PhD student at Wuhan University, documented in a 2023 field study a resident who, after getting diarrhea a week after buying cold noodles, demanded 4,000 yuan from the vendor; after repeated complaints leveraging the assessment pressure, the town government paid 1,000 yuan.

Out-of-scope complaints are not always malicious, however. A Baotou hotline official told China National Radio in 2024 that roughly two-tenths of more than 2,000 daily complaints fell outside the accepted scope, [China Youth Daily](https://news.cyol.com/gb/articles/2026-03/04/content_dqAB3NH07V.html) reported. Some out-of-scope calls reflect genuine hardship, requiring officials to guide callers toward legal aid or other proper channels.

## A National Policy Push

The local reforms align with a top-down directive. In June 2025, the State Council General Office issued an opinion requiring localities to optimize evaluation mechanisms and indicators, scientifically set handling time limits and completion standards, and avoid setting unrealistic targets or one-sidedly pursuing rankings. It also called for genuinely reducing burdens on the grassroots.

"Allowing the public to give 'dissatisfied' evaluations and have them truthfully reflected is the only way problems can be more precisely identified and solved," the People's Daily commentary argued. "Only by shedding the pressure of one-sidedly pursuing high satisfaction can staff 'travel light.'"

## The Accountability Tension

The reform is not an abandonment of satisfaction metrics but a rejection of their rigid, one-sided pursuit. The distinction matters: removing genuinely impossible cases from assessment risks creating a loophole where real failures are excused as "objectively limited." The safeguard lies in robust screening of malicious complaints and verified exclusion criteria.

As The Paper's editorial framing put it, breaking the excessive pursuit of "zero negative reviews" is "not lowering service standards, but preventing 'negative reviews' from becoming leverage for a minority to extract extra benefits."

The existence of hundreds of exclusion clauses itself illustrates the bureaucratic complexity the reform seeks to simplify. From a Guigang, Guangxi, operator's perspective, the shift reflects a grounded reality: "We are just operators, not omnipotent; most people won't blame us or give a bad review just because a problem isn't solved."

## What to Watch

How localities calibrate the boundary between out-of-scope demands and negligent failure will determine whether the reform strengthens accountability or dilutes it. The broader test is whether performance metrics can be recalibrated so that evaluation serves problem-solving — rather than forcing problem-solving to serve the metrics.
