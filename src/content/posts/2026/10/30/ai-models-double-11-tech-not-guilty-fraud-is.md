# AI Models 'Invade' Double 11: Tech Not Guilty, Fraud Is

As China's Double 11 shopping festival approaches, AI-generated virtual models are spreading rapidly across e-commerce platforms—some with visibly deformed hands, extra limbs, or missing heads. In a commentary published Friday, [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34366819) argues that the technology itself is not to blame; deception is, and merchants who hide AI-generated imagery behind a veneer of authenticity should be held to account.

The piece builds on an [investigative report](https://www.cqnews.net/web/content_1565303387029966848.html) by the outlet's reporters Wei Jiaming and intern Wang Qinyi, published a day earlier, which documented distorted AI models, near-invisible AI labels, and a booming low-cost industry for generating fake fashion imagery.

## What the Investigation Found

The anomalies were hardly subtle. Reporters observed childrenswear models with anatomically impossible hands, a model with three legs, and "headless" figures on Douyin storefronts. Suspicious AI imagery even appeared in the reviews section: a buyer account that had purchased from one store five times posted a positive review of a dress accompanied by a photo showing a model with "three feet."

Most such images carried no AI label at all. Some flagship stores used AI models while telling customers that no real-life photos were available—without disclosing the images were synthetic.

The economics explain the surge. A real photo shoot—model, venue, and photographer—costs at least 200 to 300 yuan per garment for nine images. AI generation runs as little as 6 yuan per image, or 5 yuan at lower resolution, delivered in about 30 minutes against a turnaround of one to two days for a real shoot. Vendors sell AI womenswear prompt collections for 16.9 yuan (866 copies sold) and livestream workflow tutorials for 9.9 yuan (some 37,000 copies sold).

## The Core Argument: The Test Is Deception, Not AI

The commentary concedes a point that often gets lost in the outcry: using AI-generated virtual models for product display is not, in itself, illegal. Chinese law does not prohibit the normal application of AI in e-commerce, and even real-life photos can be heavily retouched and detached from reality. As one netizen put it, "Real-life displays aren't necessarily fully authentic either; I don't reject well-made AI."

"AI is not the original sin; concealment is the trap," the commentary states. The legal yardstick, it argues, is never "was AI used?" but whether the displayed image matches the actual product and whether information was deliberately concealed.

The commentary points to Article 55 of the Consumer Rights Protection Law, under which merchants found committing fraud face punitive "refund plus triple compensation." It also cites the Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content, which took effect September 1, 2025, requiring clear, perceivable labels on AI-generated images and video. Failing to label while misleading consumers, the piece argues, infringes their right to know.

## A Legal Consensus Emerging

Beijing lawyer Fang Caihong, quoted in the investigation, drew the same line. "The law prohibits misleading and confusion, not technology itself," she said. "The law does not prohibit AI technology, but AI technology is not a ground for exemption from liability."

She added that whether AI models constitute fraudulent advertising depends on whether the product information shown matches the real item—and whether merchants make false claims or conceal defects. Where significant discrepancies are combined with deceptive customer service or removed AI labels, fraud is readily established.

Platforms are not bystanders. Taobao launched an AI fake-image crackdown in March 2025; Douyin upgraded its AI-labeling and metadata-traceability functions in September 2025; and Xiaohongshu and Pinduoduo issued AI governance rules in August 2026, with Pinduoduo threatening delisting and bans for images that violate physical, biological, or objective laws.

## What to Watch

The dispute captures a broader tension in China's e-commerce ecosystem: merchants under relentless cost pressure, consumers demanding authenticity, and regulators racing to keep pace with fast-maturing generative AI. The commentary's conclusion is less a call for a ban than for discipline—clear labeling, accurate product representation, and platforms acting as gatekeepers of consumer trust.

"Tools have no good or evil; responsibility lies with those who use them," it states. As Double 11 sales gavel nears, the question is whether labeling—and enforcement—can move fast enough to keep the AI models from becoming the story.
