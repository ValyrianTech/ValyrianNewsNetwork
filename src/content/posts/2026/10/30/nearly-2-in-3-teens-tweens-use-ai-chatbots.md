# Nearly 2 in 3 Teens and Tweens Now Use AI Chatbots

Artificial intelligence chatbots have become a routine part of American childhood. Two surveys released in late October find that nearly two-thirds of tweens and teens now use AI chatbots—and that certain kinds of use, particularly for companionship and emotional support, are linked with worse mental health symptoms, including a greater history of suicidal thoughts and attempts. The findings were [reported by NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/30/nx-s1-6022278/ai-artificial-intelligence-teens-jed-common-sense-media) on Oct. 30.

The research lands at the intersection of two rising public-health concerns: excessive screen time and emotional dependence on algorithmic companions. Together, the surveys suggest that AI has moved from novelty to daily habit for a generation—whether or not adults are ready for it.

## Adoption started early, and age limits aren't holding

One survey, conducted by the online-safety nonprofit [Common Sense Media](https://institute.commonsensemedia.org/tween-teen-census-2026), drew on a nationally representative sample of 1,211 young people ages 9 to 17. It found that nearly half of American 9- to 12-year-olds have used an AI chatbot or companion, including more than a third of 9-year-olds. By age 13, nearly three-quarters of kids have used chatbots. Across all respondents, 65% have used AI in their free time, including 77% of teens.

Most popular chatbots officially require users to be at least 13. That rule, the data suggest, is widely ignored. "Age gating doesn't appear to be working among the general population," Dr. Supreet Mann, Common Sense Media's director of research and the report's author, wrote in the findings.

A striking pattern emerges from the survey: using AI is not the same as enjoying it. Only 11% of teen users and 15% of tween users say they enjoy AI "a lot"—the widest gap between use and enjoyment of any screen-media category Common Sense measured. When asked to name the one platform they couldn't live without, just 4% chose ChatGPT; YouTube was the top pick for 66% of tweens. The likely explanation, the report suggests, is that young people treat AI as a tool—the way earlier generations used a search engine or a word processor—rather than as entertainment.

## Emotional use linked to poorer outcomes

The second survey, from the mental-health nonprofit [JED Foundation](https://jedfoundation.org/understanding-relationships-between-ai-mental-health-students-report/), drew on more than 5,500 middle- and high-school students across 21 U.S. schools and districts between October 2025 and April 2026. It found that 90% of those students had used an AI chatbot, and that nearly two-thirds used it both for schoolwork and as a companion—or when they were stressed, lonely, anxious or overwhelmed.

The report broke down usage by purpose: 58% for schoolwork, 57% when sad, stressed, lonely or overwhelmed, 28% for relationship or problem-solving advice, and 12% for friendship and companionship. The last two categories, researchers found, were most strongly correlated with worse well-being—greater anxiety and depression symptoms, more loneliness, and a higher history of suicidal thoughts. Companionship use was also linked to a history of suicide attempts.

"What stands out in these findings is not just how often teens are using AI, but also its role in their emotional lives," said Dr. Kayla Anderson, JED's vice president of research and evaluation and the report's author. "This warrants careful attention from the many stakeholders deploying these tools at a rapid pace as well as from policymakers who can help build the guardrails to protect youth mental health."

JED also found significant demographic differences: middle schoolers, girls, and many students from historically marginalized groups—including American Indian/Alaska Native, Black, Hispanic and multiracial youth—were more likely to turn to AI when emotionally struggling than high schoolers, boys and white students.

## Correlation, not causation—but a possible feedback loop

Both research teams caution that the data do not establish that AI causes poor mental health. "It's unclear, based on these data, whether AI use is contributing to poor mental health and well-being, or whether students who are already in distress in certain ways are more likely to turn to AI," Anderson said.

Independent experts suggest the relationship may run in both directions. Anne Maheux, a developmental psychologist at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill who was not involved in either survey, told NPR the findings are "remarkable" in capturing how quickly AI use has spread. She described a possible mechanism: AI eases emotional distress in the moment, but reliance on it may prevent young people from developing the skills to tolerate and navigate ordinary daily stress. In a forthcoming study of middle schoolers, Maheux and her colleagues found that students who used chatbots for companionship grew lonelier over time.

"So kids who do have worse mental health are seeking out AI to help them," Maheux said. "But that AI use may be exacerbating those mental health challenges instead of helping."

The teenagers NPR interviewed described use that straddles the line between tool and crutch. Arjun Sharda, 15, of Round Rock, Texas, uses ChatGPT and Gemini to practice sample test questions—but also recalled asking the chatbot "Will I be OK? Will my GPA still be fine?" during a bout of chemistry-test anxiety. McKenzie Craig, 17, of Raynham, Massachusetts, uses chatbots to prioritize tasks when she is overwhelmed and to get feedback on essays.

## Policymakers are moving—unevenly

The surveys arrive amid a patchwork of new state-level rules. On Oct. 28, New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed legislation requiring AI companies to verify users' ages and to offer at least one verification method that doesn't rely on government ID, [Engadget reported](https://www.engadget.com/2299823/new-york-governor-signs-ai-chatbot-child-safety-law/). Chatbots used by minors will face restrictions on simulating human relationships, soliciting unprompted emotional disclosures, and promoting self-harm. Violations can carry civil penalties of up to $25,000 each.

The state action follows a May 2026 advisory from the U.S. Surgeon General on the harms of screen use, which called for bell-to-bell school phone bans and design changes from technology companies, [according to the advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/us-surgeon-generals-advisory-warning-on-the-harms-of-screen-use.pdf). Several other states have coordinated consumer-protection lawsuits against OpenAI, and Washington State has enacted a law targeting AI companion chatbots specifically.

## What to watch

The central question remains unresolved: whether heavy AI use worsens adolescent mental health, whether distressed teens self-select into it, or—as Maheux's preliminary work suggests—both. Longitudinal studies will be needed to pin down direction and magnitude; neither public report summary quantified how large the correlations were.

For parents, schools and regulators, the surveys offer a more immediate signal. The gap between how much young people use AI and how little they enjoy it suggests the technology is filling a functional niche—homework help, task management, emotional regulation—that existing supports aren't meeting. "While AI may offer youth an immediate and accessible outlet," Anderson said, "it cannot replicate the developmental, protective, and necessary benefits of human connection."

The next test is whether new laws and design guardrails can close that gap—or whether, as the Common Sense data suggest, young people will keep finding their way around the rules.
