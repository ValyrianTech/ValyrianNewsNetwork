# CBS Poll: Democrats Keep House Edge on Price Pain

Democrats retain their advantage in the fight for control of the U.S. House of Representatives, according to a new CBS News/YouGov Battleground Tracker poll released Sunday, with voter frustration over prices emerging as the defining force of the 2026 midterm campaign.

The survey, conducted September 30 to October 2 among a nationally representative sample of 2,166 U.S. adults, carries a margin of error of plus or minus 2.5 percentage points. According to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/midterm-poll-battleground-tracker-democrats-house-advantage/), the network's House model shows Democrats holding an edge for a narrow majority of seats and a lead in national vote preference.

"Our CBS News House model estimate shows the Democrats retain their edge for a narrow majority of seats and their lead in national vote preference," wrote Anthony Salvanto, the network's executive director of elections and surveys, alongside co-authors Fred Backus and Jennifer De Pinto. "The advantage is driven by opposition to the Trump administration's policies and a Republican base that is less likely to turnout."

## Prices Dominate the Economic Debate

When Americans are asked what they use to gauge the economy, the poll found, almost all give the same answer: prices. That sentiment has persisted since the pandemic, and it now sits at the center of the midterm contest. Most voters say they believe President Trump's policies are pushing food and grocery costs higher, and most think the war with Iran is driving up gas prices. A majority also feel the administration has not focused enough on lowering costs.

The findings extend a trend from the network's [prior Battleground Tracker](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/poll-trump-battleground-tracker-house-control-2026-midterm-elections/), published in September, which similarly tied the race to Trump and found Democrats ahead in the national vote.

## "Reluctant Republicans" and Turnout Trouble

The GOP's challenge appears to be compounded by an enthusiasm gap. Republicans are less likely than Democrats to say they will definitely turn out, and they rate Trump's handling of inflation far lower than his performance on immigration or his overall job. The poll describes a bloc of "reluctant Republicans" who are sitting the election out — or in some cases switching — in part because of dissatisfaction with prices.

That dissatisfaction is measurable. Republicans who say the administration is not focused enough on prices are less likely to say they will definitely vote. Likewise, Republicans who feel their own candidates are not talking about the cost of living are less likely to turn out. And Republicans who believe Trump is performing worse in his second term than his first are nearly 20 points less likely to commit to voting.

Democrats face their own turnout question. The party likely needs strong participation from younger voters to win, but older voters are consistently more likely to say they will show up — a pattern reflected in public voter records.

## Hispanic Voters and the Redistricting Map

Hispanic voters could prove pivotal, the poll suggests. The share of Hispanic likely voters backing Republicans is lower than the share Trump won in the 2024 presidential election. That shift could matter in newly redistricted districts where Republicans would need to hold onto their 2024 gains. For Hispanic voters, the economy and inflation rank as the top issues, but immigration and deportation policy also weigh heavily; most say the administration is deporting more people than they expected.

## Trump Looms Over Both Parties' Candidates

More voters say they are casting ballots to oppose Trump than to support him. Even among Republicans, most want a candidate who mostly — but not always — agrees with the president, and candidates who oppose new tariffs are finding broader resonance. The one issue breaking toward Republicans is the debate over transgender athletes.

Democrats, meanwhile, are seen as talking about the cost of living more than Republicans are, though neither party is viewed as addressing it "a lot." Democratic voters, like their Republican counterparts, prefer candidates who do not always fall in line with the national party, and endorsements from self-identified Democratic socialists draw mixed reviews.

## Iran, AI and the Broader Backdrop

Foreign policy and technology are shaping the race in subtler ways. Some Republican candidates have called for an end to the Iran war, a position most voters favor in a candidate — including about a third of Republicans. More voters believe the war would be likelier to end if Democrats win Congress.

On artificial intelligence, voters of both parties tend to think the technology will create more problems than it solves and will take, rather than create, jobs. A candidate promising to restrict AI's use and development holds broad appeal — and those voters lean heavily Democratic, a pattern that repeats on the question of data centers.

## What to Watch

The poll arrives as the midterm campaign enters its final stretch, with control of the House — and the size of either party's majority — still unsettled. The September tracker put Democrats at 228 seats, above the 218-seat majority threshold, while independent aggregators have tracked a generic ballot favoring Democrats for much of the year.

What remains to be seen is whether the Democrats' polling edge translates into turnout. With the economy defined in voters' minds by the price of groceries and gas, both parties now have roughly four weeks to convince their bases that the stakes justify showing up.
