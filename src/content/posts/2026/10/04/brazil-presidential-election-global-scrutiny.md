# Brazil Votes as Election Draws Global Scrutiny

Brazil held the first round of its presidential election on 4 October, an exceptionally close contest between incumbent left-wing President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva and right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro that analysts say will shape the country's global alignment, regional politics and relationship with Washington. Polls point to a technical tie, with a runoff set for 25 October if no candidate crosses the 50% threshold.

## A Race Too Close to Call

According to a Datafolha poll released on 1 October, Lula held 42% of first-round voting intentions against 38% for Flavio, with a margin of error of two percentage points. In a simulated runoff, Lula led 48% to 45%. Measured among valid votes, Lula stood at 45% and Flavio at 40%, [Valor International](https://valorinternational.globo.com/politics/news/2026/10/02/lula-leads-flavio-bolsonaro-42percent-38percent-days-before-first-round.ghtml) reported.

The gap has narrowed steadily since campaign advertising began in mid-August, when Lula led by six points. It fell to five points in late September and now stands at four. Thirteen candidates are on the ballot, but the race has polarized around the two frontrunners. Augusto Cury (Avante) registered 4%, while Ronaldo Caiado (PSD) and Renan Santos (Missao) each had 3%.

Roughly 158.7 million voters were eligible, with voting mandatory for literate citizens aged 18 to 70. Brazilians also chose all 513 Chamber of Deputies seats and 54 of the Senate's 81 seats, shaping the legislative terrain the next president will face.

## Two Visions, Two Foreign Policies

Much of the international attention stems from the sharply opposed foreign policies the candidates represent, [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/world/20261003/f8c2303decc94bc482d7df6de5e2248f/c.html) noted in its analysis.

Lula, 80, is seeking a fourth term. A veteran left-wing leader, he supports multilateralism and globalization, promotes Latin American regional integration, and works to expand Brazil's influence in international organizations. Flavio, 45, a senator and lawyer, is the eldest son of imprisoned former President Jair Bolsonaro. He is expected to continue his father's foreign policy: playing down regional integration, actively developing ties with the United States, and aligning with Washington on major international questions.

"The outcome of this presidential election will determine Brazil's position on the future international stage," said Chris Thornhill, a visiting professor cited by Xinhua, who added that the two candidates diverge clearly on climate change, Global South development, regional integration and reform of international bodies.

## The Bolsonaro Shadow and US Pressure

The contest is inseparable from the legal fate of Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a 27-year sentence for an attempted coup and has endorsed his son. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/4/brazils-lula-and-bolsonaro-close-campaigns-ahead-of-critical-election) reported that Flavio has pledged to seek his father's release if elected and has echoed his father's unsubstantiated doubts about Brazil's electronic voting system, which analysts say could lay the groundwork to contest a defeat.

Beyond the candidates, the election has become a flashpoint over US influence. President Donald Trump has not formally endorsed Flavio but has said he is watching the election "very closely." Lula's camp has accused Washington of meddling, and Brazil in July refused visas for two senior State Department officials over suspected interference.

The analysis in Xinhua pointed to the broader regional context: after Trump launched his second term, his administration intensified pressure across Latin America, and several right-wing forces in the region have unseated left-wing incumbents. Analysts said a Flavio victory would signal a major Latin American economy tilting toward the United States and would strengthen a regional "right-wing comeback."

## A Bet on Caution, Not Confrontation

Not every observer expects the White House to intervene overtly. Ian Bremmer, founder of the Eurasia Group, told the BBC News Brasil in an interview published by [Correio Braziliense](https://www.correiobraziliense.com.br/mundo/2026/09/7510050-interferir-na-eleicao-do-brasil-tende-a-sair-pela-culatra-e-trump-sabe-disso-diz-ian-bremmer.html) that direct US involvement is unlikely.

"It is an incredibly tight election," Bremmer said. "If you forced me to bet, I would say Lula is ahead by a very small margin." He argued that Washington has stepped back from earlier threats because interference would more likely hurt Flavio than help him, with Lula able to use the charge of foreign meddling at the ballot box.

Bremmer added that even under a Flavio presidency, China would remain Brazil's main trading partner, as it was during Jair Bolsonaro's tenure. He also warned of a subtler risk: well-funded right-wing social media accounts, some based in the United States, spreading disinformation.

## Why Brazil Matters

At root, analysts say, the attention reflects Brazil's weight. It is Latin America's largest economy and a major exporter of agricultural and mineral commodities, with an outsized role in global supply chains. It is also a leading voice among emerging and developing nations and a key member of BRICS.

Marcos Pires, a political economy professor at Sao Paulo State University, framed the global interest as "essentially attention to the positioning of a key emerging major power in the global system, and an examination and expectation of the direction of the future world order."

## What to Watch

If no candidate wins an outright majority, the two frontrunners will meet in a runoff on 25 October. How supporters of the eliminated candidates divide will add fresh uncertainty, especially given the two frontrunners' high rejection rates, which Datafolha measured at 45% each.

For now, the contest is a snapshot of a polarized country and a world watching closely. The result will test whether Brazil continues on its multilateralist path or realigns toward Washington, with consequences that extend well beyond South America.
