# Kim Jong Un Watches Medium-Range Missile Drill

North Korean leader Kim Jong Un personally observed a medium-range strategic missile launch training exercise in the early hours of October 3, state media reported, the latest in a string of military demonstrations that have pushed inter-Korean relations to a new low. The missile was fired from the country's eastern region toward the East Sea, also known as the Sea of Japan, and hit its designated target, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/world/20261004/dc727484b02642a7a53511012d5802a9/c.html), citing the Korean Central News Agency (KCNA).

The drill marks the latest escalation in a volatile stretch on the Korean Peninsula, coming just days after land-mine blasts inside the Demilitarized Zone wounded South Korean soldiers and Pyongyang rejected Seoul's demand for an apology.

## The Launch and Its Claims

According to KCNA, the training was designed to sharpen the fire-control skills of missile troops operating what North Korea calls a "hypersonic strategic weapons system." The "strategic" designation implies a nuclear-capable design. Kim was accompanied at the site by senior munitions and missile officials, and photographs distributed by North Korean state media suggested that his daughter, Kim Ju Ae, and his wife, Ri Sol Ju, also watched the drill, as [Yonhap News Agency](https://cn.yna.co.kr/view/PYH20261004013900881) reported.

The flight-distance estimates told a story of their own. South Korea's Joint Chiefs of Staff said the missile flew more than 700 kilometers, while Japan's Ministry of Defense detected a flight of approximately 680 kilometers. KCNA, for its part, claimed the weapon struck a point at coordinates about 1,000 kilometers over the East Sea — figures that do not reconcile. Kim's sister, Kim Yo Jong, seized on the gap between the South Korean and Japanese estimates as proof that neither country could track the missile in flight, claiming that whenever North Korea applied its "wavelike trajectory mode," the missile's first skip point eluded enemy tracking.

## Kim's Framing: Defense and Deterrence

Kim Jong Un cast the exercise as defensive, describing improvements to weapons-systems performance as "a form of exercising nuclear deterrence," according to [Chosun Biz](https://biz.chosun.com/en/en-international/2026/10/04/5IOCLIUFC5BT7A4JKDOUEVLCVM/).

"Establishing a powerful and reliable nuclear deterrent and demonstrating that our military's counterstrike means are in a constant state of mobilization is a very important measure and a responsible activity for national security," Kim said. He added that North Korea must continue "without interruption, responsible efforts to make our adversaries constantly and repeatedly recognize the reliability and lethality of strategic offensive means."

In the Xinhua account, Kim said a strong deterrent force should manage various threats and guarantee a secure environment for national construction.

## A Diplomatic Breakdown

The missile launch did not occur in isolation. On September 21, land-mine blasts roughly 10 meters south of the Military Demarcation Line inside the 4-kilometer-wide DMZ injured three South Korean soldiers on a reconnaissance mission. A joint inspection with the United Nations Command concluded that the mines were planted by North Korea and that the North had contravened the Korean War armistice agreement.

South Korean President Lee Jae Myung urged Pyongyang to return to dialogue even as his military publicly demanded an apology. On October 2, Kim Yo Jong responded by calling Seoul "really despicable," ridiculing Lee's overture as "the climax of the farce that everyone should watch," and warning that the North was "ready for anything."

The next morning, the missile went up. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/03/north-korea-launches-ballistic-missile-after-seoul-demands-apology-over-wounded-soldiers) noted, the timing suggested a deliberate linkage between the mine dispute and the launch.

Seoul responded swiftly. South Korea's military heightened its monitoring and said it would share intelligence with the United States and Japan, while the presidential office's National Security Council convened an emergency meeting. Seoul characterized the launches as violations of UN Security Council sanctions and, in the words reported by The Guardian, "a serious act that does nothing to help manage the current situation."

## Analysis: Calculated Signaling

Analysts suggested the launch may have been calibrated as much for a domestic and inter-Korean audience as for Washington or Tokyo. Cheong Seong-chang, vice-president of the Sejong Institute in Seoul, told reporters that the test might be an attempt by Pyongyang to draw a response from South Korea to Kim Yo Jong's statement — and that the North might also have tested a new type of missile.

The drill fits a broader pattern. North Korea has conducted missile tests on more than 10 occasions in 2026, several of them timed to protest joint South Korean–U.S. military exercises. Pyongyang has also accelerated its hypersonic glide-vehicle program, designed to evade South Korean and Japanese missile defenses, and has commissioned new naval assets and staged live-fire exercises in recent weeks. Since 2024, Kim has formally abandoned unification as a national goal and declared Seoul a "permanent adversary" and "primary foe."

At the same time, the international picture is unusually mixed. President Donald Trump said on October 3 that he "gets along very well with Kim Jong-un" and suggested that North Korea holds 112 nuclear weapons, according to Chosun Biz — a remark that underscores how diplomatic warmth and military escalation are proceeding on separate tracks.

## What's Next

North Korea's choices in the coming weeks will hinge on how Seoul responds to Kim Yo Jong's rhetorical barrage and whether Pyongyang follows through on its threat to sever ties with the South permanently. Two variables bear watching: whether North Korea conducts further launches after the drills that regional militaries are now tracking, and whether the United States and its allies can translate renewed intelligence-sharing into a coordinated response. For now, the message from Pyongyang is unambiguous — the weapons program will continue, and it will continue in public view.
