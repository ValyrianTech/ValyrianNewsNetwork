# Tennessee Execution Fails; Prisons Chief Resigns

Tennessee's attempt to execute death row inmate Christa Pike by lethal injection collapsed on the night of Sept. 30 when she survived two full doses of the drug pentobarbital — an outcome her attorneys called "torturous" and one experts say is unprecedented in the history of American capital punishment. In the fallout, state Department of Correction Commissioner Frank Strada resigned on Oct. 3, and Gov. Bill Lee halted executions for the rest of the year and ordered an independent review.

Pike, 50, who had been on death row since she was 18, was rushed to a hospital where she remained unconscious and on a ventilator in critical condition, according to [WPLN News](https://wpln.org/post/christa-pike-was-on-a-ventilator-and-unconscious-after-failed-tennessee-execution-attorneys-say/). It was not immediately clear whether she suffered brain damage. Had the execution succeeded, she would have become the first woman put to death in Tennessee in more than 200 years.

## What Happened in the Death Chamber

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/02/nx-s1-5988832/christa-pike-reignites-death-penalty-debate), Pike was strapped to a gurney at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution in Nashville and given what was meant to be a lethal dose of pentobarbital. After several minutes, she said her arm was burning and asked whether it was supposed to feel that way, then asked, "please try up on my shoulder higher." A second dose was administered. She appeared to fall unconscious but continued breathing and at times snoring. Her lawyers made emergency calls, and the execution was halted after more than an hour.

The Death Penalty Information Center (DPI), a nonpartisan nonprofit, said Pike was the first person in the history of capital punishment to survive after being injected with lethal drugs. Other people have survived failed attempts when staff could not establish IV lines.

"This failed attempt is the worst we've ever seen and unlike any other botched execution in the modern era," DPI executive director Robin M. Maher said in a statement.

Pike's attorney Randy Spivey, who witnessed the procedure from inside the death chamber, counted at least seven needles in her left arm, one of which "appeared to be bent when it was pulled from her," [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/03/tennessee-christa-pike-frank-strada) reported.

Pike's legal team believes complications with the IV lines caused the pentobarbital to enter her body rather than her bloodstream — the veins had "blown," they argue, so the drug was entering her arm tissue in whole or in part. Rather than remedying the failure, the execution team prepared and administered a backup set of the drug.

"We at this point do not know the ramifications of the chemical torture that she has endured," Spivey said at a press briefing, calling what happened "torturous" and "cruel."

## A Pattern of Failures

It was Tennessee's second botched attempt of 2026. In May, the state halted the execution of Tony Carruthers after medical workers could not insert a second IV line; he groaned in pain and blood oozed from puncture wounds. Lee later granted Carruthers a one-year reprieve but declined to order a review at the time — a decision he did not explain when reporters pressed him after Pike's ordeal.

Strada, who came to Tennessee from Arizona where he oversaw three botched executions, was the architect of the state's new lethal injection protocols. Attorneys alleged that Pike's failed lethal injection was the fifth "botched" execution carried out under Strada's leadership in two states since 2022. He resigned effective later in October.

"Serving as commissioner of the Tennessee department of correction has been a great honor, and I continue to believe the department carried out its responsibilities in accordance with the established protocol," Strada said in his resignation statement. "Even so, stepping aside is in the best interest of Tennesseans as the independent review moves forward."

Lee thanked Strada for his "dedicated service" and said the state would investigate what went wrong. "So far as we know, the Department of Correction followed exactly the protocol as outlined," the governor said. "There was no reason to expect it to not work … But something didn't go well last night, and we need to find out why."

Critics said a resignation alone would not fix the underlying problems. "Frank Strada's resignation does not change the fact that Tennessee is shrouding its executions in secrecy," Kit Thomas, a death row attorney in the federal public defender's office in Nashville, said. "Until there is transparency and accountability, botched executions will keep happening."

## Secrecy and Medical Ethics Under Scrutiny

Tennessee uses a single drug, pentobarbital, in executions, and its lethal injection process has long been shielded from public view. Media witnesses' view is kept behind a curtain until IVs are established, so none could offer a visual account of the failed IV placement. State rules require participation of pharmacists, physicians, and other healthcare workers — a practice [WPLN News](https://wpln.org/post/after-failed-execution-health-workers-say-tennessees-rules-flout-medical-ethics/) and KFF Health News reported conflicts with the American Medical Association's code of ethics, which bars physician participation in executions. More than 40 doctors and nurses had written to Lee days before the execution calling for a pause and for the removal of health workers from the process.

Pike also suffered from thrombocytosis, a platelet disorder, that her legal team argued created a "dual, paradoxical risk": increased clotting at the IV site or catastrophic bleeding. Pentobarbital is a notoriously corrosive compound that can cause burns akin to drain cleaner if it seeps into flesh. The Tennessee Supreme Court found those concerns speculative when it denied Pike's stay on Sept. 23.

## A National Reckoning

The incident has reignited the national debate over capital punishment. Executions in the U.S. have been on the rise — 47 people were put to death nationwide in 2025, the most since 2009, with Florida accounting for 19. Polling shows public support is softer than it has been in decades: Gallup found 52% of Americans favored the death penalty in 2025.

Reaction split sharply along political lines. Republican U.S. Sen. Marsha Blackburn, who is running to replace Lee, said the state should "bring back the electric chair and deliver justice for these victims swiftly." Her Democratic opponent, Jerri Green, condemned the practice as "inhumane, expensive, and biased" and pledged to extend the moratorium.

Internationally, Amnesty International called on the U.S. to abolish the death penalty, saying the country remains "increasingly isolated" as the practice declines worldwide, according to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/3/tennessee-prisons-official-resigns-after-christa-pikes-failed-us-execution).

## What's Next

Pike's attorneys have filed an emergency motion arguing that the failed execution amounted to "cruel and unusual" punishment and seeking to have her sentence commuted — a question that could ultimately reach the Tennessee Supreme Court. The independent review ordered by Lee will examine what went wrong, though critics note it was not launched after the Carruthers failure in May.

As of Oct. 4, Pike remained in critical condition and executions in Tennessee were paused for the remainder of the year. Whether the state can rebuild confidence in its execution process — or whether this proves a turning point in a national debate already shifting — may depend on what the review finds and how quickly the legislature acts.

Pike was convicted of and admitted to the 1995 murder of her fellow teenager and classmate Colleen Slemmer, whom she saw as a love rival. A pentagram was carved into Slemmer's chest. "It took me numerous years to even realize the gravity of what I'd done," Pike said in an earlier petition for clemency. "It sickens me now to think I had the ability to commit such a crime."
