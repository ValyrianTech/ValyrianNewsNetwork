# Belgium Budget Talks Stall at Federal and Brussels Levels

Belgium's federal top ministers resumed budget negotiations on Sunday, while the Brussels regional government entered a second day of its own budget conclave, as both authorities struggle to rein in public finances under mounting deadline pressure. The federal government is seeking 10 billion euros in savings by 13 October, when Prime Minister Bart De Wever is due to deliver his State of the Union address, while Brussels aims to cap its 2027 deficit at 719 million euros ahead of a policy statement in the regional parliament on Monday.

The twin negotiations, reported by [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/federale-topministers-praten-vandaag-voort-onderhandelingen-ove~1790599460224/), reflect deepening tensions between federal and regional authorities over who bears the burden of Belgium's fiscal consolidation—and signal that even the political calendar may not be enough to force a deal.

## Federal Talks Enter the Hard Phase

Federal top ministers met Saturday from 13:00 to 18:00 for what was described as a warm-up day, agreeing to reconvene at 12:30 on Sunday, with the possibility of talks running late into the night. Political journalist Pieterjan De Smedt of VRT NWS warned the tone would sharpen considerably.

"Then it's about the 10 billion euro total exercise, and all the difficult themes come to the table: the VAT increase, savings in healthcare, whether more money should come from wealthy people," he said. "Classically, this goes along with the necessary drama before one reaches an agreement."

The agenda for the first day covered growth prospects and the so-called "nullijn"—a review of whether measures already agreed in the coalition pact have delivered. Deputy Prime Minister Vincent Van Peteghem of CD&V struck an optimistic note on arrival, saying that "with common sense and a focus on efficiency, we can arrive at a fair exercise."

Sunday's session is expected to be harder. De Wever's "white binder" of proposed measures—which was not tabled Saturday—was due to be presented, and that is where the deepest disagreements are anticipated. The Monitoring Committee had estimated the government must find at least 7.7 billion euros to keep the budget under control; the coalition has set its target higher, at 10 billion. Belgium must also comply with European spending rules, and officials have set the 13 October deadline specifically to avoid the delays seen the previous year, according to [Business AM](https://businessam.be/de-wever-begint-vandaag-zijn-zoektocht-naar-10-miljard-euro-met-bilaterale-gesprekken/).

## Brussels Conclave Drags Into a Second Day

The Brussels government, led by Minister-President Boris Dilliès of the liberal MR party, began its budget conclave at the Iris Tower at 09:00 Saturday. By around 21:30 no agreement had been reached, and talks continued into the night before ministers decided to resume on Sunday from 10:00—after roughly 15 hours of negotiation, [BRUZZ reported](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/politiek/live-volg-hier-de-aanloop-naar-het-begrotingsconclaaf-van-de-brusselse-regering-2026-10-02).

The Brussels executive is targeting a deficit of 719 million euros by the end of next year, moving toward a balanced budget by 2029. The sticking points include higher fares for the MIVB public transport network—two figures of 10 or 15 million euros in extra revenue have circulated—more expensive garbage bags, a growth trajectory for the economy, and the budget of the Common Community Commission (GGC/VGC), which handles welfare and homelessness policy. Talks over the GGC budget, [BRUZZ noted](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/politiek/spanningen-op-brussels-begrotingsconclaaf-de-rook-niet-opgehelderd-2026-10-03), had barely begun.

Tensions have been visible. Budget Minister Dirk De Smedt of the Anders party and Mobility Minister Elke Van den Brandt of Groen reportedly clashed during Saturday afternoon, though other participants downplayed the incident. De Smedt was blunt about the pace of talks.

"We could have been finished by Saturday afternoon. Apparently one must fill a whole weekend. There is insufficient willingness to land. Principles are more important," he said. Dilliès, for his part, offered a more measured line: "You always have to stay positive."

Minister Ahmed Laaouej of the francophone Socialist Party (PS), commenting on the roughly 120 million euros still to be negotiated, said simply: "It will work out."

## A Federal-Regional Clash of Narratives

The two negotiations are not unfolding in isolation. Federal savings plans—including cuts to asylum and migration receipts, the Beliris fund for Brussels infrastructure, and the so-called Brussels Deal—directly affect the regional budget. The Brussels government has refused to approve the cooperation agreement on the European Stability Pact, disputing the Brussels Deal and Beliris, according to [BRUZZ](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/politiek/brusselse-regering-weigert-goedkeuring-akkoord-europees-stabiliteitspact-2026-10-02).

The sharpest exchange came earlier, when De Wever criticized the region's finances in stark terms. "And the Brussels Region is bankrupt. That budget is not a budget. It's laughable. That ship is sinking to the bottom," he said, according to VRT NWS. Frédéric De Gucht, chairman of the Anders party, fired back: "What right does Bart De Wever have to speak about the Brussels budget?... Here, prosperity is created that flows to the other regions."

De Wever enters the federal talks with a political card of his own. The Flemish regional budget was closed with a "black zero"—a balanced budget—and political scientist Carl Devos told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/02/besparingen-op-vlaams-niveau-zijn-troef-voor-federale-onderhande/) that this strengthens the premier's hand against francophone parties. "De Wever can now say to the French-speaking parties: 'We in Flanders have done our homework, you must do yours too, otherwise the federal deficit only grows larger,'" Devos said.

A federal source, meanwhile, dismissed the link Brussels has drawn between the Brussels Deal and its own budget effort, telling VRT that "the link with the Brussels Deal is actually nonsense."

## What to Watch

The most immediate test comes Monday morning, when Dilliès is due in the Brussels Parliament for his policy statement. Failure to secure a budget deal beforehand would be politically awkward for a government that was itself formed only after 613 days of deadlock. Dilliès is the mayor of Ukkel and heads a coalition of MR, PS, Anders, Groen and Vooruit.

For the federal government, the clock runs to 13 October. Should the coalition fail to agree, it may be forced to operate on "provisional twelfths"—rolling monthly budgets tied to the previous year's spending—an outcome the government has explicitly said it wants to avoid.

The larger question is structural. Belgium's federal deficit target of 10 billion euros, layered atop regional consolidation, underscores how tangled the country's multi-level finances have become—where Brussels' fate depends on federal decisions in areas like asylum and infrastructure, and where Flemish austerity can be wielded as a negotiating weapon. Political scientist Devos' framing may prove the most telling: in Belgium's budget politics, regional bookkeeping is never just about the region.
