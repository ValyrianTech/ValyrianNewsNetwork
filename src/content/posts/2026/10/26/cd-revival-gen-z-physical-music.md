# CD Revival: Gen-Z Embraces Physical Music Again

The compact disc is staging an unexpected comeback, driven by Generation Z and a preference for physical media, according to Belgian public broadcaster [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/nostalgie-support-physique-mais-moins-cher-que-le-vinyle-le-cd-est-de-retour-chez-la-gen-z-11805688). The revival echoes vinyl's earlier resurgence but offers younger listeners a cheaper and faster way to own and collect music.

The trend is clearest in the United States, where CD sales rose 16% in the first half of 2026 to reach 16.3 million units, according to [data from Luminate](https://luminatedata.com/reports/20260715midyearreport), a music-industry analytics firm. Over the same period, vinyl sales grew by just 2.4% — still positive, but well behind the CD's pace.

## A Cheaper Route Into Collecting

For independent labels, the CD's appeal lies in its accessibility. "The CD offers accessibility to buy music on a physical format because it's true that vinyl has a certain cost," said Grégory Noël, director of the Brussels independent label Exag' Records, speaking to RTBF. Vinyl typically retails for €25 to €30 or more per record, positioning the CD as a more affordable alternative for fans building a collection.

Noël suggested that high pricing may even be holding vinyl back. "The majors tried to impose a high price to make large margins, and ultimately, it may be them who are slowing vinyl sales," he said.

The format also carries practical advantages for artists. While vinyl production lead times run to roughly three months with growing delays, a CD can be manufactured in four or five weeks and delivered a week later. For emerging artists releasing a four- or five-track EP, Noël said, a CD gives them something affordable to sell after concerts and in stores.

## The Nostalgia Effect

At the heart of the shift is a striking generational change in listening habits. According to the Luminate research cited by RTBF, 60% of Gen-Z members — those born roughly between 1997 and 2012 — now say they listen mostly to music from the 1990s or earlier, up from just 18% in 2021.

"They may be young people who had access to their parents' record collection," said Sylvestre Defontaine, thematic director of culture and music at RTBF. "There's a nostalgia effect that was also present with vinyl."

The appeal extends beyond sound. "I live in a completely dematerialised world, so I want to own something physical," Defontaine said, describing the draw of "a CD with a cover, an illustrated booklet inside." He also noted a return to the album as a complete work — with a beginning, middle and end — rather than a loose compilation of singles.

The demand is visible at the ground level. Guy Tournay, a musician with the Belgian rock project The Bernadette Maries, said fans regularly ask for CDs at concerts. "Since we got the vinyls, at every concert we get at least three or four requests for CDs," he said. "It's really all ages — from 21 to 57 — but I'd still say it's mostly the younger ones asking for CDs."

## An American Boom, Arriving Late in Europe

For now, the data-driven evidence of a CD revival comes mainly from the United States. In Belgium and Europe, the growth in CD purchases is not yet measurable, Defontaine cautioned — but he expects the trend to arrive with a lag. "Pop-culture booms in the United States always end up arriving here with a little delay," he said.

The broader physical-music market is also shifting. Mass-market retailers such as Target and Walmart have increased their share of US physical album sales, and collectable, multi-edition releases — particularly from K-pop acts like BTS, ENHYPEN and Stray Kids — have dominated CD charts. Even excluding K-pop, US CD sales still rose 6.7% year-on-year, suggesting the trend runs deeper than a single genre.

Vinyl, meanwhile, is maturing rather than collapsing. It remains the premium format, still generating more than three times the CD's revenue in the US.

The revival also extends to other analogue and early-digital devices. Searches for classic iPods have surged on resale platforms, [according to data compiled by Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/02/21/ipods-music-genz-nostalgia), a signal that younger listeners are seeking distraction-free ways to engage with music. "Old technology does not remain boring forever," the music publication Sound Matters observed. "Leave it long enough, and it becomes novel again."

## What to Watch

The coming months will reveal whether the CD revival crosses the Atlantic in measurable numbers. Much may depend on whether listeners who grew up with CDs return to the format — or whether, as with vinyl before it, a new generation simply claims it as its own. Either way, physical music is proving resilient, and increasingly it is fandom, collectability and the desire to own something real — not just sound quality — that keeps it alive.
