# Six Citizens Sue Trump Over White House Media Ban

Six private U.S. citizens [filed a federal lawsuit on Monday](https://knightcolumbia.org/documents/gxpwhx3jin) against President Donald Trump and three senior administration officials, arguing that the administration's policy of barring disfavored news organizations from White House press facilities violates not only the rights of journalists but the public's own constitutional right to receive independent information about its government.

The case, identified by the [Knight First Amendment Institute](https://knightcolumbia.org/cases/roth-v-trump) as *Roth v. Trump*, No. 1:26-cv-09327 (S.D.N.Y.), was brought by the Knight Institute at Columbia University and the Media Freedom & Information Access Clinic at Yale Law School. It reframes the long-running White House access fight around news consumers rather than news organizations. [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-media-ban-citizens-lawsuit-f5a70baaa97dba1bdd4cda365b5f635d) reports that the suit was filed in federal district court in New York against Trump and three key aides, including White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles.

## A New Kind of Plaintiff

The lawsuit's most striking feature is who is bringing it. Previous challenges to the administration's press restrictions have been filed by the news organizations themselves — CNN, MS NOW, Politico, and The Associated Press among them. This case is filed on behalf of an audience.

The six plaintiffs, as [listed by the Knight Institute](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/trumps-ban-on-the-free-press-violates-the-first-amendment-rights-of-ordinary-citizens-new-lawsuit-says), are Kenneth Roth, a human rights advocate, legal scholar and former longtime executive director of Human Rights Watch; Daniel Froomkin, a media and political journalist and editor of Press Watch and Heads Up News; Beverly Gage, a Pulitzer Prize-winning historian and professor at Yale University; Peter Schmidt-Nowara, a civics and social studies teacher at Brooklyn Technical High School in New York, according to AP; Benjamin Wittes, editor-in-chief of Lawfare and a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution; and Ethan Young, a student at the University of Pennsylvania and editor-in-chief of The Daily Pennsylvanian.

Each says they rely on White House reporting for their teaching, scholarship, journalism or advocacy. "We wanted to put the injury to the public at the center of the conversation," Jameel Jaffer, executive director of the Knight Institute, told AP. "It's one thing for CNN to say, 'We are here because the public needs us.' It's another for a member of the public to say that interference with the White House press corps undermines our right to information about the government."

## The Legal Theory

The Knight Institute said the suit argues that the spaces the White House has opened to the press corps constitute a "public forum" under the First Amendment, and that the government cannot constitutionally exclude news organizations from them on the basis of viewpoint. The complaint, as quoted by AP, asks for "a declaration that the policy is unconstitutional because it imposes restrictions on a limited public forum that are viewpoint-based and unreasonable," along with an injunction barring enforcement of the policy. The suit invokes the public's established right to receive information from a willing speaker.

"The First Amendment protects the public's right to know what its government is doing. The White House cannot decide what information Americans receive by punishing critical reporting," said Katie Fallow, the Knight Institute's deputy litigation director. "When the White House expels reporters because of their coverage and threatens others with expulsion, it isn't just the press that suffers — the public loses access to independent reporting and gets something closer to government propaganda."

David A. Schulz, director of Yale's MFIA and co-counsel, added that journalists are the public's "eyes and ears" in spaces they cannot access themselves, and that the president's policy of expelling disfavored journalists "harms the public and our democracy."

The relief sought extends beyond restoring any single outlet's access to protecting every outlet that covers the White House now and in the future. The full complaint argues that excluding some outlets inevitably deprives the public of the independent reporting of others, since every journalist "must now account for the possibility that a critical or ill-advised word about the president may result in their access being summarily restricted or revoked." According to AP, the suit contends that "the ultimate injury here … is to the democratic public."

## The Defense

The White House pushed back in a statement attributed to an unidentified official. "No person may demand access to the Oval Office," the official said, adding that outlets that peddle "misinformation and defamation are completely capable of reporting their manufactured stories from directly outside."

## Background: A Widening Press Fight

The lawsuit lands amid a broader conflict between the administration and the press, which the president has himself described as a "ban on the free press." On October 13, in a case brought by CNN, MS NOW and Politico, a federal judge in Washington, D.C., blocked the White House from barring those three outlets from White House press facilities. AP reports that a judge has extended an order preventing enforcement of the ban while that case proceeds, but that CNN's pool coverage of Trump remains affected: the network has been denied its usual slots in the video pool covering the president's activities and travel, and has threatened further action.

Separately, the AP has litigated its exclusion from the Oval Office, Air Force One and some events after declining to adopt Trump's renaming of the Gulf of Mexico. The New York Times is challenging a Pentagon policy requiring escorts for journalists, and ABC is contesting a dispute over its broadcast licenses.

## Analysis: Why This Case Matters

The reframing carries strategic weight. Courts have historically evaluated access disputes through the lens of the press entity's own rights, and government lawyers have often characterized such challenges as narrow professional grievances. By placing ordinary citizens at the center, the plaintiffs create a potentially vast stakeholder class and force a more fundamental question: whether excluding reporters injures the public that depends on them. Jaffer framed the stakes bluntly: "The First Amendment reflects a recognition that democracy requires an informed public, and that an informed public requires an independent press," he said. "The president's attack on the independence of the White House press corps should be understood in that light. It's an attack on the foundations of our democracy."

There are real obstacles ahead. It is unsettled whether White House press areas qualify as a public forum, and whether citizens — as opposed to credentialed correspondents — have Article III standing to challenge access decisions. The answers could determine whether the case proceeds on the merits at all. Because appellate courts have ruled differently on aspects of press access, the underlying questions could eventually reach the Supreme Court.

## What to Watch

The immediate test is procedural: how the S.D.N.Y. court treats the public-forum theory and the plaintiffs' standing. The Knight Institute said the filing includes a motion for a preliminary injunction. The administration's response to that motion — and whether it reasserts national security grounds — will shape the next phase. Meanwhile, the parallel D.C. case and the AP litigation continue to run on separate tracks, each capable of reaching the high court first.

For now, the six plaintiffs have done something no major newsroom had: they have argued, in court, that the harm from a media ban belongs to the people who read the news.
