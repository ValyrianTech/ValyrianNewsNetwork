# One in Ten Flemish Private Care Homes in Financial Peril

Roughly one in ten private residential care homes in Flanders is in such serious financial difficulty that the quality and continuity of its services are at risk, according to the first-ever financial inspection of the sector by the Flemish government. The findings, based on inspections of every care-home operator in Flanders and Brussels between 2022 and 2025, have been handed to Flemish Welfare Minister Caroline Gennez, who said public money misspent in the sector takes care away from residents and staff.

The wide-ranging audit, [reported by VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/26/doorlichting-woonzorgcentra-vlaamse-overheid-minister-gennez/), examined all 463 "inrichtende machten" — the operating bodies that run care facilities — of which roughly two-thirds are private.

## A Sector Under Strain

Among private operators, the picture is mixed. About three-quarters are financially healthy, and 17 percent — roughly one in six — face "limited financial difficulties" that could be resolved with targeted measures. But nearly 10 percent have problems severe enough that service provision is in jeopardy.

According to Gennez, some struggling homes stay afloat only because of support from the larger care group they belong to. "That group sometimes makes investments that benefit residents and staff," she said, suggesting the financial health of individual facilities can mask deeper fragility.

## Questions Over Public Money

The inspection also turned a spotlight on how care homes spend public subsidies. Operators were required to demonstrate with invoices and accounting records where government money went. Public operators, run by local authorities, raised no concerns. But among private operators, inspectors flagged questions in 95 cases — nearly one in three.

The problems varied. In some cases, public money was lent to sister or parent companies and recorded as "income still to be received." In others, funds were simply donated, or the subsidies appeared not to have been used for the care home's operation at all — for example, to market another branch of the business or to pay management fees at head office.

Gennez stressed that the findings do not amount to proof of fraud and sought to reassure residents and families. "I always assume good intentions on the part of care entrepreneurs," she said. "Public money that is used incorrectly takes care and support away from residents and from staff. We must act firmly against that."

She pointed to earlier measures to improve financial transparency, including the introduction of sector-specific transparent accounting. "Making a profit is not forbidden. But money that we invest in care must go to the residents, the staff and the infrastructure," she said. "The more transparency, the better we can continue to guarantee the affordability of residential care centres."

## Industry Pushback

The findings drew a sharp response from Vlozo, the umbrella body representing private care entrepreneurs. Its CEO, Candice De Windt, criticised the report for scrutinising only private operators on financial health while public facilities were assessed solely on subsidy use.

"We find it regrettable that again only private facilities are analysed. Public facilities remain entirely out of the picture. We want transparency, but then for every player," she said. She also rejected the suggestion that resources flow to shareholders or affiliated entities: "We want to respond to that formally: that is not the case."

De Windt argued that the financial difficulties stem from funding levels rather than mismanagement. "The minister has cut the subsidies to the bone. The day price covers barely 89 percent of the costs. That means every organisation has to contribute at least 11 percent more. So the framing around profit in care — sorry, that is totally wrong," she said. She called for an open debate on what elderly care should cost, conducted "in a completely transparent way."

## What the Inspection Covers

Financial inspections assess the financial health of a care provider's operating body, whether its accounting complies with Flemish welfare regulations, and whether subsidies are spent efficiently and for their intended purpose, according to the [Department of Care](https://www.departementzorg.be/nl/hoe-inspecteert-zorginspectie-wat-na-een-inspectie). The inspections are almost always announced in advance, and providers are given 14 days to respond to a draft report before it is finalised. A strict separation of functions means the inspectorate itself does not decide on recognition, permits or subsidies.

## What's Next

The report lands in the middle of a broader funding squeeze across Belgian elderly care. In neighbouring Wallonia, the regional government has moved to slow the indexation of public funding for care homes, a step employers' federations warn will cost the sector heavily by 2029 — a dispute that mirrors the Flemish argument over whether the problem is accountability or inadequate funding.

For Gennez, the priority is tightening oversight of public money. For private operators, it is a higher day price and a level playing field. How those two demands are reconciled will determine whether the one in ten facilities now at risk can keep their doors open — and what that means for the vulnerable residents who depend on them.
