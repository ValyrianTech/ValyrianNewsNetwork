# Belgian Multi-Property Owners Brace for Higher Taxes

Belgians who own more than one property are growing anxious as the federal government weighs new taxes on real estate holdings to plug a budget gap, [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/budget-federal-les-multiproprietaires-craignent-d-etre-plus-taxes-a-l-avenir-11805822) reports. The idea sits on the negotiating table of the "Arizona" coalition as it searches for roughly 10 billion euros in savings and revenue in a multi-year budget. For the roughly 900,000 Belgians who hold several properties, the concern is simple: they may be the ones asked to pay.

## A Budget Hole and a Search for Cash

The federal government remains deadlocked over a budget agreement, with parties still divided on how to raise the money needed. Among the proposals under discussion is the notion of taxing multi-property owners more heavily. Belgium counts a little over 5 million property owners; the vast majority own a single home, but around 900,000 own more than one. It is this group the federal government is eyeing as a potential source of revenue.

Concretely, Prime Minister Bart De Wever's plan would apply a surcharge to owners once their combined cadastral income — the notional rental value used as a property-tax base — exceeds 5,000 euros. That threshold would be reached sooner in some regions than others, with Brussels and the two Brabant provinces likely to be the most affected.

## Owners Divided Over Fairness

Reactions among those directly concerned are mixed. Marc, who owns several properties in Mons, is philosophical. "As an owner, you don't like paying too much tax, but you can also consider that there needs to be a certain equality in taxation," he told RTBF. "Taxation on income in Belgium is considered very high, and it is. It's less so on property, so one can consider that a government could move in that direction."

Gilles, another owner, disagrees sharply. "Once again, we're taxing people who have enough means to invest, who maybe worked hard all their lives, who put money into houses to have rental income. And once again, we're taxing in the wrong place," he said.

That view is shared by many multi-property owners. "It's always the same people who get taken to the cleaners," said Olivier de Clippele, vice-president of the national union of property owners and co-owners. He argued that property taxes have already risen sharply, since regions and municipalities draw on the same buildings. "The federal government is coming too late. It's adding itself on top of all the tax increases we're currently seeing. It's too much."

## Not All Multi-Property Owners Are Equal

Notaries, for their part, caution that a blanket approach would be unfair, because the term covers very different situations. "On one hand you have those who bought one or two houses, who are preparing their pension. It's an investment, an investment in good faith," said Sylvain Bavier, spokesperson for notaire.be. "And then you obviously have large multi-property owners who sometimes own dozens of buildings. A distinction must be made between the small and the larger ones, where taxation could be differentiated."

De Clippele warned that the measure could have wider economic consequences. "If you send this signal that property investment is being overtaxed in Belgium, you create dismay among investors. We'll be cooled off, we'll invest less, which will be bad for the Belgian economy."

## What to Watch

The debate reflects a broader tension at the heart of Belgium's budget negotiations: how to close the gap without overburdening households already stretched by high income taxes. The multi-property surcharge remains one option among many as the coalition struggles toward an agreement.

For the owners caught in the middle, the outcome hinges on whether the government opts for a broad levy or a more targeted one that spares small investors preparing for retirement. Until a budget deal is sealed, the question of who pays — and how much — remains open.
