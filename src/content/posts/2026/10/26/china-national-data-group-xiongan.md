# China Launches National Data Group in Xiong'an

China formally established the National Data Group (国家数据集团) on 20 October, a new central state-owned enterprise headquartered in the Xiong'an New Area of Hebei province, tasked with developing and commercialising the country's most valuable data resources as a formal factor of production. The initiative, announced in a [People's Daily report](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261026/7d8c04375605452a91e54b2e83e98635/c.html), marks Beijing's most concrete institutional step yet toward building a national data-element market and feeding high-quality datasets to its domestic artificial intelligence industry.

The Group is a wholly state-owned limited company funded jointly by seven shareholders: the State Council, the Xiong'an New Area Administrative Committee, the three major telecom operators (China Telecom, China Unicom, and China Mobile), and the two dominant power utilities (State Grid and China Southern Power Grid). Its core mandate spans data aggregation, governance, circulation, and trading, alongside the construction of national integrated data infrastructure.

## Why the shareholders matter

The shareholder lineup is not incidental. In Beijing's framing, it bundles the two infrastructural pillars on which the data economy depends: telecommunications and energy.

"Looking at the shareholders, several major enterprises have amassed scarce and high-quality data resources," said Sun Yi (孙毅), a professor at the School of Economics and Management at the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences. "The operators have accumulated massive data such as communications of over a billion users, and the two grid companies cover real-time operational data across national power production, transmission, distribution, and consumption. Such data is large in volume and highly structured, a rare 'rich mine' among data elements."

Sun argued that the combination enables an integrated chain running from data to computing power to energy. "The operators have nationwide network, cloud computing, and computing-power network resources; the grids can provide stable and reliable power for computing facilities," he said, adding that this helps data genuinely be "supplied, circulated, and well-used" (供得出、流得动、用得好).

That "compute-energy coordination" (算电协同) concept is a distinctly Chinese framing that treats data centres, networks, and the electricity grid as one national system. China Southern Power Grid's head of digital affairs, Yu Yun (余芸), said the utility would "proactively serve the national strategy and fully support the Group's construction and operation," pledging to build trusted data spaces for the energy sector and to connect with the Group's national operating platform.

## The name as a mission statement

A Group official explained the corporate name as a statement of purpose. "'National' means the starting point and goal of our work must revolve around 'what the nation needs and what the people hope for'; 'Data' reflects the core business — we must stay focused on the responsibility of developing and utilizing data resources without deviation," the official said.

"In the future, we hope to work with our shareholders to enlarge the value of data and lower the cost of data circulation," the official added.

## Xiong'an as a "super test bed"

The choice of Xiong'an is freighted with symbolism and practicality. The new area, announced in 2017 as a flagship "future city" and a relocation target for Beijing's non-capital functions, has been built digital-first from the ground up.

According to the report, the Xiong'an Urban Computing Center — known as the "Eye of Xiong'an" — aggregates more than 40 billion data entries, connects over one million all-domain sensing terminals, and supports more than 500 kilometres of digital roads. A Group official described Xiong'an as "an AI-native city of the intelligent era, a future city where the digital city and physical city are planned and built in sync," whose innovative genes are "highly compatible" with the new enterprise.

Liang Zhihao (梁智昊), deputy director of the Xiong'an New Area Industry, IT and Data Bureau, said the city "built a city on the cloud from zero," generating massive data across urban planning, construction, governance, and livelihoods. That, he argued, provides "an open 'super test bed' for data-element reform and data governance exploration." Liang added that Xiong'an had set up a dedicated task force and six working groups to serve the Group's relocation needs, and that the two sides now hold weekly coordination meetings covering areas such as digital identity and e-government.

## Two frontiers: public data and AI

The Group's mission is framed around two strategic goals. Internally, it is meant to improve the development and use of public data. Zheng Lei (郑磊), director of the Digital and Mobile Governance Lab at Fudan University, said China's public-data efforts have reached a critical stage where institutional implementation and practical exploration run in parallel, but challenges persist: "data is opened up but not easy to use, invocation rules are imperfect, and quality does not meet standards."

"The National Data Group is expected, as a 'national team' in data-element services, to play roles in organizational connection, technical support, and scenario transformation, pushing public data from 'visible' toward 'usable, easy to use, and safe to use'," Zheng said.

Externally, the Group is positioned to supply the fuel for AI. The report notes that the capabilities of large models and agents increasingly depend on the scale and quality of datasets. China reports having built more than 120,000 high-quality datasets, yet faces persistent shortfalls in high-quality supply, high labelling costs, and inconsistent standards. Sun Yi said the Group can leverage its standing as a central enterprise to push unified data standards and platforms, conduct centralised aggregation, procurement, and governance, and thereby lower the cost of model training across society.

## A building block in a longer arc

The Group fits into a policy architecture Beijing has assembled over several years. China is the first major economy to formally designate data as a fifth factor of production alongside land, labour, capital, and technology. The "Data 20 Articles" of 2022 established a foundational data system; the National Data Administration was inaugurated in October 2023; and a "Data Elements ×" three-year action plan has driven sectoral applications through 2024–2026. The Group's creation, timed to the opening of the 15th Five-Year Plan period, adds an operating entity to a structure previously dominated by regulators and policy documents.

That shift — from rule-making to commercial operation — is the development to watch. It raises efficiency questions that state coordination is designed to solve, but also governance questions the report does not address: how personal data held by telecom operators will be protected, what shareholding proportions and capitalisation the Group carries, and how it will relate to the National Data Administration as regulator and to existing local data exchanges. The report gives no figures on ownership breakdown or investment, and details of the Group's operating platform architecture have yet to be published.

## What's next

The immediate signals to monitor are the Group's first operating platform, its data-trading rules, and its early partnerships in Xiong'an — where officials have flagged aerospace information, energy, minerals, power, and healthcare as priority domains for integrated data applications. How those pilots perform will indicate whether China's state-led model can convert the sheer volume of data it controls into the "usable, easy to use, and safe to use" resource its AI ambitions require.

"We firmly believe that rooting ourselves in Xiong'an will allow us to better serve the whole country and help the nation's data cause flourish," a Group official said.
