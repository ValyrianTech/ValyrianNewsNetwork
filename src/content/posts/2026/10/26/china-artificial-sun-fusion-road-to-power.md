# China's 'Artificial Sun': How Far Is Fusion Power, Really?

For decades, the running joke about nuclear fusion has been that it is always 50 years away. A sweeping in-depth feature published by [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261026/1c0f2a54a32347d3a1c50f9da3c5d7a8/c.html) on October 26 argues that this familiar trope is quietly breaking down — not because fusion is suddenly imminent, but because it has crossed a threshold from science-fiction dream to officially designated national industry. The article signals that Beijing now formally counts the "artificial sun" among its strategic bets for the future, even as its own experts concede that steady, commercial power generation remains "a long road ahead."

The piece is less a report of a single breakthrough than an authoritative status review of China's fusion program, framed around two policy milestones that took effect this year: the inclusion of fusion energy as a future industry in the 15th Five-Year Plan, and the entry into force of the PRC Atomic Energy Law, which for the first time legally encourages and supports fusion research and development.

## Why Fusion, and Why Now

The appeal is straightforward. Fusion mimics the sun, fusing hydrogen nuclei into helium and releasing enormous energy. Its primary fuel, deuterium, can be extracted from seawater; the process emits no carbon dioxide and produces none of the long-lived radioactive waste associated with conventional fission. As Wan Yuanxi, an academician of the Chinese Academy of Engineering, explained, tapping this mechanism would offer a next-generation energy source that is clean, safe and effectively inexhaustible.

Reproducing the sun on Earth, however, demands three punishing conditions at once: temperatures above 100 million degrees Celsius — roughly six to seven times the sun's core — sufficient particle density, and enough energy confinement time to keep the reaction going. Li Jiangang, also an academician at the CAE, captured the difficulty with a homely image: "It's like keeping a match burning — you need high enough temperature to ignite it, enough fuel to sustain it, and a way to enclose the flame in a cage so it doesn't go out."

Scientists pursue two broad families of solutions. Magnetic confinement, the most mature route, uses powerful magnetic fields to suspend superheated plasma so it never touches the reactor walls; its best-known device is the tokamak, a doughnut-shaped vessel wound with spiral magnetic field lines. Inertial confinement instead compresses and heats a fuel target in fractions of a second, an approach exemplified by Z-pinch and laser-driven systems. According to Song Yuntao, director of the Institute of Plasma Physics at the Hefei Institutes of Physical Science, these parallel routes reflect a global, multi-front push rather than a single racehorse.

## The Chinese Record

China's fusion lineage runs deep. In 1955, nuclear physicist Qian Sanqiang and Li Zhengwu, recently returned from the United States, proposed controlled thermonuclear research; the following year it entered the country's first 12-year science plan. In 1965 Li founded China's first dedicated magnetic-confinement institute in Leshan, Sichuan. The first-generation China Circulator No. 1 was designed under threadbare conditions — researchers reportedly worked from a single diagram and a handful of formulas — and completed in 1984 at internationally competitive specifications.

The more recent record is one of accelerating milestones. The fully superconducting tokamak EAST achieved its first discharge on September 28, 2006. In 2023, the HL-3 (China Circulation-3) device, led by chief designer Zhong Wulü, achieved high-confinement operation at one million amperes of plasma current. In March 2025 it crossed the "double 100-million-degree" threshold — ion temperatures of 117 million degrees Celsius and electron temperatures of 160 million degrees Celsius — entering the burning-plasma experiment phase. Zhong has framed the mission as turning "today's science questions into tomorrow's China answers."

China is also a key party to ITER, the world's largest science collaboration. After more than a decade of work, the Southwestern Institute of Physics led the release of the fusion field's first ISO international standard, and in December 2025 the Hefei institute delivered ITER's correction field coils, independently developed and manufactured. "The outstanding wisdom and engineering capability of China's fusion community are playing an increasingly critical role in ITER and the future global controlled fusion energy system," said Luo Delong, an ITER deputy director-general.

Beyond the lab, China's next step is the Burning Plasma Experimental Superconducting Tokamak (BEST) in Hefei. As [CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-10-01/China-s-artificial-sun-project-enters-key-construction-phase-1QSYBsXj0d2/p.html) reported, the BEST project complex was officially delivered and put into operation in early October, with more than half of assembly tasks completed. The device is planned for completion by 2027 and aims to demonstrate deuterium-tritium fusion power generation around 2030.

## The Global Race

The Xinhua feature is candid that China is not alone — and that the competition is intensifying. In July 2026 the United States released a science strategy report listing fusion among six national science missions. MIT spin-off Commonwealth Fusion Systems has raised roughly $3 billion, with its SPARC demonstrator slated to begin plasma operations by the end of 2026, while Helion Energy has signed the world's first commercial fusion power purchase agreement with Microsoft, targeting supply by 2028. Britain has committed £410 million to its STEP prototype plant; Germany's W7-X stellarator held a high-performance plasma for 43 seconds in 2025; and Europe's JET retains the magnetic-confinement energy output record.

Money is flowing accordingly. Citing the Fusion Industry Association's 2025 Global Fusion Industry Report, Xinhua notes that cumulative global commercial fusion funding reached $9.766 billion by mid-2025 — evidence, the FIA argues, that fusion is "no longer a distant vision but a rising global hard-tech industry."

## Analysis: Policy Is the Real News

The most consequential element of the Xinhua feature is not any single technical figure but the framing. By formally designating fusion a future industry and legislating support for it, Beijing is treating the technology as an industrial policy question, not merely a research curiosity. That shift matters because fusion's challenges are increasingly engineering and manufacturing problems — ultra-high-precision fabrication, extreme-environment materials, ultrafast real-time control — the very domains in which China has built formidable capacity.

The feature also makes a deliberate case for patience on returns. Song notes that a fusion device is a "super-large-scale application scenario" with millions of core parts, and that decades of work have already spun off practical technologies. Low-temperature superconducting wire derived from fusion research now underpins MRI machines, with United Imaging and Western Superconducting achieving fully independent technology, while terahertz scanners used in Hefei metro stations grew out of plasma diagnostics.

Yet the caveats deserve equal weight. The commonly cited targets — BEST first plasma around 2027, demonstration power near 2030, and a demonstration reactor in the 2035–2040 window — are goals, not guarantees, and fusion timelines have slipped repeatedly before. The Xinhua piece itself closes on a note of realism: moving from laboratory breakthroughs to steady commercial operation, and from single devices to large-scale energy application, remains "a long road with heavy responsibilities."

## What to Watch

The near-term signals will be whether BEST meets its 2027 assembly and first-plasma goals, how quickly the newly formed, state-backed China Fusion Energy Co. advances a planned HL-4 facility in Shanghai, and whether the wave of private capital abroad translates into delivered power rather than press releases. For now, the honest answer to the headline question — how far away is fusion? — is that the distance has stopped growing. It is no longer perpetually half a century off, but neither is it around the corner.
