# Judge: DOJ Illegally Fired Maurene Comey as Prosecutor

A federal judge ruled Monday that the Trump administration illegally fired Maurene Comey, the daughter of former FBI Director James Comey, from her job as a Justice Department prosecutor — a decision that sharply rebukes the administration's authority to terminate career federal employees it dislikes, according to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/justice-department-maurene-comey-trump-be7de14f705def3bb6eeadafa7e60805).

U.S. District Judge Jesse M. Furman found that Comey, an assistant U.S. attorney for a decade, was fired in July 2025 despite her work on some of the nation's highest-profile cases and consistently receiving "the highest accolades from supervisors and peers alike."

## A Ruling That Extends Beyond One Case

The decision is tailored to Maurene Comey, but its significance stretches well beyond her. It repudiates the administration's claim that the Constitution permits such firings for any reason — a belief officials have cited repeatedly in a sweeping purge of the federal workforce.

After her firing, Comey [sued the Justice Department](https://apnews.com/article/justice-department-maurene-comey-trump-23fb32ac0e4402873862d3ca6d31d18e) and others, arguing her termination violated the constitutional separation of powers, the First Amendment and the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

She asserted she was fired because she was the daughter of James Comey — whom Trump has long viewed as an enemy — or because of her perceived political affiliation and beliefs, or both, the judge noted.

## Rejecting a Sweeping Theory of Executive Power

Furman rejected the government's claim that Comey could be fired because federal prosecutors exercise the president's "conclusive and preclusive" powers. He also said the government's contention that Congress is barred from regulating the removal of federal prosecutors "flies in the face of nearly two centuries of Supreme Court precedent."

"Indeed, to accept Defendants' argument would be to unsettle the very equilibrium established by this Nation's constitutional system," Furman wrote.

The firing violated the separation of powers, the Administrative Procedure Act and her Fifth Amendment due process and statutory rights, the judge ruled. He reserved judgment on Comey's First Amendment claim pending further arguments, saying he was inclined to dismiss it as largely moot since she is now in private practice and no longer seeks actual reinstatement.

Furman, who was nominated to the bench in 2011 by then-President Barack Obama, granted Comey retroactive reinstatement and back pay from the date of her firing through Dec. 20 to "make her whole" and ensure she doesn't suffer adverse employment consequences for the "unlawful termination."

## From Diddy to Epstein: A Career Under Scrutiny

Comey was fired two weeks after leading the prosecution of music mogul Sean "Diddy" Combs, which ended in a [conviction on prostitution-related charges](https://apnews.com/video/diddy-convicted-of-prostitution-offenses-not-guilty-of-sex-trafficking-and-racketeering-ap-explains-73def46af46c493986c30125ada3962c) but an acquittal on others.

She also worked on the sex trafficking prosecutions of Jeffrey Epstein, the financier who [killed himself in jail](https://apnews.com/article/jeffrey-epstein-jail-suicide-prison-death-8d194a756f2b429067f009a0c70f96c0) a month after his arrest in 2019, and Epstein's former girlfriend Ghislaine Maxwell, who was convicted in 2021 and is serving a [20-year prison sentence](https://apnews.com/article/ghislaine-maxwell-jeffrey-epstein-sentencing-aeac127f9cc3811d975ce8e10d171260).

## A Pattern of Firings — and a Pledge to Appeal

The Justice Department during the Trump administration has fired scores of career employees, including prosecutors and FBI agents, who participated in investigations of Trump or who were perceived as opposed to the administration's agenda. Some of those firings have also resulted in federal lawsuits.

The firing of Maurene Comey was seen as particularly personal, given her father's longtime adversarial relationship with Trump. The president fired James Comey as FBI director in May 2017 as the bureau was investigating ties between Russia and the Republican's successful 2016 campaign for the White House, and Trump has repeatedly called for Comey to be jailed in the years since.

In a statement, the Justice Department said it "disagrees with this decision and will appeal in due course."

Comey's lawyers — Ellen Blain, Nicole Gueron, Deepa Vanamali and Margaret Donovan — lauded the ruling. "This hard-earned victory belongs to Ms. Comey, to all civil servants, and to all fighting for the rule of law," they said. "This decision reaffirms our bedrock system of checks and balances: the President is not above the law."

## What's Next

The administration's promised appeal would send the case to the Second Circuit Court of Appeals, potentially teeing up a broader ruling on the limits of presidential removal power over career prosecutors. The outcome could influence a parallel dispute over the firing of a court-appointed U.S. attorney in Seattle, as well as other wrongful-termination lawsuits brought by dismissed federal employees.
