# Trump's inflation failure threatens GOP midterms

WASHINGTON — One week before midterm elections that will serve as a referendum on his second term, President Donald Trump is struggling to deliver on his promise to bring down the cost of living, and the same affordability frustration that powered his 2024 comeback now threatens to cost Republicans control of Congress, [the Associated Press reported](https://apnews.com/article/trump-economy-midterms-inflation-prices-96f617e2bbb42be4261a0141f787c6ed).

Filling up a basic Ford F-150 now costs about $100, up $30 from a year ago. Mortgage rates have climbed to levels that have depressed home sales. And for the past 12 months, consumer prices have risen faster than paychecks, leaving the average family with less real purchasing power.

## A "golden age" the numbers don't confirm

Trump insists the country has entered an economic "golden age" and vows that interest rates and oil prices will plunge once the war with Iran he started comes to an end. He has dismissed "affordability" as "a con job by the Democrats" that "doesn't mean anything to anybody," and claims inflation is under 2% and "under control."

The official data tells a different story. Inflation was 3% when Trump took office and [reached 3.7% last month](https://apnews.com/article/cpi-inflation-consumer-prices-affordability-3985019a54c82421e6112fb57baba048), up from 3.4% in August. Core prices, excluding volatile food and energy, rose 2.6% over the year — still well above the Federal Reserve's 2% target. Gasoline jumped 9.1% from August to September, air fares climbed 22% year-over-year, and average weekly paychecks adjusted for inflation shrank on an annual basis for the first time since May.

The drivers are largely supply shocks: the Iran war's disruption to oil and gas supplies, the artificial-intelligence and data-center buildout pushing up electronics prices, and aggressive new tariffs. On the ground, the effects are tangible. Record-high diesel prices have driven up shipping and farming costs, creating political trouble for Republicans in typically conservative states such as Iowa, Kansas, Ohio and Texas, where Democrats appear uniquely competitive.

## Voters blame Trump more than they once blamed Biden

Polling makes the danger plain. Only [17% of U.S. adults approve](https://apnews.com/article/trump-economy-approval-trump-midterms-8d5809758a31b29c73f4351a8008147c) of Trump's handling of the cost of living, and just 26% approve of his handling of the economy overall — a new low. About 7 in 10 Americans say his handling of costs has been "worse than expected," including roughly half of Republicans, and about 6 in 10 Republicans now disapprove of his record on affordability.

Perhaps most telling: 65% blame Trump's own policies, not outside forces, for high prices — a far higher share than the 44% who blamed President Joe Biden in October 2022. That asymmetry is a critical difference for GOP prospects.

"The costs of things are absolutely the first thing people talk about, but also it's their pay not keeping up with the cost," said AFL-CIO President Liz Shuler. "It's a double whammy."

Even some of Trump's allies concede the political risk. "People hate paying more money for gas, and the prices have gone up a lot," said Stephen Moore, a former Trump economic adviser. "With the Iran situation, that's really had a big effect on the economy, and Republicans may pay a high price for that." He added: "What matters is how people feel, and clearly people are feeling stressed out financially."

## The battle for the Senate runs through red states

The electoral math has put Republican-leaning states in play. Senate Majority Leader John Thune has [campaigned across Nebraska, Georgia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Mississippi, Kentucky, Florida, Minnesota and Iowa](https://apnews.com/article/john-thune-midterm-elections-senate-majority-republicans-8f1d701189db1189e07b78375f3ad805) in a scramble to hold his majority, with a stop planned in Ohio.

In Iowa, the same reporting finds, Republicans could not have envisioned Rep. Ashley Hinson's race for an open Senate seat as a tossup a year ago; she is now locked in a tight contest against Democrat Josh Turek to replace retiring Sen. Joni Ernst.

Democrats have seized the discontent, tying inflation to the Iran war and to what they allege is corruption favoring wealthy donors. "They promised to lower prices on Day One. Instead, under the failed President Donald J. Trump, it costs more than ever in American history to fill up your truck, stock your fridge, visit the doctor or power your home," said Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff, who is running for reelection. "You work harder and harder, but the treadmill gets faster and steeper and the bills get higher and higher."

Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed put the war front and center: "Trump's war abroad costs taxpayers a billion dollars a day on a war we have no business fighting." North Carolina's Roy Cooper told voters in an ad that "Washington politicians get rich while you get hurt."

Republicans have countered by casting Democrats as extreme and by emphasizing economic bright spots — rising median incomes and a record-setting stock market — while acknowledging those figures may not resonate.

## What to watch

The outcome hinges on turnout and on whether Republicans can shift the conversation to growth before Nov. 3. Two near-term variables loom: whether the Iran war winds down and eases gas prices, and whether the Federal Reserve, under Chairman Kevin Warsh, raises rates again at its Oct. 27-28 meeting after hiking for the first time in three years. Senate Republicans will also vote by secret ballot about a week after the election on whether to keep Thune as their leader.

For voters like Beti Rosales, a restaurant owner in Allentown, Pennsylvania, who backed Trump in 2024, the verdict is already in. "The reality now is that it actually didn't get better," she said. "It actually got worse." She is now considering voting Democratic. "Maybe fresh new faces might make a difference?"
