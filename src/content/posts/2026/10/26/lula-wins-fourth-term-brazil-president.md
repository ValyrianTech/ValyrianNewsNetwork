# Lula Wins Fourth Term as Brazil's President

Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has won Brazil's presidential election for a fourth time, defeating Senator Flavio Bolsonaro in a closely fought runoff, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20261026/6469f782a07d4980a7d413cabed0b2ea/c.html). The 80-year-old incumbent, of the Workers' Party (PT), took 51.79 percent of valid votes to Bolsonaro's 48.21 percent, with 99.99 percent of ballots counted, and will be inaugurated on January 5, 2027. The result was confirmed by Brazil's Superior Electoral Court (TSE).

The victory makes Lula the first politician in Brazil's history to win four direct presidential elections, after previous wins in 2002, 2006 and 2022. It also marks the first time since Brazil restored direct presidential elections in 1989 that a candidate who trailed in the first round went on to win the runoff.

## Opponent Refuses to Concede

Flavio Bolsonaro, a senator and son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, refused to accept the result in a speech in Brasilia on the night of October 25, alleging serious irregularities and vowing to challenge the outcome in court. "Under these circumstances, it is impossible for us to recognize the election result," he said, according to [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/GuoJi·ZhanLue/2026_10_26_905839.shtml). "We will use our legal team to demand that this election be declared null and void."

His specific grievance centers on a decision by Supreme Federal Court justice Cristiano Zanin to extend voting hours by two hours at polling stations still open, citing heavy rain, flooding and public transport disruptions in parts of the country. Because some stations had already closed when the order was issued, the extension applied only to those still operating. Bolsonaro argued the move changed the rules mid-vote and questioned Zanin's impartiality, noting that the justice previously served as Lula's lawyer.

Cassio Nunes Marques, president of the TSE, said the decision caused procedural difficulties but did not change the result, and expressed full confidence in the integrity of the process. No evidence has emerged that the voting-hours dispute could alter the outcome.

## Echoes of 2022

The refusal to concede recalls the conduct of Jair Bolsonaro after the 2022 election, when the former president declined to clearly acknowledge defeat and questioned the electoral system. In January 2023, his supporters stormed Congress, the presidential palace and the Supreme Court, triggering a major political crisis. Jair Bolsonaro was later convicted over an alleged coup plot and sentenced to 27 years in prison.

Analysts warn that Flavio Bolsonaro's challenge could again plunge Brazil into a political and legal dispute over the legitimacy of its elections, though the margin — roughly 4.4 million votes — and the absence of evidence of irregularities leave the result on firm legal ground.

## China Says It Respects 'the Choice of the Brazilian People'

Asked at a regular briefing on October 26 about Flavio Bolsonaro's demand to annul the result, Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said: "China respects the choice of the Brazilian people." The remark, reported by Guancha, was a notably measured response rather than an explicit congratulation.

Congratulations came chiefly from Latin American leaders. Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum wrote on social media, "The Brazilian people won! Congratulations, President Lula!" Uruguay's President Orsi said he had phoned Lula and described him as "my friend," while Cuba's President Diaz-Canel offered congratulations on a "historic victory," according to [People's Daily](https://world.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1026/c1002-40821714.html), citing Xinhua. The presidents of Bolivia and Panama also extended good wishes.

Lula has made developing ties with China a diplomatic priority since first taking office in 2003, and China has been Brazil's largest trading partner since 2009. The two countries elevated their relationship to a "community with a shared future" in November 2024, and Lula signed cooperation agreements on artificial intelligence, energy, infrastructure, minerals, space and trade during a state visit to China in May 2025.

## A Victory Speech Against 'Foreign Interference'

In his victory speech in Sao Paulo, Lula said Brazil "is not anyone's backyard" and thanked Brazilians for helping him overcome foreign interference in the election, framing the result as the defeat of "negationism and lies." He criticized social media platforms for attempting to intervene in the race and called for stronger regulation of technology companies, according to [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/GuoJi·ZhanLue/2026_10_26_905801.shtml).

His remarks followed accusations by his campaign that Meta had restricted the reach of Lula content on Facebook and Instagram. On October 15, Brazil's electoral court ordered Meta to treat both candidates equally before the runoff. Meta denied deliberate suppression, though the newspaper Folha de S.Paulo reported that internal company communications acknowledged Lula's Instagram account had been restricted.

Lula pledged a greater federal role in public security, more social-media communication with citizens, attention to the elderly and women, continued climate action, and reform of the public health system. "This election taught me a lot," he said. "I promise we will change the way this country is governed and let the people participate more in our decisions."

## Challenges Ahead

Lula's final term — which he has said will be his last — will be constrained by a conservative-dominated National Congress and difficult fiscal conditions. Brazil's public debt exceeds 82 percent of GDP, and high interest rates, slow growth and heavy household debt weigh on the economy. On the world stage, his re-election is likely to sustain a period of unusually close China-Brazil relations, even as he has signaled a desire for greater strategic autonomy. He has said he was the only person in the country without a university degree, from a metalworker background, to be elected president four times — a line that captures both his remarkable political durability and the polarized era in which he governs.

What to watch: whether Flavio Bolsonaro's legal challenge gains any traction in the courts, and how quickly Lula can consolidate his agenda before the constraints of a divided Congress and a difficult fiscal picture set in.
