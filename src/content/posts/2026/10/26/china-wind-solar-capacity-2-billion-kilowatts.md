# China's Wind and Solar Capacity Tops 2 Billion Kilowatts

China's combined installed wind and solar power capacity exceeded 2 billion kilowatts at the end of September 2026, the National Energy Administration (NEA) announced on Monday, cementing the country's position as the world's largest market for renewable generation. According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261026/6254e4035a7c444e998038b8dc5b1bfc/c.html), cumulative wind and solar capacity reached 2.01 billion kilowatts (2,010 GW), comprising 1.31 billion kW (1,310 GW) of solar and 700 million kW (700 GW) of wind.

The milestone reflects a build-out of extraordinary pace and scale, and arrives just weeks after solar overtook coal to become China's largest single source of power by installed capacity. Yet the headline figure also masks an important distinction: installed capacity is not the same as delivered energy, and coal remains central to keeping the lights on.

## Context: A build-out measured in months

The two-billion-kilowatt threshold was crossed barely a month after China reported roughly 1.992 billion kW of combined wind and solar capacity at the end of August — implying that about 18 GW of net new renewable capacity was added in September alone. The trajectory behind that number is steep. At the end of 2025, combined wind and solar capacity first passed 1.8 billion kW, a figure then likened by state media to roughly 82 Three Gorges power stations. By February 2026 it had reached about 1.88 billion kW.

The expansion is not confined to generation totals. During the 14th Five-Year Plan period from 2021 to 2025, China added 1.69 billion kW of total generation capacity, of which 1.307 billion kW — some 77.3 percent — was wind and solar, roughly 3.4 times the scale at the end of the previous planning cycle.

## Developments: Solar moves to the front

The more striking structural shift came in the summer. As of the end of July 2026, China's solar capacity reached 1.286 billion kW, edging past coal-fired capacity of 1.285 billion kW to become the country's number-one power source by installed capacity for the first time, [21st Century Business Herald](https://www.21jingji.com/article/20260902/herald/bceb2e74636a8257f52d2ac3b65f3aee.html) reported. That total comprised 704 GW of centralized solar and 582 GW of distributed solar.

The crossover was framed by industry figures as a landmark in China's low-carbon transition. Liu Zhiqiang, deputy director of the planning and development department at the China Electricity Council, told the outlet that solar's rise to the top signals the country's accelerating construction of a "new power system" led by renewables, sharply lowering the power sector's overall carbon emissions through structural change on the generation side.

From an industrial standpoint, Liu argued, the leap validates the competitiveness of China's entire solar supply chain. Large-scale domestic deployment has amplified economies of scale, driven technological iteration, and reinforced China's lead in global photovoltaic manufacturing, technical standards and engineering. In the first half of 2026, coal-fired generation's share of total electricity output fell below 50 percent for the first time in history, and wind and solar accounted for more than 80 percent of newly added capacity.

## Analysis: Capacity is not generation

The nuance that tempers the milestone is one Liu himself is careful to stress. Installed capacity does not equal actual generating capability or output. Solar is constrained by the day-night cycle and weather, giving it a highly intermittent, fluctuating profile and far lower utilization hours than coal. For now, coal remains China's main supporting power source, providing system security as a backstop.

What is changing is coal's role. Liu describes a shift from a base-load supply source toward a supporting and regulating one — no longer chasing ever-higher generation volumes but prioritizing flexibility. At midday, when renewables surge, coal units ramp down deeply to make room for wind and solar on the grid; at night or under cloudy skies, when renewable output falls sharply, coal's fast dispatchability fills the gap and smooths fluctuations. The upshot is that coal's average annual utilization hours are trending downward even as its system-security value — inertia and frequency support — becomes more prominent.

This dual reality is central to understanding China's energy picture. The country is simultaneously the world's dominant clean-energy manufacturer and installer and a continued operator of large-scale coal capacity that functions as dispatchable backup. The two are not contradictory so much as complementary in the near term — a point often lost in headline comparisons of installed capacity.

## What's Next: From scale to capability

With the capacity threshold now crossed, the policy focus is shifting from "scale growth" to "capability improvement." Liu argues the key to converting installed-capacity advantage into stable, reliable supply lies in accelerating the construction of a new power system. Drawing on recently released energy-sector plans for the 15th Five-Year Plan period, he points to several priorities: scaling up pumped-storage and new-type energy storage, building a new grid platform that coordinates main, distribution and micro-grids, and improving renewables' ability to reliably substitute for conventional generation.

For global observers, the immediate question is not whether China will keep adding renewables — it will — but how quickly the grid, storage and market mechanisms can keep pace. The 2 billion kilowatt figure is a supply-side triumph; whether it translates into a cleaner, more dependable electricity system depends on the integration work that comes next.
