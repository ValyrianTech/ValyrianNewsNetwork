# Vučić Concedes as Serbia's Student Movement Wins Election

Serbia's former president Aleksandar Vučić has conceded defeat in the country's October 25 snap parliamentary election, acknowledging that his Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) lost to the student-led "Student List – Students Are Winning" movement, [People's Daily](http://world.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1026/c1002-40821217.html) reported. The result ends nearly 14 years of SNS dominance and hands an outright parliamentary majority to a movement that only recently emerged from street protests.

## A Political Earthquake

Speaking at the SNS campaign headquarters in Belgrade on the evening of October 25, Vučić said that based on a stratified sample, the Student List was projected to win 135 to 140 of the National Assembly's 250 seats, while his own "Aleksandar Vučić – United Serbia" list would take around 105. According to [Meduza](https://meduza.io/en/news/2026/10/26/serbia-s-former-president-aleksandar-vucic-concedes-defeat-in-parliamentary-election-to-student-led-opposition), he put it plainly: "We don't have the final results yet. Based on a stratified sample, it's clear that we lost this election."

As counts were finalized, the scale of the defeat became clear. [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/political-earthquake-in-serbia-the-end-of-the-vucic-era-and-the-rise-of-the-student-list) reported a final-seat breakdown of 140 seats for the Student List against 104 for the SNS — an outright majority, with 126 needed to govern alone. The [Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/546f21f5-22d3-4696-8371-7bef8c0410b3) reported near-final figures of roughly 52.4% of the vote for the Student List against 39.4% for the SNS.

Vučić said he accepted the result and would conduct political activity as an opposition figure. At the time of the Xinhua report carried by People's Daily, the Serbian Election Commission had not yet published final official results.

## From Tragedy to Transformation

The Student List did not emerge from Serbia's traditional opposition parties, which had failed for over a decade to dent Vučić's hold on power. It grew out of mass protests triggered by the November 2024 collapse of a newly renovated concrete-and-glass canopy at Novi Sad's railway station, which killed 16 people, including two children.

That disaster became a symbol of broader grievances — corruption, a lack of institutional accountability, and media censorship — and the protests swelled into the largest demonstration movement in Serbia's modern history. What began as a social revolt has now been converted into electoral legitimacy.

The result is widely read as a referendum on 14 years of SNS rule. Euronews noted that a large part of the electorate voted not simply for a group of candidates but against a model of government defined by personalised politics, a concentration of power, and political control over institutions and media.

The earthquake extended to Serbia's smaller parties. No other list crossed the 3% parliamentary threshold, wiping out much of the traditional party landscape. Minority parties that had long been reliable Vučić allies — including the Alliance of Vojvodina Hungarians — suffered losses, signaling the disruption of the broad alliance network on which the SNS had relied.

## Voices of a Historic Shift

Figures associated with the Student List framed the result in historic terms. Dejan Šoškić, an economics professor and prominent figure in the movement, said he was proud of the students "who have brought us to this historic situation in which Serbia has freed itself, in which Serbia has a chance to become a democratic and free society again."

Radomir Saičić, an academician and Student List candidate, said citizens had demonstrated courage and a willingness for change, stressing that "serious work and the building of a new Serbia begin tomorrow."

## A European Dimension

The result drew swift congratulations from senior European officials, including European Council President Antonio Costa, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, and EU Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos. The reaction signals that Brussels views the Student List's victory as a possible opening of a new chapter in Serbia's relationship with the EU — particularly on rule-of-law and institutional issues that have long strained ties.

## What's Next

Vučić warned that he expects Serbia under the new authorities to introduce sanctions against Russia — a major foreign-policy shift for a country that has maintained close ties with Moscow while pursuing EU membership. He also cautioned that the result could send a negative signal to investors, a central pillar of his economic policy.

The harder task now falls to the Student List: transforming a protest movement into a functioning government. It must form a cabinet, rebuild institutions, and formulate policy on the economy, foreign affairs, EU integration, and the rule of law.

For the SNS, the test is different. For the first time since 2012, the party will operate without control of the state apparatus and without Vučić at the centre of government — a reckoning with how strong it is as an organisation once it no longer organises the country's political life around itself. The answer to whether a political force could challenge Vučić has now been given. It can — and the force that did it was not the establishment opposition, but a student movement that only recently stood outside the traditional political system.
