# Anthropic Files for Record IPO as CEO Warns of AI Risk

Artificial intelligence giant Anthropic filed paperwork Monday for what could be the biggest initial public offering of stock ever, even as its top executive warns that advanced AI could pose an existential threat to humanity, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-ipo-shares-public-ca2f4c282bef3201a392e59e3df2300c).

The filing sets up the San Francisco company behind the Claude chatbot for a Wall Street debut next month, potentially eclipsing the record $86 billion raised by SpaceX in its June offering. An Anthropic listing would also put it ahead of rival OpenAI in going public, setting the stage for a pair of blockbuster stock debuts by the two leading AI labs.

## A Startup Racing Toward Trillion-Dollar Status

The 335-page filing showed Anthropic's revenue growing rapidly, reaching more than $16 billion in the first half of 2026 — 14 times its revenue from the same period a year earlier. But like many tech companies going public, the company has yet to turn a profit.

Anthropic, valued earlier this year at $965 billion by private investors, is reportedly set to see that value rise to $1.5 trillion or possibly $2 trillion, though its final valuation has not been established. At $1.5 trillion, it would rank among the world's most valuable public companies, alongside Tesla. The offering could raise as much as $100 billion, according to reports, surpassing the SpaceX record.

In the regulatory filing, Anthropic said it will list its stock on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker symbol "ANT."

## The Central Paradox: Warnings and Windfalls

The filing lands at a moment of deep tension between the runaway commercialization of AI and its acknowledged safety risks. Even as the company seeks public capital at a historic scale, CEO Dario Amodei has spent the year publicly cautioning that advanced AI could pose an existential threat and calling on the industry to slow down.

The document's "risk factors" section runs nearly 100 pages. Among the hazards it describes are "malicious actors" using AI models to build chemical weapons, authoritarian rulers turning the technology against their citizens, and the possibility that its models could go rogue and "pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity." The filing also flags that the insurance industry as a whole may not be sufficient to cover liability from a catastrophic AI-related incident.

In September, Amodei said the AI industry should [slow its fast-moving development](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-ai-dario-amodei-d59552edcb27892d8ee4d98a48397706) to give safety measures time to catch up, warning that within six to 12 months AI could be capable of unleashing a swarm of agents that could take over the entire internet. He laid out a plan on his website to increase checks on the industry, including giving outside safety evaluators ongoing access to frontier systems.

"I believe that if slowing down bought us even an extra year or two before models reach critical levels of capability, and we used that time to advance alignment, we could greatly reduce the risk that something goes seriously wrong," Amodei said.

## Why Go Public Now?

David Hsu, a management professor at the University of Pennsylvania, said he sees the filing more as a move to diversify the company's financial base.

"Obviously, they feel great, or at least good enough, on the safety aspects relative to OpenAI, and so that puts them in a position to really stay on track now to go public (first)," Hsu said.

The need for cash may explain why Anthropic is rushing to tap public markets this year rather than waiting until next year, as is OpenAI's plan. Adding to the pressure is the possibility that demand for AI shares could drop. Investors have grown jittery about the sector's massive financing needs, and several tech companies have pulled IPO plans recently.

As Michael Field, chief equity analyst at Morningstar, put it, these companies "are now burning through cash to win the AI race, and public equity is the cheapest source available, particularly in a rising interest rate environment."

Anthropic reported a net loss of $80.7 billion in the first half of 2026, including a one-time $72.4 billion charge for an accounting adjustment tied to financing agreements. In the same period a year earlier, it lost $13 billion.

## Unusual Governance and a Founder Windfall

The filing grants the seven co-founders — including Dario Amodei and his sister and co-founder Daniela, the company's president — 50.1% of shareholder votes through a special class of shares. The filing didn't directly state their ownership stakes, but other figures suggest each founder will end up with about 1.4% of the company, a windfall of as much as $28 billion apiece at the top expected valuation.

Dario, 43, and 39-year-old Daniela are already worth $15.5 billion each, according to Forbes, which ranks them as the 176th and 177th richest people in the world. Anthropic has also appointed independent, financially disinterested members of a so-called Long-Term Benefit Trust, which holds power to appoint directors and is designed to ensure the company does not put financial interests ahead of public safety.

## Clashes With the U.S. Government

Anthropic has tried to set itself apart from other AI companies as the "good guys," focusing on safety and responsible development rather than growing at all costs. Some of that reputation stems from its dispute with the U.S. government, which broke out in February when the Trump administration accused Anthropic of endangering national security and designated it a supply chain risk.

Anthropic [sued the Pentagon](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-trump-pentagon-hegseth-ai-104c6c39306f1adeea3b637d2c1c601b) in March for what it called an "unlawful campaign of retaliation" over its refusal to allow unrestricted military use of its technology. ChatGPT maker OpenAI signed its own deal to work with the Pentagon hours after the government punished Anthropic for its stance. A California judge ruled in Anthropic's favor in one case, while a Washington, D.C. judge sided with the government in a narrower one. Both sides are appealing.

## What to Watch

The offering remains a bet on a company that is growing fast, losing heavily, and openly warning about the dangers of its own products. How investors weigh that paradox — and whether safety rhetoric can coexist with a $100 billion capital raise — will define both Anthropic's debut and the broader market for AI stocks.

Also unresolved: the final offering price, the pending litigation with the Pentagon, and an FTC investigation into OpenAI, Anthropic and other AI firms over consumer risks. And there is the cautionary precedent of SpaceX, whose stock fell 15% in its first week after joining the Nasdaq-100.

For now, Anthropic's filing stands as a landmark — a company racing to become one of the most valuable in the world while its chief executive says the technology he is selling could threaten civilization itself.
