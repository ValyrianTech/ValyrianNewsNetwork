# Europe Heat Deaths Top 50,000, Study Finds

A major new study estimates that more than 50,000 people died from heat-related causes in European cities this summer — the highest toll on record — and that roughly two-thirds of those deaths can be attributed to human-caused climate change. The research, led by scientists at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine and Imperial College London, was published in *Nature Health* and reported by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/oct/23/heat-killed-staggering-50000-people-european-cities-this-summer-experts) and Chinese outlet [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34325179).

The findings landed days after the UN Environment Programme's *Emissions Gap Report 2026* declared that the window to limit warming to 1.5°C has effectively closed — together framing a stark picture of a continent warming faster than any other and cities straining to adapt.

## What the Study Found

Researchers analysed expected heat mortality across 854 urban areas in 30 countries — the EU-27 plus the United Kingdom, Switzerland and Norway — between June and August 2026. Those cities account for about 40% of the region's population.

The estimated death toll of over 50,000 is the worst since at least 2000, nearly double the previous modern record of 29,480 deaths in 2022. About 65% of the deaths — roughly 32,000 or more — are attributed to anthropogenic warming driven primarily by fossil fuel burning. As [Inside Climate News](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/22102026/fossil-fueled-warming-drives-surge-in-european-heat-deaths/) reported, the share of heat deaths linked to climate change has climbed steadily: from about 42% during the landmark 2003 heatwave, to 59% in 2022, and roughly 65% this year.

The 17 June–1 July heatwave proved especially lethal, causing nearly a third of the summer's total deaths, when daily mean temperatures ran more than 5°C above the 1991–2020 average. Italy, France, Spain, Germany and the UK recorded the highest absolute tolls, while Malta, Italy and Hungary saw the highest per-capita death rates.

"In some sense, the absolute numbers of the heat-related deaths estimated across the 854 urban areas are indeed staggering," said Malcolm Mistry, a climate and health scientist at LSHTM and co-author of the study. "But perhaps what is even more alarming is the proportion of these deaths that we attribute to anthropogenic climate change."

## The Human Cost Behind the Numbers

While people aged 85 and over made up almost half the toll, younger people were not immune: 833 people aged 20 to 44 died, and roughly 6,000 people under 65 died across the 30 countries.

Naples was the hardest-hit city, with 815 deaths per million population. Mario Guarino, an emergency doctor at the city's Ospedale CTO, reported a 15% rise in emergency room visits during the hottest weeks. "The real epidemic these days is loneliness," Guarino said, describing elderly patients abandoned by families and unable to afford air conditioning or electricity to run it.

Clair Barnes, a research associate at the Grantham Institute at Imperial College London and lead author of the study, stressed the human stakes. "These statistics are behind real people whose lives were lost and real families who grieve for them," she said.

## Why Europe Is So Exposed

Europe is the fastest-warming continent, with average temperatures already about 2.5°C above pre-industrial levels — roughly twice the global rate. Its cities and health systems, particularly in traditionally cooler northern regions, were built for milder climates.

Andrew Hartle, a professor of anesthesia at Imperial College London, noted that much of the UK's hospital infrastructure is "not built to withstand these temperatures." The study estimates about 4,354 heat deaths in UK urban areas this summer.

The findings build on a companion [study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44360-026-00209-8) in *Nature Health* covering summers from 2000 to 2025, which found that the proportion of heat-related deaths attributable to human-induced warming rose from 39% in 2000 to 68% in 2025.

## Caveats and Context

The figures are modelled estimates based on observed temperatures and epidemiological relationships, not individually counted deaths — a distinction independent experts flag. David García-García of the Carlos III Health Institute in Madrid cautioned that the study does not incorporate actual death data from summer 2026, though past experience shows such models track closely with later official statistics. National agencies using their own methods produce differing country-level figures.

The study covers only urban areas and cannot be easily extrapolated to a full continental figure including towns and villages.

## What's Next

The research arrives amid rising public pressure. About 40,000 people demonstrated in Amsterdam in mid-September, followed by tens of thousands more in Germany, Belgium and Austria — the largest climate protests since 2019. Meanwhile, the UNEP report calls for emissions cuts of 18% by 2030 and 45% by 2035 relative to 2025 levels to enable a return to 1.5°C later this century.

Researchers and doctors urged governments to cut fossil fuel use, strengthen health systems, green cities, expand access to cooling, and — crucially — check on vulnerable neighbours. "We are far off track from limiting the most serious consequences of climate change," Barnes warned. "Governments need to realize that without faster and coordinated action, things will only get worse."
