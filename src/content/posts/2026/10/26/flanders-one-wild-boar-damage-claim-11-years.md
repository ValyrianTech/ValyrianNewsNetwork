# Flanders Paid Just One Wild Boar Damage Claim in 11 Years

Of the 294 wild boar damage claims filed across Flanders since a compensation scheme was introduced more than a decade ago, just one has actually been paid out, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/26/maar-1-schadedossier-voor-everzwijnen-in-limburg-vergoed-proce/). The single successful claim, a 2022 case from Limburg, yielded just €270. The figures, drawn from a parliamentary question by Flemish MP Lydia Peeters, have renewed calls to overhaul a procedure critics say is effectively unworkable.

## Context: A Growing Problem

Wild boars have spread rapidly across Flanders since the early 2000s, migrating from northern France, Wallonia and the Ardennes after being largely absent from the region for most of the 20th century. Their rising numbers have brought mounting conflict with residents and farmers, whose gardens, farmland and sports fields are routinely churned up by the animals' rooting.

The scale of the problem is visible in the hunting statistics. As [VILT](https://vilt.be/nl/nieuws/aantal-geschoten-everzwijnen-op-tien-jaar-verzesvoudigd), the Flemish agricultural news outlet, reported, the number of wild boars shot in Flanders rose sixfold in a decade — from 564 in 2015 to 3,415 in 2024. Roughly three of every four boars culled come from Limburg, which remains the epicenter of the conflict.

## The Burden of Proof

At the heart of the compensation failure is what Peeters calls the "bewijslast" — the burden of proof. Under the current rules, a claimant must demonstrate that the boars causing damage came from an area where hunting is not permitted.

"Many people have damage. Gardens, farmland and sports fields are completely churned up. It's a really big problem, so I keep raising it," Peeters said. "The biggest problem is the burden of proof. Whoever has damage must show the boars come from an area where hunting is not allowed. Of course those animals won't say where they came from."

Because wild boars roam freely across huntable and non-huntable land, that requirement makes successful claims nearly impossible in practice. Of the 294 claims filed over roughly eleven years, 216 — about 73% — came from Limburg, with most of the remainder from Flemish-Brabant.

Peeters argues the logic must be reversed. "It cannot be that you can only get compensation if you can prove the boar comes from a non-huntable area," she said. She has described the scheme as a "lege doos" — an empty box — and a "dode letter," a dead letter that exists on paper but is unreachable in practice.

## Minister Acknowledges Problem

Flemish Minister of Agriculture and Environment Jo Brouns (CD&V) has acknowledged the problem. He says both the filing process and the burden of proof "can be simpler," and has repeatedly indicated that the government is re-examining the system. Earlier this year, Peeters pressed Brouns for a new compensation arrangement, which the minister described as open to discussion — though he has stressed that his priority is curbing the wild boar population rather than expanding payouts.

The near-zero compensation rate sits awkwardly alongside the government's escalating response to the animals themselves. Last week, at a third wild boar consultation in Leopoldsburg, Brouns announced plans to deploy more hunters per drive hunt and to launch a pilot project using hunting dogs. Those measures followed earlier calls from the Flemish hunters' association Hubertus Vereniging Vlaanderen to permit bow-and-arrow hunting and night-vision equipment.

## Analysis: A Design Flaw or a Deterrent?

The 0.34% payout rate raises a pointed question: is the scheme a genuine safety net that has been badly designed, or a policy intended to discourage claims while maintaining the appearance of support? Either way, the practical effect is the same for affected farmers, who can face €5,000 to €10,000 in annual damage from rooting and crop losses.

The compensation question also intersects with a deeper debate over wildlife management. Expanding culls and hunting tools addresses the population, but the damage claims expose a gap between the state's willingness to reduce boar numbers and its willingness to compensate those who bear the cost. Limburg's disproportionate share of claims adds a regional-equity dimension: the province absorbs most of the conflict while receiving almost none of the compensation.

## What's Next

The government's review of the compensation system is the key thing to watch. The central question is whether Flanders will genuinely reverse the burden of proof, as Peeters demands, or merely streamline the paperwork while leaving the fundamental obstacle intact. How any revised scheme is designed — and whether it is paired with the expanded hunting measures — will determine whether the coming years bring real relief to residents and farmers, or simply another decade of claims that go nowhere.
