# In Ohio, an Unlikely Republican Leads the Data Center Fight

MT. ORAB, Ohio — Nick Owens describes himself simply as a small-town attorney. He is also a lifelong Republican who served as a delegate for Donald Trump in 2024. And over the past two years, he has become one of the most prominent leaders of a grassroots backlash against the AI-driven data centers transforming rural Ohio — a fight that has turned energy, land use and technology into a central issue in the state's closely watched midterm elections.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/28/nx-s1-6019276/data-centers-ai-midterms-ohio), Owens's involvement began with a simple question: what was happening on roughly 1,000 acres of farmland and trees in Brown County that property records showed had been sold to a data center developer between 2024 and 2025. Non-disclosure agreements signed by local officials kept residents in the dark about the project's details. Construction has not yet begun, and the timing remains unclear.

## A Cross-Partisan Coalition

What started as one man's search for answers grew into Conserve Ohio, a volunteer network active in nearly all of the state's 88 counties and drawing Democrats, Republicans and independents alike. Owens said the coalition is bound by a shared purpose.

"We all share the same ethos and the same passion for preserving our way of life, preserving the future of Ohio, frankly, from the hyperscale data center invasion," he told NPR. "It's really beautiful."

The group's immediate focus is a set of charter amendments going before voters in 10 communities this November that would prohibit large-scale data centers — going further than the temporary moratoriums already adopted by hundreds of municipal and county governments. Organizers are also pursuing a longer statewide constitutional amendment that would ban large-scale data centers statewide, with a goal of putting the measure to Ohio voters in 2027. That effort is too late to qualify for the November 2026 ballot, making 2027 the earliest realistic target.

## From Secrecy to a Movement

The secrecy surrounding the Brown County land deal became a rallying cry. Frustration over projects that residents said were cloaked in secrecy helped fuel the amendment drive. The statewide petition has expanded to volunteers in more than 70 counties, working through the attorney general's office and the state ballot board earlier this year.

For Owens, the case for local control is straightforward. "All too often you hear from the politicians, 'Well, the community should have a say,'" he said. "We're going to find out in ten communities whether or not the communities want them."

He frames the tradeoff in stark terms, weighing new tax revenue for schools and local government against what residents would give up. "Should the school have extra money or the local government have extra money so we have a constant hum and ridiculous amounts of emissions and pollution?" he asked. "Our community is not for sale."

## An Economic Bet Under Scrutiny

Ohio spent more than a decade courting the data center industry, leveraging cheap land, robust energy infrastructure and generous tax breaks to become one of the fastest-growing data center markets in the country. The pitch brought tax revenue, school funding and a surge of construction jobs — though far fewer permanent positions than the buildout itself creates. As the facilities grew larger and were built specifically for AI, their power and water demands climbed sharply, intensifying local anxiety.

Linda Moodie, an independent volunteer with Conserve Ohio who has been collecting signatures at community events, said the speed of the change caught many residents off guard. "It's really something that I think a lot of us didn't see coming, and all of a sudden there's just an influx of them," she said. "The closer it hits to home, the more people that are going to want to do something."

## The Senate Race Enters the Fray

The data center fight has become entangled in Ohio's closely watched U.S. Senate race between former Democratic Sen. Sherrod Brown and Republican incumbent Jon Husted. Brown has sought to make Husted — who as lieutenant governor actively welcomed tech companies and championed data center investment — the face of the issue. Husted has accused Brown of hypocrisy and pushed a federal bill requiring data centers to pay for grid upgrades; Senate Democrats blocked it as "toothless."

Even Trump weighed in, writing on Truth Social that the only reason communities should reject data centers is if they want to "end up being backwards and poor." Owens called the post "tone deaf." "I mean, no one wants to be poor or backwards," he said. "I want our regular way of life."

## An Enthusiasm Gap

Brown County voted roughly 80 percent for Trump in 2024, and Owens argues the data center issue — combined with high fuel prices — is dampening Republican enthusiasm heading into the midterms. He noted the conspicuous absence of campaign yard signs in Mt. Orab.

"You always have depressed votes in the midterms," he said. "But I think the enthusiasm gap for Republicans in particularly rural areas is going to be a lot more pronounced... than any type of enthusiasm gap for Democrats in the cities."

## What to Watch

Voters in 10 Ohio communities will render their verdict on the local charter amendments on Nov. 3. Organizers behind the statewide amendment are now aiming for 2027, while a slate of parallel bills in the Ohio General Assembly — addressing everything from non-disclosure agreements to utility cost-shifting — works through the legislature. For Owens and his allies, the fight is about more than kilowatts and tax abatements. "This is the Ohio that people brag about," he said, gazing at the landscape. "This is the small town charm... Our community is not for sale."
