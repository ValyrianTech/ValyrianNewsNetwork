# Trump DOJ Files Third Criminal Case Against James Comey

The Trump administration has brought a third federal criminal case against former FBI Director James Comey, filing a criminal information in Washington, D.C., that accuses him of unlawfully sharing national defense information with a reporter a decade ago. The charges, [unsealed Monday](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/26/nx-s1-6018628/trump-administration-attempting-to-prosecute-former-fbi-director-james-comey-again), Oct. 26, 2026, escalated a long-running legal and political battle over the Justice Department's pursuit of people the president has cast as adversaries — and immediately sparked a fight over whether the case was filed in time.

The information, filed under seal on Oct. 6, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, accuses Comey of knowingly communicating national defense information to a person not entitled to receive it, and of conspiring to violate the law against sharing such information. Prosecutors allege that Comey, through one or more co-conspirators, directed an unidentified individual to disclose classified material to a reporter for a national news publication.

## A Decade-Old Leak

The charges stem from an October 2016 disclosure to The New York Times concerning a secret Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court order requiring Yahoo to scan emails potentially linked to a foreign terrorist organization. According to the government's account, Comey sought to correct misinformation that had appeared in the press the day before and directed his staff to speak to a Times journalist — an act prosecutors say amounted to the illegal transfer of classified information.

Comey's legal team tells a different story about the same episode. Attorney Patrick Fitzgerald said the allegation was investigated twice over the past decade — first by special counsel John Durham, who wrote a classified memo in December 2018 recommending no charges, and then in a second probe code-named Tropical Vortex, opened by the D.C. U.S. Attorney's office in January 2019 and closed without charges in February 2020.

"Both times the Government determined that no charges should be brought against anyone," Fitzgerald said. "Now, ten years after the article was published, the Government filed an Information against Mr. Comey that was not approved by a judge nor issued by a grand jury. It purports to charge Mr. Comey with crimes that he did not commit."

## The Statute-of-Limitations Fight

The central legal question is timing. The charges carry a 10-year statute of limitations, meaning prosecutors had to file within a decade of the alleged 2016 offense. The DOJ filed its paperwork under seal on Oct. 6, 2026 — roughly ten years after the Times story was published — but used a criminal information, which does not require grand jury approval, rather than an indictment, which does.

The statute governing these charges explicitly requires an indictment within ten years and makes no mention of an information. That is the crux of Comey's defense. On Oct. 9, 2026, his lawyers [moved to dismiss](https://www.ms.now/deadline-white-house/deadline-legal-blog/trump-doj-comey-case-statute-limitations), arguing the limitations period had expired and that a criminal information cannot satisfy the statutory requirement.

The Justice Department counters that the filing was a standard move to stop the clock. "Filing a criminal information to toll the statute of limitations is a widely recognized practice in federal court and has been approved by multiple Circuit Courts of Appeals," a department spokesperson said. Prosecutors point to the general five-year limitations law, which references both indictments and informations, and argue the 10-year statute is about extending the period, not dictating the form of the charges.

Notably, the DOJ's own brief concedes that Comey cannot be convicted without either an indictment or his waiver of indictment. The information's purpose, the government acknowledges, is to pause the clock — prosecutors still need to secure a grand jury indictment for the case to proceed. As legal analyst Jordan Rubin observed, if Comey is correct, then the failure to obtain an indictment within the 10-year window "is not a mere technical foot fault, it's the end of the case before it begins."

## A Wider Pattern

The new case is the third the Trump Justice Department has brought against Comey in little more than a year. A federal grand jury in Virginia first indicted him in September 2025 on false statement and obstruction charges; that case was dismissed in November 2025 when a judge ruled the interim U.S. attorney who brought it had been invalidly appointed. Comey was then indicted in North Carolina in 2026 over an Instagram post that prosecutors alleged threatened the president.

The timing of the latest filing raised additional questions. It became public hours after a federal judge in New York [ruled](https://www.reuters.com/world/ex-fbi-director-comey-charged-again-by-us-justice-department-nbc-news-reports-2026-10-26/) that the DOJ had unlawfully fired Comey's daughter, Maurene Comey, from her post as an assistant U.S. attorney in the Southern District of New York. The renewed push also comes just days before midterm elections in early November.

## What to Watch

Even if the government prevails on the tolling question, it faces further hurdles. It must still persuade a grand jury in Washington to return an indictment, and the administration has reportedly struggled to win grand jury approval in politically sensitive cases there. Comey is also expected to raise vindictive- and selective-prosecution arguments, which have become a recurring feature of litigation during Trump's second term.

If the case survives, it will test the boundaries of how the Justice Department can revive a time-barred charge — and whether a decade of declined prosecutions can be reversed with a single filing at the ten-year mark.

Comey, for his part, signaled he intends to fight. "Well, they're back again," he said in a video statement. "Nothing's changed with me; I'm still innocent, I'm still not afraid of these people, and I still believe in an independent federal judiciary."
