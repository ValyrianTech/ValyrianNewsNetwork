# China's Taiwan Office: No Ambiguity on Major Issues

China's State Council Taiwan Affairs Office (TAO) declared on Wednesday that there is "no room for ambiguity on major issues of right and wrong," drawing a hard line on national sovereignty while addressing a cluster of cross-strait controversies involving entertainers, cultural exchange and military ties with the United States.

Speaking at the office's regular press conference in Beijing on October 28, spokesperson Chen Binhua said the mainland "always respects and protects" the legitimate freedom of speech and expression of Taiwanese compatriots, but stressed that on questions concerning "national sovereignty, security and development interests, there is no room for ambiguity," according to [Xinhua News Agency](https://www.news.cn/tw/20261028/d68ebe02bf3c4d59b8ddc38fe9b0759e/c.html).

## An Apology and Its Fallout

The headline remark came in response to a question about Taiwanese television host Kevin Tsai, known in the mainland as Cai Kangyong, who posted on Weibo late on October 25 that he had "never supported Taiwan independence," apologizing for the controversy his recent appearance had triggered.

The 64-year-old entertainer had been photographed in the VIP front row at the campaign headquarters opening of Shen Po-yang, the Democratic Progressive Party's (DPP) candidate for Taipei mayor. The images prompted a swift commercial backlash on the mainland: electric-vehicle maker Leapmotor issued a statement saying Tsai was not a brand ambassador and removed related content, while Harper's Bazaar China deleted collaboration material, and platforms including iQiyi, Youku and Tencent stopped recommending his programs, according to [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/ZhengZhi/2026_10_28_906077.shtml).

Chen said the mainland hopes Taiwanese entertainment figures "take pride in being upright, honorable Chinese people, and do more things conducive to enhancing cross-strait kinship and promoting cross-strait exchanges and cooperation."

## A Mirror Image: The Zhang Linghe Case

In the same session, Chen addressed Taiwan's rejection of a visit application by mainland actor Zhang Linghe, who rose to widespread popularity in Taiwan through the period drama "Zhu Yu."

Taiwan's Mainland Affairs Council cited Zhang's repeated "derogatory" statements about Taiwan's status and what it called an unclear purpose for his activities. Chen rejected that rationale, saying that mainland entertainers' expectation of national reunification and opposition to "Taiwan independence" separatism is "natural and justified," and that the DPP authorities' refusal of "a mainland artist beloved by Taiwanese audiences on completely untenable grounds won't win hearts and will surely be opposed," [Guancha reported](https://www.guancha.cn/ZhengZhi/2026_10_28_906091.shtml).

The two cases were presented as mirror images: a welcome extended to a Taiwanese figure who aligns publicly with Beijing's position, and blame directed at Taipei for blocking a mainland star. Zhang had appeared at the Straits Forum in Xiamen on June 13, an event Taipei views as a united-front platform.

## Accusations Over Exchanges and Military Ties

Turning to people-to-people exchange, Chen accused the DPP of repeatedly hyping the so-called "mainland threat" and manipulating "resist China, protect Taiwan" rhetoric for its own gain, calling the party a "roadblock" to cross-strait exchanges and a "manufacturer of cross-strait hostility."

He also weighed in on reports that Taiwan's defense ministry had confirmed a budget of more than NT$80 million to invite US personnel to observe the "Han Kuang" military exercises over 2026–2027. Taiwan media put the figure at NT$82.438 million, drawing domestic criticism that the authorities were "crying poor to the people while being generous to outsiders," per [Guancha's earlier report](https://www.guancha.cn/JunShi/2026_10_22_905405.shtml).

Chen said the Taiwan question is "purely China's internal affair and brooks no foreign interference," warning that the DPP's attempt to "seek independence by force" and "rely on outsiders for independence" is "a miscalculation and a dead end."

## Analysis: Closing the Middle Ground

The phrase "no room for ambiguity" functions less as a general principle than as a targeted message to public figures who seek to occupy a middle position—denying support for independence without affirming unification. By pairing the Cai case with the Zhang case, Beijing signaled that non-endorsement of independence is no longer sufficient; an affirmative identification as "Chinese" and support for unity is now the expected baseline for those who wish to work in the mainland market.

The rapid commercial de-platforming of Cai illustrates how market access is being deployed as a discipline mechanism, translating the political line into economic consequences within days.

The statements also land amid Taiwan's local election cycle, with the Taipei mayoral race providing the immediate backdrop to the Cai controversy, and reflect Beijing's consistent framing that the DPP—rather than the mainland—is obstructing benign cross-strait exchange.

It should be noted that the primary account of the press conference comes from Xinhua, the official state news agency, and reflects the government's stated positions. Taiwanese framings—such as the Mainland Affairs Council's rationale for rejecting Zhang and domestic criticism of the Han Kuang budget—are captured through secondary mainland and Taiwan media reports and represent opposing, partisan characterizations.

## What to Watch

Attention now turns to whether Taiwanese public figures adjust their public positioning in response to the explicit demand for clarity, and whether the commercial consequences visited on Cai become a template for future cases. Also worth watching is how Taipei responds to Beijing's characterization of its military cooperation with Washington, and whether the exchange of barbs further narrows the space for cultural and people-to-people contact ahead of Taiwan's elections.
