# Nearly 3 in 10 Machines Idle in Belgian Factories

Belgian industry is weakening further. On average, 27 percent of machines in the country's factories stood idle over recent months, with the capacity utilisation rate in manufacturing falling to 73 percent in the third quarter of 2026 — the lowest level since April 2020, at the height of the first COVID-19 crisis. The figures, published by the National Bank of Belgium and analysed by business dailies De Tijd and L'Echo, have prompted the metal and technology federation Agoria to describe the industrial malaise as ["unprecedented"](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/28/bijna-3-op-10-machines-in-belgische-fabrieken-staan-stil/).

Since the National Bank began measuring manufacturing capacity utilisation in the late 1980s, only four quarters have recorded lower levels — all of them during the 2009 financial crisis or the COVID-19 pandemic. Compared with the 2021–2025 average, the current utilisation rate is nearly four percentage points lower.

## A Widening Regional Divide

The headline figure conceals a sharp split between Belgium's two main regions. Flemish factories are operating at 75.3 percent of available capacity — only 1.3 percentage points below their long-term average. In Wallonia, the utilisation rate is just 67.1 percent, more than 10 percentage points below the region's ten-year average.

The divergence points to the structure of Walloon industry, which remains heavily weighted toward energy-intensive heavy sectors such as steel and chemicals, concentrated along the Meuse and the Albert Canal. Energy-intensive industries have borne the brunt of persistently high electricity and gas prices across Europe.

## Energy-Intensive Sectors Take the Hits

The sectors dragging down the national average are precisely those that consume the most energy: the metal industry, iron and steel processing, building materials production, and machine building. Capacity utilisation in these segments is significantly lower than in recent years.

"In our sector we have been struggling for four years with a utilisation rate below 80 percent. That is unprecedented," Patrick Slaets, chief economist at Agoria, told VRT NWS. "The cause lies in a lack of demand. There are too few investments, due to the high interest rates that inflation brings with it, and energy costs that are too high because of the geopolitical situation."

The chemical sector offers an equally sobering picture. According to a study by consultancy Roland Berger, European chemical capacity shrank by nine percent between 2022 and 2025 — and the remaining, reduced capacity is only 69 to 70 percent utilised. "It is gradually becoming clear that this crisis cuts deeper than the previous one," Gert Verreth, spokesperson for the chemical federation Essenscia, told VRT NWS.

## Idle Machines, Lost Jobs

Falling utilisation rates are not merely an accounting concern; they translate directly into job losses. "If the machines aren't running, you don't need people working on them," Slaets said. "In the technology industry, 20,000 jobs have already been lost over the past three years. That is 6 to 7 percent less capacity than before the crisis."

The human cost is visible in places like Tessenderlo-Ham in Limburg, where the chemical company Vynova announced on 27 October that it would [temporarily halt production](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/27/vynova-productiestop-door-hoge-energiekosten/) because of exceptionally high energy prices. Production will be phased out over two weeks, with all installations shut down by 5 November, putting more than 600 employees at risk of temporary unemployment.

The company is in severe financial difficulty, carrying debts of more than €275 million and searching for a buyer for months. Creditor protection was extended to 26 December 2026. Yannick Brusselmans of Vynova Belgium said the measure aims "to limit the cash outflow," while the management stressed that the layoffs are temporary and that "we believe there is a future for the factory, but the energy price must fall."

The reaction was swift across the region. "This is an enormous blow for the staff," said Yannick Urbain of the socialist ABVV union. Voka Limburg called the move "a painful signal" and "a wake-up call for all policy levels," while Tessenderlo-Ham's CD&V mayor, Marc Heselmans, said the situation was "very worrying."

## A European Problem With a Belgian Cost Structure

Belgium is not facing this alone. Essenscia's Verreth stressed that the rest of Europe is grappling with similar difficulties, and that the competitive pressure is increasingly global. "Europe must act faster and urgently show its teeth," he told VRT NWS, pointing to an increasingly aggressive industrial policy abroad — including former US president Donald Trump's tariff war and Chinese state-subsidised mass production.

But Belgium has its own particular vulnerability: its labour costs, which are already higher than in neighbouring countries and risk rising further. "If [labour cost] is not kept in line with our neighbours, we could end up in an industrial recession next year," Slaets warned.

Sector federations, including Agoria, Essenscia and Voka, are calling for urgent action on energy costs, bureaucracy, and labour cost. A discount on transmission grid tariffs for Belgian industry is due to take effect from November 2026, though analysts question whether it is large enough to offset the country's structural energy-cost disadvantage.

## What to Watch Next

Three questions will shape the trajectory of Belgian manufacturing over the coming months. First, whether the November grid-tariff discount meaningfully eases pressure on energy-intensive firms, or whether deeper structural measures are needed. Second, whether labour costs can be brought back in line with neighbouring countries before the risk of an outright industrial recession in 2027 materialises. And third, whether Vynova can secure a buyer before its creditor protection lapses on 26 December — a test case for whether Belgium's crisis-hit chemical sector can be preserved.

For now, the data paints a clear picture: nearly three in ten machines across the country sit silent, and an industry that once ran close to full tilt is operating well below its capacity — with jobs, investment, and Belgium's industrial base hanging in the balance.
