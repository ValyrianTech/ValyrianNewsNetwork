# China's Wind and Solar Capacity Passes 2 Terawatts

China's cumulative installed wind and solar power capacity has crossed the 2-terawatt threshold, reaching 2.01 billion kilowatts (2.01 TW) at the end of September 2026, according to data from the National Energy Administration. The milestone, reported by state media between 26 and 28 October, underscores the scale of the world's largest renewable energy buildout and reinforces Beijing's dominant position in the global clean energy transition.

The figures, first carried by [People's Daily](http://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1028/c1004-40822812.html), put total national power generation capacity at 4.13 billion kW, up 11.2% year-on-year. Within that total, wind capacity reached 700 million kW, a 20.4% increase, while solar capacity climbed 16.5% to 1.31 billion kW — the two components summing to the 2.01 billion kW combined figure.

## A buildout measured in terawatts

The pace of China's renewable expansion is now reshaping global energy statistics. According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/photo/20261027/06619b16575b4434bbebcc5a27147b85/c.html), the National Energy Administration confirmed the 2.01 billion kW figure on 26 October, describing it as a landmark for the country's new energy sector.

The numbers are matched by equally striking generation data. In the first three quarters of 2026, China's wind and solar plants generated 1,933.3 billion kWh, an increase of 193.7 billion kWh over the same period last year. Crucially, that growth accounted for 64% of the increase in total social electricity consumption — meaning renewables are now absorbing the lion's share of new demand rather than simply adding to supply.

At moments of peak strain, the contribution is even more pronounced. On the highest-load day during this summer's peak period, midday wind and solar output exceeded 40% of total load. Investment has followed: wind and solar projects accounted for roughly one-third of all completed investment in the energy sector during the first three quarters.

## A global leader, with room to run

China's wind power installed capacity has now ranked first globally for 16 consecutive years. Combined wind and solar capacity represents roughly half of the world's total new energy installed capacity, according to the China Electricity Council.

That dominance is visible in independent data. [Ember's Global Electricity Review 2026](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/global-electricity-review-2026/) found that China alone recorded more than half of the global increase in both solar capacity and solar generation in 2025, pushing solar and wind's share of China's generation mix to 22% — surpassing the OECD average of 20%.

"From an industry development perspective, wind and solar cumulative installed capacity surpassing 2 billion kW further consolidates China's leading advantage in the global new energy industrial chain," said Liu Zhiqiang, deputy director of the planning and development department at the China Electricity Council. He added that the milestone "injects strong momentum into cultivating new quality productive forces."

The announcement lands in the opening year of China's 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030). The "Renewable Energy Development 15th Five-Year Plan" sets four targets — total volume, power generation, non-electricity utilization and reliable substitution — and aims for average confidence output from wind and solar to reach 8% by 2030, with the two sources supplying more than 20% of electricity during summer and winter evening peaks.

"This year is the opening year of the '15th Five-Year Plan,'" said Pan Huimin, deputy director general of the National Energy Administration's new energy and renewable energy department. "The 'Renewable Energy Development 15th Five-Year Plan' proposes four goals ... and the future of wind power development is promising."

## Headwinds beneath the headline

Despite the scale of the achievement, industry analysts point to mounting pressures. As [China Economic Net's Economic Daily analysis](https://news.sina.cn/znl/2026-10-28/detail-iniwttsa6627422.d.html) detailed, Liang Zhipeng of the China Energy Research Society's renewable energy committee identified four challenges: grid consumption constraints limiting new energy's room to displace fossil fuels; power security supply still heavily reliant on coal, which restricts consumption space; sharply falling revenues for new energy projects after they enter market trading; and a gap between growing industrial demand and users' expectations for green, low-cost power.

The revenue squeeze is prompting industry self-help. Several major turbine manufacturers have extended turbine design lifespans from 20 to 25 years — a change industry calculations suggest could raise total operating-period generation profit by more than 56% and cut the levelized cost of electricity by 6% to 7%.

"Domestic new energy generators are not earning much, so extending turbine service life and improving whole-lifecycle returns has become the industry's choice for self-rescue," said Lin Zhigang, deputy general manager of Sany Renewable Energy.

The industry is also shifting from "intermittent and fluctuating" power toward "reliable and stable" supply. Companies such as Envision Energy are developing AI-integrated wind-storage units and using meteorological models for wind forecasting, while wind power is increasingly being converted into green hydrogen, ammonia and methanol for hard-to-abate sectors such as shipping and aviation.

## What to watch

The 2-terawatt milestone is less a finish line than a marker on a longer course. The coming years will test whether China can convert sheer scale into reliable, economically sustainable supply — resolving grid bottlenecks, reforming power market mechanisms and managing the transition away from coal.

One signal to watch is whether wind buildout decouples from grid construction cycles. Historically, wind capacity followed State Grid's ultra-high-voltage planning and was constrained by transmission buildout. During the 15th Five-Year Plan, wind power may increasingly be converted directly into green computing power and green fuels, potentially escaping grid infrastructure constraints altogether.

Another is how quickly the revenue problem is solved. Without healthier project economics, the momentum behind the world's largest renewable fleet could slow — a development with consequences far beyond China's borders.

For now, the direction of travel is clear. As Ember's analysis put it, clean power is increasingly meeting demand growth, and those scaling it fastest "will be best placed to reduce fossil fuel dependence in the short term and support economic growth in the long term."
