# i-Police: €75m Lost, Calls Grow for Inquiry

Belgium's flagship police IT modernisation programme, i-Police, has sunk an estimated €75 million in public money with no tangible result, and the revelations now piling up around the fiasco have prompted fresh demands for a full parliamentary inquiry commission. In an [editorial published by Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779464/article/2026-10-28/i-police-75-millions-perdus-ca-vaut-une-commission-denquete) on 28 October, the newspaper argues that the scale of the waste — compounded by suspicions of conflict of interest and an alleged attempt by the Justice Minister's office to extract confidential information from prosecutors — warrants the Chamber's heaviest investigative tool.

"i-Police was a promise: to revolutionise Belgian police computing... it has become the symbol of an incredible waste of public money," wrote Bernard Demonty, head of Le Soir's Powers desk, adding that "revelations are piling up around the i-Police fiasco, but the political world continues to dodge" its responsibility.

## A €300 million project that never delivered

i-Police was launched in 2021 with an indicative budget of roughly €300 million and awarded via public procurement to the French IT consultancy multinational Sopra Steria. Its stated goals were ambitious: interconnect data scattered across a "constellation of systems," give external partners access to certain data, equip officers with digital tools for fieldwork, and ultimately sharpen the fight against terrorism and organised crime.

Annelies Verlinden (CD&V), then Minister of the Interior under the Vivaldi government, signed off on the contract. Years later, as [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/i-police-un-fiasco-financier-de-76-millions-d-euros-un-manque-de-leadership-et-competences-du-cote-de-la-police-federale-11669282), the structure of the deal proved deeply advantageous to the contractor: "whatever the progress of the project, the invoice fell monthly, regularly, without evaluation of the progress." Belgium, in effect, signed its cheques blind.

On 26 December 2025, Interior Minister Bernard Quintin (MR) terminated the project after €75.8 million had been spent with no tangible result. RTBF's reporting stressed that €76 million is the "low hypothesis" — the amount actually paid to Sopra Steria — with further bills outstanding for the wider project. The following month, on 27 January 2026, the Brussels prosecutor's office opened a judicial investigation into potential embezzlement of public funds and "prises d'intérêt" (conflict of interest), targeting both the company and the civil servants responsible.

## From procurement failure to a separation-of-powers crisis

What began as an IT debacle has now fused with a constitutional question. According to [Le Soir and De Standaard](https://www.lesoir.be/779271/article/2026-10-27/scandale-i-police-le-cabinet-dannelies-verlinden-soupconne-davoir-viole-le), a Brussels investigating judge placed Verlinden's spokesperson, N.G., under formal investigation as co-author of a violation of professional secrecy, alongside L.D., the Dutch-speaking spokesperson of the Brussels prosecutor's office. L.D. is alleged to have passed confidential information on the i-Police investigation to Verlinden's cabinet — an alleged act of espionage inside the Justice Minister's own office.

Both individuals enjoy the presumption of innocence; inculpation means only that the judge found sufficient serious indications of guilt. Verlinden has denied the espionage allegations against her cabinet.

The affair also carries a conflict-of-interest dimension. As [Le Soir detailed](https://www.lesoir.be/778119/article/2026-10-21/fiasco-i-police-le-potentiel-conflit-dinterets-qui-embarrasse-annelies-verlinden), before entering government Verlinden had represented Sopra Steria as a lawyer, and her partner is an executive at DLA Piper, the law firm defending the contractor's interests — facts she did not disclose to the Chamber when it was investigating the fiasco.

## Cross-party pressure, institutional hesitation

The political reaction has been swift and, unusually, cuts across the coalition. In remarks reported after the inculpations, [PS, Anders and Groen](https://www.lesoir.be/779287/article/2026-10-27/scandale-i-police-le-ps-anders-et-groen-exigent-une-explication-rapide-de) demanded a rapid explanation from the minister. "She can no longer dodge and must draw her conclusions as a matter of urgency, because confidence in her is broken... Clinging to her post is no longer an option," said PS MP Ridouane Chahid.

Anders MP Arthur Orlians said the dossier "was cruelly lacking in transparency." Groen group leader Matti Vandemaele was blunter still: "It is already obvious that Minister Verlinden intervened far too late and with far too little firmness, and played a dubious role." Groen has renewed its demand for a genuine inquiry commission, and Vlaams Belang has also called for a parliamentary investigation.

Yet the machinery of scrutiny has not moved at the same pace. On 28 October, the president of the Chamber's Justice Commission, Ismaël Nuino (Les Engagés), ruled out an urgent meeting this week, arguing it is "neither urgent nor relevant" to ask the Justice Minister to comment on an ongoing judicial investigation. He prefers to await the Court of Audit's report, expected within days, before the commissions seek to establish the facts. Ortwin Depoortere (Vlaams Belang), who chairs the Interior Commission, had wanted a joint Interior–Justice hearing, ideally this week.

## What to watch

The standoff captures the story's central tension: demands for accountability versus institutional delay. Three questions will shape what comes next. First, whether the Court of Audit report — expected imminently — provides the trigger for formal hearings or a full inquiry commission. Second, whether Verlinden can survive politically, with pressure mounting from opposition parties and unease even within the coalition. Third, what the judicial investigation concludes on embezzlement, conflict of interest and the professional-secrecy inculpations.

The final cost also remains open. Beyond the €75.8 million paid to Sopra Steria, Belgium has spent a further €1.19 million on audits, legal fees and crisis management linked to the project's derailment, and litigation between the police and the contractor is under way. For a project sold as a leap forward for Belgian policing, the bill — financial, political and institutional — is still being counted.
