# Brussels cyclist may lose eye after traffic assault

A Brussels cyclist is likely to lose the sight in his left eye after being beaten unconscious by a van driver in Schaerbeek, an attack that has renewed attention on aggression between road users in the capital. Laurent Nyssen, a graphic designer who lives in the municipality, also suffered a broken jaw in the confrontation on the evening of 14 October, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/27/verkeersagressie-in-schaarbeek-fietser-loopt-gebroken-kaken-en/).

The driver, a man of around 30 whose identity has not been made public, was arrested the same night and held in custody for six days before being released under electronic monitoring. He is due to appear in court in November.

## How the confrontation unfolded

Around 18:45 on 14 October, Nyssen was cycling to collect a parcel. Traffic in the Koninklijke Sinte-Mariastraat was gridlocked, and cars were blocking a cycle lane that is wide but not physically separated from the vehicle carriageway.

"There was a traffic jam. I rang my bell at the many cars blocking the cycle path, something that happens every day in Brussels," Nyssen told [BRUZZ](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/veiligheid/verkeersagressie-schaarbeek-fietser-loopt-gebroken-kaken-en-blijvende-oogschade-op-2026-10-27), which first reported the case. A van driver behind him took offence, shouting abuse at a first traffic light. At a second light, the driver got out of his vehicle. "I have no memory of what happened after that," Nyssen said.

Two witnesses and a surveillance camera captured the attack. "He completely lost it. I got about ten blows to my head. He hit me so hard that I fell to the ground," Nyssen recounted. He was taken to hospital with two jaw fractures and severe damage to his left eye. He has since undergone two operations on the eye.

"The doctors fear that I will never be able to see with it again," he said. "There is still a very small chance that part of my sight will recover over time, but it will never be as it was before."

## A suspect at home with an ankle monitor

Police informed Nyssen at his hospital bedside in the middle of the night that a suspect had been arrested. "He spent six days in a cell and is now at home with an ankle monitor. I know nothing more about him," he said.

The victim intends to join the proceedings as a civil party and argues the case should be treated more seriously than a common assault. "The police told me I was lucky to survive. I think he should stand trial for attempted manslaughter," Nyssen said, using the Dutch term *poging tot doodslag*. That charge, which requires establishing intent, has not been confirmed by prosecutors; the judicial details in the case so far rest on the victim's account.

## "A culture of hatred against cyclists"

Nyssen frames his ordeal not as an isolated flashpoint but as part of a wider pattern. "There is a culture of hatred against cyclists in Brussels. On the street the law of the jungle prevails. And aggression against cyclists gets worse every year," he said. "Drivers feel they are king of the road in Brussels; they pay no attention at all to cyclists. And if you say something about it, you get the full force of it."

He said nearly all of his cycling friends have experienced aggression, and that one of them also ended up in hospital after an argument with a driver. He singles out Schaerbeek and Anderlecht as areas where cyclists feel particularly stigmatised, while acknowledging that some riders — notably food delivery couriers racing to complete orders — break the rules. "But that is completely out of proportion to the danger cyclists face," he argued.

## The wider stakes

Cycling is a stated policy priority in Brussels, but the capital's infrastructure remains uneven. The Sinte-Mariastraat's wide-but-unsegregated cycle lanes are a design that exposes riders to conflict with vehicles, especially in congestion. Traffic aggression is a recurring theme in local media and politics, prompting periodic calls for lower speed limits, protected cycle lanes and tougher enforcement — debates that this case is likely to reignite.

For now, Nyssen is on sick leave and adapting to life with sight in one eye. He says the brain adjusts faster than he expected and that his work as a graphic designer remains possible, though judging distances on foot has become harder. He is also building an app to help future victims of aggression navigate legal procedures and gather the right documents.

"I get a great deal of support from my girlfriend and daughter, from my family and friends," he said. "And I am determined to cycle again. Not right away, but once winter is over. I won't let myself be chased off the street."

## What to watch

The case moves to court in November, when the charges against the suspect will become clearer and Nyssen is expected to formalise his civil claim. Whether prosecutors pursue attempted manslaughter or a lesser assault charge — and whether the case prompts any action on protected cycle lanes in Schaerbeek — will determine how far its impact reaches beyond one rider's recovery.
