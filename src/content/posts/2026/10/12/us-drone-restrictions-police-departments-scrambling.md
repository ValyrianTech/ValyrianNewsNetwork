# US Drone Restrictions Leave Police Departments Scrambling

A federal push to end Chinese dominance of the American drone market is colliding with the reality of modern policing, leaving hundreds of law enforcement agencies facing sharply higher costs, diminished capabilities, or the outright loss of aerial surveillance tools they have come to depend on.

Roughly 6,000 U.S. law enforcement agencies now operate drones, and an estimated 80% to 90% of them fly devices made by DJI, the Chinese manufacturer, according to the Law Enforcement Drone Association (LEDA), which advises and certifies police drone programs. Now, as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/12/nx-s1-5888604/police-chinese-drones-bans-costs), a combination of tariffs, import bans, and a pending regulatory reclassification is threatening to sever that reliance — with taxpayers ultimately absorbing the bill.

## A National Security Fight Meets a Budget Reality

The Trump administration has moved aggressively against Chinese-made drones on national security grounds. In June 2025, the White House issued an executive order, ["Unleashing American Drone Dominance,"](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/06/unleashing-american-drone-dominance/) directing agencies to prioritize U.S.-manufactured drones and to secure the drone supply chain against foreign control. In August 2026, the administration [announced tariffs](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/08/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-bolsters-national-security-and-strengthens-u-s-supply-chains-by-imposing-tariffs-on-drones-and-their-parts-and-components/) ranging from 15% to 100% on certain foreign-made drones, taking effect on a rolling schedule through early 2027.

The Federal Communications Commission, meanwhile, is weighing a rule that would reclassify drones with integrated capabilities such as thermal imaging as "military grade," effectively banning many Chinese-made models that use that technology. The FCC already blocks the sale of some new foreign drone models without agency approval, and federal agencies and contractors are barred from using certain foreign-made drones.

Critics argue the domestic industry cannot yet match the price or performance of Chinese drones — and that local police departments are getting caught in the middle. "We're seeing lives being saved with the use of this technology," said Jon Beal, head of LEDA. "What that's going to do is cancel out perfectly viable drone programs that are doing great work out there. We don't want to cut off our nose to spite our face."

## The Cost Gap in Hard Numbers

The core problem, departments say, is arithmetic. Ocean County Sheriff's Office in New Jersey, one of the agencies profiled by NPR, breaks down the cost of a single deputy's drone kit: a DJI Matrice 300 RTK at $13,699, two batteries at $1,400, an infrared thermal camera at $11,499, a remote controller at $1,600, a flotation device at $130.99, and a payload release system at $1,099. The total comes to $29,427.99.

American-made alternatives are substantially pricier. NPR compared the DJI Matrice 400, which retails for about $10,500, with the American-made Inspired Flight IF800 TOMCAT, which sells for roughly $21,000. Beal said DJI offers a drone with a high-resolution thermal camera for under $10,000, while American-made equivalents typically cost $20,000 to $50,000 — with worse flight time, thermal imaging, and zoom.

Kevin Fennessy, the chief warrant officer who heads Ocean County's drone program, said he has flown nearly all the American models. "All their stuff is good. I've flown almost all of them. And I like them. I do. They make good products, but probably about three times the cost" of DJI tools, he said. Beal characterized Chinese drones as "light years ahead of what our American drones are able to do."

The consequences are already visible. Agencies in Florida, Texas, Mississippi, and Tennessee have had to terminate their drone programs or abandon plans for them after state lawmakers passed bans on agencies using foreign drones, according to [FOX 13 News](https://www.fox13news.com/news/lakeland-police-restart-drone-program-after-foreign-surveillance-concerns). In Lakeland, Florida, police paused their program for two years after a state law took effect in 2023, relaunching it only after a state buyback program offered $25,000 per retired drone. A broader survey of the shifting legal landscape, compiled by [Axon](https://www.axon.com/resources/new-drone-laws-take-effect), documents how Florida, Texas, Mississippi, Arkansas, and Tennessee have moved to restrict foreign drones in recent years.

## Why Replacement Is Harder Than It Sounds

Even setting cost aside, building a domestic supply chain is a slow undertaking. China controls an estimated 80% to 90% of the global drone market and dominates the supply of the critical minerals and components used to build them, according to the Center for European Policy Analysis ([CEPA](https://cepa.org/article/reality-check-breaking-free-from-chinas-drone-ecosystem-is-harder-than-you-think/)). That analysis notes that even drones on the Pentagon's approved lists have relied on Chinese components and that shifting to domestic battery production could take years.

Beal echoed that constraint. "We don't have a lot of the minerals. We can't produce the battery now. Even the American companies are using Chinese batteries," he said. "We're kind of limited to the resources that we have in-country or in an allied country, and sometimes that's just not there."

DJI, for its part, disputes the security rationale. The company was added to the FCC's Covered List in December 2025, a designation it says was made without a specific, documented vulnerability, and it has appealed. DJI commissioned an independent assessment by the U.S. cybersecurity firm OnDefend covering its Air 3S and Matrice 4E models; the firm reported zero critical, high, or medium-risk findings and no evidence of data transmission outside the United States. DJI [published the findings](https://www.dji.com/media-center/announcements/dji-drone-independent-security-assessment) in May 2026, with head of global policy Adam Welsh saying the results "confirm what DJI has consistently maintained: our products are secure, our data practices are transparent." DJI has also [denied allegations](https://viewpoints.dji.com/blog/navigating-u.s.-customs-demonstrating-djis-commitment-to-ethical-production-labor-practices) related to forced labor that prompted U.S. Customs and Border Protection to block some imports in 2024.

## Who Ends Up Paying

The National Sheriffs' Association, representing more than 3,000 sheriffs, has asked the FCC to reconsider the proposed "military grade" reclassification, arguing that the domestic market for such equipment is not yet adequate. It has recommended an exemption for state, local, tribal, and territorial law enforcement until domestic suppliers can meet demand.

Federal and state grants exist to help agencies buy American-made drones, but they are competitive and limited. Fennessy said more funding will be essential if departments are eventually forced to retire their DJI fleets. "If the federal government wants to say, hey, listen, you can't fly your DJI drone anymore, well, that's cool," he said. "How am I going to afford to buy the drones that you want me to fly? The taxpayers of Ocean County are going to absorb that. The funding's got to be there."

For now, the administration frames the transition as a strategic necessity. The FCC said in a statement that America's drone industry is "booming with new investment, manufacturing, and jobs," adding that the agency is "working hand-in-glove across government and the private sector to make sure that U.S. drone and counter-drone supply chains are safe, secure, and reliable for all." Some American manufacturers see an opening. Keith Miao, chief executive of the Detroit-based company Birdstop, said the restrictions give U.S. firms a chance to "rethink … and build out drone manufacturing," adding that his company is "going all in."

## What to Watch

The next flashpoint is the FCC's decision on the military-grade reclassification and whether it grants a law enforcement exemption along the lines the sheriffs' association has requested. How much federal funding reaches local departments — and when — will determine whether drone programs survive the transition or disappear. And the broader question remains whether the U.S. domestic industry can close the cost and capability gap before the DJI fleets now in service age out.

For departments like Ocean County's, the calculus is immediate. "To be honest, right now, I want something that's capable to go out there and do the job," Fennessy said. "And I have it."
