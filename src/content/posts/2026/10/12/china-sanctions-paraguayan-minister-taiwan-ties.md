# China Sanctions Paraguayan Minister Over Taiwan Ties

China announced countermeasures on Monday against Paraguay's Minister of Communications and Information Technology, Gustavo Emigdio Villate Samaniego, invoking its Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law over what it described as anti-China activity tied to Paraguay's continued diplomatic recognition of Taiwan. The decision was issued as Ministry of Foreign Affairs Order No. 21 and took effect the same day, October 12, 2026.

The move, published on the foreign ministry's website and carried by [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/world/20261012/408e70db3bce474a8e67e2f9ef3fdb43/c.html), marks Beijing's latest use of a legal tool it has increasingly deployed against foreign individuals it accuses of harming Chinese sovereignty. The story's official title names a second target, "Fonseca," though the text of that decision was not immediately available.

## What the Order Says

Under Order No. 21, China will freeze Villate's movable property, real estate and other assets within Chinese territory; bar Chinese organizations and individuals from conducting transactions, cooperation or other activities with him; and deny him a visa and entry to China, including Hong Kong and Macao.

The order cites Articles 3, 4, 6, 9 and 15 of the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law of the People's Republic of China. In its stated rationale, Beijing said "certain anti-China politicians in Paraguay, acting out of self-interest, stubbornly supported Paraguay maintaining so-called 'diplomatic relations' with China's Taiwan region, and took a series of egregious statements and actions on China-related issues, harming China's core interests."

It added that the measures were taken "to safeguard national sovereignty, security and development interests, and to oppose 'Taiwan independence' separatist acts." The attached countermeasure list identifies the target as Villate, male, of Paraguayan nationality.

## A Target With a Tech Portfolio

Villate, who heads Paraguay's MITIC ministry, has been a visible figure in the country's digital agenda. Through 2026 he publicly promoted fintech development, 5G rollout, data-protection legislation, digital identity systems and AI data centers, and he has been associated with technology-cooperation initiatives involving Taiwan. That overlap between his ministerial portfolio and cross-strait diplomacy appears central to Beijing's framing of the case.

The order does not detail which specific statements or actions triggered the sanctions. Analysts note that the language is deliberately broad, mirroring the general phrasing Beijing has used in previous applications of the law.

## The Taiwan Contest, in Context

Paraguay is now Taiwan's only remaining diplomatic ally in South America and one of roughly a dozen worldwide. Its alignment has made it a recurring focal point in Beijing's broader campaign to isolate Taiwan diplomatically.

The trend has run against Taipei for a decade. Ten countries - among them Panama, Honduras, El Salvador, the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua - have switched recognition from Taiwan to mainland China, according to [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/internation/2026_04_30_815501.shtml), which reported on a New York Times examination of Paraguay's shrinking global circle.

The same coverage captured the discontent inside Paraguay. Opposition Senator Esperanza Martinez, a former health minister, criticized the government's pro-Taiwan policy in blunt terms. "They are just using us," she said. "It is we who sold ourselves cheaply." President Santiago Pena has defended the stance, casting Paraguay and Taiwan as kindred underdogs: "Paraguay and the Taiwan region have one thing in common: both are the weaker side."

The economic stakes are considerable. Countries that maintain ties with Taiwan often face barriers exporting agricultural goods and commodities to the mainland, and Paraguayan ranchers and agribusinesses have complained that the alliance blocks access to Chinese markets for beef and soy.

Beijing has repeatedly urged Asuncion to switch. After Pena visited Taiwan in May 2026, foreign ministry spokesperson Lin Jian said the one-China principle was a "basic norm of international relations" and noted that 183 countries had established diplomatic ties with China on that basis, as [reported](https://c.m.163.com/news/a/KSB7DNE40512D3VJ.html) by Xinhua and carried by the Beijing News. "We advise the Paraguayan authorities to stand on the right side of history," Lin said.

## Analysis: A Signal Beyond Asuncion

Monday's order is narrow in its immediate effect - a single minister's assets and travel - but broad in its signal. By invoking the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law over a Taiwan-related grievance, Beijing is reinforcing a template it has applied in disputes involving Xinjiang, Hong Kong and Taiwan, and signaling to other governments that alignment with Taipei carries individual costs.

Several factors amplify the message. Paraguay's stance aligns with U.S. efforts to counter Chinese influence in Latin America, and Asuncion has drawn praise from Washington as a reliable partner. That dynamic makes Paraguay a symbolic target: small in economic weight, but visible in the geopolitical contest. As one U.S. Army War College scholar characterized it in the Guancha report, Paraguay offers Beijing both "symbolic meaning" and "strategic value."

The naming of a second individual - Fonseca - suggests a coordinated batch of measures rather than an isolated action. The identity and role of that second figure could not be confirmed from available sources, and the absence of a published decision text leaves a key element of Monday's announcement unresolved.

It also remains unclear whether the measures will shift Paraguay's calculus. Supporters of the current policy argue that switching recognition would reward coercion, while critics argue that the alliance delivers diminishing benefits as Taiwan's diplomatic circle contracts.

## What to Watch

Within Paraguay, pressure is building from agricultural exporters who see mainland China as a larger and more lucrative market, and from opposition figures who argue the country is getting little in return for its loyalty. Whether those voices gain traction in Asuncion - or whether the government doubles down - will shape the next chapter of the contest.

For now, Beijing has made its position unambiguous: it will keep using economic and legal tools to raise the cost of recognizing Taipei. The unanswered questions are how far it will extend the current batch of measures, and whether the target list grows beyond the two names announced on Monday.
