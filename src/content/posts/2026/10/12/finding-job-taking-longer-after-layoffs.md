# Finding a Job Is Taking Longer After Layoffs

American workers who lose their jobs are taking significantly longer to find new ones, as a sluggish hiring environment leaves unemployment benefits running out well before paychecks resume, according to an [NPR report](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/11/nx-s1-5995238/jobs-employment-economy-unemployment-benefits) by economics correspondent Scott Horsley. With just 29,000 jobs added in September — roughly one opening for every 244 unemployed workers — even a low unemployment rate is offering little comfort to those stuck on the sidelines of the labor market.

At 4.2% in September, the jobless rate remains historically low. But that figure masks a harder reality for anyone searching for work: of the more than 7 million unemployed Americans, nearly 2 million have been out of a job for six months or more.

## A Cooling Market With Few Openings

The trouble is not widespread layoffs — it is the absence of hiring. Employers added far fewer jobs in September than forecasters expected, and gains for July and August were revised down by a combined 60,000, pushing July's totals negative, NPR [reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/02/nx-s1-5989140/jobs-labor-wages-federal-reserve). Even healthcare, a reliable source of employment, added only 17,000 positions.

"The good news is you're not seeing a lot of layoffs," Sarah House, a senior economist at Wells Fargo, told NPR. "But it's really hard if you are one of those workers who loses your job... there's not a lot of turnover. So it makes it harder to get your foot in the door right now."

Wages are also lagging. Average pay in September was up just 3% from a year earlier — likely below inflation — eroding workers' real buying power even as the Federal Reserve raised interest rates in September to fight rising prices.

## Counting the Human Cost

The strain shows up in individual lives. Evan Smith lost his job at Trader Interactive, an online vehicle-trading platform, in the spring and has applied for dozens of positions since.

"You find something that you're excited about or something that you think fits your profile and you go in and over 100 people have already clicked 'applied for it' in the last two weeks," Smith said. "So you just feel like you're a number."

Cheryl Huff, laid off from a Colorado state government job about four months ago, has spent hours tailoring her résumé for each of dozens of applications, landing only a handful of interviews — all by phone. She once received two messages at once from the same employer, one saying her application was under review and another saying the job had already been filled.

"That's one of the frustrations that people are having," Huff said. "Did you even look at my stuff?"

She is paying $1,300 a month to keep her old employer-provided health insurance, relying on her husband's Social Security and her unemployment check. "It's amazing how little money that is when you go to the store and you see a bag of granola is $8.50," she said.

## When Benefits Run Out First

The clock is a central problem: in many states, unemployment benefits expire long before workers find new work. Duration varies sharply by state — Colorado offers six months, while North Carolina offers just 12 weeks, capped at $350 a week.

Keke Moore, a meeting planner in Raleigh, North Carolina, lost her job last December and has been out of work for nearly a year. After her benefits expired, her 21-year-old son began helping with household bills.

"With so many people being out of work, we're all out here, it feels like to me, fighting for scraps," Moore said. "I feel like a failure as a parent, because it's like I don't want him worrying about bills."

Research from the [Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond](https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-24) underscores how structural the problem has become. A typical unemployment insurance spell now runs 8.2 weeks, up from 6.5 weeks in 2022. And the share of claimants leaving the rolls because they ran out of benefits — rather than finding work — more than doubled, from 3.3% of all exits in 2022 to 7.3% over the 12 months through June 2026.

Claudia Macaluso, a senior research economist at the Richmond Fed, framed the shift bluntly: the labor market of mid-2026 "has become less likely to lose a job but more costly if it happens."

## The Limits of the Data

The Richmond Fed analysis cautions that official claims figures carry a built-in blind spot. Continuing claims cannot count anyone unemployed longer than their state allows benefits, so the measure understates long-term joblessness — and the understatement grows as conditions worsen. The 8.2-week figure should be read as a floor, not an estimate.

State policy amplifies the effect. Benefits are capped below 26 weeks in 16 states, and five states stop at 12 weeks — where nearly half of all exits are exhaustions. Only nine jurisdictions grant a uniform 26 weeks. Because restrictive states have fewer claimants, they weigh heavily on the national picture despite representing a small share of the total.

Only about one-quarter to one-third of unemployed people draw benefits at all, meaning the data describe job losers with sufficient earnings history, not the unemployed in general.

## What to Watch

The Fed's rate increase in September complicates the outlook: policymakers are trying to cool inflation even as the job market softens, a tension that may keep hiring subdued.

Economists say the number to watch is the exit rate — how quickly people leave unemployment — rather than headline claims, which can fall simply because fewer people are entering a pool that remains just as hard to leave. Also worth monitoring is the Labor Department's outright exhaustion rate, which directly counts the people the continuing-claims series drops.

For now, the trend points one direction. As Sarah House of Wells Fargo put it, the market offers little turnover and few open doors — a difficult combination for anyone who suddenly finds themselves on the outside looking in.
