# SpaceX's Starlink Sets Sights on Becoming a Mobile Carrier

SpaceX has agreed to acquire a nationwide portfolio of low-band wireless spectrum from investment firm Grain Management, a deal the company says supplies the final ingredient needed to turn its Starlink satellite service into a standalone mobile carrier in the United States. The announcement, made on October 8, 2026, sent shares of the country's largest wireless operators tumbling as investors began pricing in the arrival of a potential fourth national network.

According to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/spacex-starlink-mobile-carrier-radio-airwaves/), SpaceX is buying licenses for up to 14 MHz of paired low-band airwaves in the 800 MHz band. The transaction remains subject to approval by the Federal Communications Commission and review by the Department of Justice.

"This is the last critical piece of the spectrum puzzle needed for SpaceX to provide complete phone coverage in America," CEO Elon Musk said in a social media post announcing the agreement.

## Why Low-Band Spectrum Matters

The significance of the purchase lies in the physics of radio. Low-band spectrum below 1 GHz travels farther and diffracts over terrain more effectively than higher frequencies, and it readily penetrates concrete walls, roofs and energy-efficient glass. Higher-frequency mid-band and millimeter-wave signals, by contrast, are far more easily blocked by physical structures.

That property addresses the central weakness of direct-to-device satellite service, which has historically struggled to maintain reliable connections indoors. As [Satnews](https://satnews.com/2026/10/11/spacex-acquires-grain-managements-800-mhz-spectrum-portfolio-to-launch-standalone-starlink-mobile-carrier/) explained, the new 800 MHz airwaves will allow Starlink's orbital payloads to reach standard cell phones inside homes, offices and industrial facilities without external antennas or signal boosters.

SpaceX said the spectrum is compatible with most mobile devices already in use, potentially removing the need for consumers to buy specialized satellite phones.

## A Deal With a Long Lineage

The 800 MHz portfolio has changed hands several times. As [Grain Management](https://graingp.com/grain-management-announces-definitive-agreement-to-sell-nationwide-800-mhz-spectrum-portfolio-to-spacex/) noted in its announcement, the licenses were formerly part of Sprint's legacy 800 MHz Specialized Mobile Radio band, which passed to T-Mobile US after the Sprint-T-Mobile merger. In August 2026, Grain acquired the portfolio from T-Mobile in an asset swap involving cash and Grain's own 600 MHz spectrum holdings.

"Our spectrum expertise allows us to connect the strategic value of these assets with the technologies and operators that can realize their potential," said Grain founder and CEO David Grain. "This agreement with SpaceX brings that capability to bear at extraordinary scale, with the potential to change where and how Americans connect."

The financial terms of the agreement were not publicly disclosed by SpaceX or Grain Management, according to [LiveMint](https://www.livemint.com/market/stock-market-news/att-t-mobile-and-verizon-shares-tumble-up-to-11-as-musk-s-spacex-makes-starlink-mobile-push-11791560971489.html), and neither company has confirmed a dollar value for the deal.

The purchase marks the latest in a string of major spectrum investments by SpaceX. The company previously committed roughly $17 billion to acquire 65 MHz of mid-band AWS-4 and H-Block spectrum from EchoStar, plus a follow-on agreement worth $2.6 billion. While earlier direct-to-cell efforts relied on leasing spectrum from partner carriers, owning dedicated low-band airwaves gives SpaceX direct control over its service roadmap.

## Wall Street Reacts

Markets responded sharply, though the precise figures reported by different outlets reflect different moments in a fast-moving trading session. CBS News reported that in Friday morning trading, T-Mobile shares slid $18.58, or 11%, to $152.72, while AT&T slipped $1.83, or 7.4%, to $22.76. Later reporting captured a steeper move: LiveMint reported that AT&T fell 11% to $21.96, with T-Mobile and Verizon each down 11%. The two snapshots differ because they were taken at different points during the session, not because the data conflict. Shares of SpaceX, which went public earlier this year, rose $2.61, or 1.6%, to $163.18.

The sell-off reflects investor concern that a deep-pocketed newcomer could erode the pricing power and subscriber bases of the incumbent "Big Three." SpaceX's connectivity business is already its largest revenue segment, accounting for roughly 55% of sales. As of June 30, Starlink reported 12 million subscribers and more than 10,200 satellites covering 167 countries.

## Skeptics Question the Math

Not everyone is convinced the strategy will translate into a true national network. AT&T CEO John Stankey has been the most vocal critic, describing SpaceX's ambition of becoming a major challenger to traditional telecom companies as "not a viable strategy," according to the [Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/technology/tech-news/as-elon-musk-plans-to-shake-up-mobile-industry-att-ceo-john-stankey-says-that-the-economics-and-technical-aspects-of-starlinks-plan-dont-line-up/articleshow/134722265.cms).

Rather than building a sprawling cell-tower network, SpaceX has floated an alternative: installing small cellular base stations alongside Starlink dishes at homes and businesses. Stankey argued that installing enough of those stations to match a traditional network's capacity would cost roughly the same as building a full macro network, and he flagged a regulatory obstacle—current law does not permit radiating cellular signals from a person's home without their permission.

One analyst estimated that a genuine fourth-carrier build-out could cost SpaceX more than $100 billion.

Analysts advised caution on the near-term threat as well. UBS analyst John Hodulik said the airwaves are compatible with existing handsets and that he does not foresee obstacles to regulatory approval, but added that "it will take time for SpaceX to scale its wireless ambitions, and the impact to near-term wireless industry fundamentals is limited." Bernstein analyst Madison Rezaei argued that cell towers are not going anywhere soon, citing technical hurdles to extending satellite coverage indoors, limited brand awareness and customers' reluctance to switch carriers. She suggested Starlink Mobile could prove valuable in rural, remote, maritime and disaster-response scenarios but remains "a long way" from replacing terrestrial networks.

## The Convergence of Space and Ground

The deal underscores a broader shift: the convergence of satellite and terrestrial networks that is redrawing the competitive map of the wireless industry. Incumbent carriers have responded by pursuing neutral-host satellite models to blunt the growth of direct-to-cell services.

SpaceX says the combination of nationwide low-band coverage, 2 GHz mid-band capacity and its orbiting constellation will allow it to move toward a facilities-based national wireless network—one that competes directly with established carriers on both coverage and indoor connectivity.

Grain's announcement framed the transaction as consistent with the FCC's July 2026 order approving Grain's earlier acquisition from T-Mobile, which established a competitive selection process and a conditional pathway for direct-to-device deployment. The company said the deal "advances the objectives" of that order.

## What's Next

The path to becoming a mobile carrier runs through Washington. Federal regulators will review the transfer of control of the 800 MHz licenses under public-interest standards, weighing questions of spectrum concentration, competitive dynamics and the emerging framework for non-terrestrial networks.

If approved, SpaceX plans to integrate the 800 MHz band into its next-generation Starlink satellite production lines. The FCC has already approved Starlink Mobile's application to launch 15,000 satellites transmitting in the 2 GHz band to connect directly to phones—another building block in the company's ambitions.

The central question is not whether the technology can work, but whether the economics and the law will allow SpaceX to build a network that can genuinely rival the carriers it now threatens. For the incumbent operators, the message from the market was clear: the prospect, however distant, is now too large to ignore.
