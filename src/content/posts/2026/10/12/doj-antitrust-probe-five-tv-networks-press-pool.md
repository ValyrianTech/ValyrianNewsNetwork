# DOJ Opens Antitrust Probe Into Five TV Networks

The Justice Department has launched a formal antitrust investigation into five major U.S. television networks over their brief suspension of the White House television press pool, a move press-freedom advocates call an attempt to intimidate journalists. The probe targets ABC, CBS, CNN, NBC and Fox News, which jointly halted pool coverage last month in solidarity with CNN after the Trump administration barred several outlets from the White House.

The Antitrust Division is examining whether the networks' coordinated action violated Section 1 of the Sherman Act, which prohibits anticompetitive group boycotts among competitors. "Members of the television press pool have boycotted covering White House events. Group boycotts among commercial competitors can violate the Sherman Act," Justice Department spokesperson Emily Covington said in a statement to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/doj-investigating-tv-networks-suspension-press-pool-trump-media-ban/). "The Antitrust Division is simply investigating to determine whether these news organizations have violated the antitrust laws."

It was not immediately clear how the department is defining the networks' actions as an antitrust violation, and officials have not detailed the legal theory behind the probe. The investigation was first reported by The New York Times.

## How the Dispute Unfolded

The inquiry caps a turbulent month in the long-running conflict between President Donald Trump and the news media.

On Sept. 18, Trump announced on Truth Social that he was banning CNN, MS NOW (formerly MSNBC) and Politico from the White House, calling their coverage "FAKE NEWS" and warning that more outlets would follow, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/11/nx-s1-5997574/the-doj-is-investigating-5-tv-networks-for-suspending-pool-coverage-of-trump). The White House revoked the credentials of more than 70 journalists and framed press access as a "privilege — not a right."

The three outlets sued, citing First Amendment and due-process violations. U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly granted a temporary restraining order forcing the administration to restore credentials, later extending the pause while he considers a preliminary injunction. A ruling is expected by Tuesday.

The five networks that make up the television press pool jointly announced on Sept. 21 that they would suspend their rotating coverage in protest. Under a longstanding arrangement, pool members share the logistical costs of covering the president in confined settings such as the Oval Office and Air Force One, then distribute footage to outlets nationwide.

In a group statement at the time, the networks said: "The public has a vital interest in receiving accurate, independent information about its government. No administration should reject a news organization because it objects to its reporting."

The suspension lasted roughly four days and coincided with the state visit of Chinese President Xi Jinping, leaving far less video coverage of major ceremonies — a gap Trump later criticized without acknowledging his own role in restricting access.

## A Legal Theory Under Question

Press advocates and legal scholars say the antitrust framing is misplaced.

Bruce Brown, president of the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, called the investigation "a pretext for seeking records from the networks about how they are covering daily White House events during a media ban the president himself put in place." He added that "summoning antitrust authority, on top of the retaliatory actions the White House has already taken to bar certain outlets, is another and even more brazen attempt to try to intimidate journalists," as [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2026/10/trump-doj-antitrust-investigation-network-tv-pool-1237154137/) reported.

Seth Stern, chief of advocacy for the Freedom of the Press Foundation, was blunter: "The investigation is nonsense. Trump may view the presidency as a business venture, but news outlets' standing up for their constitutional rights has nothing to do with antitrust law."

Diana Moss, vice president and director of competition policy at the Progressive Policy Institute, argued the networks' conduct does not fit antitrust doctrine at all. "Group boycotts are illegal when they stifle competition, such as forcing a competitor out of the market," she wrote. "There is no anti-competitive intent or effect. The networks were responding in solidarity to repression of a free press."

Michael Dorf, a constitutional law professor at Cornell University, highlighted what he called an "oddity" in the government's position. "If the networks agreeing not to cover the White House is illegal, why isn't the press pool itself illegal?" he wrote, per NPR. He noted that if limited space justifies cooperation in coverage, then "why isn't standing up to protect the free press at least as important a justification?"

Senator Cory Booker (D-NJ), the top Democrat on the Senate Judiciary antitrust subcommittee, said the division should focus elsewhere. "Families are paying more for groceries, rent and prescriptions," he posted. "The Antitrust Division should be going after the corporations and monopolies behind those costs — not investigating newsrooms for how they cover the president."

Jose Zamora, the Committee to Protect Journalists' regional director of the Americas, echoed that concern, saying on X that "the government should not use antitrust investigations to intimidate news outlets defending press access," according to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/11/justice-department-investigates-tv-networks-trump-pool-coverage).

## A Broader Campaign Against the Press

The investigation is the latest front in a widening confrontation. ABC and parent company Disney are suing Trump's chief broadcast regulator, Brendan Carr, over a mandated review of the company's local TV station licenses. Trump previously settled lawsuits with ABC and CBS and has pursued individual suits against The New York Times, BBC News and The Wall Street Journal.

Under Carr, the FCC has launched investigations of major broadcast networks — with the notable exception of Fox, owned by Trump ally Rupert Murdoch.

The Justice Department's Antitrust Division itself has been roiled by leadership instability. Division chief Gail Slater departed in February 2026 amid internal conflict over merger enforcement, and two other senior officials were removed in a separate episode.

There is also a judicial precedent that advocates say points the other way. A federal court previously barred the FTC from issuing a civil investigative demand to the progressive watchdog Media Matters for America as part of an antitrust probe into an advertiser boycott of Elon Musk's X. Media Matters has described that outcome as a "roadmap for other newsgathering and nonprofit organizations facing, or at risk of, government retaliation."

Separately, a related dispute over Associated Press access — the wire service was barred from the pool after declining to adopt Trump's renaming of the Gulf of Mexico — remains on appeal in the D.C. Circuit.

## What's Next

The inquiry sets up a potential collision between antitrust enforcement and the First Amendment. If the department pursues formal demands for records, the networks may challenge them in court, citing the expressive and associational nature of their decision to stand with CNN.

Much depends on the parallel access case. Judge Kelly's expected ruling on a preliminary injunction will shape how far the White House can go in excluding outlets it dislikes — and whether the administration's broader pressure campaign survives judicial scrutiny.

ABC confirmed it had not been notified of the investigation but expected to be, while CNN declined to comment and the other networks did not immediately respond, according to Deadline. The White House has largely stayed silent.

Legal battles over press access have increasingly turned on established precedent. In an earlier case involving the Associated Press, U.S. District Judge Trevor McFadden found that the White House press area is a "nonpublic forum" where exclusions cannot be based on viewpoint, a ruling that [Reason](https://reason.com/2026/09/22/3-leading-news-outlets-say-their-sudden-banishment-from-the-white-house-is-unconstitutional/) noted could bear on the networks' claims.

For now, the probe leaves newsrooms weighing a familiar question: how much deference to give an administration that has openly tied coverage decisions to access — and what, if anything, antitrust law has to do with it.
