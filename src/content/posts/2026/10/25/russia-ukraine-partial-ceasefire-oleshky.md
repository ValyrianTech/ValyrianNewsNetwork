# Russia and Ukraine Agree to Partial Ceasefire in Oleshky

Russia and Ukraine have separately confirmed a localized ceasefire in the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky in the Kherson region, set to take effect on October 27, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/world/20261024/6f76b535d9c24b0a9204c30dea74ca59/c.html). The truce, aimed at allowing humanitarian aid deliveries and the evacuation of civilians, comes alongside indications that trilateral talks involving the United States, Russia, and Ukraine could be held as early as next month.

The arrangement marks a rare moment of cooperation after more than four and a half years of full-scale war — though it remains limited in scope and leaves the conflict's core territorial disputes unresolved.

## A Narrow, Humanitarian Truce

The ceasefire covers a single frontline town on the left bank of the Dnipro River, directly across from the Ukrainian-held city of Kherson. According to [The Moscow Times](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2026/10/24/russia-ukraine-agree-on-ceasefire-in-embattled-oleshky-plan-new-peace-talks-next-month-a94069), both militaries confirmed that operations would halt from 04:00 local time (01:00 GMT) on October 27 — two days earlier than the October 29 date initially announced by the White House.

Russian Chief of Staff Valery Gerasimov said Russian troops would stop firing to allow the humanitarian operation to begin, while Ukraine's General Staff pledged to observe the ceasefire and to "do everything possible to help Oleshky's residents," the Moscow Times reported.

Dmytro Lubinets, Ukraine's human rights ombudsman and its lead negotiator on the truce, cautioned that the mission would be difficult and gradual. The operation involves demining the roads into the town, delivering food, and evacuating civilians.

## A City on the Brink

Oleshky has been occupied since February 2022 and has sat on the front line for most of the war. Before the conflict, roughly 24,000 people lived there. The White House estimates that only 1,700 to 2,000 residents remain — primarily elderly people unable to leave — along with about 50 children.

As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/23/ukraine-troops-report-ongoing-problems-with-starlink-satellite-network) reported, residents have described increasingly desperate conditions amid severe shortages of food, water, and electricity, with some reportedly forced to survive on weeds, boiled grass, and meager rations. Both Moscow and Kyiv have accused each other of blocking supplies.

## Melania Trump Credited as Broker

Both Russia and Ukraine credited US First Lady Melania Trump with playing a central role in brokering the arrangement. In a [White House statement](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2026/10/first-lady-melania-trump-secures-ceasefire-between-russia-and-ukraine-localized-to-oleshky/), the First Lady described Oleshky as "a front-line war city on the brink of disaster," noting that its remaining population "is comprised primarily of elderly residents who urgently need medical attention, medicine, food, and clean water."

"Residents who wish to evacuate will receive coordinated support and transportation to their preferred destination," she said. "I anticipate some will want to go to Russia, and some to Ukraine."

Her involvement built on prior humanitarian work, including a series of Russia-Ukraine child reunifications. The Guardian noted, however, that calls for a humanitarian ceasefire had also come from Pope Leo, the United Nations, and EU officials, and that Ukrainian officials had previously accused Russia of rejecting such appeals.

## Trilateral Talks in the Offing

Parallel to the ceasefire, diplomatic channels are reopening. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov confirmed that trilateral talks are planned for early November in Abu Dhabi, according to Xinhua. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said negotiators from Ukraine, Russia, and the United States had agreed to technical-level meetings in the first half of November and hoped a leaders' summit would follow.

"The plan is to meet in the first half of November, and as early as possible, of course," Zelensky said, adding that much would depend on Russia.

Russian President Vladimir Putin, in a televised interview, stressed that any talks would focus on how to ensure security after a peace agreement is reached — not merely on a brief ceasefire.

## Analysis: Momentum Built on Fragile Ground

Analysts caution that resuming talks does not guarantee consensus. Russia continues to demand that Ukraine cede the entire eastern Donbas region, including territory its military does not occupy, while Kyiv rejects giving up land it holds. Security guarantees for Ukraine remain another unresolved fault line.

The timing carries a strong political dimension. According to Xinhua, analysts suggest Washington intensified its mediation partly to secure a political achievement ahead of the November US congressional midterm elections. The diplomatic push has unfolded amid a recent rift between Washington and Kyiv, including US-imposed restrictions on SpaceX's Starlink system and a US-Russia diesel trade arrangement that Ukraine has denounced.

A trust deficit persists on both sides, meaning the Oleshky ceasefire could yet break down. Its duration, monitoring mechanisms, and evacuation routes have not been fully disclosed; Peskov said only that the operation would take at least several days.

## What to Watch

The immediate test is whether the October 27 ceasefire holds and whether humanitarian convoys safely reach Oleshky's remaining residents. Beyond that, attention turns to whether the planned Abu Dhabi technical talks materialize and yield a framework — and whether a leader-level trilateral summit follows.

For now, the agreement represents a narrow, transactional step rather than a breakthrough. It offers a measure of relief to a devastated town while leaving the war's fundamental questions unanswered.
