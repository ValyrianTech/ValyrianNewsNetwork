# Trump Takes Midterm Swing to Michigan, Targeting El-Sayed

President Donald Trump brought his midterm barnstorming to Michigan on Sunday, headlining a rally in Port Huron for Republican Senate nominee Mike Rogers as the GOP escalates a personal, faith-inflected offensive against Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed nine days before Election Day.

Speaking to reporters before departing for the state, Trump again framed the race in starkly sectarian terms. "We don't want to see a jihadist senator," he said, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-michigan-elsayed-rogers-senate-midterms-162330c3d193ecb94538f8dd8083f916). "We don't want to see a member of the Muslim Brotherhood in our Senate."

The visit marks the capstone of a weekend during which two former presidents effectively dueled over one of the country's most consequential Senate battlegrounds. Former President Barack Obama rallied for El-Sayed in Detroit on Saturday — the first day of early voting in Michigan — hours before Trump campaigned in Georgia.

## A Seat the GOP Has Not Won in Decades

The contest to succeed retiring Democratic Sen. Gary Peters is central to the fight for Senate control. Michigan is one of the few Democratic-held seats in a state Trump carried in 2024, and Republicans have not won a Senate race there since 1994.

El-Sayed, a former Wayne County health director who ran for governor in 2018, would become the first Muslim U.S. senator if elected. Rogers, a former congressman, lost the 2024 Senate race to Democrat Elissa Slotkin by less than a percentage point.

The stakes extend beyond Michigan. Republicans have cast El-Sayed as a symbol of what they describe as a radicalizing Democratic Party, while progressives hope his candidacy demonstrates their clout in a swing state — potentially shaping the 2028 presidential primary.

## A Campaign Fought Over Faith and Loyalty

The GOP's central line of attack has centered on unproven allegations that El-Sayed is connected to the Muslim Brotherhood, an Islamic movement with branches the U.S. government has designated as terrorist organizations. El-Sayed has repeatedly disavowed the claims, and Democrats have denounced them as attacks on his faith and his Egyptian heritage.

The tension surfaced sharply in the candidates' final debate, when Rogers questioned El-Sayed's loyalty directly. "Are you for America, or are you for these terrorist groups overseas?" Rogers asked. El-Sayed called the question "shameful," responding: "I'm proud of our Constitution that guarantees all of us the right to pray as we choose to pray." Rogers denied his criticisms were religion-based.

Trump has referred to El-Sayed as "Mohammed" and said he "goes back to the days of the jihad." El-Sayed, in turn, has actively invited the president's attention, betting that a visit would backfire on Rogers. "Donald, if you're watching, don't wuss out, man," he said. He has said the country needs to "turn the page on Donald Trump."

## A Double-Edged Endorsement

The visit illustrates the political gamble at the heart of the race. Trump remains deeply popular with the Republican base Rogers needs to turn out, but he is also weighed down in Michigan by voter frustration over the economy, his tariffs, and the war with Iran — headwinds that have complicated the GOP's midterm prospects nationally.

The Obama rally underscored that dynamic. According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/24/barack-obama-abdul-el-sayed-michigan), Obama told thousands at Cass Technical High School that El-Sayed was "fighting for change, real change, that will get money out of politics and put money in your pocket," and that he would "finish the job we started" with the Affordable Care Act. The Detroit stop was Obama's first public show of support for El-Sayed after staying neutral in the bitter primary.

Obama also acknowledged the friction between the two Democrats. "We are not going to agree on every detail of every issue," he said, adding that El-Sayed would still "finish the job we started."

## Coalition Fractures Beneath the Surface

Despite the star power, [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/10/07/politics/michigan-senate-race-abdul-el-sayed-mike-rogers) reported this month that leading state Democrats privately worry El-Sayed could lose. Two constituencies he needs most — Black leaders in Detroit and Jewish leaders unsettled by his criticisms of Israel — have signaled that his outreach has been insufficient.

El-Sayed has also faced criticism for saying "hurt people hurt people" after an antisemitic attack on a Detroit-area synagogue. He later apologized: "To anyone who feels like my comments might have been hurtful, I'm really sorry. That was not my intention."

Republicans, meanwhile, are betting that the combination of heavy outside spending and Rogers' relatively low-key profile can peel away moderate and disaffected Democrats. Rogers has leaned on his relationships and a pitch that his ties to Trump are an asset for delivering results. "If you're going to have the president of the United States of one party for the next two years, why not send somebody back that can work with the president to get some of these things fixed," he has said.

## What's Next

With early voting underway and the race rated a toss-up, both parties are pouring resources into the closing stretch. The outcome will hinge on whether El-Sayed can reassemble the coalition that powered Obama and President Joe Biden to victories in Michigan — Black voters in Detroit, progressives, and organized labor — while holding enough moderate and Jewish Democrats to offset Rogers' gains.

Trump's visit is designed to energize the base Rogers needs. But it is equally a gift to El-Sayed, who has built much of his closing argument around the president as a foil.

As voters head to the polls on Nov. 3, Michigan will offer an early verdict on two competing theories of American politics: whether a polarizing president can still drag a battleground state across the finish line, and whether the Democratic left can prove it can win where it counts.
