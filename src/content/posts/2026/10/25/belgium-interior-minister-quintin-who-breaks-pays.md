# Belgium's Interior Minister Warns Rioters: 'Who Breaks, Pays'

Belgium's federal Interior Minister Bernard Quintin has issued a hard-line warning to those responsible for vandalism during weekend protests in Brussels and Liège, declaring that "who breaks, pays" and vowing that rioters would be identified and severely punished. Writing on social media after Saturday's demonstrations, the minister thanked police for their "professionalism and firm action against rioters" and insisted the perpetrators of vandalism and arson must face the consequences, as [Het Laatste Nieuws reported](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/quintin-richt-zich-tot-amokmakers-die-vernielingen-aanbrachten-in-brussel-wie-breekt-betaalt~a2483eff/).

The statement, posted on X on Saturday evening, cements the law-and-order posture the minister has struck throughout a turbulent month of protests over education savings and the cost of living.

## What happened on Saturday

Around 400 people gathered in central Brussels on Saturday, called together largely through social media, to protest savings in French-language education and to demonstrate against police and the governing "Arizona" coalition. The demonstration was declared over by police around 15:30 after some participants attempted to set fire to a collection of shared electric scooters and briefly tried to break a police line near the Jacques Franck square. Nine people were detained — five on administrative grounds and four on judicial charges linked to prohibited weapons and incitement to riot.

In Liège, roughly 200 protesters marched in a gathering that remained relatively calm, with five arrests. Quintin noted that there were "no big incidents" at the marches themselves.

The Brussels events echoed a wider wave of unrest across Europe. In France, authorities reported around 1,000 arrests nationally as demonstrations over public services and purchasing power spiralled into riots and looting, while Italy, Spain and Albania also saw related disturbances.

## A month of mounting tension

The weekend unrest followed a national trade-union demonstration in Brussels on 9 October that was marred by heavy rioting by people unaffiliated with the official march. Vandals destroyed cars, buses and bus shelters and set fire to shared bikes and e-scooters, prompting police to deploy water cannon and tear gas. Roughly twenty people were detained, most of them minors. Organisers stressed that the rioters had no link to the official action.

Quintin responded sharply at the time. "The State will never be impressed," he said, according to [RTBF Actus](https://www.rtbf.be/article/manifestation-nationale-bernard-quintin-promet-une-reponse-a-la-hauteur-des-degats-georges-louis-bouchez-veut-faire-payer-les-syndicats-11797858). "The police and judicial response will always be commensurate with the damage caused."

His message has had a dual edge. While condemning the rioters, the minister also defended the police against criticism, writing that "some spend their time criticising them: they are targeting the wrong people. The only guilty parties for the violence are these rioters and their supporters." In a separate move, MR party president Georges-Louis Bouchez accused the unions of "irresponsibility" and announced he wanted to move "without delay" in Parliament to make unions financially liable for riot damage, drawing a parallel with football clubs said to be "responsible for their supporters."

## The debate over accountability

Not everyone is convinced that hard rhetoric is matched by action. Vincent Houssin, a delegate and spokesperson for the VSOA police union, argued that authorities must apply the tolerance threshold far more strictly. "Someone who arrives masked at a station does not do so with good intentions," he told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/10/politievakbond-vsoa-over-rellen-betogingen-brussel-luik/). "Those people must be pulled out of the march very quickly and arrested."

Houssin said he felt let down by the authorities, drawing a contrast between public declarations and practice on the ground. "I notice that big statements are made in the media, on Instagram, in the Chamber, but ultimately nothing happens in practice," he said. He also rejected the language often used to describe the rioters, insisting they were "not youths or students" but "outright scum" who must be dealt with very harshly.

Houssin called for a kind of command centre at the Interior Ministry, arguing the minister is ultimately responsible for the country's security — a pressure point that underscores the political stakes for Quintin.

## Analysis: a slogan and its limits

"Who breaks, pays" is a crisp political message, but its practical application is far from simple. Both police and protest organisers stressed that the vandals were not part of the official marches — often unidentified, masked individuals operating on the fringes of legitimate demonstrations. That reality complicates any effort to attach financial liability to a specific party, and it sits at the heart of the contested debate over whether unions or organisers should bear responsibility for damage caused by people they did not control.

The tension also reflects a hardening law-and-order discourse from the MR within the governing "Arizona" coalition, set against union and civil-liberties concerns about collective responsibility and the right to protest. The question of who actually pays — the perpetrators, their parents, or the public purse — remains unresolved, and sceptical observers have questioned whether the slogan will be enforced in practice.

## What to watch

The coming weeks will test whether Quintin's rhetoric translates into concrete judicial action and whether Parliament moves on Bouchez's proposal to hold unions financially accountable. For now, the minister's message is clear: the Belgian state, he insists, will not be impressed by violence — and those who break, he says, will pay.
