# China Rejects Expanded US Outbound Investment Rules

China's Ministry of Commerce on Saturday sharply criticized a newly proposed US rule that would broaden Washington's outbound-investment screening regime, warning that Beijing "reserves the right to take corresponding measures" and denouncing the measure as a "typical practice of over-generalizing national security," according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/world/20261024/ac02855d5e1c4fd8a84c59cc90436284/c.html). The statement, issued by a MOFCOM spokesperson, came two days after the US Treasury Department unveiled the draft rule on October 22 and marks the latest friction point in a still-fragile trade détente between the world's two largest economies.

## What the US Proposed

The proposed rule, a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM), would update and expand the Outbound Investment Security Program to implement Subtitle C of the Comprehensive Outbound Investment National Security (COINS) Act. [US Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0667/) said the law—enacted in December 2025 as part of the FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act—codifies and builds upon the existing program, which previously covered only semiconductors, quantum technologies and artificial intelligence.

The expansion adds high-performance computing and supercomputing, hypersonic systems, and—using authority delegated by Congress—biotechnology, bringing the number of covered technology sectors to six. The program requires US persons to notify Treasury of certain transactions involving "notifiable technologies" and prohibits them from other transactions involving "prohibited technologies" with covered foreign persons.

"The proposed regulations would strengthen the existing Outbound Investment Security Program to prevent our adversaries from using U.S. investment to develop critical technologies that threaten U.S. national security," Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in the release.

## China's Objections

Beijing's response focused on the China dimension of the rule. The MOFCOM spokesperson said the proposal "discriminatorily expands the scope of restrictions on investment in China and increases the intensity of restrictions," arguing that it "violates the important consensus reached by the two heads of state, disrupts normal economic and trade cooperation between Chinese and American enterprises, and undermines the international economic and trade order."

China expressed "serious concern and firm opposition," pledged to "closely monitor the subsequent development of the situation," and said it "reserves the right to take corresponding measures." The spokesperson also stressed China's market appeal, stating that "investing in China is investing in the future" and that restricting US investment would harm American firms' own interests.

## Divergent Framings

The two governments are describing the same document in markedly different terms. Washington frames the rule as the statutorily mandated implementation of a bipartisan law that protects national security from dual-use technology transfers—and one that extends beyond China. According to reporting cited in the research, the proposal also broadens the list of "countries of concern" to include Russia, Cuba, Iran and North Korea, a detail Beijing's statement does not emphasize.

Beijing, by contrast, casts the measure as a targeted, discriminatory escalation aimed specifically at China. Neither characterization is factually false; they simply emphasize different aspects of a rule that is both China-focused in its practical impact and broader in its legal scope.

## A Test for the Truce

The timing is delicate. The US and China only recently extended their trade truce—reached at the so-called Busan summit in October 2025—through January 10, 2027. The new proposal also lands a little over two weeks after Treasury announced its first-ever enforcement penalty under the outbound-investment program, signaling that the rules are being actively applied rather than merely drafted.

China's phrase "reserves the right to take corresponding measures" is a standard formulation, but it is not empty. Throughout 2026, Beijing has repeatedly turned to export controls, entity lists and sanctions on firms assisting US enforcement in response to American restrictions. Whether it escalates this time will likely hinge on the final shape of the US rule and the state of the truce heading into the new year.

## What to Watch

Public comments on the NPRM are due December 7, 2026, after which Treasury will issue final implementing regulations that set an effective date. US and foreign investors now have a window to shape definitions, thresholds and the scope of covered transactions—and Beijing may be counting on industry pushback to narrow the final rule. Also worth watching is how the other newly listed countries of concern respond, given that the proposal is not China-exclusive. And with enforcement already underway, investors in Chinese AI, robotics and biotech ventures will be weighing exposure well before the rule takes final effect.

For now, the episode underscores a familiar pattern: a legalistic American expansion of technology controls, a firm but carefully worded Chinese rebuke, and a détente that both sides seem intent on managing—at least until one of them decides not to.
