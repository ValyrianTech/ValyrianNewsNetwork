# Public Colleges Hit $50,000 a Year for Locals

For a generation of Californians, the promise of the University of California was simple: a world-class education at a public price. Graduates in the 1960s paid no tuition at all. Their grandchildren are now confronting a very different ledger. This year, the all-in cost of attendance for a new in-state student at UC Berkeley reached $54,674, according to figures [published](https://financialaid.berkeley.edu/how-aid-works/student-budgets-cost-of-attendance/) by the university's financial aid office—a milestone that signals how far the state's flagship public system has drifted from its bargain reputation.

The $50,000-per-year threshold has been crossed at other campuses as well. UC Santa Barbara lists a $50,946 all-in price, and UC Irvine's cost of attendance for a student living off campus is $50,130. According to [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/25/business/california-colleges-costs.html), the figure is "likely to act as a kind of psychological tripwire for families who thought that state schools were supposed to be a bargain."

## The Real Driver Is Housing

The striking insight behind these numbers is that tuition is not the primary culprit. According to the College Board, the average list price for tuition and fees at a four-year public university nationally was $11,950 during the 2025–26 school year. At the California campuses in question, that figure runs many thousands higher—but the decisive factor pushing total costs past $50,000 is housing.

Student housing, even in university-owned residences, can cost more than tuition and related fees combined. At Berkeley, the financial aid office budgets roughly $14,386 for off-campus housing and utilities over nine months—about $1,609 a month—for the 2026–27 academic year, a figure that reflects one of the most expensive rental markets in the country.

For many California families, the result is a four-year bill of roughly $200,000. Out-of-state students, who pay an additional $39,270 in Nonresident Supplemental Tuition at Berkeley alone, will shell out more than $300,000. Important caveats apply: financial aid means many students pay far less than list price, the estimates include health insurance that some students can waive, and California is simply an expensive place to live. Still, the trend line is unmistakable.

## A Housing Crisis in Disguise

The story of rising costs at UC Santa Barbara illustrates the dynamic. The campus is hemmed in by an airport and bodies of water, including the Pacific Ocean, leaving just one walkable neighborhood: Isla Vista, an unincorporated area of roughly 50 square blocks where landlords effectively set the market. Rents at one complex start at $1,336.65 per person per month for a shared room, and prospective tenants can already join a waitlist for the fall 2027 academic year.

UC Santa Barbara recently raised its on-campus housing guarantee from one year to two, made possible by long-planned and delayed construction. But that still leaves upperclassmen to navigate a scramble for off-campus leases that begins months before they arrive.

"After October, you start hearing, 'Have you found housing for next year?'" said Nicole Meng, a student team leader at the university's Blum Center on Poverty, Inequality and Democracy, which in 2024 published an 85-page "people's guide" to the campus housing crisis. "All of my friends were like: 'What do we do? We're 18. We don't really have any resources. I'm first generation, low income—how am I going to come up with deposits? And I need to find roommates.'"

Meng's own rent is $2,988, split three ways among roommates sharing a 450-square-foot one-bedroom apartment. As Jasmine Hsieh, external vice president for statewide affairs for the Associated Students of UC Santa Barbara, put it: "This is to find *a* spot. You're not selecting. You're just hoping to be able to continue to go to school."

## Universities Reclaiming Control

When universities spend decades ceding their undergraduate housing market to nearby landlords while enrollment grows, they lose a measure of control—and eventually face a crisis. UC schools now build dormitories through donations, state grants, borrowing, or partnerships with private developers, structuring rents to cover costs.

After decades of debate, Berkeley is building a new dorm in People's Park, a site it has owned for years, and has surrounded the construction area with shipping containers topped with barbed wire following protests. At Santa Barbara, new campus housing is scheduled to come online over the next two years. Willie Brown, the campus's associate vice chancellor for housing, dining and auxiliary enterprises, said rates would be far below local market rents. "For us, this works well, because Isla Vista is so overpriced," he said.

In Berkeley, off-campus rents on older properties have actually fallen in recent years as new university housing created competition. Asked whether he takes pride in that effect, Kyle Gibson, director of strategic communications for UC Berkeley's administration division, said simply, "I do."

## A National Threshold, Not Just a California One

The pressure is not confined to the Golden State. Flagship state schools in Colorado, Connecticut, Illinois, Michigan, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Virginia have all posted in-state prices above $40,000 for at least some undergraduate programs. That $40,000 threshold marks the leading edge of a widening national trend—one that reaches well beyond California's expensive coastal markets.

Gaye Theresa Johnson, the Blum Center's director and a professor of Black studies, framed the core question: "When you want to propose a solution, whose feet do you hold to the fire? The one steady to me is the university."

## What to Watch

The coming years will test whether universities can build enough housing—fast and cheap enough—to restore the promise of an affordable public education. New dormitories at Santa Barbara and Berkeley will add capacity, but not necessarily at a pace that matches demand or guarantees four years of stability for students.

The practical advice for families is to press expensive public universities directly: ask why more housing isn't being built, whether lower-income students are disadvantaged by the absence of a four-year guarantee, and how off-campus searches align with lease-signing seasons. The best students in any state ought to be able to afford the best state schools without fearing insolvency or homelessness—and if they choose to go elsewhere, they should tell legislators why.
