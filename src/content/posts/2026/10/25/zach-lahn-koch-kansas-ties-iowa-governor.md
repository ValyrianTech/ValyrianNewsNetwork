# Zach Lahn's Koch and Kansas Ties Test His Iowa Brand

Zach Lahn swept into Iowa's Republican gubernatorial primary as a self-made prairie populist, a farmer and businessman who lent his own campaign $2.5 million and promised to fight the corporate interests squeezing ordinary Iowans. But a New York Times investigation published Sunday paints a more tangled biography — one woven through the Koch political dynasty and the state of Kansas, raising questions about money, influence and out-of-state ties just nine days before voters head to the polls.

At the center of the story is a gap between Lahn's "Iowa First" brand and a record of extensive financial and family entanglement with Charles Koch's network and with Kansas, where Lahn voted as recently as 2022 and where he owns a sprawling estate. Democrats, led by state Auditor Rob Sand, have seized on the disclosures to cast Lahn as a "Kansas carpetbagger" who is not truly of Iowa.

## A Primary Upset Built on Independence

Lahn, 40, won the GOP nomination on June 2 with 37.65% of the vote, narrowly defeating Trump-endorsed U.S. Rep. Randy Feenstra — a result [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/jun/03/zach-lahn-iowa-trump-republican) described as a rare rebuke to the president's endorsement power. He ran as an anti-establishment outsider, campaigning against big agriculture and "Big Pharma" while cultivating a right-leaning populist identity that Mother Jones dubbed "MAHA's great farm-country hope."

That self-financed, independent image is precisely what makes the Koch connection potentially damaging. According to [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/25/us/politics/zach-lahn-iowa-governor-kansas-kochs.html), Lahn was hired in 2014 to lead the Montana chapter of Americans for Prosperity, the flagship grassroots advocacy arm of the Koch political network. He held the post for about a year before moving into fund-raising into 2016.

At a Humboldt, Iowa town hall, Lahn downplayed the episode, saying he had "worked for one group that was funded by them one year — for one year — 10 years ago," according to [C-SPAN](https://www.c-span.org/clip/campaign-2026/zach-lahn-responds-to-audience-question-about-alleged-koch-connection/5207293) footage of the exchange.

## The Wonder School and a Contested 'Unpaid' Claim

The investigation traces Lahn's Koch ties to a private school he co-founded in Kansas called Wonder with Annie Koch — then the wife of Chase Koch, son of billionaire Charles Koch. The two met at a Koch-funded education conference, and Lahn moved to Kansas to launch the school, which was bankrolled by Annie and Chase.

From 2017 to 2024, IRS records say Lahn worked 50 hours a week for Wonder and was never paid by the school. Lahn insists the school "never paid me anything." But emails and invoices obtained by the Times show he was billing Annie and Chase Koch directly through an LLC for "consulting services" — with 2019 monthly billings of $20,883, or roughly $250,000 a year. One internal email referred to the payments as "salary." Campaign manager Paul Cordes told the Times the bills were "related to consulting work that also predates the school."

The arrangement is the most factually consequential discrepancy in the reporting, cutting against Lahn's portrayal of himself as financially independent and self-made.

## Marriages, Money and a Kansas Estate

Both Lahn and the Kochs divorced in 2019 and 2020. Lahn and the future Annie Lahn applied for a marriage license 11 days after his divorce was finalized in June 2020; Annie reportedly received an eight-figure divorce settlement, though the Koch divorce records are sealed.

After the marriage, Lahn's investment activity surged. In 2021 he bought a "ski-in, ski-out" lot in Whitefish, Montana, for $1.5 million, bought and sold a $4.5 million home there, and acquired a $1 million stake in FirmTech Inc., a men's sexual health company. In 2023 he bought a 1930s movie theater in Belle Plaine, Iowa, and roughly 435 acres in Benton County. Lahn says he bought a Montana apparel company for $3 million with "100 percent my own money" before his second marriage, though his campaign declined to provide documentation.

Lahn has dismissed questions about whether his wife's settlement fueled his career, calling it a "silly question," and has said, "Our family dealings are our family dealings."

## The Residency Fight

The sharpest political fault line remains residency. Lahn says he lives full-time in Iowa, but his children split time between the two states. Flight records first reported by the Des Moines Register showed his private plane — which bears "Iowa First" beneath its wings — frequently shuttling between Iowa and Kansas. The Lahns own a 26-acre Frank Lloyd Wright-inspired estate appraised at $1.8 million, complete with an indoor pool and private lake, just north of Wichita.

Lahn voted in Kansas in 2018, 2020 and 2022, and re-registered in Iowa only in October 2024 — in time to qualify to run in 2026. He cast an Iowa ballot in the 2024 general election but requested the ballot be mailed to him in Kansas, a detail not previously reported.

At an October 7 debate, the exchange turned personal. "I am not going to be intimidated on this," Lahn said of attacks touching his children, vowing to "defend my children from people who'd like to exploit them like you." Sand replied: "This is not about why you're in Kansas. This is about the fact that you're not in Iowa."

## A Note on the Koch Network's Silence

Notably, the Koch network has not rallied to Lahn's side. Americans for Prosperity Action Inc. has spent more than $13.3 million on Iowa's U.S. Senate race this year but nothing on Lahn's behalf. "They're not supporting me in any way, shape or form," Lahn said. "Not a dollar of inside help, a dollar of outside help. Nothing."

Chase Koch reportedly learned of Lahn's campaign — and the role of his children in it — only when he saw media clips.

Lahn has also gone on the offensive over wealth, attacking Sand as a "billionaire" whose wife, Christine Lauridsen, leads the Lauridsen Group conglomerate. Sand's campaign has received roughly $5.8 million from her and her family. "He doesn't get what regular folks go through," Lahn says in one ad. Republican consultant Eric Woolson told the Times the attack seems designed "more to muddy the waters" than to resonate with most Iowa voters.

## What's at Stake

The 2026 race is Iowa's first open gubernatorial contest since 2006 and, with an open Senate seat also on the ballot, the first of its kind since 1968. Incumbent Republican Gov. Kim Reynolds is term-limited. Sand, the only statewide Democrat to win in 2022, is seen as one of his party's best chances to flip a governor's seat in a state where no Democrat has won the office since 2006.

Whether the Koch and Kansas revelations move voters may hinge on how well they cut against Lahn's central promise. His appeal rests on the claim that he is beholden to no one — a message that a record of quiet consulting billings to one of America's wealthiest political families, and a ballot mailed across state lines, could complicate. With the general election set for November 3, the contest now turns on a simple, contested question: which candidate is truly of Iowa, and which is merely passing through.
