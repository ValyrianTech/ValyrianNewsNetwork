# NY Archdiocese to Pay $800M in Sex Abuse Settlement

The Archdiocese of New York has agreed to pay $800 million to settle claims brought by roughly 1,300 people who say they were sexually abused by clergy members and lay church workers, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/24/nx-s1-6015522/new-york-archdiocese-settlement-agreement). The agreement is one of the largest such settlements in the history of the Catholic Church in the United States and, measured against other American archdioceses, the second-largest on record.

The deal, reached over the weekend of October 24 and first reported by *The New York Times*, is not yet final. It requires signed paperwork, with formal signing now scheduled for the week of November 6, 2026. Attorneys say all plaintiffs have agreed to the terms.

## A Reckoning Years in the Making

The settlement resolves claims filed under New York's Child Victims Act, a 2019 law that extended the civil statute of limitations for survivors of childhood sexual abuse and opened a window for decades-old allegations that had previously been barred. The law triggered a wave of litigation against dioceses and other institutions across the state, and the New York archdiocese's case dragged on for more than seven years.

The path to this agreement was set in motion in December 2025, when the archdiocese agreed to mediation to settle the 1,300 claims. As [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/new-york-catholic-church-agrees-mediation-1300-sexual-abuse-claims-2025-12-09/) reported at the time, the church laid off staff, cut costs and put real estate up for sale in hopes of raising $300 million toward survivor payouts. That mediation target was ultimately far exceeded by the $800 million deal.

## Church Leadership Signals Relief

Archbishop Ronald Hicks, who succeeded Cardinal Timothy Dolan, announced the agreement in an email to priests of the archdiocese on Saturday afternoon. In remarks shared with NPR, he framed the deal as both a step toward healing and a chance for the church to refocus on its work.

“We are on the verge of being able to, please God, bring some measure of healing and reconciliation to all victim-survivors,” Hicks wrote. “This agreement will also allow the archdiocese to turn its full attention once again to its mission of preaching the Gospel, healing the sick, caring for the poor and needy, welcoming the stranger, and educating our children.”

The archbishop was careful to note the deal is not yet binding: “While the deal is not yet final — and won't be until the settlement is signed, now scheduled for the week of November 6th — we have, in fact, reached an understanding with all of the plaintiffs and their attorneys.”

## Survivors Describe Relief and Loss

Jeff Anderson, a prominent plaintiffs' attorney whose firm represents more than 250 of the claimants in this case, said the settlement became final pending the signed paperwork, and that all plaintiffs had consented. He described the unanimous agreement as a milestone.

“The unanimous agreement of survivors who filed claims against the Archdiocese of New York marks a powerful moment in the pursuit of justice,” Anderson said. “Through their courage, sacrifice, and determination, survivors have helped bring about a reckoning and a meaningful step toward accountability.”

He added that his clients hold mixed emotions — grief over the abuse they endured, paired with “a real sense of relief and achievement” at having stood up and helped protect others in the future.

## Part of a National Wave

The New York agreement is the latest in a series of large settlements by American archdioceses in recent years. In 2024, the Archdiocese of Los Angeles [agreed to pay $880 million](https://www.npr.org/2024/10/17/g-s1-28660/archdiocese-los-angeles-settlement-abuse) to about 1,350 survivors — the largest single child sex abuse settlement involving a U.S. archdiocese. Other major deals include San Francisco's $395 million agreement in June 2026 and New Orleans's settlement of at least $230 million, approved by a federal judge in December 2025.

Taken together, these cases reflect a broader accounting forced on the church by survivors, their advocates and, increasingly, by state legislatures that have opened legal windows once closed to victims. The church's own leaders have acknowledged a “darkness” in its past, and the New York case had been complicated by a long-running dispute with insurer Chubb over whether its policies covered the claims.

## What's Next

All eyes now turn to the week of November 6, when the settlement is expected to be formally signed. Once finalized, the agreement would resolve claims against the archdiocese, its parishes and affiliated agencies, closing more than seven years of litigation. The archdiocese — which serves Manhattan, the Bronx, Staten Island and seven counties north of the city, encompassing roughly 300 churches and 2.5 million Catholics — will still face the practical challenge of funding the payout through reserves, financing and property sales.

For the survivors who spent years fighting for recognition, the deal offers a measure of accountability long withheld. Whether it brings the healing Archbishop Hicks invoked — and how the church reforms in its wake — remains the harder question that no settlement alone can answer.
