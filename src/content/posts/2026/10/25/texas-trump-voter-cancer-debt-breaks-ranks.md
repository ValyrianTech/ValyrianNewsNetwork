# A Texas Trump Voter's Cancer Debt Breaks Ranks

McALLEN, Texas — Cindy Banda voted for Donald Trump in 2016, 2020 and 2024, driven by a lifelong anti-abortion stance. This fall, the 46-year-old single mother cast an early ballot for Democrat James Talarico — her first Democratic vote ever — after the cost of treating her breast cancer reshaped her priorities.

"Never voted Democrat before today!!" Banda wrote in a text message after voting, punctuated with a blue heart emoji, according to an [Associated Press report](https://apnews.com/article/trump-talarico-paxton-texas-cancer-healthcare-midterms-18ac9cd6e5d3f34c6cbb6dab1cd9ddc4) published Sunday. Her defection illustrates how healthcare affordability is cutting across party lines in the final stretch before the Nov. 3 midterms — and in one of the country's most closely watched Senate contests.

## A Diagnosis, Then a Debt Spiral

About a year ago, surgeons in Houston removed Banda's right breast. Even with employer insurance through her job selling internet service door-to-door for Spectrum, the surgeries, chemotherapy and radiation added up to more than $25,000 in debt she has been paying off. She is among tens of millions of Americans carrying medical bills, in a healthcare industry that takes in more than $5 trillion annually.

Banda, who cares for six children — her own three plus three nieces and nephews she took in after her sister Melissa was abducted and murdered by her ex-husband in 2020 — describes her daily survival strategy as a mantra: "Keep the house. Keep the cars. And just keep the kids stable."

The numbers press in from every side. Her household needs run roughly $7,000 a month. She had been on sick leave for a year with no sales commission since her October 2025 diagnosis. A car payment of $1,300 landed while she sat in a waiting room. Her breast reconstruction surgery hinges on insurance deductibles: she has already met this year's, but would have to pay down $7,500 again in January. "I was hoping for maybe December," she said, "only because I've met my deductibles."

Help has come from scattered places — her father's cash infusions, a $400-a-week nanny contribution from her 9-year-old's father, high school classmates who fundraised with a roadside grilled-chicken sale, two GoFundMe campaigns that raised nearly $30,000, and a forbearance on her own home while she rented her late sister's. But the strain still intrudes. "Sometimes I think, and I shouldn't think like this, but I'm worth more gone than here," she said, before adding that her goal is "to beat it, to keep the house and keep the cars."

## A Vote Reconsidered

Banda's political shift was gradual. She backed Trump in 2016, felt his first term was steady and voted for him again in 2020; she blamed Democratic President Joe Biden's tenure — the tail end of the pandemic and rising inflation — for what felt like bedlam, and voted for Trump again in 2024. But two years later, fighting an expensive disease, she found no relief from high prices.

Then Talarico, a Presbyterian seminary student and Texas state legislator, visited one of her fundraisers and spoke about his own diabetes and his work in the statehouse to lower insulin prices. "Imagine if he can do that for the cancer," she said.

The contrast between the candidates' healthcare prescriptions is stark. Talarico wants every American to be able to join Medicare, a public option he argues would lower costs through competition with private insurers, and he supports a nationwide law guaranteeing abortion access — a position Banda voted against for years but now considers superseded. Paxton, the state attorney general, has proposed a $25,000 tax deduction for out-of-pocket medical expenses, plus $25,000 per dependent, as the centerpiece of his affordability agenda, [the Texas Tribune reported](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/07/31/texas-ken-paxton-us-senate-race-economic-agenda-tax-deductions/) when he unveiled the plan in July.

For Banda, the math has simply outweighed the culture war. "Because it's what I'm living," she said of healthcare costs. "I'm living it."

## A National Current

Banda's story lands against a broad shift in voter sentiment. Heading into the midterms, about half of registered voters called healthcare costs a major problem for their families, up from a little more than one-third four years ago, according to a September Fox News poll. An [AP-NORC poll](https://apnews.com/article/democrats-republicans-trump-midterms-prices-healthcare-congress-109768f7544c9e0fcc7e716de0dc2157) from the same month found voters give Democrats a clear edge on handling the cost of living and healthcare — and frustration with Republicans has grown as prices remain high.

Nationally, the strain is widening. The uninsured rate among working-age Americans rose to 11.2% in 2026, up from 8.9% in 2024, according to a [Commonwealth Fund survey](https://www.aha.org/news/headline/2026-10-23-commonwealth-fund-survey-finds-uninsured-rate-among-working-age-americans-rose-11-2026). In Banda's home region, the pressure is sharper still: about 30% of Rio Grande Valley residents lack health insurance, among the highest uninsured rates in the country, and roughly 24% live below the poverty line, [the Texas Tribune reported](https://www.ksat.com/news/texas/2026/08/13/rio-grande-valley-residents-are-feeling-more-negative-about-their-lives-that-could-spill-into-the-voting-booth/).

The Senate race itself is tight and closely watched, with the contest among Democrats' best chances in years to win a statewide office in Texas. The Rio Grande Valley, where Banda lives, has historically seen low turnout — a dynamic that reflects, in part, how difficult daily life has become there. "People are really busy just surviving," Álvaro Corral, a political scientist at the University of Texas Rio Grande Valley, told the Tribune. "Thorough engagement in politics, at a midterm elections level, is almost like a luxury good."

## What's Next

Banda will learn on Election Day whether her cancer is in remission or has spread to her organs. She recently started a cancer-targeting medication, Kisqali, that lists at $16,000 a month but is reduced to $500 by insurance; its side effects already forced her to miss a meeting to close her first sale since falling ill.

On a recent evening, she joined her family to replace flowers at her sister's tomb, walking along the wall of crypts. She paused at a blank one beside Melissa's.

"This one's for me," she said. Then, with a smile at her daughter's protest, she added: "in a hundred years."

Whether healthcare costs move enough voters like Banda to tip the Texas Senate race — and the wider midterm map — will be answered in the days ahead. For now, her ballot and her prognosis arrive on the same date, a convergence that captures the stakes of an election shaped by bills as much as beliefs.
