# Meta's Filter Deal Asks States: What Is Too Much Beauty?

How much filter is too much filter when a teenager posts a selfie? That is the question state attorneys general across the United States are now being forced to answer, following a landmark settlement with Meta that requires the company to block certain AI-powered beauty filters for minors—while leaving the hardest task, defining exactly which filters cross the line, to regulators.

The agreement, reached in August between Meta and 47 states plus Washington, D.C. and three territories, committed the company to barring "cosmetic procedure filters" for users under 18, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/25/nx-s1-6011190/meta-facebook-settlement-beauty-filters-ai). But the deal does not impose a blanket ban on all digital effects, and it does not define what counts as a bannable filter. That burden now falls on state officials, who set themselves a two-month window to send Meta written guidance on where harmless fun ends and harmful manipulation begins.

## A Settlement With Blurry Edges

The August agreement resolved a federal child safety lawsuit accusing Meta of designing Facebook and Instagram to be addictive for minors and of concealing the risks. As [the settlement document](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/attachments/press-docs/23-05448-ecf-572-1-exhibit-1-mdl-consent-judgment-final-settlment-agreement-fully-executed.pdf) lays out, Meta agreed to pay up to $17 billion over a decade and to make sweeping operational changes—including default time limits, nighttime restrictions, and limits on displaying "like" counts on minors' posts.

The filter provision sits within that broader framework, and its scope is deliberately narrow. Filters that turn users into playful characters—elves, puppies—remain available to young people. So do filters offering subtle beautification, such as smoothing skin tone or adding a slight eye glow. What is barred is a narrower category: filters mimicking extreme transformations like cosmetic procedures or heavy makeup.

That distinction is precisely what worries youth advocates. For teenagers awash in curated images, the most corrosive editing may not be the obviously fake kind.

## The Trouble With 'Subtle' Editing

Mia Lerner, a 17-year-old high school senior in California and a youth body image activist, told NPR that experimenting with beauty filters contributed to her eating disorder and left her struggling with her self-image. Her ambivalence captures the paradox at the heart of the debate.

"I've even tried to fix my nose with AI," Lerner said. "My face looks a lot better, I think, in [the] photo that I manipulated with these beauty filters. But I'm still living with this nose that I see in the mirror."

Yet Lerner said the filters she finds hardest to resist are not the extreme ones. "When I see something that has been so obviously AI-generated, it's very easy for me to logically say, 'Well, that's obviously been manipulated so I can't compare myself to that,'" she said. "But when I see these videos of people who are just slightly edited, it's a lot easier for me to go down that comparison path."

She nonetheless called the settlement "a very important step in the right direction." The scientific literature supports her concern. A study co-authored by Kelly Frasier in the [Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12809008/) describes "social media dysmorphia" as a digitally mediated crisis of self-image, finding that adolescents are especially vulnerable because they are in formative stages of identity and rely heavily on social validation. The research links filter use to lower body image satisfaction and diminished appearance self-esteem, reinforcing a cycle of comparison, distress, and platform re-engagement.

## A Moving Target

Even if states could agree on where to draw the line today, the line would not stay put. Digital beauty standards evolve quickly, and the tools used to chase them evolve faster.

"If the states want to define what is a beauty filter, they should define what is beauty," said Florencia Solari, a creative technologist who has spent years developing AI and augmented reality experiences for global brands.

Solari noted that the "Instagram face"—a look of small button nose, high cheekbones and pouty lips coined by The New Yorker writer Jia Tolentino in 2019—is already falling out of favor. "People are starting to move away from that because it's starting to look a bit clownish," she said. "Now it's going towards extreme thinness. So that's another problem."

The implication is uncomfortable for regulators: a filter branded "extreme" in 2026 may look unremarkable in 2027, and the tools that produce the next ideal may not be filters at all. Frasier argued that legislators are asking the wrong question. "And to be honest, filters will probably become completely obsolete within the next few years," she said, pointing to a near future in which teens use AI to build entire fake universes around themselves rather than merely retouch a feature.

## Legal and Regional Fault Lines

The definitional problem is compounded by a legal one. Nothing prevents individual states from reaching conflicting conclusions about what the settlement's "cosmetic procedure filter" language means.

"What we don't really have a sense of is what happens if the states disagree with each other?" said David Greene, senior counsel at the Electronic Frontier Foundation. "What if one state finds, you know, eyebrow lifting to be completely acceptable and another one thinks it's awful?"

Meta says it plans to enforce the ban on cosmetic procedure filters by early next year. The four lead plaintiff states—California, Colorado, Kentucky, and New Jersey—either declined to comment or did not respond to NPR's questions about the guidelines process.

Beyond the U.S., some regulators are trying to sidestep the definitional debate altogether. In Europe, the proposed [KIDS Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex:52026PC0681), designed to regulate youth social media use, takes a sweeping approach. "Beauty filters—not extreme beauty filters; beauty filters full stop—are forbidden for minors," European Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier said, adding that EU officials are watching the U.S. settlement's implementation closely. "We want child-friendly services for our children. A beauty filter has nothing to do with a child-friendly service."

## What's Next

The coming months will test whether a settlement built on a moving, contested definition can actually be enforced. Meta has already taken earlier steps—banning filters that explicitly promote cosmetic surgery, shutting down its third-party filter platform, and introducing AI-content labels—yet a federal judge found this summer that harmful aesthetic filters persistently slipped past those guardrails.

The deeper question may be whether teens themselves will have a seat at the table. "So often it's just the government or these companies in these conversations," Lerner said. "The company should be asking us for our actual perspectives."

As state guidance takes shape and Meta prepares enforcement, the debate over beauty filters is becoming a proxy for a larger fight: how far governments can and should go in regulating the digital images that shape how a generation sees itself—and whether any line drawn today can keep pace with the technology redrawing it.
