# U.S. and Iran Edge Toward Full War on Mutual Fear

Nearly eight months into a U.S.-Israeli war against Iran, Washington and Tehran are sliding into a volatile new phase in which mutual suspicion, contested intelligence and the political calendars of two leaders facing imminent elections are converging in ways that could ignite full-scale conflict. According to [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/24/us/politics/trump-netanyahu-iran-new-phase-of-war.html), American officials now warn that Iran is in the "advanced stages" of planning lethal attacks on U.S. personnel, even as Iranian leaders appear convinced that Washington and Jerusalem are preparing a third major offensive aimed at forcing their surrender.

The central danger, the Times reported, is not a single decision but a self-reinforcing escalatory dynamic: each side reads the other's preparations as evidence of hostile intent, and preparations on one side validate the fears of the other. "This is how countries can escalate into full-scale war," several diplomats and outside experts told the newspaper.

## A War Entering a New Phase

The conflict began on Feb. 28, 2026, when the United States and Israel launched what was described as a decapitation strike that killed Iran's then-supreme leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, in its opening hours. That campaign was itself the third major attack in a 16-month confrontation, and by late October the conventional military campaign, punishing economic sanctions and a U.S. naval blockade had failed to bring Tehran back to meaningful negotiations.

The current alarm was triggered by a memo from Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton to Secretary of State Marco Rubio on Friday, Oct. 23, stating that Iran had "substantially increased efforts to target U.S. personnel" and that "the lethal plotting efforts by the Iranians against U.S. citizens have reached advanced stages in both the Middle East and certain other countries." In response, the State Department ordered nonessential employees and their families to depart Saudi Arabia and placed missions across the region on restricted operating status. U.S. agencies now assess the threat to Americans at the highest level since the war began.

## Ambition Versus Capability

Yet multiple officials cautioned that Iran's ambitions consistently outstrip its capability. Iranian officials "often exaggerate their ability to carry out covert overseas attacks," officials told the Times, and the danger of any specific plot "depends on the competence of the criminal hired." That gap between intent and capacity is central to the story's uncertain picture: the underlying intelligence remains highly classified, and only a small group of congressional leaders were briefed before the warning went public.

The public messaging has nonetheless been forceful. U.S. officials say the intelligence suggests Iran may seek to kill American ambassadors and other officials in the Middle East and on U.S. soil, and that Tehran may have operatives inside the United States who are "not just simply Iranian nationals." Senior European officials warned of the consequences of assuming the threat is real. If it is, one senior official said, "one gunshot that appears aimed at a U.S. official could touch off a conflagration."

## Preparations on Both Sides

On the other side of the divide, Iranian leaders appear convinced the United States and Israel are readying a decisive blow. The two militaries have drafted plans for a renewed, short but intensive strike campaign after the U.S. midterm elections on Nov. 3, with potential targets including energy infrastructure, nuclear facilities and missile and drone arsenals — possibly including the deeply buried nuclear site known as Pickaxe Mountain. The U.S. will not have its full complement of military assets in place until early November.

Iran has made clear that any new war would be fought asymmetrically and far beyond its own borders. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, warned that "there can be no arrangement in which Iran's economic lifelines come under attack while the economic architecture enabling those attacks is considered untouched and immune." Iran has threatened to target power grids, desalination plants, refineries, undersea fiber-optic cables, data centers and AI facilities across as many as seven Gulf countries, and has declared "zero red lines."

"We are in a surprise phase of the war, and we will not fight a classic war," said Mahdi Mohammadi, a senior adviser to Iran's lead negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf. "It will be an asymmetrical, fierce and conclusive war that will target the enemy's strong points and weak spots." Reza Nasri, an international lawyer close to Iran's foreign ministry, warned that "a third wave of war would not stay contained," adding that power grids, desalination plants, refineries and ports across the region would burn and that global oil markets would spike.

## Election Time and the Pretext Problem

The risk is amplified by timing. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu faces an election on Oct. 27, and President Trump faces midterms on Nov. 3. Trump has publicly said he would not strike before the midterms but has hinted that "all bets are off" afterward, and officials say that in private he appears to be leaning toward another round of major strikes. He has repeatedly pulled back from attacking Iran in recent months, including vetoing a near-final plan.

The crisis echoes earlier intelligence failures and misreadings. The Times invoked the Cuban Missile Crisis and the 2003 Iraq invasion as cases in which partial or faulty intelligence proved catastrophic, warning that selective intelligence can be used as a pretext for military action. Dana Stroul, a former top Pentagon Middle East policy official now at the Washington Institute for Near East Policy, noted the limits of the campaign so far: neither the conventional military effort nor sanctions and the naval blockade "have not brought the regime back to the negotiating table in a meaningful way."

Iran, meanwhile, has built its leverage strategy around economic pain. "The war has made us understand that the best leverage for pressuring Trump is increasing oil and energy prices because it will hurt the American people," said Peiman Salehi, a political analyst in Tehran.

## A Dangerous Convergence

The story's core insight is that this is less a tale of deliberate decisions than of a spiral in which each side's defensive preparations become the other's justification for escalation. A stray explosion, an apparent assassination attempt or a strike on a buried bunker could provide the spark that neither leadership may consciously intend.

With both capitals seemingly resigned to another round of fighting, the diplomatic track continues in parallel. Iran has offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days and to discuss its nuclear file in exchange for the lifting of sanctions, and foreign ministers from Europe, Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Egypt have convened to explore an off-ramp. Whether diplomacy can outrun the war drum remains the central question — and the days between two elections will be decisive.

## What to Watch

Three questions will shape the coming weeks: whether Trump authorizes strikes after Nov. 3, when his military assets are fully in place; whether Netanyahu survives his vote or, even if defeated, launches a final strike as a caretaker prime minister; and whether the Istanbul talks produce any arrangement to reopen Hormuz and ease sanctions before the window closes. For now, the most immediate risk is not a planned war but an accidental one — a single event that both sides, primed to see the worst in the other, could read as cause.
