# US and Iran Edge Toward Full War as Fears Escalate

The United States and Iran have entered what diplomats and analysts describe as a new and more dangerous phase of their confrontation, driven by two mirror-image fears and a pair of imminent elections that could tip the standoff back into full-scale war. Washington believes Tehran is in the advanced stages of planning attacks on US officials at home and abroad, while Iran's leaders are convinced that Israel and the United States are preparing a third major offensive in 16 months to force their surrender, [The New York Times reported](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/24/us/politics/trump-netanyahu-iran-new-phase-of-war.html).

As of Sunday, the immediate threat appeared to have eased slightly: President Donald Trump publicly ruled out a strike "today" and floated the possibility of a deal, while Qatari mediators met with Iranian officials in Tehran, [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/liveblog/2026/10/25/iran-war-live-trump-hints-at-possible-sunday-iran-attack-or-deal-reached). But the underlying military buildup and reciprocal threat posture remain in place, and a single incident could still ignite a wider conflict.

## A War Already Underway

The current conflict began on Feb. 28, 2026, when a joint US-Israeli opening strike killed Iran's previous supreme leader and senior commanders in a decapitation attack. Iran's current leadership, now under Mojtaba Khamenei, survived that strike and has since managed the country through two rounds of major US-Israeli bombardment. An interim agreement signed in June briefly paused hostilities, but talks toward a final settlement have stalled.

Trump has described the campaign as having "obliterated Iran's nuclear enrichment capacity," yet Tehran refuses to surrender its stockpile of enriched uranium and insists that any final deal begin with an end to the war, relief from US sanctions and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. The gap between the two positions has remained wide.

## Two Fears, One Powder Keg

The escalation is being fed by a dangerous symmetry. US intelligence disclosed in the week of Oct. 20 that Iran may be planning to kill US ambassadors in the Middle East and American officials on US soil, an assessment that echoes the long-standing ambitions of Iran's elite Unit 840, which has historically carried out kidnappings and assassinations abroad. The Times cautioned, however, that Iran's "desires often outstrip its capabilities" — it has at times hired hapless operatives arrested before they could act.

On the Iranian side, officials describe the next conflict as existential. "We see the next conflict as an existential threat. The third war in two years means a threat to existence. Our strategy is 'zero red lines,'" said Mehdi Mohammadi, an adviser to Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Ghalibaf, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/22/iran-threatens-seven-gulf-countries-us-israel-renew-attacks). Mohammadi added that Iran would "break the temporal and spatial dimensions of the conflict," vowing the war "will continue longer and in a wider geographical area than the enemy anticipates."

Iran's security chief, Mohsen Rezaei, warned that Tehran would strike oil facilities, undersea cables, data centres and AI facilities across as many as seven Gulf countries in the event of a fresh US-Israeli attack. "There can be no arrangement in which Iran's economic lifelines come under attack while the economic architecture enabling those attacks is considered untouched and immune," he said.

## The Electoral Clock

Both leaders face imminent elections that analysts say are feeding the risk. Israel votes on Oct. 27 and the United States holds midterms on Nov. 3 — a combination of deadlines that, combined with heightened intelligence and reciprocal threat perception, is precisely how countries can slide into full-scale war, diplomats and outside experts told the Times.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has reportedly told US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth that he would prefer Iran to strike first. Even if defeated at the polls, Netanyahu could launch one last strike that a successor might hesitate to carry out. Trump, meanwhile, aware that the war is unpopular even among his strongest supporters, may want to demonstrate that his commander-in-chief authority is undiminished.

Yet Trump has also pledged not to launch new strikes before the midterms and has not authorized another offensive. Reporting has described plans for a "short, intense" operation — potentially about three days — targeting Iran's nuclear facilities, missile and drone arsenals and energy infrastructure, to follow the Nov. 3 vote. A US military buildup, including three aircraft carriers, thousands of Marines, additional fighter aircraft and special operations forces, is expected to be complete by early November. US personnel have been quietly moved from bases in Jordan, Qatar and Kuwait as a precaution, and the State Department ordered the departure of non-emergency personnel and eligible family members from Saudi Arabia amid Houthi attacks.

Israel has staged its own show of force, with dozens of fighter jets and refuelling aircraft flying more than 800 miles through Syrian and Iraqi airspace toward Iran's western border — turning back before entering Iranian airspace — along a route resembling prior strike paths.

## Hormuz and the Global Economy

Much of the danger concentrates on the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint for roughly a fifth of global oil. Iran's Revolutionary Guard has attacked commercial shipping transiting the strait, with some vessels reportedly hitting mines; there has been no independent verification of the number of ships struck. Brent crude traded at $113.4 a barrel, a recent high, propelled by fears of a renewed assault.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the waterway will not return to its previous arrangements. "The shipping routes through the Strait of Hormuz will no longer be the same as before, nor will the strait be managed as it was in the past," he said, according to Iranian state media. Reza Nasri, an international lawyer close to Iran's foreign ministry, warned that "a third wave of war would not stay contained," predicting that power grids, desalination plants, refineries and ports across the region would burn and that "Hormuz would close completely, oil would spike, and the global economy would tip into recession."

The economic toll of the war is already mounting. Estimates cited in regional reporting put the cost of the US campaign through Aug. 1 at $38 billion, rising by roughly $3 billion a month, with the war potentially raising US inflation by 0.5% in early 2027. Reuters reported that the US has used virtually all of its long-range precision missiles, and at least 21 US service members have been killed and hundreds wounded.

## Diplomacy on the Edge

A diplomatic track remains alive, if fragile. Foreign ministers from three European countries, plus Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Egypt, were due to meet in Istanbul to coordinate policy on the linked Iranian, Yemeni and Palestinian crises. Qatar's mediators visited Tehran on Sunday, and Pakistan's army chief, Field Marshal Asim Munir, travelled to the Iranian capital to try to revive mediation.

Iran's parliament speaker, Ghalibaf, struck a defiant tone, saying the United States had become "embroiled in a quagmire as a result of its miscalculations." Vice President JD Vance, in a rare acknowledgment, conceded "a lot of mistakes and a lot of fundamental failures" in Washington's handling of the war.

## What to Watch

The Times invoked the Cuban Missile Crisis and the 2003 Iraq invasion as cautionary tales about the danger of partial or faulty intelligence being used as a pretext for war. That warning cuts both ways: an attack on a US official, or a stray explosion near a bunker sheltering Iranian leaders, are the identified tinderbox scenarios that could trigger a conflagration neither side fully controls.

The coming days will test whether the easing of rhetoric holds. The key questions are whether Trump authorizes a post-midterm strike, whether Netanyahu acts before or after Israel's election, and whether Qatari and Pakistani mediation can close a gap that remains wide — with Washington demanding nuclear concessions first and Tehran insisting on sanctions relief and a reopened Hormuz before any substantive bargaining. Until those questions are answered, the two sides remain balanced on the edge of a war that neither may be able to keep contained.
