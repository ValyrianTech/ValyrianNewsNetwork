# Europe Heat Killed 50,000 This Summer, Study Finds

A new study estimates that 50,391 people died from heat across 854 European cities during the summer of 2026 — the deadliest summer for heat mortality on modern record — and that roughly two-thirds of those deaths can be attributed to human-caused climate change. The peer-reviewed research, led by scientists at Imperial College London and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM), was released on 23 October and accepted for publication in the journal *Nature Health*.

According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/oct/23/heat-killed-staggering-50000-people-european-cities-this-summer-experts), the researchers concluded that planet-warming pollution was responsible for an additional 32,619 deaths in urban areas between June and August. Of the total, about 65% — or "two in every three" — were linked to anthropogenic warming. The study's primary page at [Imperial College London](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/grantham/publications/all-publications/heat-related-urban-mortality-attributable-to-human-induced-climate-change-in-europe-in-summer-2026.php) confirms the analysis covered 854 urban areas across 30 countries — the EU-27 plus the UK, Switzerland and Norway — representing about 40% of the combined population of those nations.

## A Record, and a Rising Share

The death toll is the highest since the research team's records began in 2000. As [NPR and the Associated Press](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/23/g-s1-149498/europe-heat-deaths) reported, the summer 2026 figure is 71% higher than the previous deadliest summer, 2022, when the same team estimated 29,480 deaths. The authors stressed that the 2026 number is likely an underestimate: it excludes a deadly May heatwave and covers only the 854 cities studied, leaving out towns and rural areas.

Perhaps more striking than the absolute number is the growing climate fingerprint. The share of heat deaths attributable to climate change has climbed from about 40% at the start of the century to roughly 65% this summer. "In some sense, the absolute numbers of the heat-related deaths estimated across the 854 urban areas are indeed staggering," said Malcolm Mistry, a climate and health scientist at LSHTM and a co-author. "But perhaps what is even more alarming is the proportion of these deaths that we attribute to anthropogenic climate change."

## How the Numbers Were Built

The study relies on a "detection and attribution" method widely used in climate science. Researchers combined established relationships between heat and mortality with 2026 temperature data, then ran computer simulations of a world without climate change and compared the results. The difference between the two scenarios is the death toll attributed to warming.

These are model-based estimates rather than counts drawn from death certificates — a necessary approach because heat is rarely recorded as a contributing cause. As Garyfallos Konstantinoudis, a biostatistician at Imperial College London and a co-author, explained, the team's model produced 2,700 estimated heat deaths for British heatwaves in May and June, closely matching the UK statistics agency's figure of 2,800. Independent researcher David García-García of the Carlos III Health Institute in Madrid, who was not involved in the study, called it a "valuable contribution" but cautioned that the figures are "estimates based on observed temperatures and epidemiological models, which do not incorporate actual data on deaths occurring in the summer of 2026."

## The Human Profile of a Deadly Summer

The toll fell hardest on the elderly. According to The Guardian, people aged 85 and over made up almost half of the deaths, though younger people were not spared: 833 people between the ages of 20 and 44 are also thought to have died from the heat.

Geography shaped the crisis. Per-capita death rates were highest in Malta, Italy and Hungary, while absolute tolls were greatest in Italy, France, Spain, Germany and the UK, which saw an estimated 4,354 deaths. Naples was the hardest-hit city, with 815 deaths per million people on an age-standardised basis. Mario Guarino, an emergency doctor at Ospedale CTO in Naples, told The Guardian he saw a 15% rise in emergency room visits during the hottest weeks, and described the toll as compounded by loneliness and poverty. "Many elderly people are left alone during the summer, abandoned by their families, and due to their low income, they can't afford air conditioning or, if they do have it, avoid turning it on because of the cost of electricity," he said.

## Why Europe Is Especially Exposed

Europe is the fastest-warming continent, now roughly 2.5°C above pre-industrial levels — about twice the global average. Some European cities recorded temperatures up to 12°C above normal this summer. About one-third of the deaths occurred during a particularly intense heatwave from 17 June to 1 July, when daily average temperatures ran roughly 5°C above the 1991–2020 baseline in some areas.

Lead author Clair Barnes, a climate scientist at Imperial College London, said much of Western and Northern Europe has infrastructure that was simply not designed to cope with the sort of temperatures now being seen as a result of human-caused warming. "Behind these statistics are real people whose lives were lost and real families who grieve for them," she said.

## Analysis: A Public-Health Reckoning

Beyond the headline figure, the study sharpens a debate about how societies should adapt to a hotter world. Outside experts who reviewed the findings praised the methods while urging action on both adaptation and emissions. "We need to prepare for the heat we cannot now avoid, with urgent investments in heat early-warning systems, equitable access to low-carbon cooling, and neighborhood buddy systems that check on and support people most at risk — while rapidly reducing the fossil fuel use driving the crisis," said Dr. Courtney Howard, president-elect of the Canadian Medical Association, who was not involved in the research.

Dr. Jonathan Patz of the University of Wisconsin–Madison framed the choice bluntly. "We can't just be mopping up the mess on the floor without thinking about turning the faucet off," he said. "We need better cooling and protecting vulnerable populations... But we also need to go to the root of the problem and absolutely address the burning of fossil fuels."

The timing is pointed. The findings arrive days after the UN Environment Programme's *Emissions Gap Report 2026* declared that the window to limit warming to 1.5°C "has closed" — the first time the agency has stated so explicitly. UK climate transition minister Katie White called the death figures "shocking," saying they "represent thousands of families who have lost a loved one this summer due to the climate crisis."

## What to Watch

The study's authors caution that the 854-city toll cannot be simply scaled to a whole-continent figure, and that national public-health agencies' own tallies differ by country and method. Still, the trajectory is clear: as heatwaves grow more frequent and intense, the line between climate policy and public-health policy is dissolving. The coming years will test whether governments treat extreme heat — a slow, largely invisible killer — with the urgency the numbers now demand.
