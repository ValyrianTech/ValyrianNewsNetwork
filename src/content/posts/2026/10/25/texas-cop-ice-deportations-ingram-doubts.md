# A Texas Cop Helped Deport 10% of His Town. Now He Has Doubts

In a Hill Country town of roughly 1,900 people beside the Guadalupe River, a single patrol officer became the local face of the Trump administration's mass deportation campaign. Over the course of a year, Jaron Alexander and his small police department handed the equivalent of one-tenth of Ingram, Texas, to federal immigration authorities. Now, in a joint [ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/deportations-ingram-texas-287g-jaron-alexander) and [New York Times Magazine](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/25/magazine/ice-deputy-texas-police.html) investigation, Alexander is expressing second thoughts about the work—even as he defends it.

## How a Tiny Department Signed On

Ingram's police force enrolled in U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement's 287(g) program, named for a section of the 1996 Immigration and Nationality Act. Under its most aggressive form, the [task force model](https://www.ice.gov/identify-and-arrest/287g), local officers are deputized as deputy immigration agents and can enforce federal immigration law during routine police work. The model had been dormant for about a dozen years before the Trump administration revived it in January 2025, encouraging "any law-enforcement agency, no matter how troubled or small" to sign up.

Alexander suggested the idea to his chief, Dustin Breshears, who was under City Council pressure to generate more traffic stops and arrests. He completed an online ICE course a few weeks after responding to a noise complaint at a family's baptism party in early October 2025, qualifying for time-and-a-half pay when assisting the agency.

Within three months, Alexander and the department's other patrol officer had delivered at least 70 people to immigration agents. Within a year, they had handed over the equivalent of 10% of Ingram—a town that was roughly 45% Latino and had been affectionately called "mini Guanajuato" for the central Mexican state where many residents have roots. According to Breshears, Ingram had logged 194 "immigration-related incidents" as of August 2026.

The financial incentives were substantial. A September 2025 ICE announcement offered a $100,000 vehicle stipend, a $7,500 equipment stipend per participating officer, quarterly performance bonuses, and salary and overtime reimbursement. Pay stubs showed Alexander made $18,793.25 from task force hours in nine months—more than a quarter of his roughly $70,000 base salary.

## A Community Unraveled

The story traces how the enforcement reshaped Ingram and tore apart specific families. Joanna Martinez's husband, Ulices Macias, was detained on Dec. 28, 2025, in front of his 2-week-old daughter and 4-year-old stepson. A half dozen attendees of the October baptism party would soon be gone from Ingram, detained and deported or leaving to join relatives who had been.

The economic damage rippled outward. At a local Mexican restaurant, daily takeout orders dropped from 200 to 50. A corner store lost roughly half its revenue. A contractor's crew shrank from 30 workers to eight, stretching a four-day job into two weeks—slowing reconstruction after a 2025 flood that killed at least 139 people along the Guadalupe River.

Even conservative business owners in a county that voted 77% for Trump in 2024 grew frustrated. "I didn't vote for this. I voted to get the bad guys out," Neal Secor, a local cabin owner, told the reporters, recounting a conversation with the mayor. "There's still a lot of bad guys here. These guys aren't them." Steve Edelstein, rebuilding a damaged strip mall, put it more plainly: "Technically local law enforcement is doing their job. But it isn't helping the community."

## A Nationwide Surge

Ingram is one of a rapidly growing number of communities where local police have become extensions of federal immigration enforcement. By late 2026, more than 1,900 agencies in at least 30 states had signed up for the task force model, with more than 29,000 deputized officers, according to the reporting. ICE recently hit a record of more than 50,000 immigration arrests in August. The federal government has earmarked $240 billion for the deportation apparatus.

The expansion has revived concerns that prompted the program's earlier phase-out. Under President Barack Obama, ICE phased out 287(g) after early participants like Joe Arpaio of Maricopa County, Arizona, used it to disproportionately target Latinos; Justice Department investigations found task-force deputies pulled over Latino drivers at up to nine times the rate of other drivers. Texas law now requires sheriffs in counties operating jails to seek 287(g) agreements, with a Dec. 1, 2026 compliance deadline, though cities and police departments are not required to participate.

A [Marshall Project](https://www.themarshallproject.org/2026/09/10/ice-arrests-immigrants-local-police-287g) analysis found the program dramatically boosted local immigration arrests, and the independent [287(g) Watch](https://287g.recoveredfactory.net/) tracker counted more than 2,200 local agencies with agreements by late 2026.

## The Officer's Doubts

Alexander's own account is marked by contradiction. He describes the system he enforced as "skewed" and "weird," yet insists he was simply doing a job. "If you're taking federal money to do a job," he told the reporters, "I think you should do the job."

But the encounters accumulated. To a woman confronting him about her detained relatives, he said: "If I'm being 100% honest, I don't enjoy when families are separated, like with the Joanna situation. That kind of fucked me up." In early June, Martinez recalled him saying: "I don't feel good about this. I signed up to be a cop, not an ICE agent." Alexander says he could have said something like that.

He has also let at least 10 people go—many of them mothers of young children. "It's like one free pass, I guess," he said of one such decision, adding that if he encountered the same man again he would probably hand him over. He denies targeting Latinos. "I don't care who's in the car," he said. "I really don't."

The reporters note that the number of actual deportations, as opposed to handovers, is uncertain; Breshears could not say whether every handover led to a deportation, and Alexander does not know how many of the 194 incidents were his stops.

## What to Watch

Ingram offers a granular window into a national question: as thousands of local agencies become partners in federal deportation, what happens to the communities they police—and to the officers themselves? In Ingram, the answer has included emptied neighborhoods, a fractured Latino community, an economy still digging out from floods, and one officer who now does his shopping 30 minutes away to avoid being recognized.

Alexander, meanwhile, frames his choices as evidence of his humanity. "For context, I've deported like six of their family members, and they're still friendly to me," he said. "So I'm not the monster they make me out to be."
