# China Launches 28th Batch of Satellite Internet Satellites

China successfully launched the 28th batch of satellites for its low-orbit satellite internet constellation on Sunday, deploying the payload aboard a Long March 8A rocket from the Hainan Commercial Spaceport in Wenchang and marking another step in the steady build-out of a domestic space-based broadband network designed to rival SpaceX's Starlink.

According to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261025/3573644b269e466f83510360cb84eff4/c.html), the rocket lifted off at 10:03 a.m. Beijing time, and the satellites smoothly entered their designated orbit. The mission was declared a complete success. The state news agency reported that the launch used a Long March 8A carrier rocket, developed by the China Academy of Launch Vehicle Technology.

## Context: China's Answer to Starlink

The satellites belong to the GW constellation, also known as Guowang, China's first mega-constellation for satellite internet. Operated by the state-owned China SatNet, the project is planned for 12,992 satellites, making it the world's second-largest low-Earth-orbit constellation after Starlink, as outlined in a [technical explainer](https://www.chinaaet.com/article/3000178413).

The network uses a two-layer architecture — an ultra-low orbit layer below 500 kilometers and a near-Earth orbit layer at 1,145 kilometers — balancing low latency with wide coverage. The project is projected to complete a backbone network by the end of 2026, initially achieving nationwide coverage across China.

## An Industrialized Launch Rhythm

A single batch launch is incremental, but the 28th batch underscores how routine the constellation's construction has become. Batch 28 lifted off just 15 days after batch 27, launched on October 10, according to a [launch-history compilation](https://www.chinaaet.com/article/3000180876) that traces the program from its first "one rocket, ten satellites" networking launch in December 2024 through a rapid 2025 ramp-up and an accelerating 2026 cadence.

Industry analysts describe the shift as a structural one. "Constellation construction has moved from 'mission-type ensuring success' into an industrialized networking stage of 'operations-type ensuring rhythm,'" a researcher told [21st Century Business Herald](https://m.21jingji.com/article/20260918/herald/8c1eb61aa7b7929e9810e2f94908ca56.html), which documented China's push into high-density launch operations. The report noted that commercial space financing exceeded 8 billion yuan in the first quarter of 2026, up 460 percent year-on-year.

## Operational Readiness on Display

The constellation is not merely being built — it is beginning to deliver services. Six days before this launch, Xinhua reported that China's domestically built satellite internet had, for the first time, achieved connectivity across the "three poles" — the Arctic, Antarctic, and the Tibetan Plateau, as covered by [electronic engineering outlet AET](https://www.chinaaet.com/article/3000181144).

Aboard the icebreaker Polar on China's 16th Arctic expedition, the system supported clear voice and video links between the ship, Qinling Station, the Tibetan Plateau, and a Beijing video conference. The test verified the network under extreme cold, high altitude, strong wind-blown sand, and ice fog, and supported the first polar remote online medical consultation, connecting the ship to hospitals in Qingdao. The expedition transferred more than 300 files and 100 gigabytes of data and held over 40 video consultations.

## Analysis: Strategic and Economic Stakes

Orbit and spectrum resources are allocated on a first-come, first-served basis, creating urgency for China's state-led and municipal-led mega-constellations to stake claims and build scale. Alongside GW, the Shanghai-backed Qianfan constellation — which had 256 satellites in orbit as of mid-September and targets 324 by the end of 2026 — is pursuing initial commercial service in key global regions, the Herald reported.

The Chinese government has thrown its weight behind the sector. The 2026 Government Work Report explicitly called for "accelerating the development of satellite internet" and listed it as an annual priority task, while the 15th Five-Year Plan outline included satellite internet infrastructure among 109 major engineering projects. Chinese analysts frame the industry as a strategic emerging sector with trillion-yuan market potential, reinforced by expanded access to the STAR Market's listing standards for commercial space firms.

## What's Next

With the stated target of a completed backbone network and nationwide coverage by the end of 2026 now imminent, batch 28 materially advances toward that goal. Several questions remain open: how many GW satellites are actually in orbit and operational, when commercial services will open to consumers and at what pricing, and how the GW and Qianfan systems will interoperate or compete.

What is clear is that China's satellite internet program has crossed from demonstration into industrial rhythm — and each routine launch like Sunday's moves it closer to the coverage and service targets its planners have set.
