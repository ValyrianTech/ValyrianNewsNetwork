# Judge Halts Noncitizen Voting Probe in 9 States

A federal judge in California has ordered the Trump administration to halt its investigations into whether noncitizens are registered to vote in nine states, ruling that the effort rests on error-riddled data and is intimidating the people who help others vote. U.S. District Court Judge Araceli Martinez-Olguin, sitting in Oakland, issued the ruling Wednesday and made it public Thursday, finding the government's effort ought to stop until polls close on Election Day, Nov. 3, 2026.

She placed her own order on hold until Friday to give the administration a chance to appeal a decision that [the AP reported](https://apnews.com/article/trump-states-noncitizen-voting-investigation-california-ac8a87b135f7a2ae9fe961bab3b28825) marks a significant legal setback to the administration's election-integrity push.

## The Ruling

The order applies to investigations in California, Connecticut, Georgia, Missouri, Nevada, New York, Pennsylvania, Washington and Wisconsin. It came as the administration was hunting for noncitizen voters nationwide in the months before the midterm elections, framing the issue as a major national problem even though voting by noncitizens is [widely documented as rare](https://apnews.com/article/noncitizens-voting-republicans-election-2024-immigration-09b86e6768f755fd875f3c51b0e8ea70) — a reality underscored by the administration's own cases.

The lawsuit was filed by the California Nurses Association and National Nurses United, along with the Voto Latino Foundation, the city and county of San Francisco and Santa Clara County. In her [written ruling](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cand.479338/gov.uscourts.cand.479338.87.0.pdf), Martinez-Olguin wrote that the investigation's underlying lists of voters, drawn from state and local governments and public information, were "voluminous and unreliable."

According to legal filings, investigators were instructed to target naturalized citizens to determine whether they had voted before becoming U.S. citizens. During a "surge" phase beginning in late August, agents were ordered to complete reviews of flagged individuals in no more than 12 minutes per person.

"Even under the best conditions, there is a high likelihood of error in review because there is no federal database containing citizenship status of U.S. residents," Martinez-Olguin wrote. "The time limitations implemented during the Surge further exacerbate the risk of error."

## Intimidation Concerns

The plaintiffs argued that some U.S. citizens felt they were wrongly identified and investigated as part of the probe. Martinez-Olguin said those fears "are well understood as intimidation" that chilled people from attending campaign events and from working as poll monitors during the election.

Beau Tremitiere, a lawyer representing the nurses' unions and the voter group, said the ruling affirmed their position. "It was clear from the start that DHS built its lists on bad data, never checked how often it was wrong, and recklessly sent agents to people's homes weeks before an election," Tremitiere said in a statement.

In response, the Department of Homeland Security issued an email statement Thursday saying it is focused on "removing aliens who vote and dilute the votes of U.S. citizens," while adding, "We have no plans to question or investigate people we believe to be U.S. citizens."

## A Pattern of Setbacks

The decision adds to a string of federal court defeats for the administration's election-integrity agenda. The Department of Justice has been demanding detailed, unredacted voter-roll data from states and has sued the 30 states that refused to turn it over, [losing each of those cases so far](https://apnews.com/article/justice-department-voter-list-new-hampshire-trump-8d490c0f19b8658abe00f0b6b2cba408). Martinez-Olguin noted that for states that refused to share their records, the administration sought them through "unreliable commercial versions of voter roll data."

Details of the investigation, known as the "Unlawful Voter Initiative," surfaced last month after Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer of New York and Sen. Alex Padilla of California released a [whistleblower report](https://apnews.com/article/trump-election-noncitizen-voting-homeland-security-whistleblower-57f6d4bbc11347815020d2e5f241d6f9) alleging that federal employees were required to misrepresent themselves on state voter lookup tools and to create federal law enforcement records based on unreliable data. The disclosure, produced by the watchdog group Democracy Defenders Fund, warned that the practice could wrongly implicate U.S. citizens.

## Analysis: High Stakes Before the Midterms

The ruling lands in the final stretch of a campaign season in which control of Congress is in play, and it sharpens a fundamental dispute over how far the federal government may go to police voter rolls. Democrats have framed the initiative as election interference; the administration casts it as election security.

The practical effect may be more immediate than the legal precedent. With the probe frozen in nine states until the polls close, federal agents cannot act on flagged registrations in some of the country's most closely watched battlegrounds during the final days of voting — precisely the window in which the plaintiffs alleged the intimidation was most acute. The judge's emphasis on the absence of any federal database of citizenship status also undercuts the technical premise on which the broader initiative depends.

## What's Next

The administration has until Friday to appeal Martinez-Olguin's order. The central question is whether an appellate court will stay or overturn her ruling before Nov. 3. Beyond that, the decision's interaction with separate litigation over the revamped SAVE voter-eligibility database — which the Supreme Court has allowed the administration to use for now — remains unsettled, as does the political fallout as both parties make their closing arguments to voters.

For now, the ruling stands as a judicial check on a program that a federal judge concluded was built on unreliable lists and carried out under time pressure too severe to protect the citizens it touched.
