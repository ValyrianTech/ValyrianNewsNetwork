# Asbestos Fund paid €21.84m to victims in 2025

Belgium's Asbestos Fund (Fonds amiante / Asbestfonds, AFA) paid out €21.84 million in compensation during 2025 to people suffering from asbestos-related illnesses and their beneficiaries, the fund reported in its latest activity report published on Thursday. The figure lands as the federal government pushes through the most sweeping overhaul of asbestos liability in the fund's history.

According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-fonds-amiante-a-verse-21-84-millions-d-euros-d-indemnites-en-2025-11804344), mesothelioma — a cancer primarily associated with asbestos exposure — accounted for 82.4% of the amounts paid, underlining how heavily the compensation system is weighted toward the disease's most lethal manifestation.

## A fund built on recognition, not cure

The AFA is a federal public service, administered through Fedris, that indemnifies people suffering from asbestos-related diseases. Coverage extends to private-sector employees, self-employed workers, civil servants, and so-called "environmental" victims — people exposed outside the workplace, for instance by living near an asbestos-processing plant or alongside an asbestos worker. In the event of death, certain relatives may also receive compensation.

The fund is financed by the State, employers' contributions, social security contributions for the self-employed, and donations and legacies. Historically, it was financed half by the State and half by the asbestos industry — which received legal immunity in exchange for those contributions, a bargain that victims' groups have spent years trying to unwind.

## The numbers behind the payout

The 2025 activity report paints a detailed picture of a system processing a steady stream of claims. The AFA issued 344 first-time decisions during the year, of which 210 were positive — a recognition rate of 61%. Of those positive decisions, 151 concerned people suffering from mesothelioma.

In total, across all conditions, the fund granted 1,451 compensations during 2025, a figure that includes continuing and periodic payments rather than only new decisions. Some 77% of compensation went directly to victims, with 23% paid to their beneficiaries.

The geography of the payouts reflects the historical footprint of Belgium's asbestos industry. A majority of beneficiaries reside in Flanders, receiving a total of €14 million, the report notes — a concentration that mirrors the weight of companies such as Etex, formerly Eternit, in the Flemish region.

Beyond compensation, the fund pursues information and prevention work. Its social assistants made 51 home visits in 2025, its stand at the Batibouw construction fair received 746 visitors, and 457 hospital social services were briefed on victims' rights. In February 2026, the AFA launched a revamped website with educational tools to help the public identify asbestos-containing materials in homes.

The fund also acknowledges a persistent gap: some rights, notably the "third-person assistance" allowance for those who need help with daily tasks, remain insufficiently known.

## A reform years in the making

The 2025 figures arrive against an unusually active legislative backdrop. In April 2026, Parliament unanimously extended AFA coverage to broncho-pulmonary (lung) cancer — a measure the Belgian Association of Asbestos Victims (Abeva) had reportedly demanded for a decade.

On 12 June 2026, the Council of Ministers approved a draft bill to reform the fund. According to the [Fonds amiante](https://www.fondsamiante.be/fr/news/le-fonds-amiante-evolue-pour-mieux-soutenir-les-victimes), the proposed changes include harmonising healthcare reimbursement rules across victim categories, increasing funeral-expense support, establishing a free phone line, centralising asbestos registers, clarifying victims' rights to sue third parties, adapting self-employed contributions, and allowing civil fines against parties found liable for asbestos exposure.

Two days later, a political agreement was reached within the federal government to end the immunity enjoyed by asbestos-sector companies. Under the draft bill, "environmental" victims — those not exposed occupationally but through their surroundings — could, for the first time, both receive an AFA indemnity and sue the responsible company in court for additional compensation. Professional victims are not affected by this change.

As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/amiante-les-victimes-environnementales-pourront-poursuivre-les-entreprises-en-justice-11740076) reported, Health Minister Frank Vandenbroucke (Vooruit) called the agreement "an important step towards more justice for asbestos victims." N-VA president Valerie Van Peel, who tried five times over a decade to lift the immunity, hailed the end of what she described as a historic injustice. Abeva welcomed the move cautiously, awaiting scrutiny of prescription-period rules and other legal objections. The draft was sent to the Council of State before becoming a formal bill for parliamentary debate.

## The gap between recognised and actual victims

The fund's recognition data tells a more sobering story. The AFA recognised 219 victims in 2024, down from 253 in 2023, averaging roughly 197 recognised victims per year over two decades. Between its creation in 2007 and 2024, the fund recognised 4,833 victims in total, as [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/societe/2025/03/14/le-fonds-amiante-a-reconnu-219-victimes-de-lamiante-en-2024-6GQQ5FKNVBBVLOZGXYRUGYQX4Y/) reported. Of the 253 victims recognised in 2023, 177 were occupationally exposed and 76 were environmental victims.

Abeva argues the true toll is far higher. Its spokesman, Marc Molitor, contends that multifactorial asbestos-linked lung cancers are hard to attribute and therefore go unrecorded. The association estimates roughly 850 asbestos victims per year in Belgium, of whom only about 250 are covered by the fund.

That gap — between the €21.84 million paid and the estimated burden of disease — is central to the debate over whether the fund's resources are adequate. The reform's new funding mechanisms, including adjusted self-employed contributions and civil fines, are an implicit recognition that the liability and compensation burden is growing.

## The next battle: decontamination

Victims' groups are already looking past compensation to the removal of existing asbestos from buildings. Abeva has called on Wallonia and Brussels to take up the decontamination file and insists that a significant contribution from Etex (formerly Eternit) to finance the work is "an absolute necessity." In September 2025, Van Peel called for Eternit to pay €1 billion to Flanders, as [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/amiante-la-presidente-de-la-n-va-veut-qu-eternit-paie-un-milliard-d-euros-a-la-flandre-11597602) reported.

The symbolic weight of the campaign is hard to overstate. Former Abeva president Eric Jonckheere, who lost family members to asbestos and campaigned for years on their behalf, died in December 2024 of an asbestos-related disease himself.

## What's next

For now, the fund's 2025 payout figure stands as both a measure of support delivered and a marker of how far the system still has to go. With the reform draft awaiting Council of State review and eventual parliamentary debate, the central questions are whether coverage will keep pace with a disease burden that advocates believe is significantly under-counted, and whether the companies that profited from asbestos will contribute to the costly work of removing it from Belgium's building stock.

A spokesperson for the fund framed its mission in broad terms: to guarantee access to compensation for people with asbestos-related illnesses, improve information, and contribute to prevention. The coming months will test whether the reform delivers on that promise.
