# 700,000 Belgian Households Overpay for Energy

Around 700,000 Belgian households are paying too much for electricity and gas—an average of roughly 700 euros a year, according to the federal energy regulator CREG. With winter approaching and energy prices still under pressure from the war in the Middle East, consumer broadcaster [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/21/energiecontract-elektriciteit-gas-prijs-v-test-creg-scan/) has published a four-step guide to help households review their contracts before committing to a new one.

## A costly pattern

The problem is not that Belgians fail to switch suppliers. It is how they switch. According to CREG spokesperson Kurt Hernot, Belgium ranks among the European leaders in changing energy providers—yet many consumers end up on a more expensive contract rather than a cheaper one.

The reason, Hernot told VRT NWS, is that consumers often do not know their current contract. They go to a price comparator, pick the cheapest-looking offer and never pause to consider what they are already paying or what conditions their existing deal carries. "The most important advice I can give is: first take out your own contract and don't make hasty decisions," he said.

That habit is what turns a routine switch into a loss. A comparison only works if it starts from what you actually pay today—and according to CREG's latest figures, reported by VRT NWS, roughly 700,000 households are on contracts that leave them an average of about 700 euros a year worse off.

## The four-step check

The VRT guide keeps the process deliberately simple.

**1. Take out your bills.** Establish which contract you currently have. This is the step consumers most often skip, and skipping it is what leads to bad comparisons.

**2. Run the CREG scan.** At [cregscan.be](https://www.cregscan.be/), enter your contract type and the month you signed it. In under two minutes, a bar shows whether your deal is expensive (red), average (orange) or cheap (green) against what is available today. A green result means there is little reason to change.

**3. Compare with the V-test.** The Flemish regulator's [V-test](https://www.vtest.be/) lists gas and electricity offers with the cheapest at the top. Brussels residents can use Brusim, run by the Brussels regulator Brugel. Keep your annual consumption figures—found on your annual bill—handy for accuracy. "You don't need to be an expert to fill in and understand everything," VRT NWS energy expert Luc Pauwels said.

**4. Adjust your contract—or not.** In principle, households can sign a new contract every month, and it does not have to be with a new supplier. A new deal with the current supplier is possible and can move quickly. A new contract typically starts a month later at the price of the offer at the time of signing.

## Read the fine print

The cheapest headline price is not always the best deal. Pauwels warned that contracts vary in duration, additional costs and specific conditions—and that customer service quality matters too. Consumers should also weigh whether they want the certainty of a fixed contract, the flexibility of a variable one, or, for electricity, a dynamic contract whose price shifts every quarter-hour and can pay off for households with solar panels or a home battery.

One trap is hidden termination fees. Pauwels cited a supplier that charges a year's "fixed fee"—effectively a subscription charge—that is not always refunded on termination. One example: a supplier that immediately charges six months of the fixed fee if a customer leaves within half a year. The V-test flags such disadvantageous exit arrangements.

Another issue is the so-called "sjoemelkortingen"—misleading welcome discounts that were officially banned in Belgium from the beginning of October 2026. Hernot cautioned that such discounts may still apply to contracts already in force, so households on older deals should check carefully.

## What to watch

The broader backdrop explains the timing. The war in the Middle East, including tensions around the Strait of Hormuz, has kept European gas reference prices—which set the benchmark for Belgian tariffs—under pressure, while the war in Ukraine continues to weigh on the market. VRT NWS reported in October that gas prices had smashed a previous record and that gas and electricity together cost households more than 900 euros more than in March.

For consumers, the takeaway is less about predicting prices than about knowing their own situation. The tools are free, the checks take minutes, and the potential saving is measured in hundreds of euros a year. The one rule that matters most, according to the regulator: look at what you have before you look at what is on offer.
