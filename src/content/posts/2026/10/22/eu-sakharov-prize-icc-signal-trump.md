# EU awards Sakharov Prize to ICC in signal to Trump

The European Parliament on Thursday awarded its 2026 Sakharov Prize for Freedom of Thought to the International Criminal Court and its officials, a decision widely read as a pointed act of solidarity with the court — and a rebuke to the United States, which has moved to sanction the tribunal as an institution. Parliament President Roberta Metsola announced the laureate in the Strasbourg hemicycle, where the choice was greeted with a standing ovation, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/22/sacharovprijs-van-europees-parlement-voor-internationaal-strafho/).

The prize, the European Union's highest tribute for human rights work, is awarded collectively to the court and its staff. The formal ceremony is scheduled for December in Strasbourg during a plenary session, the [European Parliament](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20261018IPR48263/the-international-criminal-court-and-its-officials-awarded-the-sakharov-prize) said in its announcement.

## A prize with a political edge

Metsola framed the award around the bloc's founding commitments. "The European Union is built on respect for multilateralism and international institutions," she said. "With the award of the Sakharov prize to the International Criminal Court and its officials, the European Parliament recognises the Court's important work in upholding international law and justice."

The timing is deliberate. The award comes less than two weeks after the Trump administration announced sweeping, institution-wide sanctions against the ICC — an escalation beyond earlier measures targeting individual judges and prosecutors. The US curbs cut the court off from US-based financial services and technology, block dollar transactions, bar its jurists from travelling to the United States and give US companies and individuals a six-month wind-down period to end business with the tribunal.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio cast the sanctions as a defence of American sovereignty, saying the measures would "cut off its resources and paralyse its operations" against what Washington calls a rogue court. The US grievance centres on ICC arrest warrants issued in 2024 for Israeli officials, including Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, and a now-closed investigation into the conduct of US soldiers in Afghanistan.

## The court's response

The ICC thanked the Parliament and pointedly linked the honour to the pressure it now faces. "This award comes at a time when the Court and its officials face unprecedented challenges, including sanctions aimed at disrupting the Court's judicial mandate," the court said in a [statement](https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/international-criminal-court-statement-following-award-2026-sakharov-prize-freedom-thought). "In a world marked by rising conflicts, there is no peace without justice and no security without law."

The recognition caps a remarkable month for the court. Days before the US sanctions were unveiled, the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize went to Navi Pillay, a former ICC judge — a juxtaposition that European officials and commentators seized on as evidence of a widening divide over international justice.

## Europe weighs its leverage

The Sakharov Prize is symbolic, but it aligns the EU's institutions squarely behind the court at a moment when its material support may matter more. In its 2025 annual reports on foreign policy and human rights, the European Parliament expressed "deep concern" at what it called "serious attacks on the international justice system," and MEPs have repeatedly urged the European Commission to activate the EU's so-called Blocking Statute. That 1996 regulation would nullify within the EU the effect of extraterritorial US measures and allow European operators to recover damages, offering a potential shield for ICC staff and the European companies that serve the court.

EU leaders had already signalled their displeasure at a summit the week before, saying the bloc "deeply regrets" the sanctions and urging Washington to reverse them, while noting the six-month grace period "creates space for diplomacy." They tasked the Commission with protecting the court and EU firms doing business with it.

## What to watch

The court's practical vulnerability remains the central question. If the ICC cannot access US financial plumbing or technology, legal experts warn it may struggle to run fair trials and appeals — a scenario that could complicate the detention of high-profile suspects in The Hague.

For now, the Parliament has chosen symbolism over confrontation, betting that a prestigious European honour will strengthen the court's standing rather than provoke Washington further. Whether that moral clarity translates into the financial and legal cover the ICC urgently needs will be the real test of Europe's commitment to the rules-based order it says it is defending.
