# Omar to Move Out of DC Home After Federal Search

Rep. Ilhan Omar said she will move out of her Washington, D.C., residence following a federal search of the home, telling an interviewer that the raid has made her address public and left her family feeling unsafe amid ongoing death threats. The announcement came in her first interview since Homeland Security Investigations agents searched the house on October 12, part of a criminal probe into potential fraud in her family's immigration paperwork.

"Obviously, I can't continue to live there," Omar told MS NOW's Chris Hayes in the interview, which aired Wednesday, according to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/21/ilhan-omar-congresswoman-raid-trump). "So we have to figure out where to move to, because our address has been public and with the death threats that I deal with, it's no longer going to be feasible to be in that house and have my kids, like, visiting off and on, having people track them."

The Minnesota Democrat, who is running for reelection in a deep-blue district centered on Minneapolis, said she is "confident in the fact that I didn't do anything wrong." The search occurred 22 days before the November 3 midterms, injecting a volatile legal and political fight into the closing stretch of a campaign that will decide control of Congress.

## What the Search Entailed

According to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/ilhan-omar-home-search-immigration-probe-21e4b07a2c3a18addc751e4535f2954f), Omar recalled waking around 6 a.m. to Homeland Security investigators banging on her door with a warrant. She said roughly 15 to 25 agents surrounded the home, searched for four to five hours, and seized 17 items — including phones, notebooks, a Somali-language children's book, and a vintage camera her husband had received as a gift.

Omar, her husband, and her 20-year-old son were home at the time. She described thinking "something was on fire" as she scrambled to get dressed before agents broke her front door. "You don't show up to somebody's house that early in the morning unless you're trying to freak them out," she said.

The Justice Department has released few details beyond confirming that HSI agents executed a "judicially authorized search warrant" as part of an "ongoing federal criminal investigation." Federal authorities have also seized Omar's phone and issued more than a dozen grand jury subpoenas, including to some of her relatives, a person briefed on the probe told the AP on condition of anonymity.

## The Allegations and Omar's Defense

The investigation centers on potential fraud in Omar's family's immigration paperwork, a theory long promoted by conservative critics who have alleged — without substantiated proof — that she married her brother to circumvent U.S. immigration law. Omar has repeatedly denied the accusation, calling it "disgusting lies."

Addressing the conspiracy theory directly in the interview, Omar dismissed it as implausible. "It would be a huge undertaking to, I suppose, indict a 12-year-old for so cleverly conspiring to defraud immigration services to come to the United States," she said. "I'm sure there are a lot of smart 12-year-olds, but that seems like a leap."

Omar arrived in the United States at age 12 in March 1995 after her family fled Somalia's civil war and spent four years in a Kenyan refugee camp. She became a naturalized U.S. citizen in 2000. In a separate track, U.S. Attorney for Washington, D.C., Jeanine Pirro said there are "several investigations going on" related to Omar, including one examining her financial disclosures — a claim first reported by Fox News and referenced by the AP.

## A Fight Over Timing and Norms

Omar has framed the search as political retaliation, describing it as an "attempt to target political enemies and weaponize federal law enforcement." "For 10 years, Trump has targeted me with baseless lies and smear campaigns," she said. "This is just the latest escalation in his campaign of political intimidation. I will not be silenced."

Her attorneys sent the Justice Department a letter on October 16 demanding the immediate return of seized property, arguing the search was "neither justified nor lawful" and "vindictive, retributive, and politically motivated," according to [AP reporting](https://apnews.com/article/ilhan-omar-justice-department-investigation-search-42c86bdbd54679e974e32ac4a924b402). A Justice Department spokesperson, Emily Covington, called the letter "riddled with false allegations and inaccuracies" and said the department "complied with all court orders and legal obligations."

The timing has drawn scrutiny from former federal prosecutors. The Justice Department has an informal, longstanding practice of avoiding overt investigative actions involving candidates within roughly 60 days of an election. The search fell well inside that window.

"In my experience, there was always a high level of sensitivity about doing things close to an election cycle," B. Todd Jones, a former Minnesota U.S. Attorney, told the [Minnesota Star Tribune](https://www.startribune.com/ilhan-omar-home-raid-doj/601900281). "But she is a member of Congress and it is unusual for this to happen this close to an election, particularly with the involvement of HSI as compared to the FBI."

The involvement of HSI rather than the FBI — which typically handles criminal probes of sitting members of Congress — signals the inquiry is immigration-related. Former federal prosecutor Doug Kelley told the Star Tribune the circumstances "doesn't pass the smell test for me."

## Political Fallout

The search drew swift condemnation from Democratic leaders. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries said there was "nothing there relative to behavior that should merit this type of extraordinary raid, let alone inside of 30 days from the date of an election," according to [The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/house/6145363-jeffries-ilhan-omar-raid/).

President Donald Trump has said he had "nothing to do" with the search and that it is "really up to the DOJ," while also sharpening his personal attacks on Omar. Asked whether she could face deportation or jail over alleged misstatements, Trump replied, "Well, she could be. She could also go to jail." He has repeatedly denigrated Omar, calling her a "sick, very dishonest person who hates our country."

Omar rejected the president's denial of involvement. "He's got everything to do with it," she told Hayes, according to [MS NOW](https://www.ms.now/news/ilhan-omar-raid-interview-trump).

## What to Watch

The investigation remains in its early stages, and no charges have been filed. The scope of the Minnesota grand jury's work, the fate of the 17 seized items, and whether the financial-disclosures inquiry proceeds as a separate track are all open questions. Whether the probe produces an indictment — or remains a pressure campaign that never yields charges — will shape how the episode is remembered.

For now, Omar is pressing ahead with her reelection bid while preparing to leave the home that federal agents searched. "In my faith teachings, we believe that God doesn't give you more than you can handle," she said. "I think I'm designed uniquely by God to withstand all of this."
