# NYC to Ban Horse-Drawn Carriages by 2028

New York City will phase out its iconic horse-drawn carriages by June 1, 2028, after the City Council voted 40-7 with two abstentions on Thursday to pass "Romanch's Law" — a landmark victory for animal-welfare advocates that now heads toward a likely federal legal battle. The legislation, named for an 18-year-old tourist killed in a Central Park carriage accident over the summer, is veto-proof and bars the issuance of any new carriage licenses.

According to [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/new-york-city-ban-horse-drawn-carriages-2028/story?id=137490461), the bill will prohibit the sale of carriage horses to slaughterhouses and establish a "workforce transition program" for the roughly 150 unionized drivers, offering job placement, training, career counseling and financial compensation.

## The Catalyst

The ban's decisive momentum traces to June, when Romanch Mahajan, an Indian tourist, was killed after a horse got loose with the carriage and his family still inside. Investigators said the carriage clipped the wheel of another carriage and toppled. Mahajan's aunt and uncle were present for Thursday's vote.

City Council Speaker Julie Menin, speaking before the vote, framed the issue as a matter of public safety. "They don't belong in an area that attracts 42 million people a year, pulling a 1,000-pound carriage in record heat," she said.

The Central Park Conservancy, which has pushed for the ban for years, said Mahajan's death "was the ninth carriage-related incident in just over a year — a number we could not ignore as the stewards and caretakers of Central Park." Previous incidents include a January case in which a loose horse crashed into cars.

## A Long-Sought Goal

For more than a decade, animal-welfare groups and park advocates have campaigned to retire the carriages, which are described as a nearly 170-year-old city tradition. Former Mayors Bill de Blasio and Eric Adams both backed a ban during their tenures but lacked sufficient Council support. The push revived sharply after Mahajan's death, culminating in an October agreement between Mayor Zohran Mamdani and the Council speaker to end the rides citywide.

"The horse carriage industry is coming to an end in New York City," Mamdani wrote on X. "Our administration will make sure workers and owners are protected and the horses are safe."

## Union Vows to Sue

The Transport Workers Union, representing the drivers, protested outside City Hall and sent a letter to the city's law department warning it will seek a temporary restraining order "the minute the ink dries."

"We will see them in court," said Alexander Kemp, administrative vice president for TWU Local 100. "This bill is blatantly unconstitutional, providing nothing but a plan to draft a plan. There's nothing concrete for hundreds of workers, carriage owners and family members who face total economic devastation."

At the center of the dispute is the transition program's compensation. While the law's supporters describe it as including financial compensation, the union contends the bill only commits to "consideration" of such support, leaving any payout uncertain. Speaker Menin said the city is prepared to defend the law. "We are very confident in defending this law and will vigorously defend it," she said.

## Wider Implications

The vote carries political weight beyond Central Park. President Donald Trump criticized the ban on Truth Social, calling the carriage a "quintessential New York experience" and lamenting the loss of "another piece of History." Meanwhile, Mayor Mamdani — who has positioned himself as a champion of labor — faces criticism from some drivers who feel betrayed by a pro-union mayor backing a measure they say jeopardizes their livelihoods. Many carriage medallions are held by immigrant-run family businesses passed down through generations, and their value could be wiped out by the phaseout.

## What's Next

The bill now goes to Mamdani for signature. A workforce transition plan is to be developed in 2027, and carriage operations are slated to cease by June 1, 2028. The central question in the months ahead will be whether the courts allow the law to take effect as scheduled — or whether the drivers' threatened federal lawsuit and restraining order freeze the phaseout in its tracks. For now, the city's carriage trade, a fixture of the skyline for generations, is living on borrowed time.
