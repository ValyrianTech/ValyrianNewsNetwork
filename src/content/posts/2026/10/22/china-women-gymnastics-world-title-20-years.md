# China Ends 20-Year Wait for Women's Gymnastics World Title

China's women's gymnastics team won the team title at the 54th Artistic Gymnastics World Championships in Rotterdam on 21 October, ending a 20-year drought and handing the United States its first defeat in the event since 2007. China finished with 165.764 points, edging the Americans by just 0.233 points, with Japan taking bronze, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/sports/20261022/e4107399e719440b8ec548cc07890a13/c.html).

The victory is China's first women's team world title since the 2006 championships in Aarhus, Denmark, and snaps a seven-title winning streak for the United States. Because the top three teams in the final earn automatic berths, China, the U.S. and Japan all secured their places in the women's team event at the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics.

## A debut thrown into chaos before it began

China's final very nearly came apart before the first routine. Vault specialist Zhang Yihan suffered a foot injury, forcing a last-minute lineup change roughly half an hour before the final got under way, per [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34305459). With her planned vault of 5.2 difficulty removed, China fielded two vaults of 5.0 and one of 4.4 — a calculated gamble to salvage as many points as possible from a weakened rotation.

Zhang Qingying, called up as the replacement, said she barely had time to process the moment. "I just ran over, warmed up, and could vault, so I went up. I didn't even have time to get nervous," she said, according to Xinhua. She scored 13.233 on vault and went on to compete all four apparatuses — the only Chinese gymnast to do so on the night.

Chang Cheng, director of the Gymnastics Sports Management Center under the General Administration of Sport of China, said such disruptions had been anticipated. "This kind of situation — we actually had a contingency plan," he said.

## Beam brilliance, floor tension

The team final format offered no margin for error: three gymnasts competed on each apparatus and every score counted, with no dropped routine. China built its victory on the balance beam, where it posted 43.366 in the third rotation to take a 2.200-point lead into the final rotation, Xinhua reported. The American squad, strong on floor exercise, chipped away at that cushion, but China held on to win by the narrowest of margins.

"To fight for every 0.1 point, everyone had to contribute to the team," Chang said. "We did it — all twelve routines [were] completed."

The victory capped a year in which China's core lineup — Du Siyu, Ke Qinqin, Zhang Qingying, Qiu Qiyuan, Zhang Yihan and substitute Qin Xinyi — won three consecutive team titles, from the Asian Championships to the Asian Games to the World Championships. Chang likened the run to "a quiz, a mid-term, a final exam."

Du Siyu, competing in her first full World Championships after previously serving as a substitute, said the gold affirmed her perseverance: "[It] affirms my own persistence."

## A shift in strategy — and an honest scorecard

Behind the title lies a deliberate change in approach. According to Xinhua, China's program has prioritized the team and all-around events over individual specialization during the Los Angeles cycle, under the guiding principle of "team over individual." The squad has poured extra effort into vault and floor exercise, long considered its weak links, and has drawn on judges and international federation personnel to study the rules and sharpen details.

Yet the scoreboard laid bare the work that remains. China ranked first on balance beam but only fifth on vault and sixth on floor exercise among the eight finalists. The 0.233-point margin is both a triumph and a warning.

"Floor exercise difficulty is not enough — this is what we need to improve next," Chang acknowledged, pledging to continue strengthening vault and floor ahead of Los Angeles.

## What's next

The gold delivers confidence and an Olympic ticket, but the competitive picture is not settled. Xinhua noted that several Russian mainstays missed these championships due to visa issues, meaning the field in Los Angeles is likely to be tougher than the one in Rotterdam. A new generation of backup athletes reaches competition age next year, giving China more lineup options, and Chang has promised open, fair selection to draw out talent.

The next phase of China's Olympic preparations will hinge on a familiar question: can a settled, cohesive team convert its beam-and-bars excellence into multi-apparatus strength? In Rotterdam, every tenth of a point mattered. In Los Angeles, China will need a great many more of them.
