# Trump Drops PSLF Legal Fight

The Trump administration has abandoned its legal effort to overhaul the Public Service Loan Forgiveness program, dropping two appeals that sought to revive a contested rule limiting which employers qualify for the benefit. The Department of Justice moved Wednesday to end its fight in single-sentence joint agreements stipulating that each side would pay its own fees, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/public-service-loan-forgiveness-trump-debt-0e25878fbf2b6742131a93260164c63b). Federal officials offered no explanation for the retreat.

## Context: A Program Under Pressure

Congress created Public Service Loan Forgiveness in 2007 to draw college graduates into government and nonprofit work, promising to cancel federal student loans after borrowers made 120 qualifying payments—roughly a decade—while employed full-time in public service. The program serves teachers, firefighters, public hospital employees, government workers, and staff at tax-exempt nonprofits. It has canceled loans for more than 1 million Americans.

In fall 2025, the administration finalized a rule—published October 30 and set to take effect July 1, 2026—empowering Education Secretary Linda McMahon to strip employers of eligibility if their work was deemed to have a "substantial illegal purpose." The vaguely worded standard was widely understood to target organizations supporting undocumented immigrants or transgender youth, and it defined "chemical castration" to include hormone therapy and puberty-delaying drugs.

## The Legal Battle

A coalition of nearly two dozen states, led by New York Attorney General Letitia James, sued in October 2025. Just hours before the rule took effect, two federal judges struck it down. U.S. District Judge Myong Joun in Massachusetts vacated the changes, ruling the agency had overstepped its power and threatened First Amendment protections. He faulted the department for failing to connect its definitions of illegal activity to criminal statutes.

"The Department cannot create new criminal prohibitions through rulemaking," Joun wrote in his June 30 ruling. He also questioned the justification, noting the department's own estimate that fewer than 10 employers would be barred each year: "The Department offers no explanation for why a Final Rule with such sweeping consequences is necessary to address the possibility that, at most, ten employers each year may be engaging in illegal activity."

In Washington, D.C., District Judge Amir Ali issued a similar ruling the same day in a case brought by nonprofit organizations including Student Defense, as [AP News reported](https://apnews.com/article/public-service-loan-forgiveness-trump-debt-5cbe13349bff45bea6ae5fc330d7b617).

The Education Department notified the court in August that it would appeal Joun's decision to the First Circuit Court of Appeals, keeping the vacatur in effect during the proceedings, [NASFAA reported](https://www.nasfaa.org/news-item/39696/ED_Appeals_Court_Decision_That_Struck_Down_PSLF_Final_Rule). That appeal, along with a parallel one, was dropped on Wednesday.

## What It Means

Advocates for borrowers celebrated the retreat. "The Trump administration's attempt to weaponize public service loan forgiveness against organizations it disagrees with was anti-democratic and unlawful from the start," said Dan Zibel, an attorney for Student Defense. "This is a significant victory for millions of Americans who have dedicated their careers to public service."

Because the rule was never in force while the appeals were pending, no employer was ever barred under it. The retreat nonetheless marks a notable legal concession: pursuing the appeals risked a broader appellate ruling that could constrain executive discretion over program eligibility in other contexts.

The administration has not said whether it will try a narrower rule or pursue limits through Congress. Republican supporters of the original overhaul, such as Rep. Tim Walberg of Michigan, framed the effort as protecting taxpayers from subsidizing organizations that break the law—a position that remains on the record even as the legal effort ends. Meanwhile, the Education Department has pursued related priorities through other channels, including restructuring forgiveness programs under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

## What to Watch

For now, PSLF remains intact in its traditional form, preserving relief for public-sector workers and nonprofit employees. Key questions remain: whether the administration will attempt a narrower rule, how the department will manage the roughly 70,000 borrowers in the PSLF pipeline amid staffing changes, and whether congressional Republicans will take up legislative limits on the program.

For the more than a million borrowers who rely on the program—and the thousands still working toward forgiveness—the immediate takeaway is stability. The decade-long promise of public service loan forgiveness, at least for now, still stands.
