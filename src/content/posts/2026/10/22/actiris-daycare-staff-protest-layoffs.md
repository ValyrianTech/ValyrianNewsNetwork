# Actiris Daycare Staff Protest Third Wave of Layoffs

Around thirty staff of the Maison d'enfants d'Actiris, a publicly funded daycare non-profit in Brussels, gathered in the rain, wind and cold on Thursday morning at the foot of the Astro Tower in Saint-Josse-ten-Noode to protest a fresh round of redundancies. According to the unions, nine people are set to be laid off the following week — the third wave of dismissals in a rolling restructuring that has already shrunk the network's workforce and its number of crèches.

The Astro Tower houses both the Actiris regional employment office and one of the association's three remaining daycare centres. The protest, reported by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/saint-josse-des-travailleuses-de-la-creche-maison-d-enfants-d-actiris-manifestent-contre-des-licenciements-11804220), was accompanied by a strike and action notice filed by the joint union front of Setca (FGTB), CNE (CSC) and Synova (CGSLB). All three of the association's remaining crèches closed for the day to allow staff to mobilise.

## A network dismantled "piece by piece"

The dispute is the latest episode in a restructuring that has unfolded over roughly two years. Nadia Azaal, a childcare worker and FGTB delegate, said the workforce has fallen from around 150 people eighteen months ago to 98 today. The association once ran five crèches; two have closed — one in Molenbeek and one on the territory of the City of Brussels — leaving three in Saint-Josse, Molenbeek-Saint-Jean and Anderlecht.

The Maison d'enfants d'Actiris (MDE) is not an ordinary childcare provider. Created on the initiative of the ORBEm — the predecessor of Actiris — and financed by the regional employment office, it cares specifically for children under three whose parents are Brussels job-seekers. It offers occasional drop-in care while parents carry out job-search steps, or temporary three-month places when a parent returns to work. In that sense it functions as both a care provider and an active labour-market instrument, removing childcare as a barrier to employment for vulnerable families.

That dual role is what makes the cuts contentious. Unions argue the restructuring is not merely shedding jobs but eroding a public service designed to help unemployed parents get back into work.

## "Salami-slicing" without a social plan

The union front acknowledges the association's financial difficulties, which are tied to the Actiris budget allocated for 2025. But it contests the manner in which the restructuring is being carried out. In a press release announcing Thursday's action under the banner "stop au démantèlement par vagues" (stop the dismantling by waves), the unions described a structure being taken apart in stages.

"For two years, workers have watched their structure be dismantled piece by piece," the union front said. "They need prospects and stability, not a succession of layoffs without a social plan."

The sharpest point of conflict is the criteria used to decide who loses their job. Unions denounce the exclusive reliance on seniority (ancienneté) and object to management's refusal to weigh other social-protection criteria. They point to the first wave in 2025, when they firmly opposed the dismissal of a pregnant colleague. "Seniority is not a sufficient criterion," a union representative said.

Bahia Fatiha, a Synova delegate, said the cuts keep hitting the same job categories — cooks, childcare workers and cleaners — while coordination posts are reportedly spared. "We would like it to be transparent," she said, calling for a social plan. She described laid-off workers coming in to serve their notice in tears.

For Naima Zarbouh, the CNE's designated confidential counsellor, the damage goes beyond the numbers. "Trust is broken," she said.

Among those still in post, the strain is palpable. "The quality of work is no longer the same," Azaal said, describing the stress and anxiety of remaining staff as colleagues depart.

## The demands

The union front has set out a clear list of demands: an agreement on the criteria for current and future dismissals; the reopening of dialogue on so-called essential jobs (métiers essentiels) and the support team (équipe d'appui); and the opening of a genuine consultation procedure on the future of the MDE and its staff. In practice, that amounts to a social plan guaranteeing transparency.

Unions also question whether the reflection on essential roles goes far enough, arguing that all trades — including the management and support team — should make an extra effort. Their framing echoes a complaint voiced during the earlier June 2025 wave, when ten workers were targeted and unions filed a strike notice over a lack of information and consultation: "always the same who suffer."

## Why it matters beyond one crèche

The MDE dispute sits within a broader squeeze on Brussels childcare capacity. Local reporting has covered the closure of individual crèches and a recognised shortage of childcare places, alongside sector-wide mobilisations over funding. What sets the MDE case apart is its explicit employment-policy dimension: because it is financed by Actiris, the row is ultimately tied to Brussels regional budget choices.

The workforce is overwhelmingly female — the French-language coverage consistently refers to "travailleuses" and "puéricultrices" — and the dismissals fall disproportionately on female-dominated frontline roles. The union critique that coordination posts are shielded while carers, cooks and cleaners bear the burden raises a gender-equity question that resonates well beyond this one employer.

Union appeals to political decision-makers to "take their responsibilities" point squarely at regional employment and early-childhood funding. The dispute thus links a local labour conflict to the wider fiscal pressures bearing down on Brussels public services.

## What to watch

Several questions will determine how the standoff unfolds. First, whether the nine dismissals proceed as planned in the week of 26 October, and whether further industrial action follows if they do. Second, whether management agrees to reopen dialogue on dismissal criteria and a social plan. Third, whether a fourth wave materialises — something unions say everything indicates is coming, warning that "the staff cuts are not finished." And finally, how Actiris and the regional government respond on the long-term financing and future of the three remaining crèches.

For the workers gathered outside the Astro Tower, the demand is simple: as one union statement put it, they "have the right to know their employer's long-term vision." Until that vision is articulated, the protest signals that the conflict is far from resolved.
