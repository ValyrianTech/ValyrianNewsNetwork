# Judge Orders Trump to Testify in BBC Suit by Dec. 11

A federal magistrate judge on Thursday ordered President Donald Trump to sit for a deposition in his $10 billion defamation lawsuit against the BBC by December 11 — or face escalating court sanctions that could include dismissal of the case entirely.

The order from U.S. Magistrate Judge Enjoliqué Lett resolved, at least for now, a months-long standoff in which the British broadcaster accused the president of deliberately stalling his own sworn testimony. The ruling lands in a case that pits a sitting American president against one of the world's most prominent public broadcasters, and it raises a pointed question: can the commander-in-chief be compelled to testify in a civil suit he himself filed?

## What the Judge Decided

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-bbc-media-defamation-0a26fa60b265f55a9bcf7ed88cfacab8), the order requires Trump to be deposed by December 11 or face sanctions up to dismissal of his lawsuit — unless the discovery period, currently set to close December 18, is extended. Lett noted that only the presiding district judge can alter the trial schedule, and the defense signaled it would be open to a later deposition if the broader trial timeline were also moved back.

The dispute over the deposition has grown increasingly sharp. The BBC's lead attorney, Charles Tobin, said that with roughly two months left in the discovery period, "we still haven't been able to depose, and do not even have a schedule to depose, the most central figure in this case: Plaintiff himself."

At the heart of the BBC's complaint is a missed appointment. The two sides had agreed to depose Trump on October 16. On September 28, his counsel told the broadcaster the president "is no longer available for his deposition on October 16 due to other matters of national importance," according to reporting by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2026/oct/13/bbc-trump-lawsuit-deposition). A replacement date never materialized, and the BBC later learned Trump had no availability before December 18 — offering instead only the second or third week of January 2027, well past the discovery deadline.

Tobin disputed the national-importance rationale outright, telling the court that Trump was in fact campaigning in Georgia on the October 16 date. "He was participating in party politics and prioritized that over sitting for the deposition," he said.

## A Long-Brewing Institutional Crisis

The lawsuit, filed in December 2025 in federal court in Florida, stems from a 2024 BBC Panorama documentary, "Trump: A Second Chance." Trump is seeking $10 billion in total — $5 billion for defamation and $5 billion under Florida's Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act, [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/media/trump-threatened-judge-sanctions-he-doesnt-sit-deposition-10-billion-bbc-defamation-suit) reported.

The underlying controversy centers on how the documentary edited Trump's January 6, 2021, speech to supporters before the storming of the U.S. Capitol. The BBC has acknowledged that filmmakers spliced together quotes from different sections of the address to make it appear Trump was directly urging violence. In reality, his "fight like hell" remark came roughly 54 minutes after his call to protest peacefully — an edit that distorted his meaning by juxtaposing the two.

The broadcaster apologized and pulled the program. The fallout was severe: BBC director-general Tim Davie and BBC News CEO Deborah Turness both resigned, an upheaval the New York Times described as "one of the worst crises" in the broadcaster's 103-year history. Yet the BBC denies that the episode amounts to defamation, arguing that an editing error does not support such a claim.

Trump's legal team has offered a sharply different characterization. "The formerly respected and now disgraced BBC defamed President Trump by intentionally, maliciously, and deceptively doctoring his speech in a brazen attempt to interfere in the 2024 Presidential Election," a spokesperson said, describing the suit as holding the broadcaster "accountable for its defamation and reckless election interference."

## The Jack Smith Dispute

A parallel discovery fight concerns a BBC subpoena of former special counsel Jack Smith, whom the broadcaster wants to testify about the January 6 events. Trump's lawyers have argued that Smith "lacks any personal knowledge regarding the issues in this matter" and that his testimony would be duplicative given his lengthy public report and prior testimony.

The BBC countered that few figures are better positioned to speak to the events. "It is difficult to imagine a more authoritative source on these issues than Mr. Smith, who spent years gathering evidence on those topics with the force of the Justice Department and the power of the grand jury behind him," its lawyers wrote.

## What It Means

The significance of Thursday's order lies in a simple legal reality: because Trump sued the BBC in his personal capacity, he is the plaintiff in an ordinary civil action and is subject to civil discovery rules like any other litigant. His status as sitting president is a logistical complication — involving security and scheduling — rather than a legal shield. Courts have broad authority to sanction parties who defy discovery orders, from monetary penalties to adverse-inference instructions to outright dismissal.

That last possibility carries real weight. A dismissal would end Trump's lawsuit against the BBC altogether — a considerable downside for the plaintiff in a case he chose to bring. The deposition order also echoes a pattern the BBC has sought to highlight: Trump has often avoided sworn testimony, most notably in 2023 when he voluntarily dropped a $500 million lawsuit against former lawyer Michael Cohen days before he was set to be deposed in that case.

The case also fits a broader pattern of Trump litigation against major media outlets, which critics view as pressure tactics and supporters cast as accountability for what the president calls "fake news."

## What to Watch

Several questions remain unresolved. The most immediate is whether Trump will actually appear by December 11 — his team has resisted for months, and the court has now made clear that further delay carries consequences. It also remains unclear whether the discovery deadline of December 18 will be extended, a move that would require the intervention of the presiding district judge.

Also pending is the fate of the Jack Smith subpoena, which the court was expected to address at the same Thursday hearing. And beneath the procedural skirmishes lies the central unresolved question on the merits: whether an acknowledged editing error rises to actionable defamation under Florida law — and whether Trump can prove the actual malice such a claim requires.

For now, the clock is running. The president has until December 11 to sit for questions under oath, or risk seeing the very lawsuit he filed dismissed from the docket.
