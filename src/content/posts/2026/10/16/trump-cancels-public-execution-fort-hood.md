# Trump Cancels Public Execution at Fort Hood, But a Line May Already Have Been Crossed

President Donald Trump on Thursday halted a plan to livestream the firing-squad execution of Nidal Hasan, the former U.S. Army major convicted of killing 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009. The execution will still proceed as scheduled on December 3, but it will no longer be broadcast publicly — ending a week-long controversy that critics say revealed how far officials were willing to push the boundaries of military justice.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/15/nx-s1-6001794/fort-hood-execution-hegseth-christian-nationalist), the reversal followed an intense public backlash, and an anonymous U.S. official said Trump was persuaded that such an event would run counter to American values and could fan recruitment for radical Islamist terrorist groups, further putting U.S. forces in the Middle East at risk.

In a post on Truth Social, Trump argued that broadcasting the execution "might have been a strong deterrent for the World to witness how America deals with Terrorists and SCUM like Hasan," but said that "many very good and well meaning people would rather not watch him die, through streaming, or through any other visual device." He added that the execution "may be viewed in person by the immediate families of the victims, if they so choose."

## How the Plan Unfolded

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced the public execution plan on October 8 in an interview with Real America's Voice, a right-wing streaming network, saying he wanted people to "understand that there's serious consequences for these types of things." Hasan, he said, "is going to get a firing squad of soldiers — as it should be."

The announcement drew immediate and sweeping condemnation from lawmakers in both parties, religious leaders, and international bodies. The plan, as [NPR detailed](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/09/nx-s1-5996529/firing-squad-livestream-execution-fort-hood-shooter), would have been the first public execution in the United States in nearly a century and the first ever to be livestreamed.

Vice President JD Vance appeared to contradict Hegseth within a day, telling reporters he doubted the livestream would happen at all. "I don't know that that's actually going to happen," Vance said, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/trump-administration/vance-nidal-hasan-execution-livestream-hegseth-rcna602551). "I'm not going to be watching it."

Trump's own position shifted through the week — approving the plan on October 9, saying he had not made a final decision on October 10, and finally canceling it on October 15. The Pentagon said Trump and Hegseth spoke about the decision not to livestream and that Hegseth "supports the President's decision, and they are completely aligned."

## A Rare Execution, and a Divisive One

Hasan, a former Army psychiatrist, was sentenced to death by a military court in 2013. He is paralyzed from the waist down after being shot by law enforcement during the attack and now uses a wheelchair.

His would be the first military execution in more than 60 years. The U.S. military last executed someone in 1961, when Army Pvt. John Bennett was hanged, and last carried out a firing-squad execution in 1945, when Pvt. Eddie Slovik was put to death for desertion during World War II, as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/07/nx-s1-5993539/firing-squad-execution-nidal-hasan-fort-hood).

The last high-profile public execution in the U.S. was the 1936 hanging of Rainey Bethea in Owensboro, Kentucky, which drew roughly 20,000 spectators and a festive atmosphere that historians say helped end the practice in America.

For some, the proposal raised a deeper question about why Hasan — a Muslim — was being singled out for a form of punishment not applied to others.

"The question is, why is he being punished differently than everyone before him?" said Joshua Salaam, president of the Association of Muslim Chaplains. "And the answer that most people from my community will say is it has something to do with his religious beliefs, his religious identity."

## Questions About Religion and the Pentagon

The episode drew scrutiny to Hegseth's religious background and its influence on military policy. Hegseth affiliates with the Communion of Reformed Evangelical Churches, or CREC, whose de facto leader openly identifies as a Christian nationalist. According to reporting [from The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/15/pete-hegseth-crec-church-execution-livestream), church allies backed the plan for a public execution.

Julie Ingersoll, a professor of religion at the University of North Florida, told NPR that within Hegseth's church, support for public execution is not an outlier. "The crimes discussed in the Hebrew Bible or the Old Testament that carried the death penalty are understood by them to still carry the death penalty," she said. "So execution, and even public execution, is very much a part of the way they see the punishment for crime in general."

Ingersoll said the most troubling aspect of the episode is what it normalizes. "Just saying it out loud moves the boundary," she said.

Matthew Taylor, a visiting scholar at Georgetown University's Center on Faith and Justice, was blunter. "Pete Hegseth is a religious extremist," he said. "He holds ideas that are far outside the mainstream. He is trying to implement those ideas within the U.S. military structure within the Pentagon."

## A Global Backlash

The plan provoked condemnation far beyond Washington. Cardinal Pietro Parolin, the Vatican secretary of state, said turning death into a spectacle was unacceptable, as [Vatican News reported](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/vatican-city/news/2026-10/cardinal-parolin-comments-usa-public-execution-announcement.html). "However guilty the person may be, there are other ways," he said.

Archbishop Paul Coakley, president of the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops, called the plan "a barbaric promotion of a culture of death" in a [statement](https://www.usccb.org/news/2026/usccb-president-condemns-plans-livestream-fort-hood-shooters-execution-barbaric-promotion) and urged reconsideration. The United Nations' human rights office warned that broadcasting the killing would amount to a public execution and breach the prohibition on torture.

Republican senators, including Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis and John Curtis, joined Democrats in objecting — a rare bipartisan rebuke that unfolded with the November midterm elections approaching. "Broadcasting a death diminishes us and is not an effective deterrent," Curtis wrote.

## What to Watch

With the livestream canceled, the December 3 execution is set to proceed out of public view, though legal experts note Hasan could still file appeals that delay it by years.

Yet the deeper controversy may not be over. Analysts say the episode demonstrated how quickly an extreme idea can move from the fringe into official consideration — and how that shift, once made, is difficult to reverse.

As Ingersoll put it, the damage was done the moment the plan was announced. Whether the execution is streamed or not, the debate over the politicization of military justice, and over the role of religious ideology in the Pentagon, is far from settled.
