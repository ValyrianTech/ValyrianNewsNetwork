# Belgium to Cap Flexi-Jobs for Long-Term Sick at 6 Months

Belgian Health Minister Frank Vandenbroucke has proposed limiting the ability of people on long-term sick leave to hold a "flexi-job" to a maximum of six months, but the plan has immediately exposed a rift within the federal coalition, with partner CD&V demanding the practice be banned outright.

The advisory opinion, first reported by Het Laatste Nieuws and confirmed by the minister's spokesperson to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/16/vandenbroucke-langdurig-zieken-maximaal-6-maanden-flexi-job/), is not yet a formal decision. Under the proposal, someone off sick would be barred from taking a flexi-job entirely during their first month of absence.

## What the Proposal Says

A flexi-job is a form of flexible work that allows a person to earn extra income on top of an existing job or pension, with favourable tax and social treatment. To qualify, a worker must generally already be employed at least four-fifths of the time, or be retired.

People on long-term sick leave are not automatically entitled to a flexi-job. Anyone receiving a sickness benefit may only work if it is medically justified and if the advisory doctor of their health insurance fund gives permission. The advisory doctor does not currently assess the type of contract involved.

Vandenbroucke argues that a temporary flexi-job during a period of incapacity should remain an option, describing it as "a useful first step" to guide people back to work. "It can also give the person in question energy, because you come back among people and feel useful," he said. "That will only promote recovery."

## Coalition Partner Pushes for a Full Ban

For coalition partner CD&V, the proposal does not go far enough. The Christian democrats want flexi-jobs for the long-term sick scrapped altogether, and MP Nathalie Muylle filed a bill to that effect several months ago.

Muylle's central argument is that flexi-jobs undermine sustainable reintegration into the labour market. "We see in the story of the long-term sick that sustainable employment remains a very big pain point," she said. "Allowing a flexi-job contributes absolutely nothing to that — quite the opposite. A flexi-job is often fleeting and temporary, without career-progression prospects."

Writing on [her official website](https://www.nathaliemuylle.be/een_ziekte_uitkering_en_flexi_jobben_gaan_niet_samen), Muylle framed the issue as one of fairness and motivation. She offered the example of a small business where a colleague has been absent for months: "To then see that the sick colleague in question is selling bread in the bakery on Sunday — that really rankles with the people who have to cover the absence."

She also argued that the current system blunts the incentive to return to full-time work. "We see today people who are placed on 80% invalidity and 20% flexi. In this situation one keeps 100% of the benefit plus the untaxed flexi-job on top. Why would one go back to work more?"

CD&V draws a distinction in its own proposal: someone who already had a flexi-job before becoming ill could keep doing it in a limited way, without increasing their hours, while anyone wishing to start one during incapacity would no longer be permitted. That mirrors existing rules for combining a flexi-job with leave schemes such as parental leave.

According to CD&V, slightly more than 1,300 people in Belgium currently combine a sickness or unemployment benefit with a flexi-job. Muylle warned that the number will only grow, especially as the government prepares to expand flexi-jobs to all sectors. "We risk opening the floodgates," she said.

## Budgetary and Broader Context

Muylle called Vandenbroucke's opinion "difficult to understand in today's budgetary context." Reducing the billions spent on sickness benefits is a priority for the federal coalition, and Vandenbroucke has said long-term savings could run into hundreds of millions of euros for social security.

The flexi-job debate is one element of a wider package of reforms around long-term sickness. In June, the government approved plans requiring the long-term sick to be assessed by a doctor every year to obtain a new certificate of incapacity, a measure Vandenbroucke described as an "important step" in the broader "Back to Work" programme. The government hopes to reduce the number of people on invalidity by 100,000 by 2030.

The scale of the challenge is significant. More than 570,000 people in Belgium were on invalidity leave due to long-term illness last year, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/06/16/langdurig-zieken-arts-attest-jaar/). Vandenbroucke has previously argued that the assessment of a person's remaining work potential "must start from a blank sheet."

Not everyone agrees with the emphasis on checks. Bart Dhondt, chair of the opposition Greens (Groen), said he agreed that long-term illness is a major problem but criticised the minister's approach. "The only answer I hear from Minister Vandenbroucke today is ramping up checks on those who are sick," he said. "About the cause of the problem — namely that our workplace today makes people sick — I hear nothing."

## What to Watch

The proposal remains an advisory opinion rather than settled policy, and its fate will depend on how the coalition resolves the divide between Vooruit and CD&V. The expansion of flexi-jobs to all sectors — a separate government commitment — will intensify the debate, since critics argue it will make the loophole harder to police. Whether Belgium opts for a six-month cap or an outright ban will be an early test of how far this coalition is willing to go in reforming one of its most expensive social security challenges.
