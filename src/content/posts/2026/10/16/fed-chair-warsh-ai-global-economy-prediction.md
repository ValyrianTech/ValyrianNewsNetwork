# Fed Chair Warns AI Transition Will Be 'Very Bumpy'

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh described artificial intelligence on Friday as "probably the most consequential change to the U.S. and the global economy in my lifetime to this point," predicting the technology will remake how central banks set interest rates while warning that the road to those gains will be turbulent. Speaking at the IMF-World Bank Annual Meetings in Bangkok, Warsh also declined to signal the Fed's next policy move, underscoring what he called "a new chapter" at the central bank.

The remarks, delivered during a fireside chat with International Monetary Fund Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, add a powerful voice to the escalating global debate over how AI will reshape jobs, productivity and price stability.

## AI as Both a Shock and a Windfall

Warsh framed AI as a near-term disruption that would eventually deliver lasting economic benefits. According to [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/live-news/ai-use-spacex-official-testify-nyc-after-subpoena), he said the technology is "obviously hitting the demand side of the economy before it's going to hit the supply side," adding that "it's a positive supply shock in the medium term. I do fundamentally believe that."

He cautioned that the adjustment would not be smooth. "The path from where we are to there is likely to be a very bumpy one, bumpy in our understanding of the technology, bumpy because we really don't know what the market structure is going to be of these new technologies," Warsh said.

Warsh said AI's effect on employment over the next 12 to 18 months remains uncertain, even as he predicted the technology would make economies that "put it to good use more productive" and generate greater wealth.

## A New Era for Monetary Policy

Beyond its impact on growth, Warsh argued that AI would fundamentally change how central banks operate. He described the current process of sifting through economic data as a form of torture and predicted that AI tools would soon give policymakers instant access to global information.

"Not so long from now, it won't be torture. We will have access at the fingertips of central banks to all the information in the world, and we'll be able to use new analytical tools to distill that data... So I think it's going to fundamentally change the conduct of monetary policy," he said.

## No Signal on Interest Rates

Warsh's appearance was closely watched for any hint about the Fed's October decision, but he offered none. "I don't prejudge meetings. I don't believe in saying now what I might do at the meetings that we have in the balance of this year," he said, according to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/16/feds-warsh-says-ai-development-path-will-be-very-bumpy-.html).

The stance reflects Warsh's long-standing skepticism toward forward guidance, a tool he has argued allows markets to lean on the central bank rather than interpret economic data. "It's a new chapter at the Fed [and] we're not in that business anymore," he said.

Investors nonetheless drew their own conclusions. Following the speech, traders priced a roughly 72% chance that the Fed would hold rates steady at its Oct. 27-28 meeting, with a majority expecting a 25 basis point hike in December, according to CME FedWatch data cited by CNBC.

Goldman Sachs economists led by Jan Hatzius wrote that Warsh's comments "indicate that the FOMC is set to pause in October," adding that they "continue to expect a second and final hike in December." S&P 500 and Nasdaq futures edged higher after the remarks, while Asian stock indexes and Treasury yields were largely stable.

## Synchronous Global Tightening

Warsh took over as Fed chair in May 2026 and has pledged that the central bank will deliver price stability. In September, the Fed raised rates to a range of 3.75% to 4% — its first hike since 2023.

The move came amid an inflation resurgence driven in part by an energy shock following the U.S.-led war on Iran launched in February, which sent world oil and gas prices soaring. The simultaneous surge in AI investment has added further pressure to prices across major economies.

Warsh characterized the global response as a coordinated effort. "If I look back over the last few months, while we each did the right thing by our own judgment, by our own remit, it was a synchronous tightening in monetary policy in most places around the world," he said, per [The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/asia/se-asia/ai-most-consequential-change-to-economy-in-my-lifetime-feds-kevin-warsh). "And that means there's no free-riders."

He noted that most wealthy nations, including those behind the European Central Bank, have begun raising rates in response to surging inflation. The Fed, however, has failed to meet its long-term 2% inflation goal for more than five years. U.S. inflation rose to 3.7% in September 2026, surpassing forecasts.

## Optimism, Tempered

Despite the sobering inflation picture, Warsh struck a cautiously hopeful tone about the broader economy. He said the global economy had shown "resilience I'm not sure I would have predicted five or six months ago" and described the ongoing investment surge as a major positive force.

"It's probably the most important thing that's happening in the global economy on the upside," he said, according to [The Economic Times](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/world-news/ai-most-consequential-change-to-us-and-global-economies-in-my-lifetime-fed-chair-kevin-warsh/articleshow/134981818.cms).

Still, he acknowledged shared anxiety among his peers. "It's hard to be in a discussion with central bankers and not compare notes and hear the same thing about the surge in prices. It obviously affects each of us differently," Warsh said.

The Fed chair also offered a candid reflection on his own risk bias, telling the audience, "I might be over-scared from global financial crisis," a reference to his experience during the 2008 meltdown.

## What to Watch

Warsh's framing sets up a delicate balancing act. On one side sits persistent inflation, fueled by war-driven energy costs and an AI-driven investment boom; on the other, the promise of a productivity revolution that could eventually ease price pressures. The Fed must navigate that tension while the White House has openly criticized its tightening path — White House senior counselor Peter Navarro previously called Warsh's September rate hike a "rookie mistake."

Compounding the challenge is a global debt backdrop that is on pace to exceed 100% of world GDP, a level last breached after World War II, even as the U.S. dismisses IMF warnings about relying on growth alone to manage its obligations.

Warsh's message to markets was one of measured conviction. "I want you to hear from me optimism, but optimism leavened by the seriousness of purpose," he said. Whether that optimism holds will depend less on the technology itself than on how quickly economies — and the institutions that govern them — can adapt to a shock that has only just begun.
