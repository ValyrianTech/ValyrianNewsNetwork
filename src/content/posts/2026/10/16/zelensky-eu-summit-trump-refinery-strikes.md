# Zelensky Woos Europe as Trump Turns Up the Heat

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky appealed directly to European Union leaders in Brussels on Friday for at least €70 billion in additional military and financial support for 2027, while urging the bloc to unlock hundreds of billions in frozen Russian state assets before Moscow can reclaim them.

His visit to a two-day European Council summit on 15-16 October unfolded against a sharply deteriorating relationship with Washington, after US President Donald Trump condemned Ukraine's renewed strikes on Russian oil refineries as "a disgrace" and said he found Zelensky harder to deal with than Russian President Vladimir Putin.

## A Summit Framed by a Widening Transatlantic Rift

Zelensky entered the Council chamber to applause, a gesture EU leaders used to signal solidarity amid his bitter public row with Trump. Standing alongside him, European Council President António Costa said Ukraine could "count on us not only to support you in war" but also on the path to negotiating EU membership, as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/16/trump-calls-ukraines-resumption-of-strikes-on-russian-oil-refineries-a-disgrace) reported.

The Ukrainian leader said he had received "support and only support" from the leaders he met, framing the summit as a critical lifeline at a moment when US backing appears increasingly uncertain.

"I defend the interests of Ukraine and the Ukrainian people exclusively. We were attacked, occupied and our people were killed. It is only logical that we have the right to defend ourselves," Zelensky said at a press conference, according to [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/16/eu-summit-leaders-to-rally-behind-zelenskyy-amid-bitter-row-with-trump).

## The €70 Billion Ask

In his address to leaders, Zelensky said the war will cost Ukraine €155 billion next year and that Kyiv needs at least €70 billion more from Europe for defence, weapons and resilience. The EU is already rolling out a €90 billion support loan across 2026-2027 — a package Zelensky said is no longer sufficient.

"Right now, we could produce two or three times more drones — depending on the type. But we simply don't have enough money for that," he told leaders, adding: "Please, invest in defence, in our factories in Ukraine, and of course — in our joint production of drones and other weapons in your countries."

The request lands at an awkward moment for the bloc, which is grappling with rising debt and borrowing costs. Kyiv has flagged a shortfall of roughly $78 billion (about €70 billion) for 2027 combining military and financial needs, per Euronews.

## The Frozen Assets Dilemma

Central to the funding debate is roughly €193-200 billion in immobilised Russian central bank reserves, the bulk of it held at the Brussels-based clearing house Euroclear. In December 2025, the EU opted to borrow €90 billion on capital markets rather than seize the assets directly, partly because Belgium — which hosts Euroclear — feared legal claims and reputational risk.

Zelensky pressed the case again on Friday, warning that inaction would hand Moscow a victory.

"We must ensure that all assets are fully protected and can actually be used," he said, per [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/zelensky-tegen-europese-regeringsleiders-moeten-geblokkeerde-ru~1789060001669/). "And believe me: if Europe does not take this step, Putin will find a way to take that money."

Belgium's position has not moved. Prime Minister Bart De Wever remains the most prominent opponent of using the assets directly, and a meeting with Zelensky on the summit's eve produced no breakthrough, with neither side mentioning the issue publicly, the [Kyiv Independent](https://kyivindependent.com/zelensky-meets-belgian-prime-minister-de-wever-but-silence-reigns-on-russian-assets/) reported.

The politics are complicated further by a US proposal, reported by the Financial Times, to transfer a share of the assets to American jurisdiction as an incentive for Moscow to engage in peace talks. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz rejected the idea outright. "These are assets frozen in Europe and they will remain frozen in Europe. That is our issue, we are the ones who decide," he said, according to VRT NWS.

## Trump Turns the Screw

The summit's backdrop was a transatlantic relationship in open disrepair. In an interview with Newsmax that aired on the summit's eve, Trump lashed out at Ukraine's decision to resume strikes on Russian refineries.

"All he's doing is making the world oil price higher. Well, I think that's a disgrace," Trump said. "I'm not a big fan of Zelenskyy. I think they actually both would like to see it end, but I think I find Zelenskyy to be more difficult to deal with than Putin."

Ukraine resumed the strikes after concluding that Russia had continued attacking its energy infrastructure despite an "energy ceasefire" Trump unilaterally announced on 11 October — a truce neither belligerent recognised. Zelensky said the announcement "came as news to me."

Trump's criticism is bound up with a diesel deal announced around 10 October, in which the US eased sanctions on Russia in exchange for Moscow releasing diesel onto global markets — widely seen as an attempt to curb US fuel prices before the 3 November midterm elections. The [Moscow Times](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2026/10/16/russia-repels-644-ukrainian-drones-as-trump-calls-attacks-on-russian-refineries-disgrace-a93997) reported that Russia's Defence Ministry said it intercepted 644 Ukrainian drones over 26 regions and annexed Crimea overnight, as the tit-for-tat strikes intensified.

Zelensky warned the diesel deal could be the first of many concessions. "We cannot accept these diesel deals, these agreements, because gas deals will follow. I am absolutely certain of this. After gas, sanctions will be lifted on other things as well," he said.

## Fraying Military Cooperation

Relations were strained further this week when Ukraine said its Starlink service had been suddenly throttled, slowing drones above roughly 160 km/h and disrupting missions. The service was later reported restored. Zelensky called it a "dangerous and unpleasant surprise" and said he felt "betrayed" by Trump.

EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas acknowledged Europe's limited room to manoeuvre. "Starlink is really necessary for Ukraine. And unfortunately, in the short term, Europe can't replace this capability," she said, per Euronews, adding that the coming winter in Ukraine would be "really grave."

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen promised a 22nd round of sanctions against Russia and insisted the bloc would not relent. "We will not lift any sanctions unless Russia stops the war," she said.

## Signs of Movement — and Reasons for Caution

Not all the news was bleak for Kyiv. Hungary's new prime minister, Péter Magyar, who succeeded Viktor Orbán, held his first bilateral meeting with Zelensky and expressed "full solidarity" with Ukraine, signalling a reset after years of tension. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk offered an emphatic message online: "YOU ARE NOT ALONE."

But the summit also exposed Europe's limits. The migration debate consumed much of the second day, with Denmark, Italy and Poland pushing amendments critical of Spain's migration policy — a reminder that the bloc's internal divisions can crowd out external priorities even at a summit billed around Ukraine.

Analysts caution against reading too much into the warm words. "Zelensky feels the EU is gradually his last lifeline," Steven Van Hecke, professor of European politics, told VRT NWS. "The Russians are escalating... Trump is also turning away from him and choosing the side of the Russians. There's a shortage of money... Then those Russian assets are just sitting there glistening."

## What to Watch

The coming weeks will test whether Europe can convert expressions of solidarity into hard commitments. Two questions loom: whether member states can overcome Belgium's resistance to unlocking the frozen assets, and whether the €70 billion request can survive a budget fight already straining net contributors such as Sweden.

Meanwhile, the transatlantic rift shows no sign of closing. With US midterms days away and Trump openly siding against Kyiv's tactics, Zelensky's strategy increasingly rests on a single bet — that Europe, however reluctantly, will fill the gap. "We will keep living, fighting, and most importantly, living with dignity," he said.
