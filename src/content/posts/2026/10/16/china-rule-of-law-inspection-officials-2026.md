# China Launches Rule-of-Law Inspection of Officials

China's Office of the Central Commission for Comprehensive Law-Based Governance has launched a new rule-of-law inspection targeting whether leading officials comply with legal procedures in their work, the [Ministry of Justice](https://epaper.gmw.cn/gmrb/html/content/202610/16/content_27446.html) announced. Approved by the Party Central Committee, the campaign deploys eight inspection teams to conduct 10-day on-site reviews across eight provincial-level regions—Hebei, Heilongjiang, Fujian, Shandong, Tibet, Shaanxi, Qinghai and Xinjiang—as well as the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps.

The move is the latest stage in Beijing's broader effort to strengthen the rule of law, curb arbitrary decision-making and hold officials accountable for lawful governance.

## Targeting Enterprise-Related Enforcement

According to [People's Daily Online](http://society.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1016/c1008-40815715.html), the inspections will be "problem-oriented," focusing on four key areas: leading officials' law-based decision-making, the regulation of enterprise-related law enforcement, the formulation and application of administrative normative documents, and the governance of investment promotion.

The teams are instructed to root out a set of chronic abuses: arbitrary fees, inspections, fines and seizures, as well as irregular cross-jurisdiction law enforcement and profit-seeking enforcement. The stated aim is to push officials at all levels to adopt a "correct view of political achievements" and to perform their duties in accordance with the law.

Each inspection team is led by a ministry-level cadre drawn from central Party and state organs, and includes business specialists from relevant units and localities, experts and scholars, National People's Congress deputies and Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference members. The teams are required to comply strictly with the Central Eight-Point Regulation—the Party's 2012 austerity and conduct rules—and with the leadership's campaign to reduce formalism and ease burdens on grassroots officials.

## Building on a 2024 Precedent

The 2026 round extends a template first tested in 2024. That September, the central law-based governance office dispatched six teams to six provinces—Anhui, Hunan, Guangxi, Sichuan, Gansu and Ningxia—for seven-day reviews, according to [NBD](https://m.nbd.com.cn/articles/2024-09-22/3565325.html), which reported at the time that it was the first comprehensive rule-of-law inspection since the 20th Party Congress. That earlier campaign focused broadly on implementing Xi Jinping Thought on the Rule of Law and on the responsibilities of the "key minority" of leading officials.

The 2026 version expands the geographic scope to eight regions plus the corps and sharpens the focus squarely on officials' personal compliance—a shift from institutional reform tasks toward individual accountability.

## An Economic Dimension

The inspection's emphasis on enterprise-related law enforcement connects it to a campaign launched in March 2025 to curb abusive enforcement practices harming businesses. A [Xinhua explainer](https://news.youth.cn/gn/202602/t20260226_16527978.htm) on the 15th Five-Year Plan recommendations described a long-term mechanism to "lawfully safeguard personal rights, property rights and personality rights" and to prevent "irregular cross-jurisdiction and profit-seeking law enforcement."

The explainer singled out leadership as the decisive factor, stating that building a standardized long-term mechanism requires "capturing the 'key minority' of leading officials" and strengthening "supervision and inspection of leading officials' law-abiding conduct." The goal, it said, is to create a "market-oriented, law-based, internationalized first-class business environment"—language that ties rule-of-law discipline directly to China's economic priorities.

The [Central Commission for Discipline Inspection](https://www.ccdi.gov.cn/toutiaon/202602/t20260226_476303.html) reported that in 2025 authorities investigated and corrected nearly 57,000 cases of enterprise-related administrative enforcement problems and that national fiscal revenue from fines declined 8.2 percent year-on-year. Spotlighting the scale of prior enforcement abuses, the commission cited provinces such as Shandong, where 1,772 officials received Party and public disciplinary sanctions and 140 cases were transferred to prosecutors.

## The 'Key Minority' Doctrine

The inspection flows from a doctrinal framework in which state law and internal Party regulations are treated as complementary. A [theoretical article](https://www.hezheng.gov.cn/hzx/zwdt/ywzz/art/2026/art_b3ca16cf40134ca7bb87480fd79c17a7.html) republished by the Hezheng County government recounts Xi Jinping's Jan. 12, 2026 address to the 20th Central Commission for Discipline Inspection's fifth plenary session, in which he said that "no one has privilege before regulations and there are no exceptions in executing them," and that "top officials must take the lead in executing regulations."

The article noted that the 2025 edition of the study outline for Xi Jinping Thought on the Rule of Law added "adherence to the organic unity of law-based governance and rule-based Party governance" as a twelfth core tenet—an explicit elevation of the principle underpinning the inspections.

## What's Next

With eight teams now in the field, observers will watch whether the inspections yield publicly disclosed findings and corrective measures, as the 2024 round did. The campaign's dual signal—disciplining officials while reassuring businesses—reflects Beijing's effort to present rule-of-law enforcement as both an accountability tool and an economic stabilizer. Whether the 10-day reviews translate into durable changes in local enforcement behavior, rather than periodic campaigns, remains the central open question.
