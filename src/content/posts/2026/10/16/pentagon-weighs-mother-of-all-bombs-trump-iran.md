# Pentagon Weighs 'Mother of All Bombs' as Trump Eyes Iran

The U.S. military has drawn up new strike plans against Iran that could include the use of the GBU-43/B Massive Ordnance Air Blast bomb — the largest non-nuclear weapon in the American arsenal, nicknamed the "mother of all bombs" — according to multiple U.S. officials cited by CNN. President Donald Trump moved swiftly to rule out nuclear weapons the same day, while confirming that Iran's deeply buried Pickaxe Mountain nuclear site is under consideration for attack.

Speaking to Newsmax on Thursday, Trump stressed that the MOAB is a conventional weapon. "The mother of all bombs is not nuclear," he said, according to [Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/live-news/iran-war-trump-strait-hormuz-oil-prices-midterms-october-16). "This is just an extremely large bomb. It's like a tiny nuclear bomb, but the mother of all bombs is one big bomb, but it's not nuclear." He added bluntly: "I wouldn't use nuclear. We don't have to."

Trump also named Pickaxe Mountain — known in Iran as Kolang Kouh — as a possible target, telling the interviewer, "We may have to go there. We'll take a shot at that one. We'll knock it out fast." He ruled out deploying U.S. ground troops to the country: "No, I don't see that."

## What the reporting suggests

The strike planning, first reported by CNN, comes as officials suggested operations could unfold either before or after the Nov. 3 midterm elections. A source familiar with the discussions told CNN that strike packages include "a lot of energy infrastructure" alongside the potential MOAB use.

Yet the same reporting raises doubts about what such a strike would achieve. Officials told CNN that the MOAB is unlikely to destroy Iran's nuclear capability, with some describing its use as a "psychological operation," according to [Antiwar.com](https://news.antiwar.com/2026/10/15/us-considers-using-mother-of-all-bombs-against-iran/). One planning source put it starkly: "POTUS has unfortunately put himself in a situation where he is almost compelled to do something... you have to look at psychological effects for which the MOAB is well-equipped."

## A weapon built for spectacle, not depth

The MOAB, first manufactured between 2002 and 2003, contains roughly 8,500 kilograms (18,000 pounds) of high explosives and weighs about 9,800 kilograms. It is deployed from a C-130 Hercules cargo aircraft because it cannot fit inside a stealth bomber — a limitation that leaves any delivery aircraft potentially exposed to Iranian air defenses.

As [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/15/what-is-the-mother-of-all-bombs-us-could-use-in-iran) explained, the weapon is designed to explode a few feet above the ground, sending out lethal blast waves capable of destroying everything above ground within roughly a one-kilometer radius. It has been used operationally only once — against an Islamic State cave complex in Afghanistan's Nangarhar province in 2017 — a strike that local reports said destroyed about 30 civilian homes.

The critical problem is that the MOAB is a surface-blast weapon, not a deep penetrator. Iran's most sensitive nuclear sites, including Fordow and the under-construction Pickaxe Mountain, are buried deep inside mountains. The U.S. military's most powerful bunker buster, the GBU-57 Massive Ordnance Penetrator, can plow through at least 60 meters of earth before detonating, but even that may not reach a facility as deep as Pickaxe Mountain.

Military expert Elijah Magnier told Al Jazeera that a MOAB strike would not guarantee destruction of Iran's underground installations. "If the answer centres on the spectacle of the explosion, the proposal is stronger as political theatre than as military strategy," he said. Colonel Nidal Abu Zeid, also speaking to Al Jazeera, said the blast "will destroy everything above ground within a 1km radius" — but that is a different objective from eliminating a buried enrichment hall.

## Pickaxe Mountain: the target at the center

Pickaxe Mountain sits on the south side of the Natanz enrichment complex, about 225 kilometers south of Tehran. Construction began in 2020, after repeated sabotage attacks on Natanz, and experts assess the facility is buried 80 to 100 meters underground, according to [Channel NewsAsia](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/world/iran-us-war-pickaxe-mountain-underground-nuclear-6270226). Protected by anti-aircraft batteries, fencing and Iran's Revolutionary Guard, the sprawling site has never been inspected by the IAEA and has never been bombed.

Iran denies any nuclear activity at the site. Foreign ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baqaei has called Washington's focus on Kolang Kouh "nothing more than a fabricated pretext for aggression, destruction, and sabotage." Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian, meanwhile, insisted Friday that Tehran is not seeking nuclear weapons. "They think they can force us to surrender, but we will not submit to coercion," he said, as reported by Fox News.

Trump framed the confrontation in existential terms. "We want to make it so that they will never have a nuclear weapon, because if they had it, they would use it, in my opinion, instantaneously," he said, as [The Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/middle-east/iran-news/article-911901) reported. He nonetheless expressed optimism the conflict would end soon, saying Iran was "negotiating" and had "asked us to please stop."

## A wider war already underway

The nuclear debate unfolds against a conflict that has already reshaped the region. The 2026 Iran war began on Feb. 28 when the United States and Israel attacked Iran, killing Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and targeting nuclear and military infrastructure. Iran retaliated against U.S. bases across the Gulf and struck energy and civilian infrastructure, while effectively closing the Strait of Hormuz, according to the [Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/global-conflict-tracker/conflict/confrontation-between-united-states-and-iran).

The human and economic costs have been severe. The Human Rights Activists News Agency has documented 1,701 civilian deaths in Iran, a U.S. strike on an elementary school killed at least 175 people, and up to 3.2 million Iranians have been displaced. Thirteen U.S. service members have been killed. A Pakistan-mediated interim deal in June reopened the strait and halted large-scale fighting for 60 days, but both sides have accused each other of violations.

Attacks at sea continue. The UK Maritime Trade Operations center reported Friday that 12 ships were attacked around the Strait of Hormuz this week, bringing the total to 55 of 74 incidents occurring along the southern route near Oman since July 6. Iran's Revolutionary Guard struck a Singapore-flagged vessel, which U.S. Central Command said sank, and two container ships went down after missile strikes on Oct. 14. Reuters has reported that Iran has carried out at least 135 precision strikes on 84 Gulf infrastructure sites since February.

The United States imposed fresh sanctions Thursday, targeting an Iraqi bank accused of funneling dollars to Iran.

## What to watch

For all the attention on the MOAB, military analysts caution that the bomb may be more useful as leverage than as a war-winning tool. Magnier's question — "What will this weapon accomplish that another available weapon cannot?" — is likely to echo through the Pentagon's deliberations in the weeks ahead.

The immediate variables are timing and restraint. Officials have signaled strikes could come before or after the Nov. 3 midterms, tying military planning to domestic politics. Trump has simultaneously threatened escalation and predicted a swift end to the war, leaving unclear which outcome his administration is actually pursuing. What is certain is that the standoff over Iran's buried nuclear sites — and the weaponry aimed at them — remains one of the most combustible flashpoints in the world.
