# Inflation Hits 3.7% as Social Security Gets 3.6% Boost

U.S. consumer price inflation accelerated to 3.7 percent in September, lifted by a spike in energy costs tied to the war with Iran, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/14/nx-s1-6000593/inflation-consumer-prices-cpi-social-security-benefits). In a release dated Wednesday, the Social Security Administration announced that benefits will [rise 3.6 percent in 2027](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/press/releases/2026-10-14.html) — the largest cost-of-living adjustment since 2023.

The twin developments underscore a challenging moment for American households: prices are climbing faster than paychecks, and even a sizable benefit increase may not fully close the gap for retirees.

## Prices Climb Faster Than Paychecks

The September reading, up from 3.4 percent in August, marked a 0.6 percent increase from the prior month. Stripping out volatile food and energy costs, core inflation came in at [2.6 percent](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/14/cpi-inflation-report-september-2026.html) annually, also higher than August's 2.4 percent.

Energy was the main culprit. The energy index jumped 5.2 percent for the month, with gasoline rising 9.1 percent and accounting for roughly half of the overall monthly increase. Over the past year, gasoline is up 34.2 percent and fuel oil has surged 74.2 percent — a painful reality for New England families who rely on heating oil to stay warm.

"It's not looking good," economist Brian Bethune of Boston College told NPR. "The main problem, of course, that everybody knows is gasoline prices. But also heating oil."

The pressure extends beyond fuel. Higher memory chip costs driven by the artificial intelligence boom have pushed up consumer electronics prices; Apple raised the price of older-model iPhones by $100 apiece last month. Average wages rose just 3 percent in September, meaning the typical worker's buying power has eroded over the past year.

"People are kind of saying, 'Something isn't right with that,'" Bethune said. "I'm working harder. I'm more productive. And this is not a square deal."

## A Boost for Retirees — But Is It Enough?

Shortly after the inflation report was released, the Social Security Administration confirmed a 3.6 percent cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) for 2027. The increase amounts to about $75 more per month for the average retirement benefit. Nearly 72 million Social Security beneficiaries will see higher payments beginning in January 2027, while increased payments to roughly 7.3 million Supplemental Security Income recipients start December 31, 2026.

"Social Security will implement this cost-of-living adjustment for 76 million Americans, giving them greater financial stability," Commissioner Frank J. Bisignano said.

Advocates for seniors broadly welcomed the news but warned it may fall short. "It helps millions of Americans keep up with rising costs for groceries, routine bills, healthcare and other everyday expenses," AARP CEO Myechia Minter-Jordan said. Yet Max Richtman, president of the National Committee to Preserve Social Security and Medicare, told NPR the adjustment "won't be enough for most seniors to even tread water financially."

Part of the squeeze comes from healthcare costs. The standard Medicare Part B premium is projected to rise to [$209.50 in 2027](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/14/social-security-cola-2027.html), up from $202.90 this year — potentially offsetting a portion of retirees' COLA gains. The COLA itself is tied by statute to the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W), which rose 3.9 percent over the 12 months ending in September.

## A Federal Reserve on Edge

The persistent inflation has complicated the Federal Reserve's path. The central bank raised rates in September 2026 — its first hike in more than three years — lifting its benchmark to a 3.75–4 percent target range. It will weigh the new data at its October 27–28 meeting, where markets see roughly even odds of another increase.

Officials worry that repeated supply shocks — first President Trump's double-digit tariffs, then the energy disruption from the Iran war — could embed higher expectations into the economy. Beth Hammack, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, warned of an "inflationary mindset" taking hold, in which expectations of higher prices become a self-fulfilling prophecy that makes monetary policy less effective.

"When people expect higher prices, they make decisions with this in mind," Hammack said. "It can become a self-fulfilling prophecy that makes it harder for monetary policy to bring inflation back down."

Core inflation has now exceeded the Fed's 2 percent target since March 2021. The September reading was not as high as the 2022 peak that followed Russia's invasion of Ukraine, but each time prices appear to stabilize, a new shock intervenes.

## What to Watch

Attention now turns to the Fed's rate decision later this month and to how much of the COLA increase retirees will actually keep once higher Medicare premiums take effect. Beneficiaries with online "my Social Security" accounts can view their COLA notices starting in late November; those who want paperless delivery must enroll by November 18.

With wages lagging inflation and energy costs still elevated, the coming months will test whether the current bout of price pressure proves temporary — or whether, as Hammack fears, it becomes part of the economic landscape.
