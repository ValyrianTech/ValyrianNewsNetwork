# Do Americans Own the President's Papers? Appeals Court Weighs In

A federal appeals court heard arguments Friday on whether the Presidential Records Act, the nearly 50-year-old law that declares presidential papers to be the property of the American people, is unconstitutional - a case that could reshape how the White House's records are preserved and whether the public ever gets to see them.

Three judges of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit considered the Trump administration's appeal of a lower-court ruling that blocked its effort to stop complying with the law, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/16/nx-s1-5996836/trump-who-owns-presidential-records-act-appeals-court). At stake is whether a president can treat the documents created in the Oval Office as personal property - a question with profound implications for transparency, historical memory and executive accountability.

## A Post-Watergate Law Under Challenge

Congress passed the Presidential Records Act (PRA) in 1978 in the aftermath of the Watergate scandal, when President Richard Nixon resigned rather than face removal and then sought to keep control of his own tapes and papers. The law declared that presidential records belong to the United States, not to the president personally, and required that they be preserved and eventually transferred to the National Archives. Every president since has complied - until this year.

The current dispute traces back to an April 1, 2026 opinion from the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel (OLC), [first reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/20/g-s1-117887/trump-justice-department-presidential-records-lawsuit) by NPR in April, which concluded that the PRA is unconstitutional "in its entirety" and advised President Trump that he "need not further comply" with it. The next day, the White House Counsel's Office issued guidance making record preservation voluntary rather than mandatory, and specifically instructed staff not to preserve text messages except in narrow circumstances, according to the [American Oversight](https://americanoversight.org/press-release/appeals-court-presidential-records-act/) watchdog group, which sued along with the American Historical Association.

"The Presidential Records Act is unconstitutional and a clear violation of the separation of powers," White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis told NPR.

The administration argues that keeping every record imposes a burden on the White House and could discourage advisers from having candid conversations with the president.

## A District Judge Invokes Orwell

In May, U.S. District Judge John D. Bates, an appointee of President George W. Bush, ruled against the White House and issued an injunction requiring the White House Office, the National Security Council, the U.S. DOGE Service and advisors to the president to comply in full with the law. Bates opened his ruling with a quotation from George Orwell's 1984: "Who controls the past controls the future; who controls the present controls the past."

Legal experts warned that applying the OLC's position could block access to more than 700 million White House emails alone. American Oversight's executive director, Chioma Chukwu, framed the stakes bluntly.

"Friday's argument comes down to a simple question: Is the president above the law?" Chukwu said. "For nearly 50 years, every administration of both parties has been bound by the Presidential Records Act, the law Congress passed after Watergate to make clear that presidential records belong to the American people, not to any one president. If the Trump administration can stop following the law, they can rewrite history to cover up corruption and avoid accountability."

"Right now, the court's order is all that stands in the way," she added. "If it falls, the court-ordered requirement to preserve this White House's records ends immediately. Records that are destroyed are gone for good."

## The Argument Over Access

The groups' position is backed by friend-of-the-court briefs from 106 members of Congress, 25 former senior national security officials who served under presidents of both parties, and seven former senior leaders of the National Archives, including former Archivists of the United States.

Among those officials is John Bellinger, a former legal adviser to the National Security Council under George W. Bush, who said access to a predecessor's papers is vital to governing.

"Access to the records on national security issues of former presidents is really vital for the continuity of our government," Bellinger told NPR. "Often the only record of what a president has said to a foreign leader - or perhaps even more important, what a foreign leader, friend or foe, has said to the president of the United States - is in a written document."

To lose that access, he said, would leave future presidents at a "terrible disadvantage."

On the other side of the argument sits the administration and its allies, who contend that the PRA represents an overreach by Congress into the executive branch. Conservative lawyer Josh Blackman, writing for the [Civitas Institute](https://www.civitasinstitute.org/research/trump-refights-the-war-that-congress-and-the-burger-court-waged-against-president-nixons-tapes), argued that the law conflicts with the Supreme Court's 2020 decision in Trump v. Mazars, which held that Congress needs a valid legislative purpose to subpoena a president's records.

"In short, Congress demanded documents that had not yet been created from not yet elected presidents for not yet specified reasons," Blackman wrote. He added that OLC was correct to conclude that Nixon v. Administrator of General Services, the 1977 decision upholding the Watergate-era records law, was wrongly decided.

## The Constitutional Fight

The legal heart of the case is whether Congress had the authority to pass the PRA at all. The OLC opinion advanced two arguments: that the law exceeds Congress's enumerated and implied powers, and that it "aggrandizes the legislative branch at the expense of the constitutional independence and autonomy of the executive."

Critics of the administration's position say those arguments overlook the most obvious source of congressional power. In a [published conversation](https://www.execfunctions.org/p/who-owns-presidential-records) on the Executive Functions podcast, Georgetown Law professor Marty Lederman, a former deputy assistant attorney general in the Office of Legal Counsel, noted that the Constitution's Property Clause gives Congress power to make rules for property belonging to the United States - a provision the OLC opinion barely addresses.

"I haven't run into anyone from any political persuasion or any administration who thinks that these arguments - which take up the bulk of the OLC opinion - have any plausibility at all," Lederman said. He added that even Nixon, who raised a half-dozen constitutional objections in the 1970s, never argued that Congress lacked the affirmative authority to regulate presidential records.

The administration's appeal asks the court to declare the entire law facially unconstitutional - a sweeping request that would, if granted, undercut nearly a half-century of settled practice.

## Political and Historical Undercurrents

The timing of the challenge is difficult to separate from Trump's own history with presidential documents. The National Archives had to recover records taken to Mar-a-Lago after his first term, and Special Counsel Jack Smith later charged Trump with unlawfully retaining classified documents. That case was dismissed on technical grounds related to the prosecutor's appointment, and the Justice Department dropped it after Trump won reelection.

Records from Trump's first term became eligible for public access under the PRA on January 20, 2026 - the same day American Oversight filed a sweeping set of Freedom of Information Act requests. The OLC opinion could be read to undermine the legal basis for the earlier criminal investigation into Trump's document handling.

Observers on both sides expect the case to end up before the Supreme Court. Conservative lawyer Michael Edney told NPR he believed the administration has a larger goal in mind.

"They want this to go to the Supreme Court," Edney said. "I think they want this resolved at a time of their choosing, with arguments of their choosing, and a court of their choosing."

## What to Watch

The D.C. Circuit's panel now holds the immediate fate of Judge Bates's injunction - and, with it, whether the White House must preserve its records while the litigation continues. A ruling against the administration would likely be appealed, setting up a potential Supreme Court showdown over one of the most consequential post-Watergate limits on presidential power.

For historians, the stakes extend beyond any single presidency. As Dr. Sarah Weicksel, executive director of the American Historical Association, put it: "Every time a record is destroyed, a piece of history is lost. We lose the ability to consult historical evidence, to fact-check, to assess one version of events over another."

"What is ultimately at stake in this case," she said, "is the ability of Americans to access the full documentary record of their history - something that is essential in a democracy."
