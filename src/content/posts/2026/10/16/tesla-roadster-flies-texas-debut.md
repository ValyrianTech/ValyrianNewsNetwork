# Tesla's Roadster Flies in Texas Debut, Hitting 60 in 0.81s

Tesla unveiled its long-delayed, second-generation Roadster on Thursday, October 15, in a dramatic livestreamed demonstration near Waco, Texas, where the electric supercar drove upside down on a curved ramp, performed a midair flip over a lineup of Tesla vehicles, and accelerated from 0 to 60 mph in 0.81 seconds. Citing only one caveat, CEO Elon Musk declared: ["Turns out the future does have flying cars."](https://www.foxbusiness.com/technology/teslas-new-roadster-makes-flying-debut-hitting-60-mph-under-second)

The event, held at SpaceX's rocket development and testing facility in McGregor, Texas, showcased a vehicle that fuses Tesla's electric drivetrain with aerospace engineering borrowed from its sister company. Production is promised for 2028, and reservations opened immediately — with deposits ranging from $50,000 for the "Performance" trim to $250,000 for the "Thrust" version. Tesla did not announce a price for the car itself.

## How the Stunt Worked

The centerpiece of the demonstration relied on two pieces of hardware with genuine aerospace pedigree. According to [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/15/tesla-reveals-spacex-powered-roadster-that-flies-and-falls-with-style/), the Roadster uses a fan-based suction system to generate up to two Gs of downforce, holding the car against a steeply inclined ramp until it was completely upside down. Integrated cold-gas thrusters — developed in collaboration with SpaceX — then launched it off the ramp, flipped it in midair, and eased it to a landing.

"We have a fan suction system on the bottom of the car that can pull up to 2Gs (twice the force of gravity) in order to make the car upside down," said Lars Moravy, Tesla's Vice President of Vehicle Engineering. "Then we borrowed some technology from SpaceX to do the rest." The [New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/10/16/business/elon-musks-three-word-reaction-after-unveiling-tesla-roadster-flying-car-that-hits-60-mph-in-under-a-second/) reported that the car executed its "kick flip" over a Model 3, Model Y, Cybertruck, Semi, and Cybercab at an altitude of roughly 21 feet. No human driver was inside; Musk said he did not want to "kill anyone."

Tesla said the thrusters are powered by 600 liters of compressed air — roughly 250 kilograms — stored in Carbon Fiber Overwrapped Pressure Vessels (COPVs), the same type of vessel involved in multiple SpaceX rocket explosions. Moravy said the air tank can be recharged.

## Three Trims, and a Long Wait

Tesla will offer the Roadster in three configurations, according to [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/tesla-roadster-2-launch/): a two-seat "Performance" version described as road-ready but without the thrusters shown on Thursday; a two-seat, race-ready "Thrust" variant with rear-mounted cold-gas thrusters; and a single-seat "Rocket" edition carrying 10 thrusters that is available by invitation only. All three claim more than 300 miles of range and top speeds exceeding 230 mph.

That figure may be conservative. Musk mused during the event about reaching "Mach 0.5" — roughly half the speed of sound, or about 380 mph — a claim that drew visible amusement from the executives beside him. YouTuber Marques Brownlee was less indulgent, posting on X: "They really just got on stage and said this newly designed 4000+ pound car can do flips and go half the speed of sound (380+mph) and will ship in 2028. This is a prank, right?"

The Roadster's path to Thursday's stage has been anything but smooth. Tesla first revealed a second-generation Roadster prototype in 2017 as a surprise at the end of its Semi unveiling, promising deliveries by 2020. Musk floated the idea of adding rocket thrusters in 2018. But the project repeatedly slipped as Tesla weathered "production hell" with the Model 3 and pivoted to the Model Y, Cybertruck, and later its autonomous-vehicle and robotics ambitions. Musk delayed the reveal in 2020, 2021, 2023, and 2024, then postponed it again to April 1, 2026 — a date he said would give him "some deniability" — before severe weather pushed it once more.

## Analysis: A Spectacle With Unresolved Questions

The demonstration was, by any measure, a feat of engineering showmanship. It also left several consequential questions unanswered. The [Verge](https://www.theverge.com/transportation/1012552/tesla-roadster-next-gen-reveal-elon-musk) noted the event ran less than 30 minutes and "felt more like a stunt show than a product reveal," with no mention of price or firm release date.

Road legality is among the sharpest uncertainties. Musk acknowledged as much at the event, saying he was "not necessarily sure it's legal in all jurisdictions." WIRED reported that the Federal Aviation Administration "will likely have thoughts" about a production car capable of leaving the ground. The Verge and other outlets have already begun questioning whether the flying capabilities will translate to street-legal vehicles at all.

Musk has also been candid that safety is not the priority. "If safety is your number one goal, do not buy the Roadster," he said on a podcast earlier this year, adding that Tesla would "aspire not to kill anyone in this car." That framing sits uneasily alongside the vehicle's 4,000-plus-pound mass and rocket-like acceleration — and it arrives at a delicate moment for the company.

The debut lands at a strange juncture for the EV market. As WIRED noted, US electric-vehicle sales have stalled after federal support was rolled back, even as demand booms elsewhere. Luxury hypercars have proven especially difficult: Lamborghini shelved its own EV plans earlier this year. Meanwhile, Tesla has repositioned itself as an AI and robotics company, has stopped selling its Model S and Model X, and is betting its future on autonomy — making a manually driven halo car something of an outlier in its own lineup.

## What's Next

Production remains scheduled for 2028, though Tesla's history of missed timelines invites skepticism. The company will report third-quarter 2026 financial results on October 21, and investors may seek clarity on how — or whether — the Roadster fits into a business increasingly oriented around autonomy and Optimus robots.

For now, Tesla has delivered something rare: a genuine spectacle. Whether the flying car becomes a road car, a regulatory battleground, or simply the most elaborate marketing stunt in automotive history is a question that will not be answered until the thrusters are quiet and the production line is real.
