# FBI Probes White Powder Mailings to Election, Hill Offices

The FBI and the U.S. Postal Inspection Service are investigating a nationwide wave of suspicious mailings — many containing a powdery white substance — sent to state election officials and congressional offices just weeks before the November 3 midterm elections, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/15/nx-s1-6003857/suspicious-mailings-election-congressional-offices). More than 100 letters were reportedly dispatched to people and groups across the country, including at least two U.S. senators, election offices in at least 15 states, and a polling organization. Preliminary testing indicates the substance is not hazardous, but the investigation remains active.

## Context

The mailings mark an unusually widespread example of election workers being physically targeted during a highly charged campaign season, [Votebeat](https://www.votebeat.org/national/2026/10/15/suspicious-letters-white-powder-election-officials-threats/) reported. They echo similar incidents from previous cycles: during the 2024 election, more than 15 state election offices received suspicious mailings containing white powder, and in 2023, election offices in six states received letters that in some cases contained trace amounts of fentanyl. With early voting already underway in numerous states, the latest wave has renewed concerns about the safety of the people who administer American elections.

## Key Developments

According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/authorities-investigate-suspicious-mailings-congressional-offices-rcna603712), election offices in at least 15 states — including Arizona, Delaware, Georgia, Idaho, Kansas, Louisiana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, South Dakota, Tennessee, Washington, and Wisconsin — said they were mailed suspicious packages. Three election officials briefed on the investigation told Votebeat that more than 100 letters were sent, though it remains unknown how many went to election officials versus politicians or other recipients.

At least six Democratic Senate offices received letters, most of which were intercepted before delivery. Confirmed targets include Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer of New York, Alex Padilla of California, Chris Murphy of Connecticut, Chris Coons of Delaware, Kirsten Gillibrand of New York, and Lisa Blunt Rochester of Delaware.

In Louisiana, an employee who opened a letter sent to the Secretary of State's office on Tuesday was not injured but was taken to the emergency room as a precaution, [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/suspicious-letters-election-officials-congressional-offices-fbi-us-postal-service/) reported. In Oklahoma, the State Election Board received an envelope containing white powder Tuesday morning, prompting an evacuation; state troopers determined the substance was non-hazardous. In Arizona, Secretary of State Adrian Fontes said his office received a letter filled with white powder Thursday afternoon, leading to the evacuation of the state capitol. In Cook County, Illinois, an envelope sent to Treasurer Maria Pappas turned out to contain baking powder.

"Some of the letters have contained an unknown substance, and we're working closely with our law enforcement partners to respond to each incident," said Brendan Donahue, Deputy Chief Inspector of the U.S. Postal Inspection Service. "We are committed to protecting our nation's election officials from threatening and dangerous mail. Any threat or attack on an election official is unacceptable."

Sen. Padilla said his office was in touch with federal authorities after a suspicious package was intercepted before reaching his staff. "Still, this incident is a stark reminder of the very real dangers facing those who serve the public and who help administer our elections in the face of increasingly hostile political rhetoric — especially from Donald Trump," he said.

## Analysis and Implications

The Senate Sergeant at Arms sent a notice to congressional staff warning that law enforcement is working to intercept additional letters but that some may still reach their destinations. Staff were advised to treat suspicious mail as potentially hazardous. The notice cited warning signs including discoloration, powder emanating from the envelope, and postmarks that do not match the return address; an image included in the communication showed a return address labeled "DSA" in New York City.

Former FBI Supervisory Special Agent Jason Pack told NBC News that even if the letters prove to be a hoax, a crime may still have been committed. "There's several different statutes that may come into play here, whether that's mailing a threat or mailing something believed to be a hazardous substance," he said. "And that could be five to 20 years behind bars." Pack added that the timing was "somewhat suspicious with the election ahead of us."

Election officials across the country struck a defiant tone. "This is a sobering moment that highlights the reality of election administration in this country today," Fontes said. "We will not be scared out of doing our jobs on behalf of the voters of Arizona." Meagan Wolfe, Wisconsin's chief election official, echoed that resolve: "Let this be very clear: this incident will not shake us."

The National Association of Secretaries of State and the National Association of State Election Directors said in a joint statement that "threats against election officials and workers have no place in our democracy. Those who administer our elections should never fear for their safety while carrying out their essential duties."

## What's Next

The FBI said it is working with partners to identify the individual or individuals responsible and has urged recipients to report any suspicious mail. The agency has declined to confirm specific cases or say what the purported substance may have been, citing the ongoing investigation. As of Friday, no injuries had been reported.

With Election Day less than three weeks away, the coming days will test whether law enforcement can intercept the remaining letters and determine who sent them. The White House has not yet publicly commented on the mailings, and congressional and election officials alike say they remain focused on carrying out a safe and secure vote.
