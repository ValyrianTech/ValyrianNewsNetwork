# Gen Z Trump Voters Voice Regret as Midterms Near

Nearly two years after helping propel President Trump back to the White House, a group of Gen Z voters is expressing deep frustration and, in some cases, outright regret over their 2024 choice — a shift in sentiment that could ripple through the midterm elections now underway.

The findings come from a focus group of eight Gen Z Trump voters conducted virtually on October 8 by Navigator Research, a left-leaning polling group, and reported by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/16/nx-s1-5993835/gen-z-trump-voting-support). Participants, aged 20 to 29, spanned five states Trump won by double digits — Florida, Mississippi, Ohio, Texas and South Carolina — two of which are home to competitive Senate races key to control of Congress.

## Grades That Tell a Story

When asked to grade Trump's job performance, six of the eight participants gave him either a D or an F. Only two selected a C. For many, the disappointment centers on what they see as a string of broken economic promises.

"I just feel like nothing went correctly. I just feel like everything got worse," said John, a 29-year-old Black man from Florida who gave the president an F. "The economy went down, just, like, every, every single thing got worse... Nothing went how it was supposed to go. Everything right now is just horrible."

John, who identifies as a Republican and has voted for Trump since 2016, said he now plans to back Democratic candidates this fall.

The focus group, though not a nationally representative survey, echoed broader [polling](https://navigatorresearch.org/navigating-the-vote-buyers-remorse-gen-z-on-trump/) released by Navigator this month. That research found more than a third of Gen Zers — those under 30 — regret voting for Trump, a rate higher than among older generations. According to the group's figures, 35% of Gen Z Trump voters regret their choice, compared with 24% of millennials, 16% of Gen X and just 8% of baby boomers.

## An Economy That Disappointed

The economy is the central grievance. Among Gen Z, net approval of Trump's handling of the economy sits at minus 39 points — 29% approve and 68% disapprove — and half of Gen Z respondents cited jobs and the economy among the most important issues facing the president and Congress.

Participants described a job market that has left even college graduates struggling. "I'm a college graduate, and it's so hard to get in my career field with no experience," said one 25-year-old woman from Ohio. "We going to school, paying all this money to go to college, get degrees, and when it's time to find a good job in this economy, we can't."

## Not a Clean Break From the GOP

Yet regret over Trump does not translate neatly into support for Democrats. Of the eight participants, four said they still planned to vote Republican this year, three said they would vote Democratic, and one remained undecided but leaned Republican.

Melissa Toufanian, managing director at Navigator, cautioned against reading the frustration as a wholesale shift to the left. "As we see people regret their vote for Trump, sour on the Republican Party and Republican policies, that doesn't necessarily mean that they're swinging right over to Democrats," she said. "A lot of people are moving to this middle, this category that 'I don't trust either party.'"

That ambivalence was visible in the remarks of Courtney, a 20-year-old white woman from South Carolina who does not regret her vote. She said she has grown more distrustful of both parties, particularly on foreign policy. "Education, the economy, everything else would be so much better if we weren't pumping money out into other countries that aren't ours," she said, referring to U.S. aid for Israel and the war with Iran.

## A Broader Pattern of Erosion

The Gen Z findings align with a wider pattern of fading support that has emerged across multiple focus groups. In separate sessions with 12 Georgia swing voters who backed Joe Biden in 2020 and then Trump in 2024, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/15/nx-s1-6001714/georgia-swing-voters-focus-groups) that 11 of 12 were disappointed with Trump's job performance and 10 said they regretted voting for him.

Trump has also lost ground with independents and Latino voters who shifted right in 2024 — a coalitional erosion that comes as Republicans face difficult midterm headwinds.

The focus group also touched on other sources of disillusionment. Several participants pointed to broken promises and corruption, while one 25-year-old Black man from Texas raised the influence of AIPAC funding. "I think that Israel, I think we've helped them a little bit enough, and I think that they need to not rely on us so much," he said.

## Priced Out of the American Dream

Beyond politics, participants expressed a deeper economic anxiety. Many said the American Dream feels further out of reach than it was for their parents' generation, particularly when it comes to home ownership and financial stability. Several also discussed their relationship with artificial intelligence — using it nearly daily while nonetheless favoring strict government regulation of the technology.

Despite the frustration, participants remained resilient. Asked to imagine a letter from their 50-year-old selves, many struck a hopeful tone. "Even though what's going on right now is difficult, things can always change later on," one Florida participant said. "So just keep pushing and just keep on going."

## What to Watch

With early voting already underway and Election Day approaching, the question is whether the disaffection documented in these focus groups translates into measurable turnout shifts. Focus groups offer insight into voter sentiment but are not statistically representative, and the participants' split intentions reflect genuine uncertainty rather than a decisive realignment.

Still, the trend matters for both parties. For Republicans, it signals that the young voters who expanded Trump's 2024 coalition may not show up with the same enthusiasm. For Democrats, it presents an opening — albeit a conditional one — as they seek to convert frustration into support rather than see it harden into broad-based distrust of the political system itself.
