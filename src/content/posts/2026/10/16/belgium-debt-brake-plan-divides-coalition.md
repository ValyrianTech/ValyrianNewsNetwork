# Belgium's Debt Brake Plan Divides Coalition

Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever has proposed a 'schuldenrem' — a debt brake — that would let the government freeze roughly 3 to 4 billion euros in spending for 2027 if coalition parties fail to agree on a full multi-year budget. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/15/begroting-schuldenrem-voorstel-n-va-premier-bart-de-wever/), the mechanism is not the constitutional debt brake used in Germany and Switzerland, but an emergency fallback aimed at reassuring financial markets. The proposal has raised eyebrows among coalition partners Vooruit and CD&V, who question both its label and its politics.

## An Emergency Brake, Not a Constitutional Rule

The term 'debt brake' invites comparison with the Swiss 'Schuldenbremse' of 2001, which constitutionally ties spending to long-term revenue, or Germany's 2009 rule limiting structural deficits to 0.35 percent of GDP. De Wever is proposing neither. Amending Belgium's constitution would require a two-thirds parliamentary majority, which VRT NWS describes as unrealistic in the short term. Instead, the premier wants a pre-agreed package of spending freezes that the government could activate if budget talks drag on — a way, in his framing, to avoid governing by 'provisional twelfths,' the automatic emergency budget that caps monthly spending at one-twelfth of the previous year's total.

Belgium already fell back on provisional twelfths for three months last year — a situation De Wever has called a humiliation for a government with full powers. He has said that scenario is not an option given current conditions in the financial markets.

The measure is chiefly a signal to investors, aiming to prevent a political crisis inside the government from becoming a financial crisis. Without such a guarantee, markets could punish Belgium's political gridlock by pushing up borrowing costs, feeding the so-called 'interest snowball.'

## Why Markets Are Watching

The timing is not incidental. Belgium's long-term interest rate has climbed above 3.8 percent, the highest since 2012, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/19/stijgende-rente-gevolgen-begroting/) in August. The National Bank has calculated that Belgium's annual interest bill could triple to nearly 53 billion euros by 2035, [De Morgen noted](https://www.demorgen.be/nieuws/als-de-rentefactuur-verdrievoudigt-dreigen-de-pijnlijke-jaren-80-voor-belgie-terug-te-keren~bbad7947/).

Economists warn of a 'rentesneeuwbal' — an interest snowball — that forms when the rate on public debt stays higher than economic growth for a sustained period. "A growing share of the budget goes to interest payments, leaving less and less room for other spending," economist Selien De Schryder of Ghent University told VRT NWS. "Then expenditures must come down or revenues must go up."

Her colleague Willem Sas of UHasselt and KU Leuven stressed that stability matters more than the precise size of the savings. "The crucial thing is that an agreement is reached and that the government radiates stability," he said. "If a budget crisis leads to political instability, financial markets view that much more negatively than the difference between, say, 8 or 10 billion euros."

## The Politics of the Proposal

The government is seeking 10 billion euros in savings by 2029. De Wever's latest strategy breaks the problem into pieces: a first tranche of 3 to 4 billion euros for 2027, with the multi-year budget to 2029 negotiated in parallel. The top ministers — the 'kern' — could decide on the debt brake as early as Friday or Saturday, after which it would still need parliamentary approval.

N-VA faction leader Axel Ronse defended the plan in [VRT NWS's liveblog](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/kernkabinet-samen-over-begroting-minister-prevot-haalt-uit-naar~1790599460224/), casting it as a precaution. "The financial markets are really watching us," he said. "The premier has been working on that list for weeks. He has simply asked whether we can provide an emergency button, in case the financial markets start to react." Ronse added that the risk of failure is too high: "If it fails, there will be elections. And that is the worst thing we can imagine, at an immensely high cost."

The proposal is drawing fire from within the coalition. Both Vooruit and CD&V are skeptical of the label and the substance. Vooruit reads the 3 to 4 billion euros as mainly non-indexation of health budgets, civil servant salaries, benefits and pensions — not a basis for further negotiation. Both parties argue the plan cushions one member of the coalition, the French-speaking liberal MR, by deferring harder measures for 2028 and 2029. "De Wever is simply bowing to Bouchez," a source told VRT NWS, referring to MR chair Georges-Louis Bouchez.

Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem of CD&V struck a more somber tone in the Chamber, where De Wever was absent at an EU summit. "Our country cannot afford immobilism," Van Peteghem said, in comments carried by the liveblog. "That responsibility is too great to push it ahead of us and only look to tomorrow." Finance Minister Jan Jambon of N-VA insisted the broader goal remains intact: "The ambition to improve the budget deficit by at least 10 billion euros by 2029 remains fully preserved."

Opposition parties were blunter. PVDA leader Raoul Hedebouw accused the government of freezing services to paper over its own failure. "Because you can't work it out, you want to freeze everything: attacking pensions, stopping the indexation of funds for the sick, limiting the funds of the NMBS, fire brigade and police," he said. "How is that possible, that arrogance?"

## Analysis: A Label That Carries Risk

The central problem with De Wever's proposal is semantic as much as fiscal. By branding an emergency spending freeze a 'debt brake,' the premier borrows the credibility of the German and Swiss models without their legal force. That gap is exactly what coalition partners are seizing on — they can support fiscal discipline in principle while rejecting a measure they see as tilted toward one party.

Yet the economists' warnings cut the other way. De Schryder argued the deeper issue is not the debt stock but persistent deficits. "For years it has been warned that Belgium must put its finances in order while it still can," she said. "The longer you wait, the greater the costs ultimately become." Sas added that no single measure solves the problem: "You have to simultaneously pursue more economic growth, more efficient government spending and structural reforms."

De Wever also holds a structural lever. A government collapse would be especially damaging to the MR, which governs without the French-speaking socialists and Greens; analysts see that as leverage keeping coalition partners at the table. As journalist Bart Verhulst put it on VRT NWS Villa Politica, N-VA is "putting the knife to the coalition partners' throat by pretending this is the last alternative."

## What's Next

The kern could sign off on the debt brake within days, but parliamentary approval is far from assured. The deeper fight — a multi-year budget to 2029 — remains unresolved, with the VAT reform that divided the coalition shelved for now. Les Engagés chair Yvan Verougstraete has not ruled out new federal elections.

The coming weeks will test whether De Wever's emergency brake is a genuine safety mechanism or a political instrument that deepens the distrust inside his own coalition. For investors, the message is simpler: Belgium's ability to govern its finances is being watched as closely as the numbers themselves.
