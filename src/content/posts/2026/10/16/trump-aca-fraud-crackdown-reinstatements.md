# Trump Adds Some Back After Boot of 760K From ACA Plans

The Trump administration has reinstated a small number of people whose Affordable Care Act coverage was canceled by mistake, after removing roughly 760,000 people from ACA health plans in a sweeping anti-fraud action, [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/insurance/trump-aca-affordable-care-act-obamacare-cms-fraud-cancellations-reinstatements/) has learned. The reversal, first reported in partnership with [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/15/nx-s1-6002957/aca-fraud-trump-health-insurance) and confirmed by insurance industry sources, underscores a deepening dispute over how much of the canceled enrollment was genuine fraud and how many legitimate consumers were caught in the dragnet.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) canceled approximately 315,000 enrollments covering more than 760,000 individuals on Aug. 31, 2026, the agency said in a [fact sheet](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/federal-marketplace-ffe-sbe-fp-anti-fraud-actions), describing the move as the result of reviews that confirmed the enrollments were unauthorized. CMS estimated it would recoup about $2.2 billion in advance premium tax credits tied to the canceled plans.

## What Happened

CMS in June selected nearly 1.2 million enrollments for review based on what it deemed four warning signs for fraud: an application with no Social Security number or immigration document number; the involvement of a broker in the sign-up; a plan with no monthly premium payment; and an enrollee who had no contact with the insurer and no claims filed. According to the agency, carriers were instructed to use at least two methods of contact — such as a phone call and a letter — and policyholders were given 30 days to respond with the missing information. Those who did not respond were disenrolled.

Insurance industry sources told KFF Health News that some people whose coverage should not have been canceled have since been reinstated, and that more could follow as enrollees discover they are uninsured when they try to use their coverage. David Merritt, senior vice president of external affairs at the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association, confirmed that some BCBS members are among those reinstated and said CMS has an expedited review process for people who find they were improperly disenrolled.

A second phase of the effort is underway. An additional 419,000 sign-ups are being scrutinized, and insurers must tell CMS how many enrollees fail to respond to outreach by "around mid-to-late November, after the midterm elections," Merritt said.

## A Disputed Dragnet

Supporters of the crackdown argue the sweep was long overdue. Brian Blase, president of the Paragon Health Institute, told KFF Health News that "this was clearly phantom enrollees," estimating that more than 99% of the cancellations involved zero-premium coverage signed up by rogue call centers without people's knowledge, or for people who do not exist at all.

Outside experts are less certain. "It may be true that some of those enrollments were not authorized, but it can be equally true that people got lost in the shuffle and have a valid enrollment. Without more information, it's almost impossible to tell," said Katie Keith, director of the Center for Health Policy and the Law at Georgetown Law. "That they are not being very transparent is telling too."

CMS has declined to provide the breakdown of why enrollments were canceled, with its press office telling KFF Health News that "currently, there is no additional data to share." Johnny Wallin, chief operating officer of Texas-based Marketplace Insurance Exchange, described an "all-hands-on-deck" effort to reach clients, noting that insurers asked his organization for help and that his agents still could not reach about 60 people. "Getting them to answer the phone isn't easy," he said.

Policy analysts also note that some fraud markers are ambiguous. Matthew Fiedler, a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution, told KFF Health News that a missing Social Security number does not automatically prove fraud — it could be an oversight, or a number that was not required for a family member not applying for coverage. "There will be legitimate people who don't have a claim or don't respond to an inquiry from an insurer," Fiedler said. "If the goal is to target fraudulent enrollment, what CMS did is better than tossing people at random, but is likely to miss the mark."

Ronnell Nolan, president and CEO of the trade group Health Agents for America, framed the tension bluntly. "We're against fraud, and CMS has allowed a lot of fraud to happen," she said. "But we don't want people who just didn't go to the doctor to be canceled."

The episode follows a broader Republican push to emphasize fraud in government health programs. At an Oct. 14 press conference, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz touted the removal this year of subsidies for about 189,000 ACA enrollees with duplicate Medicare, Medicaid, or Children's Health Insurance Program coverage — citing not fraud but a failure to detect concurrent enrollments or continued coverage for deceased people.

Federal officials have also moved against brokers. Since January 2026, CMS has sent termination notices to more than 200 non-compliant agents and brokers and issued 569 notices of intent to terminate Exchange Agreements, the agency said. It has barred new brokers from enrolling clients on the federal exchange for the upcoming open enrollment period and announced a temporary moratorium on registering agents and brokers for 2027 who lack an active 2026 Exchange Agreement.

## A Smaller Insurance Pool

The cancellations arrive amid a broader contraction of the ACA market. Overall ACA enrollment fell by nearly 3 million in the first months of 2026, to about 19.2 million, driven largely by the expiration of enhanced subsidies at the end of 2025, according to [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/medicaid/aca-fraud-crackdown-skyrocketing-prices-enrollment-decline/). Average ACA premiums rose 58% to $178 a month, while deductibles climbed 37% to nearly $3,800 a year.

The administration has taken some credit for the enrollment decline, attributing it to its focus on weeding out improper enrollments. Health policy experts dispute that framing, arguing that rising costs — not the fraud crackdown — are the primary driver. "We know that lots of people have seen higher premiums, and there's really good evidence that when premiums go up, people drop coverage," Fiedler said.

Notably, the cancellations target only states that use HealthCare.gov, the federal marketplace. The 21 states that run their own marketplaces generally have more robust fraud prevention programs, such as multifactor authentication, and have reported few or none of the kinds of unauthorized sign-ups that have plagued the federal exchange.

## What's Next

The political stakes are high. Healthcare costs are tied with gas prices as a top voter concern ahead of the November midterm elections, and many of those removed from ACA plans are likely in red states such as Texas, Florida, Louisiana, and Mississippi that saw rapid enrollment growth from 2020 to 2025. As Adrianna McIntyre, an assistant professor of health policy and politics at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, told KFF Health News, "To the extent there is a reaction depends on whether the government got it wrong, whether people try to get care and then find out their coverage is no longer active."

With the second-phase reporting deadline set for after the midterms, the coming weeks will test whether the administration's process holds up — and whether more of the 760,000 will find their way back onto the rolls.
