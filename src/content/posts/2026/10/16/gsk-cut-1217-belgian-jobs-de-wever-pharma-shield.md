# GSK to Cut 1,217 Belgian Jobs as De Wever Urges Pharma Shield

British pharmaceutical giant GSK intends to cut up to 1,217 jobs in Belgium, a move announced at an extraordinary works council in Wavre on 14 October and set to unfold over the next two years. The restructuring lands squarely on Wallonia, where GSK is the largest private employer, and has triggered a union blockade, cross-government mobilisation and a national debate over how a highly profitable company can shed more than a thousand posts while receiving hundreds of millions of euros in public support.

The announcement came as Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) told reporters at the European summit in Brussels that Belgium's pharmaceutical sector must be protected — not because it is uncompetitive, but because of pressure from the United States and China.

## What GSK Is Cutting

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/16/gsk-farmasector-moet-worden-beschermd-tegen-vs-en-china-zegt-de/), the losses break down into 735 management and executive posts and 482 white-collar employee positions. Manual workers are not affected in this round. All are permanent contracts, and the job losses would be spread over the next two years. The company has not proposed closing any Belgian site.

The restructuring follows the so-called Renault procedure for collective redundancies, which opens an information and consultation phase before any dismissals can proceed — giving staff and unions time to study the impact and explore alternatives.

GSK's Belgian footprint is concentrated at three sites: Wavre, its largest global vaccine production facility; Rixensart, the historic vaccines campus; and Gembloux, which handles logistics. Together they account for more than half of GSK's global vaccine output by volume.

## A Profitable Company Under Pressure

The cut is politically explosive because GSK is not in distress. As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/gsk-pourquoi-la-belgique-c-ur-mondial-de-ses-vaccins-paie-le-prix-du-virage-strategique-du-groupe-pharmaceutique-11800477) reported, the group's net profit doubled to about EUR 7 billion in 2025 on revenue of nearly EUR 40 billion. The company says it will invest roughly EUR 300 million a year in its Belgian sites and is developing an mRNA vaccine there.

Instead, the restructuring reflects a strategic pivot. Under chief executive Luke Miels, who took over in January 2026, GSK is targeting EUR 2 billion in annual savings by 2029 through AI, automation and simplification, while shifting its focus away from vaccines — which account for 28% of revenue and stagnated last year — toward oncology and specialty medicines such as asthma and cancer treatments, which now represent more than 40% of revenue. GSK plans 25 late-stage clinical trials by year's end, more than half in cancer and only one in a vaccine.

Several forces are converging. GSK's flagship HIV drug faces a patent cliff from 2028, and according to the FGTB union, vaccine production volumes have fallen about 40% over five years. In Germany, the group will close a vaccine production site in Dresden, cutting more than 640 jobs.

## The US and China Factor

At the heart of De Wever's argument is the squeeze between Washington and Beijing. "If you have the American president who obliges investment in the US and refuses to import vaccines any more, then companies like GlaxoSmithKline get into difficulty," the prime minister said. "Ultimately there are victims somewhere, even if one is still competitive."

He also pointed to what he called unfair Chinese competition. "What China does with clinical trials is unfair competition, which is massively subsidised," he said. Belgium's pharma industry is caught in the middle, according to Sonja Willems, chair of the Observatory for the Pharmaceutical Industry, who [told VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/14/gsk-sector-farma-vs-china/) that "the share of China in global clinical trials rose in ten years from 13 percent to nearly 50 percent."

GSK itself has committed at least USD 30 billion over five years to US research and manufacturing, stating that the US must rank first globally for the number of studies, sites and clinical trial participants. GSK has not confirmed that the US pledge drove the Belgian cuts, though unions suspect it is a major factor. Union leaders have pointed to that commitment as a partial explanation. GSK's US market accounts for more than half its sales, while Europe accounts for only about 23%.

## Political Fallout and the Public Aid Question

The scale of the announcement — Belgium's largest intended collective redundancy of 2026 — triggered a 24-hour-plus blockade of the Wavre and Rixensart sites and a joint, indefinite strike notice. Unions lifted the blockade on the afternoon of 15 October after an extraordinary works council was scheduled for the following morning.

Left-wing parties seized on the contrast between profits and aid. As [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-ptb-exige-le-remboursement-des-aides-publiques-en-cas-de-licenciements-ces-aides-servent-a-maintenir-l-emploi-ou-a-arroser-les-actionnaires-11800487), PTB MP Sofie Merckx said GSK "made more than [EUR] 2 billion in profit in Belgium, distributed more than [EUR] 5 billion in dividends and, as the cherry on the cake, benefited from more than [EUR] 500 million in public aid." According to CSC figures, GSK received at least EUR 481 million in public aid and tax advantages in 2025 alone. The PTB is demanding that public aid be conditioned on maintaining employment.

GSK's own position is that the changes are meant to preserve its Belgian future. "It is absolutely out of the question to close our sites in Belgium," a company spokesperson said, adding that the restructuring aims to "guarantee the sustainability of our sites in Belgium."

## What's Next

De Wever framed the GSK case within a broader push for European competitiveness, arguing the solution lies in deepening the single market while defending it against unfair practices — and warning against "pointless protectionism." He also said Belgium's petrochemical sector faces similar pressure, tying the single company's restructuring to national industrial policy amid budget negotiations.

Several questions remain open. Whether the consultation phase yields any reduction in the number of job losses is unclear, as is whether Belgium will move to condition public aid on employment guarantees — a step critics warn could make the country less attractive to investors. With more than half of GSK's global vaccines still made in Belgium, how the pivot to oncology reshapes the so-called Pharma Valley will be the number to watch.
