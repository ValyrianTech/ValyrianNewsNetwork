# Six Flags to Add Seat Belts After Goliath Rider Death

Six Flags Entertainment Corporation said it will install seat belts on the Goliath roller coaster at Six Flags Over Georgia and on other coasters that use a similar restraint system, a safety retrofit announced less than two weeks after a rider was ejected from the ride and fell to his death. The [announcement](https://www.sixflags.com/overgeorgia/park-update), issued Friday by President and CEO John Reilly, comes as Goliath and eight other Six Flags coasters across North America remain shut down amid an ongoing investigation.

The rider, 42-year-old Hassan Jihad Ibn Kariem of Atlanta, died on October 12 after being thrown from the Goliath, according to [Cobb County police](https://www.cnn.com/2026/10/24/us/six-flags-goliath-seat-belts-death). He struck several other passengers before falling to the ground below the ride. Four other riders, all minors, suffered non-life-threatening injuries.

## What Is Changing

"We are reinforcing safety precautions at our parks and taking additional measures, including adding seat belts on Goliath and other coasters with a similar restraint," Reilly said in the statement. "Safety has always been fundamental to the experience we provide for our guests."

Reilly did not specify which additional coasters will receive the upgrade. The company has also engaged independent engineers and ride inspection specialists to review safety procedures across its entire portfolio of rides and attractions, [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/roller-coaster-death-six-flags-georgia-90a9bfe6a6fc934ab7f2a1a6bf52d520) reported. Six Flags has not said when the closed coasters will reopen.

The company's most recent update struck a contrite tone. "I know trust is earned through actions and not words," Reilly said. "As CEO of Six Flags, I take that responsibility personally."

## The Restraint at the Center of the Case

Goliath, which opened in 2006 in Mableton, about 15 miles northwest of Atlanta, rises 200 feet and reaches 70 mph over a roughly 3.5-minute ride. It does not go upside down, and it carries riders without an over-the-shoulder harness or seat belts. Instead, it uses what the industry calls a "clamshell" restraint: a padded lap-and-leg bar mounted on a vertical post between the rider's knees.

All nine of the Six Flags coasters taken offline were built by the Swiss manufacturer Bolliger & Mabillard and share that restraint design. A 2001 U.S. patent for the system described it as giving riders the sensation of being "seated on a flying carpet," while aiming to guarantee "optimum security." The manufacturer said it is investigating and would not comment further "until we have a better understanding of what transpired."

Investigators have not determined what went wrong. Witnesses said Kariem rose out of his seat, appeared to hover above the moving car, fell headfirst back into it and was then ejected on the ride's final turn.

"It looked like he was hovering because the car was moving underneath him," witness Nick Mendenhall, who had just ridden Goliath with his son, told AP. "It felt like slow-motion, but it happened in an instant."

Some riders who boarded the coaster earlier that day said they had concerns about the restraints. Summer Bates, at the park with her husband and three children, said her older children kept pushing their restraints down tighter and that on her own ride the bar "just didn't feel secure and tight."

## A Fleet-Wide Precaution

The closures extend well beyond Georgia. In addition to Goliath, Six Flags temporarily halted Raging Bull at Six Flags Great America in Illinois, Nitro at Six Flags Great Adventure in New Jersey, Fury 325 and Thunder Striker at Carowinds in North Carolina, and Leviathan and Behemoth at Canada's Wonderland in Ontario. Kings Island in Ohio also suspended coasters.

The ripple reached other operators. SeaWorld Orlando closed its Mako coaster and La Ronde in Montreal shut a ride also named Goliath, both out of caution; SeaWorld reopened Mako on Tuesday after about a week offline.

"Obviously it was something with the restraint," said Dennis Speigel, a theme park consultant and past president of the International Association of Amusement Parks and Attractions, explaining why so many similar rides were pulled at once.

## Why Seat Belts, and Why Now

Adding a secondary restraint is a relatively fast and inexpensive engineering fix that can be applied across an entire fleet of similar rides without waiting for investigators to finish their work. It signals both a precautionary posture and an effort at reputational repair for the largest regional theme park operator in the United States, which runs more than 220 coasters.

The decision also carries strategic weight. Because so many coasters share the same manufacturer and restraint type, a single finding about the cause could implicate a large installed base of rides industry-wide—not just at Six Flags. Some safety experts, including University of Florida professor Brian D. Avery, have long advocated backup restraints, though the industry has debated whether secondary systems add boarding time and reduce capacity.

The episode has renewed attention on how U.S. amusement rides are regulated. There is no federal oversight of major theme parks; responsibility falls to states, and the rules vary widely. Georgia's Office of Insurance and Safety Fire Commissioner, which oversees annual inspections, has launched an "in-depth and comprehensive" review. The coaster's most recent state inspection, on March 16, reported no violations, deficiencies or citations, Commissioner John King said—though a separate inspection report earlier this year noted that bushings, which reduce vibration, needed replacement.

## Beyond This Investigation

The death has also drawn scrutiny to Six Flags' broader safety record. [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/six-flags-add-seat-belts-roller-coaster-georgia-man-death-rcna605398) noted that the company was sued in September by riders who alleged brain injuries on the X2 coaster at Six Flags Magic Mountain in California—a ride Six Flags permanently closed the same month. A 2025 investor class-action lawsuit alleges the company deferred basic park maintenance before its merger with Cedar Fair.

Kariem was the information technology manager at Wesley International Academy, a public charter school in Atlanta, and had previously been a technology teacher. The school said it was devastated, describing his "dedication to our students, and commitment to our school community" as evident in everything he did.

## What to Watch

Several questions remain unresolved. Investigators have yet to say whether the restraint failed mechanically, was improperly secured, or whether the rider's position contributed. Six Flags has not addressed specific rider complaints publicly, nor has it said which additional coasters will get seat belts or when the nine closed rides will return. Whether SeaWorld, La Ronde and other operators of similar Bolliger & Mabillard coasters follow suit—and whether the Georgia review produces citations, fines or new rules—will determine how far this change spreads.

For now, Reilly framed the retrofit as the beginning of a longer reckoning. "We will continue to communicate about our progress and hold ourselves accountable to our commitments to you," he said.
