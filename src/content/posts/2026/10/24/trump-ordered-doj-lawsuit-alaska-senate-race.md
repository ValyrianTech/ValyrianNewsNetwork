# Trump Says He Ordered DOJ Suit Over Alaska Senate Race

President Donald Trump said Friday that he personally directed the Justice Department to [file a lawsuit](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.akd.80263/gov.uscourts.akd.80263.1.0.pdf) seeking to block Alaska from certifying the result of its U.S. Senate race if Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan loses — an extraordinary federal intervention in a state election that could help decide control of the chamber.

"I'm the one that said do it," Trump told reporters as he arrived for a campaign rally in Fort Myers, Florida, according to [the Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/alaska-senate-race-sullivan-name-lawsuit-certification-d8f4a3c7b5247cc427a05f796d7c0d82). "They have two guys ... named Dan Sullivan, so the Democrats put up a guy."

## A dispute over two Dan Sullivans

The lawsuit, filed late Thursday in federal court in Alaska and signed by Associate U.S. Attorney General Stanley Woodward, centers on the presence of a second candidate named Dan Sullivan on the Nov. 3 ballot: Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., a retired schoolteacher from the small fishing community of Petersburg. Republicans worry the pair of names could confuse voters in a race expected to be decided by a few thousand votes.

Republicans have argued that Daniel J. Sullivan is a Democratic plant meant to siphon support from the incumbent, who appears on the ballot as "Dan S. Sullivan," listed as a "registered Republican" and "incumbent." Daniel J. Sullivan's name appears without a party designation. Democrat Mary Peltola, a former U.S. House member from Alaska, is also on the ballot.

The suit asks a federal judge to block the state from certifying "any result that may be impacted by voter confusion ... pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan." According to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/23/alaska-senate-election-doj-lawsuit-sullivan.html), the department did not explain how anyone could determine a voter's intent — a formidable obstacle given the anonymity of ballots.

## High stakes in a toss-up race

The contest carries outsized weight: Republicans hold a narrow Senate majority, and the Cook Political Report rates Alaska as one of seven "toss-up" races. A FiftyPlusOne polling average shows Peltola with roughly a 2-point lead over Sullivan. In August's nonpartisan primary, Peltola led with 49.5% to Sullivan's 41.4%; Daniel J. Sullivan drew 2.5%, about 4,107 votes.

Alaska's ranked-choice voting system adds another layer. Voters rank candidates by preference, so a winner may not be known for weeks — a scenario the lawsuit argues could allow votes for Daniel J. Sullivan to transfer to Peltola after his elimination. A ballot initiative to repeal ranked-choice voting is also before voters next month.

Trump has escalated his complaints about the ballot in recent days, calling the situation "CHEATING" in a Wednesday post on Truth Social and vowing to pursue "powerful legal options to remove the fraudulent Daniel J. Sullivan Jr."

## Bipartisan criticism and a thin legal footing

The move drew swift criticism, including from a fellow Republican. Alaska Sen. Lisa Murkowski, who has endorsed the incumbent Sullivan, said in a social media post that "Alaskans don't need any help voting for their preferred candidates, and the State of Alaska has already handled this by clearly distinguishing the candidates on this year's ballot. The Department of Justice should never have filed this lawsuit."

Peltola, speaking at a forum in Anchorage, called the suit "just flagrant, flagrant corruption in my opinion" and said it was "another diversion from the issue that Alaskans want to talk about, which is affordability."

Legal experts are skeptical of the suit's prospects. It rests on the preamble of the National Voter Registration Act, which says states must "protect the integrity" of elections — language experts say is too vague to justify a federal court overturning a state result. "The statute simply doesn't let the Department of Justice enjoin certification," Derek Muller, a law professor at the University of Notre Dame, told the AP, adding that the case could be mooted by a decisive outcome either way and would likely be dismissed.

As [the AP explained](https://apnews.com/article/dan-sullivan-alaska-trump-lawsuit-8b650aa32e24a21d229a5076845b54e2), the suit is essentially a placeholder that only becomes operative if Sullivan narrowly loses. The Constitution reserves the running of elections to the states, though Congress sets standards for federal races.

The Justice Department defended the action, saying Alaska's own election officials determined Daniel J. Sullivan's candidacy was intended to mislead voters. "Therefore, the Justice Department, consistent with the National Voter Registration Act enacted by Congress, is taking the necessary steps to ensure that this frivolous candidacy does not undermine the integrity of Alaska's election or the will of its voters," a spokesperson said.

## A first step, and a precedent question

The Alaska filing marks the first formal step toward what critics fear could become a broader effort to block certification of midterm results the president's party loses. It follows an unusual, last-minute DOJ suit demanding California segregate ballots from roughly 20,000 voters the administration believed might be noncitizens. Attorney General Todd Blanche had said earlier Thursday — hours before the Alaska suit was filed — that it was "never our intention to, improperly interfere with elections or the state certification process."

Sullivan's campaign declined to say whether the senator supports the intervention. "We'll let the DOJ complaint run its course," spokesman Nate Adams said.

With just 11 days until Election Day and months since the ballot was set, the immediate question is whether a federal judge will act before Alaska certifies a winner. Legal observers expect the case to hinge on an outcome that may not be known for weeks — and to test how far the federal government can go in challenging a state's handling of its own elections.
