# Liège Cabinetmaker Crafts Eco-Friendly Wooden Duck

For decades, the yellow rubber duck has been the unlikely star of charity duck races, where thousands of numbered plastic birds are floated down rivers to raise money. Now, a master cabinetmaker in Liège has spent the past year turning that disposable symbol into something built to last — a hand-carved wooden duck that floats, keeps its head above water, and, he hopes, could one day replace its plastic cousin.

Christian Dalimier, an ébéniste with more than 30 years of experience, unveiled his "canard liégeois" — a registered trademark — in April 2026. RTBF, the French-language Belgian public broadcaster, [profiled](https://www.rtbf.be/short/cet-ebeniste-liegeois-fabrique-des-canards-en-bois-6489) the creation this week, presenting it as a piece of craft born from a client's offbeat idea.

"It is made of wood, it floats, it is ecological, and soon it may perhaps replace the thousands of plastic ducks adopted for charity events," RTBF wrote in its summary of the feature. "Here is Christian Dalimier's latest creation: the wooden duck."

## A 'Slightly Crazy' Request

The project began in November 2025, when a client approached Dalimier with what the craftsman himself describes as a somewhat wild idea. As he recounts on his [official product page](https://www.christian-dalimier.be/le-canard), the client wanted a wooden duck with the same characteristics as the yellow plastic version — one that floats and keeps its head out of the water no matter what.

A prototype followed a few months later, and by April 2026 the canard liégeois had officially been born as a branded product. The ducks are distributed through the Liège retailer Le Chapeau d'Or on rue de la Madeleine, while Dalimier works from his workshop on rue Large in Chênée.

## Craft First, Novelty Second

The wooden duck is less a gimmick than an extension of a long artisan practice. Dalimier's love of wood began in the Belgian Ardennes during his childhood; he trained as a cabinetmaker at Don Bosco and later honed an uncommon skill for restoring old furniture alongside recognised antique dealers. In 2001, a meeting with cabinetmaker Denis Bruyère led him to reinvent his craft through original floor marquetry.

His workshop's output has long ranged across custom furniture, interior fittings, restoration and contemporary marquetry that incorporates everything from stone and metal to precious materials such as gold and diamonds. Each duck, like his other pieces, is shaped and sanded by hand, with a pared-back design that he says is inspired by nature and Scandinavian furniture.

Nor is the duck his first animal figure. Dalimier is perhaps best known in Liège for the Souris Liégeoise — a small wooden mouse produced as magnet-keychains, doorstops and jewellery. Local guide Petit Futé [notes](https://www.petitfute.com/v68315-chenee-4032/c1085-maison-deco-jardin/c1089-artisan-travaux/c560-ebeniste/1428092-christian-dalimier.html) that "all of Liège" snaps up the little wooden mice, sometimes precious, which he creates with considerable humour. Businesses have adopted them as communication tools, and they are exhibited in galleries decorated by renowned artists.

The duck sits neatly within that same tradition: playful design wedded to high-end craftsmanship, and a growing family of Liège animal-themed objects.

## An Ecological Pitch

The environmental argument is straightforward but potent. Charity duck races depend on thousands of mass-produced plastic ducks, many of which are used once and then discarded. A wooden version, by contrast, is durable, repairable and biodegradable — a craft object rather than a disposable one.

Whether the canard liégeois could realistically displace the plastic duck at large-scale fundraising events remains an open question. Cost, volume and convenience all favour the plastic original, and a hand-made wooden duck cannot be produced by the thousand in the way a moulded plastic one can. Still, the pitch reflects a broader shift in Wallonia and across Belgium toward sustainable, locally made objects — and a media appetite, particularly at RTBF, for spotlighting the artisans keeping traditional trades alive.

The broadcaster's short vertical-video format is well suited to such personalities, offering an intimate glimpse of the workshop and the hands behind the work.

## What's Next

For now, the canard liégeois is a small-batch novelty with a serious craft pedigree — one more chapter in a career that has moved from furniture restoration to marquetry to a wooden mouse beloved across Liège. Whether the duck follows its predecessor into the city's affections, and perhaps onto the starting line of a future charity race, will depend on how many hands can shape one at a time.

What is certain is that in an age of disposable plastic, a duck that floats, is made of wood and is built to last is a quietly subversive idea. In Liège, at least, it now has a trademark to prove it.
