# Brussels Airport Flight Paths Put Health at Risk

A growing number of residents in northern Brussels say the constant roar of aircraft descending toward Brussels Airport is damaging their health and quality of life, and they are turning to the courts for relief. At the heart of the dispute is runway 07L and a satellite-guided approach path that shifted a river of low-flying jets directly over some of the Belgian capital's most densely populated and least affluent neighbourhoods.

According to an [RTBF investigation](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-sante-de-toujours-plus-de-bruxellois-survoles-mise-en-danger-mais-quel-est-le-probleme-de-brussels-airport-11798700) published on 23 October 2026, residents in Molenbeek, Koekelberg, Ganshoren, Jette, Berchem-Sainte-Agathe and Dilbeek now endure overflights every two to three minutes on days when easterly winds prevail. Dozens of communes, the Brussels regional government and citizen collectives have launched or joined legal action against the Belgian State, arguing that official measures have done little to reduce the burden.

## How a runway became a flashpoint

Brussels Airport, also known as Brussels-National and located at Zaventem, operates several runways. Under the dominant south-westerly winds, traffic uses the near-parallel runways 25R for take-offs and 25L for landings. When winds turn easterly, operations shift to runway 07R for departures and either runway 07L or runway 01 for arrivals.

Runway 01 crosses all the other runways, so aircraft cannot safely use crossing paths at full volume. Activating it cuts airport capacity by roughly 25% — from 67 movements per hour with 07L to 54 with 01. Historically, runway 07L was used only sparingly because the air traffic controller Skeyes regarded its conventional approach as dangerous.

That changed in July 2025, when landings during easterly winds were redirected onto 07L while other runways underwent works. The route stayed in service after the works ended, and the path acquired a nickname: the "Crucke route," after federal Mobility Minister Jean-Luc Crucke. [BX1](https://bx1.be/categories/news/survol-de-bruxelles-retour-sur-la-saga-de-la-route-rnp07-dite-route-crucke/) has chronicled the saga, noting how a temporary operational fix hardened into a permanent grievance.

## The safety fix that moved the noise

The technical pivot came from a safety concern. The old approach to 07L relied on a ground-based VOR radio beacon, requiring pilots to make a sharp turn during descent. Some crews reported sudden altitude losses below safety thresholds. In February 2026, Skeyes wrote to Crucke flagging "urgent safety concerns" with the VOR procedure and proposed replacing it with an RNAV approach — a satellite-based (GPS) system that draws a straight, stabilized descent.

The RNAV approach removed the dangerous turn, but it also shifted the entire flight path toward the centre of Brussels. According to a ULB study, the old VOR approach affected 161,082 people; the new RNAV path affects far more. A CGX study commissioned by the minister put the RNAV impact at roughly 458,954 people at the 45-decibel threshold, versus about 326,981 under an alternative "VOR plus GPS" scenario.

Skeyes justified the change in part by a European obligation to equip runways with RNAV. Critics counter that Europe mandates equipping runways with the system, not how often it is used — leaving the choice of routing, and the resulting noise, a matter of discretion.

## A flipped balance sheet

The numbers tell the story of a decisive shift. In 2024, runway 01 handled 8,157 landings against 3,184 on 07L. In 2025, that moved to 13,366 on 01 and 6,078 on 07L. In the first nine months of 2026, the pattern inverted entirely: only 5,957 landings on 01 versus 9,189 on 07L — the first time 07L has been used more than 01.

That matters because Brussels Airport is pursuing ambitious growth. It handled around 204,000 flights and 24.4 million passengers in 2025 and aims to exceed 350,000 movements by 2040, an expansion of more than 75%. Relying more heavily on runway 01 would undercut those targets, giving the airport and its principal shareholder, the Flemish Region, a powerful incentive to keep 07L busy.

## The health toll

The consequences for residents are increasingly well documented. A To70 noise-impact study published in May 2026 found that 104,298 people living near the airport have their sleep severely disrupted by aircraft noise, calculated using World Health Organization sleep-disturbance methodology. The Brussels Region recorded 3,815 noise-standard breaches between January and April 2026 — about 954 per month, roughly double the 2025 rate — with around 80% originating from a single measuring station in Molenbeek-Saint-Jean.

Other estimates are starker still. About 670,600 people, or 55% of Brussels' population, were exposed to aircraft noise above the Lden 45-decibel threshold in 2021 data. Ultrafine particle peaks of 65,000 to 80,000 particles per cubic centimetre have been measured near the airport, with effects felt up to seven kilometres away. Belgium's Superior Health Council has estimated the combined health cost of noise and air pollution at more than €1 billion a year, and has called for a halt to flights between 23:00 and 07:00.

For residents like Frédérik, a Molenbeek artist quoted by RTBF, the burden is physical. "And listen to that noise. That's what we endure when the flight path to runway 07L is active. Every two minutes. It's unbearable!" he said. He described the frequency of jets on windy days as higher than that of city buses.

## Measures dismissed as too modest

In early October 2026, Crucke announced a package of measures intended to ease the pressure: aircraft would begin final approach at 4,000 feet rather than 3,000, descend continuously rather than in steps, and face a tighter wind norm so that Skeyes can no longer anticipate wind shifts hours in advance. The wind threshold for switching to runways 01 and 07L would rise from 7 to 8 knots.

Experts describe the impact as marginal. Acoustician Philippe Brux estimated the measures could cut noise by 5 or 6 decibels in certain spots but only 1 to 2 decibels overall — "not much, but it's something." Specialists estimate the wind changes reduce landings on 01 and 07L by only about 5%, and continuous descent is already used in 80% of landings. The federal mediation service and the CGX study both concluded the plan yields "no significant gain" in the number of people overflown.

Flemish politicians, meanwhile, fear that easing the burden on Brussels simply shifts it over the Flemish periphery. Ben Weyts, the Flemish budget minister, warned that raising the wind threshold "will only make things worse," and the Flemish government has referred the matter to the Concertation Committee, threatening a conflict-of-interest procedure.

## A question of equity

Underlying the dispute is a pointed question about who bears the cost of a national airport. The affected north-west Brussels communes are densely populated and comparatively less affluent, with less capacity to mount legal challenges — unlike wealthier residents elsewhere. Frédéric Dobruszkes, the ULB geographer who co-authored the study comparing runway approaches, noted that disadvantaged areas "do not have the economic and cultural capital that allows them to activate all the procedures, legal for example, to make themselves heard." He raised — while insisting he had no evidence to prove intent — whether the routing could amount to "social racism." Crucke rejected any suggestion that the path was deliberately routed over poorer areas.

## The legal road ahead

The conflict is now firmly in the courts. The Brussels Region has filed an action to halt the route, joined by nine communes including Koekelberg, Ganshoren, Jette, Schaerbeek, Evere and the City of Brussels, with a hearing set for 11 January 2027. Separately, 42 families are pursuing an action before a Dutch-speaking court, with pleadings scheduled for 8 February 2027 — the first time hundreds of Brussels families have united to sue the Belgian State over overflights. The State has already been condemned at least twice for noise-rule breaches linked to runway 01 landings, and penalties of €18,000 per infraction, capped at €18 million per route, hang over the proceedings.

## What to watch

All eyes are on 31 October 2026, the deadline by which Crucke says a definitive decision on the RNP 07L route will be made — a choice that will reveal whether the "Crucke route" is here to stay or finally redrawn. Two 2027 court battles will test whether the Belgian State can be forced to change course, and whether an airport bent on 75% growth can reconcile its ambitions with growing demands — from the 19 Brussels mayors and the Superior Health Council alike — that it operate as a true urban airport, with a night-flight ban and a lighter footprint over the neighbourhoods beneath its approach paths.

For the residents of Molenbeek and Koekelberg, the coming weeks will determine whether the roar overhead remains a permanent feature of daily life — or a problem that Belgium's institutions finally decide to solve.
