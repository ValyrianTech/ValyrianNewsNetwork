# Over 50,000 Heat Deaths in Europe: Two-Thirds Climate-Linked

Extreme heat killed more than 50,000 people in European cities during the summer of 2026, the highest heat-mortality toll on record for the continent, according to a new modelling study. The research, published on 23 October, estimates that roughly two-thirds of those deaths can be attributed to human-caused climate change — a share that has climbed sharply since the start of the century.

The findings, led by the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (LSHTM) and Imperial College London and [published as an analytical note](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/grantham/publications/all-publications/heat-related-urban-mortality-attributable-to-human-induced-climate-change-in-europe-in-summer-2026.php) by the Grantham Institute, bring the human cost of accelerating warming into stark relief as Europe reckons with its hottest summer on record. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/oct/23/heat-killed-staggering-50000-people-european-cities-this-summer-experts) reported that the toll was the worst on record, with hot days pushing vulnerable bodies into overdrive and warm nights preventing them from recovering.

## A Record Margin

The precise modelled figure is 50,391 heat-related deaths across 854 cities in 30 countries — the 27 EU member states plus the UK, Norway and Switzerland. That is roughly a 71% increase over the previous deadliest year, 2022, when 29,480 heat deaths were estimated. The new toll is nearly double each of the two next-deadliest summers on record.

"In some sense, the absolute numbers of the heat-related deaths estimated across the 854 urban areas are indeed staggering," said Malcolm Mistry, a climate and health scientist at LSHTM who co-authored the study. "But perhaps what is even more alarming is the proportion of these deaths that we attribute to anthropogenic climate change."

The share of deaths due to climate breakdown has risen from about 40% at the start of the century to 65% this summer. The modelled number of deaths attributable specifically to human-caused warming in 2026 is 29,330, with a 95% estimate interval of 23,495 to 34,420.

## Where the Toll Fell

People succumbed to heat at the highest rates in Malta, Italy and Hungary, while absolute death tolls were greatest in Italy, France, Spain, Germany and the UK. The hardest-hit city was Naples, Italy, with an age-standardised rate of 815 deaths per million people. UK researchers estimated 4,354 heat deaths across 135 cities.

The deadliest window was the heatwave of 17 June to 1 July, which accounted for nearly one-third of the summer's deaths as daily average temperatures climbed more than 5°C above the 1991–2020 baseline. France alone recorded more than 5,700 excess deaths during the June heatwave.

People aged 85 and over made up almost half of the death toll, though younger people were not immune: about 833 deaths occurred among people aged 20 to 44.

## The 'Silent Killer'

Heat is often called the "silent killer" because its role in deaths is rarely recorded on death certificates. A stroke victim or lung cancer patient may be pushed past their limits by dangerous temperatures without heat ever being named as a contributing factor — meaning many families never know that rising temperatures hastened a relative's death.

In Naples, Mario Guarino, an emergency doctor at Ospedale CTO, said he saw a 15% increase in emergency room visits during the hottest weeks. He described the "defining characteristic" of the period as the complexity of health problems exacerbated by heat, with loneliness and poverty making matters worse.

"The real epidemic these days is loneliness," Guarino said. "Many elderly people are left alone during the summer, abandoned by their families, and due to their low income, they can't afford air conditioning or, if they do have it, avoid turning it on because of the cost of electricity."

## Estimates, Not a Body Count

Researchers are careful to note that the figures are modelled estimates, not directly counted deaths. The study combined established relationships between heat and mortality with observed 2026 temperature data to produce rapid, near-real-time estimates. Prior evidence shows such model estimates generally align closely with subsequent national health-agency statistics, though country-level figures may differ.

David García-García, a researcher at the mortality monitoring system at the Carlos III Health Institute in Madrid, who was not involved in the study, called it a "valuable contribution" that allows cross-continent comparison. "However, it is important to bear in mind that these are estimates based on observed temperatures and epidemiological models, which do not incorporate actual data on deaths occurring in the summer of 2026," he said.

Raquel Nunes, an associate professor in health and environment at the University of Warwick who was not involved in the research, said the findings "further confirm the lethality of extreme heat — though its effects are not always immediately apparent, which is why it is called the 'silent killer.'"

## A Continent Warming Twice as Fast

The summer of 2026 was the worst on record for western Europe, with more than half the continent seared by "very strong" heat stress that felt hotter than 38°C, according to the EU's Copernicus climate monitoring service. The report lands amid a broader pattern of grim climate news. On 21 October, the UN Environment Programme's annual *Emissions Gap Report* stated for the first time that the window to meet the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C target "has closed."

Europe is the fastest-warming continent, already about 2.5°C above pre-industrial levels — roughly twice the global average rate. As Claire Barnes, an extreme weather and climate researcher at Imperial College London and co-author of the study, put it: "I think we can say with considerable confidence that summer 2026 is likely to be the year with the highest heat-related mortality rate on record for most of Europe."

"We are far off track to limit the most severe consequences of climate change, and governments need to realise that without faster and coordinated action, things will only get worse," she said, adding: "Behind these statistics are real individuals who lost their lives, and families devastated by grief."

## An Economic Toll, Too

The human cost is mirrored by an economic one. Bloomberg reported that the summer's extreme heat wiped roughly 0.17% from EU GDP between April and September — close to €17 billion (about $18.9 billion). The broader damage from a season of burning forests and emptying rivers has been estimated at €180 billion across the continent.

## What's Next

Scientists and doctors are urging governments to act on two fronts: cutting the fossil fuel emissions that drive rising temperatures, and bolstering health systems, greening cities, rolling out air conditioning and encouraging people to check on vulnerable neighbours and relatives.

UK climate transition minister Katie White called the figures "shocking" and said they "represent thousands of families who have lost a loved one this summer due to the climate crisis."

The study's authors stress that the cities analysed account for only about 40% of the European population, meaning the true continental toll — including towns and villages — could be higher still. With Europe warming at twice the global rate, the question is no longer whether extreme heat will reshape public health on the continent, but how quickly governments respond.

As Barnes warned, the trajectory can still change — but only with "faster and coordinated action." The alternative, she said, is that "things will only get worse."
