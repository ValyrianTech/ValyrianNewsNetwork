# US Birth Rate Hits Record Low as Older Mothers Rise

The U.S. general fertility rate fell to its lowest level ever recorded in 2025, even as women in their 30s and early 40s had more babies than the year before, according to new federal data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The [CDC's National Center for Health Statistics](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db571.htm) reported 3,612,012 registered births in 2025, a decrease of less than 1% from 3,628,934 in 2024, with the general fertility rate slipping 1% to 53.2 births per 1,000 women ages 15 to 44.

The aggregate decline masks a striking counter-trend. Birth rates dropped sharply among younger women while rising among older ones, a divergence that demographers say reflects both changing preferences and shifting life circumstances.

## A Generation Delaying Motherhood

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/24/nx-s1-6012804/birth-rate-population-cdc), which first reported the findings, the one-year drop among women in their early 20s was especially pronounced. Brady Hamilton, a CDC demographer who co-authored the report, called the 6% decline in the birth rate for women ages 20 to 24 noteworthy, and noted that women in their late teens saw a 7% decline.

The CDC data show birth rates decreased for females ages 10 to 29, to record lows, increased for women ages 30 to 44, and were unchanged for women ages 45 to 49. The rate for women ages 30 to 34 rose 3%, from 93.7 to 96.3 births per 1,000 women; the rate for ages 35 to 39 rose 2%; and the rate for ages 40 to 44 ticked up 1%.

"When you have a baby in your mid-30s, what you gain in wisdom and experience you also lose in terms of energy," Sarah Harris, 36, of Vermont, told NPR. Harris was laid off four months after her daughter was born and said the high cost of raising a child convinced her that one is enough.

## Choice and Affordability

The rise in older motherhood reflects more than personal preference. Dr. Tanise Branche, medical director of labor and delivery at Temple Health in Philadelphia, told NPR that more of her younger patients are following a different life script, prioritizing careers and stability before children. Branche, who was pregnant with her fourth child at 42, said she sees the net positive as having a choice.

A separate [study released by the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta](https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/publications/policy-hub-papers/2026/09/30/08-declining-birth-rates-in-us-its-not-only-about-preferences) in September found that Gen Z women are dramatically less likely to want children than previous generations. The research identified two forces behind the decline: a sharp drop in the desire to have children among the youngest cohort, and an increase in the medical difficulty of conceiving among all women since the Baby Boomer generation.

"The fact that there are a lot of people who still say they want families, but don't feel like they're able to have them [is a concern]," Anna Louie Sussman, author of *Inconceivable*, told NPR. "Young people say, I can barely take care of myself I can't imagine taking care of someone else."

## A Demographic Shift With Consequences

The cumulative effect is a nation growing older and, in places, shrinking. Roughly 710,000 fewer children were born in the U.S. last year compared with the 2007 peak, according to NPR. The [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/older-adults-outnumber-children.html) reports that the under-18 population has fallen steadily, dropping by more than 1 million from 2010 to 2020, and that older adults now outnumber children in 11 states and nearly half of U.S. counties.

The median age of the U.S. population reached [39.4 years in 2025](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/04/age-and-sex.html), up from 39.2 in 2024, according to the Census Bureau. More states, including Vermont, are seeing actual population decline, a trend with implications for schools, health care, and the workforce.

"I think about it a lot because I live in a very small place where we're hoping to keep the doors of our elementary school open for as long as we can," Harris told NPR.

There were some positive maternal-health signals in the data. The percentage of mothers beginning prenatal care in the first trimester rose to 76.3% in 2025, with increases for all mothers under 40. The cesarean delivery rate held flat at 32.4%, falling only for mothers ages 20 to 29.

## What to Watch

As [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/us-fertility-rate-declines-record-low-rcna604439) noted, researchers say the long decline largely reflects a drop in teen pregnancies and a rise in women delaying childbirth until their 30s. A century ago, the U.S. fertility rate was roughly double its current level.

The open question is whether policy can change the trajectory. Experts told NPR that women might choose to have more children with better supports, including affordable housing, daycare, and health insurance. For now, the data point to a country where motherhood is increasingly delayed, more deliberate, and less common, with consequences that will play out across the coming decades.
