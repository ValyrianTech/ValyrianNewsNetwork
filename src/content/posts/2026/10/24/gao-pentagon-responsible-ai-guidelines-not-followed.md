# GAO: Pentagon Often Skips Its Own Responsible AI Rules

The U.S. Department of Defense does not consistently follow its own guidelines for responsible artificial intelligence when developing AI-enabled weapons and tools, according to a Government Accountability Office report that raises fresh questions about oversight of military AI. The 24-page review, published October 14 as [GAO-27-107837](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-107837) and reported by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/23/nx-s1-6012296/ai-department-of-defense-war) on October 23, found gaps in monitoring, staffing and internal coordination—even as the Pentagon accelerates toward what Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth calls an "AI-first" warfighting force.

## What the Report Found

GAO surveyed 10 Defense Department officials overseeing 10 AI-enabled projects that automatically identify targets, process battlefield surveillance and improve tactical decision-making. Those projects are a fraction of the more than 300 AI efforts under development at the Pentagon.

Six of the 10 officials said their teams had identified risks with using the projects "in an operational environment." Four said their projects' requirements were not "aligned with Responsible AI tenets." And three said they did not know whether their projects would be continuously monitored after deployment—despite the guidelines' emphasis on real-time monitoring.

Most projects had taken at least initial steps toward compliance, GAO noted, and some were still in early stages. But the findings point to a structural problem: the Pentagon's six responsible AI tenets, laid out in a 2021 memo, were never binding rules.

"It's a memo, right, at the end of the day, so it's not a department instruction," said Alissa Czyz, the GAO director who led the review. "It's something that the department has signaled that should be implemented."

## An Oversight Structure Under Strain

The report documents weaknesses in the bodies meant to enforce those tenets. The Responsible AI Working Council, run by the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office (CDAO), did not meet from late 2024 until August 2025 and is at risk of lapsing again because it lacks a charter defining its roles and meeting frequency. The Responsible AI Division is also badly understaffed: authorized to hire 10 people in 2023, it had filled only three positions as of February 2026. GAO said the Pentagon "is unlikely to fill vacant positions or increase staffing."

"That's a very small number of folks tasked with a large responsibility of responsible AI throughout the department," Czyz said.

GAO issued two recommendations: that the Secretary of Defense direct the CDAO to issue a charter for the working council, and that the Secretary ensure the division's workforce needs are addressed. The Pentagon concurred with both by email but had not implemented them as of October 22, a GAO representative confirmed to NPR. The Pentagon did not respond to NPR's request for comment.

## Racing Toward 'AI-First'

The findings land amid a sharp policy shift. Under Hegseth, the Pentagon has reframed "responsible AI" around "objectively truthful" and lawful use rather than ethical oversight and monitoring, and has pushed aggressively to speed adoption. In a January speech, he warned that the risks of moving too slowly outweigh the impacts of "imperfect alignment."

"That old era ends today," Hegseth said, according to [DefenseScoop](https://defensescoop.com/2026/01/13/hegseth-ai-tech-hubs-reorganization-dod-dow/). "We are done running a peacetime science fair while our potential adversaries are running a wartime arms race."

The administration's posture has extended into procurement. A separate October 20 memo embedded "truth-seeking" and "ideological neutrality" requirements into AI procurement templates, as [DefenseScoop reported](https://defensescoop.com/2026/10/23/pentagon-guidance-ai-procurement-trump-directive/). The Pentagon has also announced a new Autonomous Warfare Command focused on drones and autonomous weapons.

## Why It Matters

The stakes are not abstract. AI researchers and international organizations have long urged strict regulation of weapons that could become unpredictable or operate with little human control, warning of risks to soldiers and civilians and the danger of rapid escalation. The [International Committee of the Red Cross](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-position-autonomous-weapon-systems) has called for binding limits on autonomous weapon systems.

The GAO findings also echo earlier warnings. A prior review, [GAO-24-105645](http://www.gao.gov/product_recommendations/GAO-24-105645), found that the Pentagon had not defined responsibilities for AI collaboration or addressed workforce gaps—recommendations that remain open. GAO now suggests Congress consider directing the department to implement those earlier recommendations, including defining roles across DOD organizations and tackling AI workforce challenges.

The tension is clear: the Pentagon's own guidance is soft and its oversight bodies are thinly staffed, while its political leadership explicitly deprioritizes slow, committee-based review. Whether the department can reconcile speed with accountability may define how safely military AI evolves.

## What's Next

Both of GAO's new recommendations remain open, and Congress has the option to apply pressure through legislation or funding. Watch for whether the Responsible AI Working Council receives a formal charter, whether the division's staffing grows, and how the new Autonomous Warfare Command incorporates—or bypasses—the tenets the Pentagon says it values.
