# Europe Erupts: Brussels Arrests, Paris Water Cannon

A simultaneous wave of protests swept across Europe on Saturday, placing the continent's security forces on high alert as Brussels police detained nine people and French authorities deployed a water cannon at the close of a mass demonstration in Paris. The unrest, which touched everything from Italian fuel prices to Spanish pensions, reflects a broad wave of public anger now playing out on multiple fronts across Europe at once.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/politie-in-parijs-zet-waterkanon-in-aan-eindpunt-van-betoging-9~1792507723130/), the Brussels demonstration drew roughly 400 people to the Albertina Square following calls circulated on social media, with no official march planned. The crowd moved toward Anderlecht and Saint-Gilles before splitting into smaller groups at Brussels-South station, where police ended the gathering around 15:30.

## A Tense But Contained Brussels

Belgian police struck a measured tone in their post-operation assessment. Five people were administratively detained for disturbing public order, while four were subject to judicial arrests for possession of prohibited weapons and incitement to riot, the Brussels police force said in a written statement. Authorities reported no other notable incidents.

The Belgian capital had braced for the worst. Retailers along the Rue Neuve boarded up shopfronts preemptively, and the city banned shared e-steps and bikes from its central Pentagon district from Friday morning until Saturday night, after such vehicles had repeatedly been vandalized and set alight during earlier protests. At one point police deployed a water cannon near Lemonnier, close to the South station, and used tear gas and pepper spray at the Jacques Franck square in Saint-Gilles.

Some demonstrators arrived prepared for confrontation — youths aged 10 to 16 among them, though mostly activists — equipped with ski goggles and helmets. A handful briefly tried, unsuccessfully, to set scooters and bicycles ablaze. Legal experts noted the pre-emptive confiscation of masks and potential weapons helped keep the day from spiraling.

## Paris: Calm Until the Final Stretch

In Paris, the day followed the pattern that has become familiar over the past week. Police counted about 110,000 marchers in the capital, and the route was rerouted at the last minute from Bastille–Nation to Porte d'Italie–Porte de Vincennes. The march remained peaceful until the endpoint, where confrontations erupted around 17:30.

Police said more than 500 rioters turned on officers, hurling projectiles before police fired tear gas and brought in a water cannon to disperse the crowd. Authorities reported 65 arrests in Paris and more than 3,000 checks. Nationwide, the Interior Ministry put turnout at 410,000, with roughly 1,000 arrests reported by late evening — a figure expected to rise.

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said marches over the cost of living "passed off well" but that officers later faced "an extremely elevated level of violence" from what he described as "ultra-left" groups. VRT NWS journalist Steven Decraene, reporting from the French capital, offered a more granular read: "As last week, the gathering degenerated into riots and vandalism, but not of the same order as last week. The police are better prepared and present in greater numbers than last week. So the situation is more or less under control."

## France as the Epicenter

The scale of the French mobilization dwarfed events elsewhere. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/23/france-police-demonstrations-civil-unrest) reported that France deployed about 70,000 police and gendarmes nationwide on Saturday — a roughly 20 percent increase over the previous weekend — including elite units normally reserved for counter-terrorism and hostage rescues.

The government's strategy has been to contain and channel rather than suppress outright, though the line has been contested. Authorities declared bans on protests in central Lyon, Marseille, Nice, Toulon and Bordeaux. In Paris, a march from Place de la Bastille was authorized but barred from the city center, while a separate student and gilets jaunes march was not permitted. Partial night-time curfews were imposed in parts of several cities, and the Louvre, Musée d'Orsay and Picasso Museum were closed on Saturday for security reasons.

The crisis has deep roots. The movement began in September in a Créteil high school over staff shortages and dilapidated buildings, then spread nationwide with strikes, blockades and high-school demonstrations. It has since broadened into a wider revolt over living costs and government policy, six months before France's presidential election.

[The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/22/macron-ban-illegitimate-demonstrations-france-unrest) reported that President Emmanuel Macron said on Thursday that "illegitimate" demonstrations would be banned, insisting that "the priority is a return to calm." On Friday he held a 2.5-hour meeting with about 20 student representatives at the Élysée, promising a reform of the Parcoursup university admissions platform. Students said they would keep mobilizing until concrete measures arrive, and were due to meet Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu on Monday.

The preceding weekend brought some of the worst street violence in years. In Limoges, the town hall was set on fire and largely destroyed; between 200 and 300 shops were ransacked in Lyon. Justice Minister Gérald Darmanin said 174 of some 2,500 people arrested since then had been remanded in custody, with more than 900 being prosecuted.

## A Continent-Wide Wave

What distinguishes this moment is that the grievances are simultaneous and cross-border. In Italy, thousands protested against the high cost of living, fuel prices and defense spending, with slogans against rearmament and NATO. Police used batons in Milan, tear gas and water cannon in Bologna, and pushed back protesters with shields in Turin; demonstrations also unfolded in Rome, Naples and Florence. The Italian government deployed 500 soldiers to Milan, without police powers.

In Spain, some 25,000 people marched through Madrid between Atocha station and Calle de Alcalá to defend the state pension system under banners reading "guaranteed pensions" — a flashpoint weeks before a snap parliamentary election. In Albania, the 12th day of student-led protests in Tirana saw police use water cannon, tear gas and rubber bullets at the Education Ministry after demonstrators threw paving stones, prompting human-rights criticism over the "indiscriminate use" of rubber bullets.

Further afield, Greece saw peaceful student demonstrations for free public education, Portugal faced protests over housing prices, and tens of thousands of students marched in Vienna against university budget cuts.

## Analysis: Anger Without a Center

The 24 October protests do not appear to be orchestrated by a single coordinating body. They are a confluence of national grievances that happen to peak on the same day: education funding in France, pensions in Spain, fuel and defense spending in Italy, housing in Portugal, university budgets in Austria. Social media amplifies cross-border contagion — Belgian students have reportedly been in contact with French protesters — producing the appearance of a synchronized European wave.

The political stakes are considerable. In France, the unrest has become a battleground six months before the presidential election: Macron seeks calm, La France Insoumise leader Jean-Luc Mélenchon frames the movement as popular resistance and accused police of "shooting indiscriminately," while the far right, which re-elected Jordan Bardella as National Rally president with 99 percent of the vote in Orléans, capitalizes on public-order fears. Across Europe, the far right has seized on the violence to push law-and-order and anti-immigration agendas.

## What's Next

The gilets jaunes movement has already called a further action for Saturday 31 October, with demonstrations and sit-ins planned outside the Assemblée Nationale and town halls, declaring that "there is anger in the country that is not heard by the authorities." On Monday, Macron's government faces a meeting with education stakeholders over the students' ten-point plan, including a reform of study grants and the creation of 250,000 additional places in higher education.

Whether this movement becomes a sustained, election-shaping force across Europe or remains a series of episodic clashes is the key open question. For now, the continent's capitals are bracing for a long autumn.
