# Maël's CPAS case exposes welfare work trap

When Maël sent his pay slips to his local CPAS, he expected nothing more than a routine adjustment. Instead, the welfare centre that has supported him since he left home at 18 suspended his income for weeks while it recalculated his file. His Revenu d'Intégration Sociale (RIS), due on 1 August, arrived on 1 September. The same happened in September. His crime: earning a few hundred euros in a communications internship.

The case, [reported by RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/mael-beneficie-du-ris-en-tant-qu-etudiant-le-cpas-ne-m-encourage-pas-a-travailler-11804694), is small in itself. But it captures a discomfort at the heart of Belgium's welfare architecture: a system built to make work pay that, in practice, can punish the very beneficiaries it is meant to encourage. Set against the strain of the 2026 federal unemployment reform, Maël's experience raises uncomfortable questions about how the CPAS handles the transition from welfare to employment.

## What happened to Maël

Maël has received CPAS support since he was 18, when he moved to Brussels to study. Thanks to the RIS, he rents a room in a shared flat in Molenbeek. He has just graduated and is now looking for work.

Over the summer he took on paid freelance work as a self-employed person in communications, earning 250 euros in July and 210 in August. Under the socioprofessional exemption rules, a beneficiary may earn small amounts without losing part of their integration income. The ceiling was 315.67 euros a month when Maël worked, rising to 321.99 euros from 1 September, according to [SPP Intégration Sociale](https://primabook.mi-is.be/fr/droit-lintegration-sociale/montants-ris). Both of Maël's payments fell well under the threshold.

"When you're at the CPAS, you have the right to work for small amounts without losing part of the integration income," Maël told RTBF. "What happens is that the CPAS recalculates everything. In the meantime, it suspends my aid."

With no savings left, Maël says he relied on the goodwill of his landlord to bridge the gap. "The payment that should have arrived on the first of August was sent on the first of September. And same again in September. I had no savings left, I had used them to live the previous month. Luckily I have a very understanding landlord!"

## A legal procedure, but a heavy cost

The CPAS de Molenbeek confirmed to RTBF that the delays follow established procedure. By law, the centre has 30 days to re-examine a beneficiary's file and decide whether to maintain the allocation, then eight more days to make the payment. "No preferential or accelerated treatment of requests is applied," the CPAS said.

The Fédération des CPAS bruxellois agreed the delay is "not abnormal," describing it as the product of "necessary verifications" that are broadly similar in other communes. Asked whether waiting times had lengthened since the unemployment reform placed extra strain on the CPAS, the federation said: "Not substantially."

Yet the arithmetic is unforgiving. For a beneficiary living close to the margin, a six-to-eight-week gap between expected and actual payment can mean borrowing, falling behind on rent, or turning down the very work the system is designed to reward. In Brussels, social workers have each taken on roughly twenty additional files since the reform began.

The mechanics are confirmed by [Droits Quotidiens](https://www.droitsquotidiens.be/fr/question/je-trouve-un-job-etudiant-vais-je-garder-mon-ris), which explains that the socioprofessional exemption applies to student income provided the beneficiary follows full-time studies, has signed an individualised social integration project (PIIS), and was under 25 when their RIS entitlement began. There is no difference between students with and without study grants.

## A welfare system under historic strain

Maël's case lands at a moment of acute pressure. Belgium's federal unemployment reform, introduced by the programme law of 18 July 2025, has pushed large numbers of people off unemployment and onto the CPAS. According to the [Brussels Governance Monitor](https://governance.brussels/fr/dossiers/cpas-bruxellois), 26,986 Brussels residents reached the end of their unemployment benefit between January and June 2026, or 27.2% of the national total. Some 43.9% of those went on to obtain a revenu d'intégration.

Molenbeek, where Maël lives, is on the front line. As of 31 August 2026, the commune's CPAS counted 1,740 files directly linked to unemployment exclusions, and projected 16.3 million euros of RIS costs would remain on its own books from 2027 to 2029 as federal reimbursement tapers off.

The human impact of that overload is visible elsewhere in Brussels. At the [CPAS of Ixelles](https://www.rtbf.be/article/cpas-d-ixelles-on-est-vraiment-a-flux-tendu-face-a-l-afflux-des-exclus-du-chomage-11713175), 1,071 people presented themselves after being excluded from unemployment in the first two waves, and new RIS requests in January 2026 were up 59% on the year before. "We are really stretched thin, a bit like tightrope walkers on a wire," said Françoise Verheyden, who coordinates the centre's social workers. The service hired 14 additional social workers to cope.

Against that backdrop, the federation's insistence that delays have not grown "substantially" longer is not necessarily contradictory. The statutory clock may still be met even as the surrounding workload swells. But the margin for error narrows, and the cost of any delay falls hardest on those with the least cushion.

## The employment trap

The contradiction at the centre of Maël's story is one the sector itself has flagged. The Fédération des CPAS bruxellois has argued that to raise the employment rate of RIS beneficiaries, it is essential to avoid "employment traps"—work must be financially worthwhile, and the advantages of a job must be clear enough to draw people into the labour market for the long term.

Maël's experience is a case study in how administrative friction can undercut that goal. The exemption is designed to make a first job pay. Yet the re-examination it triggers—suspending aid for up to 38 days—can create weeks of instability precisely when someone starts earning. For a beneficiary without savings, the lesson is not that work pays but that work is risky.

"What frustrates me is that we should be encouraged to work instead of being plunged into more anxiety and precariousness," Maël said. "Moreover, the jobs I did corresponded to my studies. How do you build stability when every small income earned can lead to weeks of waiting before getting your aid back?"

There are limits to what the case proves. It is one person's account, corroborated on its mechanics—the timing rules, the exemption, the suspension—by the CPAS, the federation and legal information services, but not established as representative. The CPAS advises beneficiaries to notify it as early as possible when they start work, and it is not reported whether Maël did so promptly. Some of the delay may have been avoidable; the structural tension is not.

## What to watch

The question now is whether the re-examination mechanism itself will change. One option, floated by critics of the current system, would be to allow a provisional continuation of payment while a file is recalculated, so that beneficiaries moving into work are not left without income. Whether the federal or regional level takes that up remains open.

The broader funding picture will shape the answer. Brussels local authorities have warned of a structural shortfall, with the CPAS federation and the Conference of Mayors estimating a 1.718 billion euro gap across 2025-2029, of which federal compensation covers only about a quarter. As the unemployment reform continues to funnel new claimants through CPAS doors, the pressure on the 30-plus-8-day clock will only grow.

For Maël, the immediate future is a job search. His case may be a single story, but it asks a question that applies to thousands: in a system meant to reward work, why does taking a first step toward it so often mean weeks of uncertainty?
