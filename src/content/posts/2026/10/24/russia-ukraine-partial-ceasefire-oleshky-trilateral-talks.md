# Russia and Ukraine Agree Partial Ceasefire in Oleshky as Trilateral Talks Loom

Russia and Ukraine have separately confirmed a localized, temporary ceasefire in the Russian-occupied front-line town of Oleshky in Ukraine's Kherson region — a narrow humanitarian arrangement that both sides say will enable aid deliveries and civilian evacuations. The truce comes as Washington, Kyiv and Moscow edge toward a new round of trilateral talks, potentially in Abu Dhabi as early as next month.

According to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/world/20261024/6f76b535d9c24b0a9204c30dea74ca59/c.html), citing Agence France-Presse and Reuters, the two militaries confirmed that the ceasefire would take effect at 01:00 GMT on October 27 — two days earlier than the October 29 start date the White House had earlier announced. That discrepancy has not been fully explained.

The Xinhua report describes the deal as confined to a single town and not a broader peace agreement. Its stated purpose is to open a humanitarian corridor into Oleshky, deliver aid, and evacuate the remaining civilian population. The report identifies US First Lady Melania Trump as a central coordinator of the arrangement, and says both sides credited her role in brokering it; the White House issued a statement on October 23 describing her as having secured the ceasefire. The arrangement stops well short of a peace settlement.

## A Town on the Brink

Oleshky lies on the east bank of the Dnipro River, across from Ukrainian-controlled Kherson city, and has been a front-line war zone since falling under Russian occupation in the early days of the 2022 invasion. According to the White House account cited by Xinhua, most residents have already left, but an estimated 1,700 to 2,000 civilians remain — primarily elderly residents unable to leave — along with about 50 children. Ukrainian officials have described acute shortages of food, water and medicine, with mined roads and drone attacks making evacuation near-impossible.

According to Xinhua, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov confirmed that Russian forces were working to coordinate the truce. "The military is working to coordinate a ceasefire in that area to facilitate this humanitarian mission. Those who wish to leave the settlement will be able to do so. Naturally, this requires extensive and complex preparation," he said. Peskov declined to give specifics on evacuation routes or duration, noting only that the operation would "certainly take at least a few days."

## The Mechanics of a Fragile Truce

Dmytro Lubinets, Ukraine's parliamentary human rights commissioner and the lead negotiator on the ceasefire, cautioned that the operation would be painstaking. In a video address cited by Xinhua, he said the truce was hard-won and that the operation "will take place gradually; it is extremely complex to carry out." Demining of the roads into Oleshky must precede any aid delivery or evacuation — a step that could take days and carries substantial risk. He did not detail a monitoring mechanism or the terms of aid delivery.

No monitoring mechanism or defined duration has been publicly detailed, leaving the arrangement vulnerable to collapse. Russia's Chief of the General Staff, Valery Gerasimov, said Russian forces would ensure the "safe entry of humanitarian convoys" into the town, according to the Associated Press account cited by Xinhua.

## Trilateral Talks in the Offing

The ceasefire coincides with fresh momentum toward renewed US–Ukraine–Russia negotiations. According to Xinhua, a US official said on October 23 that the three countries could meet within two weeks in Abu Dhabi or Switzerland, while Peskov confirmed the same day that trilateral talks were planned for early November in the Emirati capital. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said the exact date depends on Moscow and that Kyiv hopes the talks are held soon.

Russian President Vladimir Putin, in a televised interview broadcast on October 22, stressed that any talks would focus on how to guarantee Russian and Ukrainian security after a peace agreement is reached — not merely on a "short-term" ceasefire. Zelensky, by contrast, has argued the process must escalate from the technical level to a leaders' summit, because "key issues can only be resolved at the highest level."

## A Diplomatic Opening, With Caveats

The thaw is unfolding against a backdrop of rising US–Ukraine friction. Xinhua reports that Kyiv has recently accused Washington of restricting Ukrainian forces' use of the Starshield satellite communications system, of failing to respond to Ukraine's shortage of Patriot interceptor missiles, and of easing sanctions on Russian diesel trade.

The Xinhua analysis attributes the diplomatic push partly to US domestic politics, with the administration seeking a deliverable ahead of November's midterm elections, and notes that both Russia and Ukraine have incentives to engage Washington. It also cautions that core disagreements over territorial division and security guarantees remain unresolved — meaning that resuming talks does not guarantee agreement.

## What to Watch

The immediate test is whether the truce holds from October 27 and whether demining can proceed quickly enough to let aid and evacuations begin within days. Beyond that, the focus shifts to Abu Dhabi: whether the early-November talks materialize, whether they elevate from technical discussions to leadership level, and whether a single-town humanitarian pause can serve as a stepping stone toward something larger — or remains an isolated, politically timed exception in a war with no end in sight.
