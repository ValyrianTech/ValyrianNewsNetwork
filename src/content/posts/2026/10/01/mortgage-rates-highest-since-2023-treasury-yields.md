# Mortgage Rates Hit Highest Since 2023 on Treasury Surge

U.S. mortgage rates have climbed to their highest level in nearly three years, with the average 30-year fixed rate reaching 7.6% late Wednesday — a direct consequence of a deepening selloff in the Treasury market that pushed benchmark borrowing costs to their loftiest levels in more than two decades.

The yield on the 10-year Treasury note rose as high as 5.34% in early trading Thursday, its highest since 2002, while the 30-year Treasury yield spiked to 5.69%. As [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/economy/mortgage-rates-treasury-yields-markets-rcna600894), over the three months ending Wednesday the 10-year yield posted its largest quarterly surge since 1994.

## Why Treasury Yields Are Rising

Mortgage rates track the yield on the 10-year Treasury bond. As bonds sell off, their yields rise — and those higher yields translate directly into costlier borrowing for consumers.

The selloff is being driven primarily by persistent inflation stemming from elevated energy prices linked to the wars in Iran and Ukraine. The average U.S. regular gasoline price remained 47% higher than in late February, when the Iran war began, while diesel prices have soared 70% over the same period.

While diesel is not typically used by consumers, it powers the shipping and farming sectors, meaning higher fuel costs quickly filter down to store shelves and amplify broader price pressures.

The bond rout is global in scope. The [Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/business/live/2026/oct/01/uk-house-prices-september-mortgage-rates-bonds-stock-markets-manufacturing-latest-news-updates) that the turmoil intensified amid fears the U.S. deficit is reaching unsustainable levels, with UK 30-year gilt yields hitting 6% for the first time since 1998. Stock markets also sold off heavily, with the pan-European Stoxx 600 falling to a three-month low.

## Inflation Data Offers Little Comfort

On Wednesday, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge appeared cooler than expected — but economists cautioned that the improvement was largely illusory, driven by a technical change in how the data are calculated.

The PCE index rose 3.4% year-over-year, below the 3.7% economists expected, while core PCE — which excludes food and energy — came in at 3%, also below forecasts. But "super core" PCE, which strips out energy and housing, rose 0.4% in September.

"Cooler on paper, hot underneath," KPMG chief economist Diane Swonk summarized the reading. "The measuring stick moved. The inflation problem did not."

On the super core figure, Swonk was blunter still: "Sticky is an understatement."

The stubborn inflation picture was reinforced by fresh business surveys. S&P Global reported last week that "price pressures intensified in September" at the fastest rate in four years, and the Institute for Supply Management's September manufacturing report flagged "a notable increase" in costs. Markets declined after the ISM data, with the S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite falling 0.3% and the Dow dropping 300 points.

## A Fed Rate Hike Comes Back Into View

With inflation running hot and energy prices continuing to climb, market odds now show an approximately 60% chance of a Federal Reserve rate hike at the central bank's early December meeting — a striking reversal from the rate-cut expectations that prevailed earlier in the year.

The Fed already raised its key rate in mid-September, defying pressure from President Donald Trump, and has signaled that further tightening may be necessary.

According to [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/treasury-10-yield-hits-highest-072955743.html), overnight-indexed swaps have fully priced in a Fed rate hike by year-end as the world's largest economy continues to show resilience despite higher borrowing costs. Strong investment in artificial-intelligence infrastructure is also adding to demand for capital, pushing up borrowing costs globally.

## The Diesel Crisis and a Looming Policy Fight

Beneath the bond market turmoil lies a fuel supply crunch that has become a central political problem for the White House ahead of November's midterm elections.

U.S. diesel prices hit a record $6.53 per gallon on Sept. 22, driven by the Iran conflict, Russia's ban on diesel exports, Ukrainian attacks on Russian refineries, and a partial closure of the Strait of Hormuz. The United States is the world's largest diesel exporter, and Trump has said he is still considering an export ban.

"We're thinking about it very seriously," Trump told a Fox News reporter on Sunday. "That can oftentimes lead to a little bit of an increase on gasoline for cars, so we're looking at it very seriously. We may do it."

But the idea has drawn firm pushback. According to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/28/diesel-oil-trump-export-ban-fuel-prices.html), analysts warn an export ban could backfire by pushing global diesel prices higher and triggering a feedback loop into U.S. gasoline prices as refinery runs adjust.

"Restricting U.S. energy exports would only compound the problem — exacerbating refining challenges and ultimately hurting consumers," said Mike Sommers, CEO of the American Petroleum Institute. "The answer is more supply and more flexibility — not new restrictions that risk making a difficult situation worse."

Commodity strategists at Morgan Stanley echoed that concern. "Not only would diesel prices be higher globally, but there could be a feedback loop to U.S. gasoline prices as refinery runs adjust," they wrote in a research note.

Benedict George, head of European product pricing at Argus Media, told CNBC that the U.S. has supplied roughly half of Europe's diesel imports in recent months, meaning a ban could send European prices to "a new unprecedented level." He cautioned that no measure has yet been announced and that it remains "very unclear whether there will be a measure at all."

The Trump administration is also pressing European allies, including Germany and France, to release emergency diesel stockpiles. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said he had raised the idea with his French counterpart and that Washington "would love to have a collaborative response." The European Commission said it would hold an "important" meeting with the International Energy Agency on Friday about diesel supplies.

## What It Means for Homebuyers

The combination of rising mortgage rates and near-record home prices is squeezing affordability on multiple fronts. The average national home price stood near a record $429,100 in August, while unsold inventory reached 1.62 million homes — the highest in more than a decade.

The median age of a first-time homebuyer has reached 40, an all-time high, and a record 25.2 million adults under 35 lived with their parents last year, underscoring how far the traditional path to homeownership has receded for younger Americans.

"Affordability concerns extend beyond the government's inflation measures," economists at PNC wrote, adding that "some of its methods may not fully capture the experience of consumers, particularly prospective homebuyers." They offered a sober near-term view: "There may be some relief coming on affordability, but in the near term the cost of living will continue to stress households."

## What to Watch

Several signals in the coming weeks will determine whether the pressure on mortgage rates eases or intensifies. The Federal Reserve's December meeting is the pivotal event: a hike would push consumer borrowing costs higher still, while a pause could offer modest relief. In the interim, the trajectory of energy prices — and any decision on a diesel export ban — will shape both the inflation outlook and the direction of Treasury yields.

For now, the bond market is sending an unambiguous message: with inflation still running hot, the era of cheap money remains firmly out of reach.
