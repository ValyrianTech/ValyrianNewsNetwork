# Supreme Court to Weigh Trump Immigrant Detention Policy

The Supreme Court announced Thursday that it will decide whether the Trump administration may detain immigrants facing deportation indefinitely without giving them a chance to argue for release on bond — a case that could define the reach of executive power over immigration enforcement. At issue in Rhoney v. Barbosa da Cunha is the administration's mandatory detention policy, which applies to noncitizens who entered the country without formal admission, including those who have lived in the United States for years and have no criminal record.

The case was one of three the justices agreed to take up ahead of the new nine-month term beginning Monday, with rulings due by the end of June. The Court's eventual decision could either endorse or end what critics call the broadest mass-detention-without-bond scheme in U.S. history.

## The Case at the Center

The dispute centers on Ricardo Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha, a Brazilian national who entered the United States without inspection in the mid-2000s. He applied for asylum in 2016, was granted a valid work permit, bought a home and ran a small business, and has never been convicted of a crime, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/supreme-court/supreme-court-decide-lawfulness-trumps-immigrant-detention-policy-rcna600251).

In September 2025, he was detained following a traffic stop in Norwood, Massachusetts, and the Department of Homeland Security initiated removal proceedings. When he requested a bond hearing, an immigration judge denied it on the grounds that he was subject to mandatory detention without bond under federal immigration law. He then filed a federal habeas petition arguing that his detention without a hearing violated the Constitution's Due Process Clause. A federal judge in New York ruled in his favor, ordering a bond hearing within 10 days, and he was released on bond in late October 2025.

The Trump administration appealed. In April 2026, the New York-based 2nd U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals rejected the mandatory detention policy, becoming the first federal appeals court to do so. The panel described the policy as the "broadest mass-detention-without-bond mandate in our nation's history for millions of noncitizens" and warned that the administration's reading of the law would "send a seismic shock through our immigration detention system and society, straining our already overcrowded detention infrastructure, incarcerating millions, separating families, and disrupting communities," as [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/supreme-court-trump-mandatory-detention-policy-rhoney-v-barbosa-da-cunha/) reported.

## A Reversal of Decades of Practice

The legal battle turns on which provision of immigration law governs people detained in the interior of the country. For roughly 30 years and across five administrations, most interior arrestees were held under a provision that allows discretionary custody and eligibility for bond. In July 2025, however, DHS issued a guidance memorandum reclassifying virtually any noncitizen who entered without formal admission as an "applicant for admission" subject to mandatory, no-bond detention — reversing that settled understanding.

The Supreme Court's docket frames the question precisely: whether the relevant statute "mandates the detention pending removal proceedings of aliens who, like respondent, are present in the United States without having been admitted," according to [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/rhoney-v-barbosa-da-cunha/).

Lower courts have fractured over the answer. The 2nd Circuit struck the policy down, while the 5th and 8th Circuits have upheld it, and hundreds of district judges have rejected it. That clean circuit split is the classic trigger for Supreme Court review, which the Court deliberately sidestepped when it left the bond-hearing question open in Jennings v. Rodriguez in 2018.

## The Stakes for Thousands

The policy's reach is vast. Thousands of people have already been detained under it, and it could apply to millions if fully expanded. ProPublica data cited by [Latin Times](https://www.latintimes.com/pardon-killed-scotuss-top-immigration-case-heres-whats-stake-latinos-now-599710) show roughly 45,000 habeas corpus petitions filed since January 2025 — a surge one federal judge in Georgia described as "an administrative judicial emergency."

The litigation is straining federal prosecutors and Justice Department offices nationwide, forcing the government to shift resources away from criminal and other civil cases. Meanwhile, the enforcement backdrop has only intensified: Immigration and Customs Enforcement detained more than 46,000 people in July 2026, surpassing the roughly 43,000 booked in June, as the administration broadens its crackdown.

The case does not concern immigrants convicted of certain crimes, who under federal law may be detained until deported, though separate lawsuits have raised questions about whether they too should be eligible for bond hearings. Rather, it tests the government's authority to hold longtime, law-abiding residents without any individualized review.

## What Both Sides Argue

"Try as it might, the government cannot twist the law to detain immigrants for months or even years on end without a bond hearing," said Michael K.T. Tan, deputy director of the ACLU's Immigrants' Rights Project. "Not only is it cruel and inhumane; it is unlawful." ACLU lawyers said in court papers that the policy is a "radical reinterpretation" of immigration law, noting that some of those detained were young children when they crossed the border and arguing that those challenging their confinement pose neither a flight risk nor a danger to the public.

Solicitor General D. John Sauer, representing the administration, called the issue a "critically important question of immigration law." He argued that the policy removes a "perverse incentive" under which people who evade detention when they enter the country receive more lenient treatment than those caught at the border, and said the litigation is "disrupting the orderly administration of immigration law."

## A Term Shaped by Pardons and Procedural Jockeying

The road to Thursday's announcement has been unusually circuitous. In late August 2026, New York Gov. Kathy Hochul granted a full and unconditional pardon to Keisy G.M., a Dominican lawful permanent resident who had spent 21 months in ICE custody without a bond hearing. The pardon eliminated the conviction that had authorized his detention, mooted the administration's case, and prompted the Supreme Court to dismiss Genalo v. Black on September 11 — vacating a scheduled October 13 argument.

Ten days later, the administration filed a replacement petition, Genalo v. D.C., seeking expedited review of the same constitutional question in a new case involving a Dominican man in the United States since 1982. If the Court accepts both cases, the 2026-27 term could yield two simultaneous landmark rulings — one on detainees with prior criminal records and one on the government's power to hold longtime residents without individualized review.

## What to Watch

The justices will hear arguments in Rhoney v. Barbosa da Cunha after the term opens Monday, with a decision expected by the end of June 2027. How the Court defines who counts as an "applicant for admission" will determine not only the fate of thousands already detained but the legal boundaries of the administration's broader immigration crackdown. Open questions remain — including whether the Court will consolidate the two detention cases or issue separate rulings, and how broadly it will define the due-process limits on holding people without a hearing.
