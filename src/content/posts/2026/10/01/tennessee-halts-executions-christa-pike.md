# Tennessee Halts Executions After Botched Christa Pike Case

Tennessee has suspended executions for the remainder of the year after a lethal injection attempt against Christa Pike, the only woman on the state's death row, failed to kill her -- leaving her alive and reportedly snoring after two doses of the sedative pentobarbital. Governor Bill Lee ordered a comprehensive third-party review and halted the state's remaining 2026 execution, while Pike was transported to an off-site hospital.

The extraordinary episode, which unfolded Wednesday evening at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution in Nashville, marks the second time in 2026 that a Tennessee execution did not proceed as planned. It has renewed scrutiny of the state's secretive capital punishment procedures and the rare -- and legally unsettled -- question of what happens when a state tries to execute someone and fails.

## What Happened in the Chamber

According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/-was-mess-tennessee-fails-execute-inmate-2-lethal-injections-rcna600863), the process began after the U.S. Supreme Court vacated a lower court's temporary stay earlier in the day, clearing the way for the execution to proceed. Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan, and Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented.

Media witnesses described a scene that quickly diverged from the expected. The chamber curtain opened for the first time at 7:26 p.m. At 7:34 p.m., Pike was heard saying, "My arm feels like it's about to burst open," according to WKRN reporter Tori Gessner, who has witnessed five executions in the state. Pike asked, "Does this happen like that?"

The curtain closed at 7:46 p.m. and reopened at 7:49 p.m., when witnesses reported Pike was still awake, her mouth fell open, she gasped, and she began snoring loudly. A second dose of the drug was administered around 8 p.m. The curtain closed for the final time at 8:06 p.m., but Pike could still be heard snoring until the microphone was cut at roughly 8:53 p.m.

"Nothing about today was normal, typical, at all," Gessner said at a news conference.

In an emergency federal court filing, Pike's lawyers said she "has not lost consciousness and still has a heartbeat" and was "audibly snoring," requesting a halt and immediate medical care. They later confirmed she was being treated at a hospital, though her condition remained unknown.

## A State Defends Its Protocol

The Tennessee Department of Correction said officials "followed every step" of the state's lawful, established protocol. Spokesperson Dorinda Carter said the "lethal injection chemical in the protocol has consistently been effective" and that the protocol "does not allow for additional procedures beyond what was carried out this evening."

Pike's attorneys sharply disputed that framing. "Tonight the State of Tennessee once again failed to carry out a lawful execution," they said in a statement, adding that "the concerns raised by Ms Pike proved to be true: difficult vein access, blown veins, degraded pentobarbital, no emergency medical care available when things inevitably go wrong."

Governor Lee said Thursday he was "disappointed ... about what happened last night" and acknowledged unanswered questions. "Tennesseans deserve a justice system that is fair, that's consistent, that is faithfully administered," he said, while vowing to "uphold the sentences that Tennessee juries have handed down."

## A Rare and Uncharted Outcome

Legal scholars say the case is nearly without precedent. Austin Sarat, an expert on failed executions, distinguished a "failed" execution -- in which the person remains alive -- from a "botched" one, in which something goes wrong but the person still dies. He noted there have been fewer than 10 true failed executions in the last 80 years.

Most prior failed lethal injections involved an inability to find a vein before drugs flowed. Pike's case is different: the drugs were administered, and she survived. Sarat called it "uncharted territory."

Robin Maher, executive director of the Death Penalty Information Center, called the attempt "singular and unparalleled." She said her organization knows of seven other people who survived medical problems due to IV failure, but none who remained alive after receiving the drugs.

Clive Stafford Smith, a human rights lawyer who has been in the execution chamber with clients, told [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/1/christa-pike-execution-fails-what-next-when-a-lethal-injection-is-botched) that failed drugs and difficult vein access were likely factors. He noted that doctors are not permitted to participate in executions in the U.S., leaving "incompetent people administering such drugs."

"I've been in the execution chamber with six of my clients and it's never the clinical process they'd have you believe," he said, questioning whether capital punishment "makes the world a better, more civilised place."

## The Case and the Woman at Its Center

Pike, 50, was convicted of the January 1995 murder of 19-year-old Colleen Slemmer, a classmate at the Knoxville Job Corps. Pike was 18 at the time; authorities said she and two teenage accomplices tortured and killed Slemmer, carving a pentagram into her chest -- a detail that fed the "satanic panic" of the era. Pike does not deny the killing.

She had been set to become the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than 200 years, according to historical records cited by the Death Penalty Information Center. Executions of women are rare nationally: since 1976, 18 women have been executed in the U.S., roughly 1 percent of all executions.

Pike's appeals centered on claims that her trial counsel failed to present evidence of severe childhood sexual abuse, which she says she suffered beginning at age 11 and which the state did not dispute at a special hearing in August. She was later diagnosed with bipolar disorder and PTSD. Her legal team had also requested an all-female firing squad, citing fear of pain and small veins -- a request that was denied.

"I was a mentally ill 18-year-old kid," Pike wrote in a clemency petition. "It took me numerous years to even realize the gravity of what I'd done."

Earlier, in a letter to NBC News shared by her spiritual adviser before she learned clemency had been denied, Pike wrote: "Whether my clemency is granted or not, I am at peace. I am not afraid to die. I'm only nervous about the process."

## The Victim's Family Reacts

May Martinez, Slemmer's mother, who wanted the execution carried out, was furious with state officials. "It was a mess," she told NBC News by phone early Thursday. "They went to try to execute her, and she didn't die."

Martinez criticized how officials communicated with the victims' family, describing being asked to leave without explanation and learning only later that the execution had failed. Asked for her message to the governor and attorney general, she was blunt: "They f----- up."

She also spoke of her ongoing grief, telling [WPLN News](https://wpln.org/post/tennessee-set-to-carry-out-rare-execution-of-a-woman/) that "not a day goes by, or a minute goes by that I do not think about Colleen," adding that her daughter would have turned 51 in late September.

## What Comes Next

As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/01/christa-pike-tennessee-governor-executions-year-pause) reported, Governor Lee's order suspends all remaining 2026 executions, including the December 3 scheduled execution of Gary Wayne Sutton. The scope and administrators of the third-party review have not been detailed.

The suspension follows a similar breakdown in May, when Tennessee called off Tony Carruthers' lethal injection after executioners could not place an IV for more than an hour; he received a one-year reprieve.

For the [second time this year](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/01/christa-pike-what-we-know-so-far), the state has acknowledged it could not carry out a sentence as intended. Pike's hospitalization now raises untested legal questions: whether she can pursue new appeals on cruel-and-unusual-punishment grounds, and whether surviving an execution has precedent for resentencing. State officials have not explained why two doses of pentobarbital failed to kill her.

Laura Porter, executive director of the U.S. Campaign to End the Death Penalty, argued that calls for a pause since May went unheeded. "The state of Tennessee has shown the world it cannot be trusted to implement capital punishment in a way that preserves human dignity and the rule of law," she told WPLN. "This has to stop now."

For now, Tennessee's death chamber is idle -- and the answers about what occurred there Wednesday night are only beginning to emerge.
