# China Filed 800,000 Corruption Cases in First Nine Months

China's discipline inspection and supervisory organs filed 800,000 corruption cases and disciplined 680,000 people between January and September 2026, according to figures published by the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection and the National Supervisory Commission (CCDI/NSC) and carried by [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261027/f31a9d1649324b168850210c243c95a4/c.html) on October 27. Among those disciplined were 105 officials at the provincial, ministerial level or above, underscoring the continued intensity of the campaign that President Xi Jinping has made a defining feature of his rule since 2012.

## The Numbers Behind the Crackdown

The cumulative notification, which covers supervision, inspection and case investigations, offers a detailed window into how the anti-corruption apparatus is performing. According to the Xinhua report, the organs received 3.506 million petition letters and visits during the period, 847,000 of them whistleblowing-type complaints, and handled 2.149 million problem leads.

The case-filing data reveals a hierarchy-wide sweep. At the apex, 73 officials at provincial/ministerial level or above were placed under investigation. Below them, authorities filed cases against 4,203 bureau-level officials, 34,000 county-level officials and 114,000 township-level cadres. The campaign also reached the grassroots: 79,000 sitting or former village Party secretaries and village committee heads were investigated.

Of the 680,000 people disciplined, 505,000 received Party disciplinary action and 222,000 faced state supervisory sanctions. The stratification of those punished mirrors the case-filing pattern: 105 at provincial/ministerial level and above, 3,680 at bureau level, 28,000 at county level, 92,000 at township level, 89,000 ordinary cadres and 467,000 rural, enterprise and other personnel.

## A Graduated Approach to Punishment

The report also details how the "Four Forms" framework — a graduated disciplinary doctrine introduced in 2015 — was applied across 1.385 million person-times. The first form, encompassing criticism and education aimed at early intervention, accounted for 696,000 cases, or 50.2% of the total. The second form, covering light discipline, made up 41%. The third form, for heavier discipline and major position changes, accounted for 4.7%, while the fourth form — serious violations resulting in expulsion and criminal referral — accounted for 4.1%.

The distribution reflects the campaign's stated "grab early, grab small" philosophy, under which the majority of minor transgressions are handled through admonition rather than punishment. The data also showed enforcement against the supply side of corruption: authorities filed cases against 29,000 bribe-givers and transferred 3,020 of them to procuratorates, a reflection of the "investigate bribe-taking and bribe-giving together" policy emphasis.

## A Shift Toward Higher-Level Targets

Set against prior reporting, the headline totals suggest the campaign's overall intensity has plateaued even as its focus has sharpened. In January-September 2024, discipline inspection organs disciplined 589,000 people, including 53 officials at provincial/ministerial level or above, according to the [Guangming Daily](https://epaper.gmw.cn/gmrb/html/2024-10/27/nw.D110000gmrb_20241027_8-02.htm). The 2026 total of 680,000 disciplined represents a modest rise over two years, but the count of senior officials disciplined has begun to climb more markedly — a shift toward bigger, more symbolically significant names rather than a broad expansion of enforcement volume.

The notification does not identify the 105 senior officials by name. Individual "tiger" cases are typically announced separately through the CCDI website, meaning the full picture of which senior figures were felled this year requires cross-referencing individual case disclosures.

## Why It Matters

China's anti-corruption architecture runs through two parallel structures: the CCDI, which handles Party discipline, and the National Supervisory Commission, a state body established in 2018 to extend oversight to all public officials, not just Party members. Together they function as the "discipline inspection and supervisory organs" that issued the notification. The periodic publication of aggregate statistics serves a dual purpose — a transparency mechanism for a system often criticized as opaque, and a deterrent signal to officials across the bureaucracy.

The framing of the campaign as targeting both "tigers and flies" — senior officials and low-level offenders alike — remains central to its public legitimacy. The heavy share of rural and enterprise personnel among the disciplined (467,000 of 680,000) and the investigation of tens of thousands of village-level cadres speak to a sustained effort to address "micro-corruption" that most directly touches ordinary citizens.

## What to Watch

The release came shortly after a major leadership document, "Stay clear-headed on the new exam and win historical initiative," was published on October 24 following the late-October plenum period — a juxtaposition that keeps anti-corruption governance in the foreground of the political calendar. Whether enforcement intensity carries into the fourth quarter will be closely watched as an indicator of the campaign's trajectory.

Two open questions remain. First, the sharp rise in senior officials disciplined invites scrutiny of which individuals and which sectors — finance, energy, defense or local government — are being prioritized. Second, the roughly 11% year-on-year growth in petition letters and visits may signal either greater public willingness to report misconduct or broader access to complaint channels; the notification offers no explanation of the drivers. For now, the figures confirm that a campaign now in its fourteenth year shows no sign of winding down.
