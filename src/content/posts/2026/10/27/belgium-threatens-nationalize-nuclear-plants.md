# Belgium Threatens to Nationalize Nuclear Plants

Belgium's federal government is preparing to forcibly nationalize the country's nuclear power plants if stalled negotiations with the French energy giant Engie fail to produce a deal before a self-imposed November 4 deadline, according to reporting carried by [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/federale-regering-dreigt-met-nationalisatie-van-kerncentrales~1790599460224/). The threat, first reported by the business newspapers De Tijd and L'Echo, marks an extraordinary escalation in a dispute over who controls Belgium's nuclear fleet and who pays its multi-billion-euro decommissioning bill.

## A negotiating lever, not yet a decision

The offices of Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) and Energy Minister Mathieu Bihet (MR) declined to confirm the plan to VRT NWS. According to the broadcaster, the timing of the leak — ahead of the deadline — serves as "an extra pressure tool in the negotiations." If carried out, it would be a "highly exceptional step" in Belgium.

Talks between the government and Engie began in April 2026, when Brussels set out to take full ownership of the nuclear park rather than merely extend the life of its two youngest reactors. A joint statement at the time announced that all dismantling activities would be halted so that "all options remain open for the Belgian state," [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/04/30/engie-federale-regering-nucleaire-activiteiten/) at the launch of the negotiations.

## How the plan B would work

Under the contingency plan, draft legal texts are reportedly ready that would allow the state to expropriate Engie's Belgian assets and compensate the company. Crucially, the government would set the compensation amount unilaterally, based on its own assessment of a fair price, according to [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/dernieres-depeches/2026/10/27/prolongation-du-nucleaire-bart-de-wever-menace-engie-les-centrales-nucleaires-dune-nationalisation-forcee-ZS6ZPW7K3FDX5K74GIE3C256FU/), citing Belga.

Engie would retain the right to challenge that valuation in court. But the government could press ahead with nuclear production while any legal dispute unfolds — giving it practical leverage even before a verdict. The stakes extend beyond ownership: the negotiations also cover responsibility for the billions of euros needed to decommission the plants.

[Business AM](https://businessam.be/belgie-overweegt-nationalisatie-van-kerncentrales-door-vastgelopen-onderhandelingen-met-engie/) noted that the closest precedent for such a move is Germany's 2022 seizure of Gazprom's European assets following Russia's invasion of Ukraine — an unusual state expropriation in a European energy market.

## An ideological inversion

The move is striking because it comes from a centre-right coalition led by the right-liberal N-VA and MR, parties that ordinarily oppose state takeovers. Nationalizing Engie's production park has long been a demand of the far-left PVDA, whose leader Raoul Hedebouw has applauded the shift. Party chairman Georges-Louis Bouchez (MR) has framed state control as strategically necessary amid geopolitical uncertainty, while suggesting the reactors could later be re-privatized.

De Wever has argued that a large share of the expected costs is already covered by the Hedera and Synatom funds — provisions for radioactive waste storage and decommissioning worth roughly €25 billion — and that the takeover "need not be a structural burden." Critics dispute that accounting, pointing to the contested decommissioning liability at the heart of the talks.

## Expert skepticism

Not everyone is convinced the threat is wise. Energy expert Ronnie Belmans of KU Leuven has warned that nationalization would take years, inject "enormous uncertainty" into the energy sector, and raise questions about whether a state operator would be more efficient — citing the financially troubled French utility EDF. He characterized the maneuver as either "a panic reaction" or "pure chest-thumping."

## What to watch

The immediate test is whether a deal lands before November 4. If it does not, Belgium could embark on a legal route that risks a prolonged court battle with Engie even as the government tries to extend the life of its nuclear fleet. Beyond the deadline, three questions loom: the precise compensation formula in the draft legislation, Engie's formal response, and whether the state intends to restart older reactors that were shut between 2022 and 2025. The outcome will shape Belgium's electricity supply, prices, and nuclear policy for decades — and, for a right-leaning government, carries both budgetary and reputational risk.
