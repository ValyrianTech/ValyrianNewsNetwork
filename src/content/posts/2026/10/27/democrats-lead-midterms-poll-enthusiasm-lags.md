# Democrats Lead Midterms Poll, but Enthusiasm Lags

Democrats hold a clear edge heading into next week's midterm elections, but the final national survey of the cycle suggests their advantage may be smaller than their supporters hope. A new [NPR/PBS News/Marist poll](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/27/nx-s1-6017479/2026-midterms-npr-poll-democrats-trump-approval) released Tuesday finds Democrats leading the generic congressional ballot by 8 points among likely voters — but flagging enthusiasm among key parts of their coalition, combined with a newly drawn political map, may limit the size of any wave.

## A Consistent, If Narrowing, Lead

On the generic ballot — which party's candidate voters would back in their district if the election were held today — Democrats lead Republicans 52% to 44% among likely voters, according to [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/poll-likely-voters-favor-democrats-as-trump-disapproval-drives-early-turnout). Among registered voters, the Democratic lead is 10 points, down from 12 in September but still within the survey's margin of error. Democrats have led every Marist survey since November 2025.

The findings were released a week before Election Day on Nov. 3 and mark the first survey this cycle in which Marist applied a likely-voter screen. The poll of 1,289 registered voters was conducted Oct. 19–20 by phone, text and online, with a margin of error of roughly 3.7 percentage points for registered voters and 3.8 points for likely voters.

Independents are breaking toward Democrats by double digits — 52% to 39% among likely voters, a 13-point gap. “The underlying factor is that Democrats are getting more Republicans than Republicans are getting Democrats,” Lee Miringoff, director of the Marist Institute for Public Opinion, told NPR. “So there's a net advantage just from some partisan crossover.” Add the independent shift, he said, and Democrats “should have a good night.”

## Trump's Drag on the GOP

The poll paints a bleak picture of President Trump's standing one week out. Just 38% of registered voters approve of his job performance and 61% disapprove — the highest disapproval Marist has recorded in either of his terms. Among likely voters, 40% approve and 59% disapprove, including 53% who strongly disapprove. His favorability among likely voters is even lower: 37% favorable to 59% unfavorable.

That unpopularity is shaping the vote. By a margin of more than 2 to 1, likely voters say their congressional vote is mainly a vote against Trump (44%) rather than for him (21%). Among independents, the ratio is more than 3 to 1. Trump is not on the ballot, but he has asked voters to “pretend” he is, holding rallies in more than a dozen states since mid-September — nearly all of them states he won in 2024.

“If your approval rating is upside down, the president doesn't usually say, 'I'm on the ballot,'” Miringoff said. “He's been helping to nationalize it to the concern of some of the congressional folks who are putting distance between themselves and him.”

## Enthusiasm Gap — With Warning Signs

Democrats do hold the enthusiasm advantage: 71% of Democratic likely voters say they are “very enthusiastic” about voting, compared with 61% of Republicans and 48% of independents. Voters who backed Kamala Harris in 2024 are 15 points more likely than Trump voters to be highly energized.

But the survey carries caution flags for Democrats. Several of their core constituencies are notably less enthusiastic. Only 42% of likely voters under 45 say they are very enthusiastic, compared with 68% of those 45 and older. Among non-white voters it is 53%; among white women with college degrees, 56%. By contrast, pro-Trump groups are more energized — MAGA voters at 68%, white women without degrees at 66% and white evangelical voters at 61%.

“Republicans are demotivated,” Democratic strategist Joel Payne told PBS NewsHour. “They are very clearly demotivated and less excited and less likely to show up.” Miringoff framed the gap as a Republican problem more than a Democratic triumph: “That's the gap that the Republicans are feeling — not because the Democrats are so enthusiastic, but I think the Republicans just are hard to drum it up.”

## Redistricting and Nerves Temper Expectations

History suggests the Democratic lead is consequential. In October 2018, Democrats held a 7-point edge on the generic ballot and went on to flip 40 House seats; in 2006 they led by 12 and won the chamber with at least 30 flips. But the map has changed. A rare mid-decade redistricting push — begun when Trump urged Republican-led states to redraw their maps after 2024, with Democrats following suit — has tilted House seats toward Republicans nationally, a structural counterweight to the Democratic popular-vote advantage.

Those dynamics appear to have bred nervousness inside the coalition. While 98% of Democratic likely voters say they will vote for a Democrat, only 81% believe the Democrat will win — a 17-point gap between intention and expectation.

“The House feels fairly settled for Democrats,” Payne said. He pointed to Republican spending in states like Kansas and Mississippi as a sign the party is defending seats it should hold easily. On the Senate, he was more cautious: “a slight lean to Democrats taking the Senate back, but it wouldn't shock me if Republicans held the Senate.” Republican strategist Melik Abdul conceded the House is “very much out of our reach” but predicted Republicans keep the Senate. “I don't think it will be the blue wave that people are suspecting,” he said.

## What's Driving Voters

The cost of living tops the list of concerns at 34%, followed closely by preserving democracy at 33%. Immigration, central to Trump's second-term agenda, trails at 12%. The priorities diverge sharply by party: Democrats rank democracy first (43%), Republicans rank the cost of living first (31%).

Economic anxiety is concrete for many. Thirty-eight percent of likely voters say their family finances worsened over the past year, while 15% say they improved. Among registered voters, the share reporting a financial hardship rose to 35%, up 6 points since September. “The economy is at the top of my list right now,” said Shun Maurice, a 47-year-old Arizona independent who leans Democratic. “They're just too expensive for the American people in general, and I don't like that.”

And 55% of likely voters say it is more important to back candidates with new ideas who will bring change — a signal of an electorate tilting against the party in power. “This year is different,” said Chris Agan, a 43-year-old Democrat in Tyler, Texas. “It feels like every opportunity to vote is a referendum against the party in power.”

## What to Watch

More than 30 million ballots have already been cast nationwide, and about 1 in 5 likely voters say they have already voted — a group that favors Democrats 68% to 31%. Those yet to vote are evenly split. Half of Republican likely voters plan to cast ballots in person on Nov. 3, compared with 31% of Democrats, making Election Day turnout a critical variable.

The poll also found a notable split on election integrity. Three-quarters of likely voters are confident their state and local governments will run fair elections, and 81% say a losing candidate should accept the results — a figure up 10 points from before the 2024 election. But Republicans lag Democrats by about 20 points on that question, and just 65% of MAGA supporters say a losing candidate should accept the outcome.

For Democrats, the path to a strong night runs through converting a durable lead among independents and a motivated base into actual votes — while overcoming the enthusiasm shortfall among young and non-white voters. For Republicans, the challenge is reversing a motivation gap that even their own strategists acknowledge. With one week to go, the poll offers both parties reason for hope — and reason for worry.
