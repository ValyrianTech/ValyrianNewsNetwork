# WWII Bomb Halts Berlin–Hamburg Rail Line in Germany

The discovery of an unexploded World War II munition at a railway station in northern Germany forced a 200-metre evacuation and briefly shut one of the country's busiest rail corridors on Tuesday, before bomb disposal experts made the device safe and lifted all restrictions.

Construction workers unearthed the device at around 08:40 local time in the ground near Müssen station, a village of roughly 1,200 inhabitants in the Herzogtum Lauenburg district of Schleswig-Holstein, east of Hamburg. Police said the find was initially treated as a "suspicious object" that could have been a warhead, according to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779341/article/2026-10-27/une-bombe-de-la-seconde-guerre-mondiale-decouverte-provoque-une-evacuation-sur), which carried an AFP wire report on the incident.

## Rail Corridor Closed

All construction work was halted and traffic on the main rail line was suspended while authorities assessed the device. The closure hit both long-distance and regional services, with the **Berlin–Hamburg main line** shut to traffic and long-distance trains rerouted across a wide area, causing cancellations and delays.

According to [BILD](https://www.bild.de/news/eilmeldung-bombenfund-bahnstrecke-hamburg-berlin-gesperrt-6ae06b7041a652332eb6d3c6), the disruption also affected the RE1 regional express service between Hamburg and Rostock, for which Deutsche Bahn laid on bus replacement services. The operator advised passengers to use alternative connections or regional trains while the operation was under way.

## Device Identified as Warhead

The bomb disposal service, known in Germany as the *Kampfmittelräumdienst*, was called in and identified the object as a warhead — designated "SAP 60" — of a presumed British air-to-ground rocket, with both fuse and explosives still intact.

"Because the fuse and explosives were still present, the warhead was recovered and will now be rendered safe by the bomb disposal service in an appropriate manner," police said, as quoted by BILD. The device was transported away and made inert rather than detonated on site, and no injuries were reported.

## Evacuation and Shelter

Residents of the Bahnhofstraße and Bergstraße were asked to leave their homes ahead of the operation, and an emergency shelter was set up in the Müssen sports hall. The exact number of people affected was not disclosed. The roughly 200-metre exclusion radius reflected the estimated blast area of a device of this size and type.

By Tuesday afternoon, the warhead had been recovered and made safe, rail restrictions were lifted and residents were allowed to return home. The [Berliner Zeitung](https://www.berliner-zeitung.de/article/bahnstrecke-berlin-hamburg-wegen-weltkriegsbombe-gesperrt-10534810) reported that ICE trains were briefly halted between Hamburg and Berlin and between Hamburg and Rostock, with the Berlin–Hamburg–Amsterdam service rerouted via Hanover between Berlin and Osnabrück before the line reopened.

## A Persistent Wartime Legacy

The incident is a routine but high-impact reminder of the durable legacy of Allied bombing during World War II. An estimated 1.5 million tonnes of bombs were dropped on Germany during the conflict, and a significant share failed to detonate. Tens of thousands of unexploded devices — known in German as *Blindgänger* — remain buried, and several thousand are found each year, often during construction, railway projects or farm work.

Le Soir noted that Germany "remains riddled with unexploded bombs, regularly discovered during construction works." The same phenomenon is familiar to Belgian readers: the country itself regularly uncovers wartime ordnance, particularly in the Ardennes — a battleground of the 1944 Battle of the Bulge — and around the World War I battlefields of Flanders.

## Why a Single Find Matters

The newsworthiness of Tuesday's discovery lies less in the device itself than in its location. The Berlin–Hamburg line is one of Germany's most heavily used long-distance routes, and closing it forces significant rerouting that cascades delays across the network. A single warhead, safely removed within hours, thus rippled across national transport schedules.

German bomb-disposal protocols and rail-emergency procedures resolved the incident quickly and without casualties. But the episode underlines a public-safety challenge that persists more than 80 years after the war ended — and one that will continue to surface, literally, wherever construction digs into ground that was once a battlefield.

## What to Watch

Authorities have not indicated any wider risk in the area, and normal rail service has resumed. The broader question is one of frequency: as infrastructure and construction projects expand, so too will the odds of unearthing further wartime munitions — a reminder that the physical remains of the 20th century's largest conflict are still with us.
