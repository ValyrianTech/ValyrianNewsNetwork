# Greenhouse Gas Levels Hit Unprecedented Record, UN Warns

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases reached new record highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) reported on Tuesday, warning that the planet is locked into further warming and more extreme weather for decades to come.

Globally averaged surface concentrations of carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O) all climbed to unprecedented levels last year, according to the WMO's annual Greenhouse Gas Bulletin, released shortly before the COP31 UN climate conference opens in Antalya, Türkiye, next month. The figures drew a blunt assessment from the UN agency: "Another year, another new record."

The Bulletin measures concentrations — the accumulated amount of gas in the atmosphere — rather than annual emissions. Because CO2 persists for centuries, past emissions continue to shape the climate long after they are released, making the record levels a signal of committed future warming rather than a single-year fluctuation.

## A Record That Keeps Breaking

The 2025 figures set new benchmarks across all three major gases. CO2 averaged 426.8 parts per million, or 153% of the pre-industrial baseline of 1750, [the WMO reported](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high). Methane reached 1,948 parts per billion, 267% of the 1750 level, while nitrous oxide climbed to 339.1 parts per billion, or 126% above pre-industrial levels.

"Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue. CO2 has a very long life-time and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries."

The pace of accumulation has itself accelerated. According to the full [WMO Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22](https://wmo.int/resources/publication-series/greenhouse-gas-bulletin/no-22), CO2 growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from roughly 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year during the 2011–2020 decade. When the Bulletin series began in 2004, CO2 stood at 377.5 ppm — 49 ppm below the current level.

Between 2024 and 2025, CO2 rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional 2023/2024 surge. WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed the moderation to a rebound in land carbon sinks and fewer wildfire emissions in 2025 after the strong 2023/2024 El Niño.

"However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," she cautioned. "We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026 because of fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño which will impact land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024."

## Concentrations Versus Emissions

A central point of the Bulletin is the distinction between concentrations and emissions — a gap that frequently muddies public debate. About half of all CO2 emitted each year remains in the atmosphere; the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans. Those natural sinks are themselves under strain, meaning human emissions and the planet's capacity to absorb them are moving in opposite directions.

CO2 accounts for roughly 66% of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases, methane about 16% and nitrous oxide about 6%, [Le Soir reported](https://www.lesoir.be/779336/article/2026-10-27/une-nouvelle-annee-un-nouveau-record-la-concentration-gaz-effet-de-serre-atteint). Methane is roughly 65% human-caused — from cattle, rice farming, fossil fuel extraction, landfills and biomass burning — with the remaining 35% coming from natural sources such as wetlands that are themselves sensitive to a warming climate.

Separately, NOAA's Annual Greenhouse Gas Index shows radiative forcing by long-lived greenhouse gases rose 56% between 1990 and 2025, with CO2 responsible for about 81% of that increase.

The Bulletin also flags two less abundant but potent gases tied to the energy transition. Sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), used as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, now sits at a record high and carries a global warming potential around 24,300 times that of CO2, with an atmospheric lifetime exceeding 1,000 years. Molecular hydrogen, increasingly used as a clean-energy carrier, is not itself a greenhouse gas but exerts an indirect warming effect, and its concentration rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024 — a warning that leakage from production, pipelines and fuel cells could erode the climate benefits of hydrogen adoption.

## Why It Matters Now

The Bulletin is timed to feed directly into COP31, where negotiators will attempt to move from pledges to implementation. The record concentrations underscore the central problem facing the talks: even as the shift to clean energy accelerates, the atmosphere keeps accumulating the gases that drive warming, and the margin for error continues to narrow.

"Every additional part per million matters because it traps additional heat," Barrett said. "In effect, we are supercharging the ocean and the atmosphere. That means that heatwaves, tropical cyclones, and rainstorms are intensifying faster than our historical baselines prepared us for."

She nonetheless framed the energy transition as essential, provided it is carefully managed. "The energy transition is essential and welcome," she said. "But as with every transition, it has to be done in a smart way and informed by environmental observations."

## What to Watch

Two factors will shape whether 2026 brings yet another record. First, the expected development of a powerful El Niño could again weaken land carbon sinks and drive a fresh CO2 surge, as it did in 2023/2024. Second, the trajectory of fossil fuel combustion itself — currently the dominant driver — will determine how quickly the accumulation slows.

For negotiators in Antalya, the Bulletin provides the scientific backdrop to a familiar political challenge. The question is no longer whether concentrations are rising, but how fast emissions can be cut before the accumulated burden in the atmosphere commits the world to consequences that adaptation spending will struggle to keep pace with.
