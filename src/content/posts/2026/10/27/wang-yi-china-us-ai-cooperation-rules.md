# Wang Yi Urges China-US Cooperation on AI Rules

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi has called on China and the United States to jointly develop and govern artificial intelligence, urging the two powers to agree on global rules that keep the technology “under human control,” according to [Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-10-27/wang-yi-calls-for-china-us-cooperation-to-develop-and-govern-ai-102497862.html). The remarks, made during a high-profile forum in northern China, signal Beijing’s push for formal bilateral engagement on AI governance even as technological rivalry between the two countries intensifies.

Wang made the comments on October 23 in Xiong’an New Area, Hebei province, during a meeting with John L. Thornton, co-chair of the Board of Trustees of the Asia Society, and Thomas L. Friedman, a New York Times columnist. The two Americans were in China to attend the 2026 Global Governance Forum (Xiong’an), a three-day gathering focused on reforming the international system.

## What Wang Said

According to an [English-language readout](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/eng/wjbzhd/202610/t20261025_12046393.html) from China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Wang argued that the two nations bear a shared duty given their leading positions in the field.

“As countries at the forefront of AI, China and the U.S. have both the capability and the responsibility to jointly develop and govern AI well, ensuring that its development remains under human control and benefits the people,” Wang said. “We should not only guard against risks but also engage in cooperation to jointly discuss globally applicable rules and standards so that AI serves to promote common prosperity and safeguard common security.”

The minister also framed AI as one of the emerging domains where Beijing says new governance rules are needed. According to the same readout, he opened the discussion by describing multiple “governance deficits” in peace, development and security, presenting China’s Global Governance Initiative—first put forward by President Xi Jinping in September 2025—as a contribution to addressing them. In China’s broader policy framing, frontier domains such as the deep sea, polar regions, outer space and AI are treated as areas that require updated governance rules and that should function as space for international cooperation rather than as contested arenas driven by zero-sum thinking. These are long-standing positions in Beijing’s public diplomacy rather than remarks attributed to Wang at this specific meeting.

## A Rare Point of Convergence

Strikingly, the American visitors echoed the concern about AI risk. Per the same readout, Thornton and Friedman congratulated President Xi Jinping on his “successful visit to the United States” and said that stability lies at the core of a constructive US-China relationship—with “the greatest risk to achieving stability” being “the malicious use of AI.” They expressed hope that the two countries would deepen AI cooperation and deliver public goods for the world.

The convergence on “human control” of AI is notable given the wider friction between Washington and Beijing over export controls, semiconductor restrictions and competition among AI model developers. Cooperation on governance rules, in other words, is unfolding alongside sharp competition on capability.

## The Diplomatic Backdrop

Wang’s remarks did not emerge in isolation. They follow Xi’s state visit to the United States in September—his first in more than a decade—during which the two presidents agreed to make good use of a China-US AI dialogue mechanism to exchange views on AI risks and benefits and to jointly prevent abuse of the technology.

The Xiong’an meeting can be read as Beijing publicly reaffirming and elevating that channel. It also fits a broader institutional campaign: in July, China launched the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization (WAICO) in Shanghai with 29 founding member countries, an intergovernmental body operating outside the United Nations system, as [Caixin reported](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-07-17/china-launches-shanghai-based-ai-governance-body-with-29-founding-nations-102465524.html). That initiative underscored Beijing’s ambition to shape global AI standards and infrastructure, particularly for the Global South.

## A Forum Built Around AI

The setting itself carried a message. The Xiong’an forum, hosted by the Chinese People’s Institute of Foreign Affairs under the theme “Rebuilding Confidence and Cooperation,” drew nearly 400 representatives from more than 90 countries and international organizations—with AI emerging as a central theme, according to [Global Times](https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202610/1372926.shtml).

Participants debated practical applications, safety, risk and equitable access. Pan Jingyu, director of the China-SCO Countries AI Application Cooperation Center, told the outlet that global AI governance is entering a critical period for shaping rules and standards, and warned that threats are evolving—from the weaponization of large language models to prompt injection, model poisoning and unauthorized actions by AI agents.

Andrea Renda, director of research at the Centre for European Policy Studies, described China as “a pioneer in artificial intelligence” whose people-centered, applied approach could offer lessons for Europe. Lyu Deqiang, president of the Singapore chapter of the Global Responsible AI Council, cautioned that the widening AI divide makes fairness in global governance increasingly urgent, noting that rules are currently shaped largely by major powers.

## Analysis: Governance as Soft Power

Wang’s intervention reflects a deliberate strategy of “governance diplomacy.” By hosting a large multilateral forum and pairing it with a meeting with influential American thought leaders, Beijing projects itself as a responsible stakeholder in AI governance. Its emphasis on inclusiveness and benefits for developing countries contrasts implicitly with what Chinese commentators portray as a US “AI race mind-set.” Courting Europe and the Global South is central to this pitch.

The approach carries both opportunity and ambiguity. A shared vocabulary around “human control” and “malicious use” creates room for practical cooperation through the newly established dialogue mechanism. Yet the absence of a public US-side readout of the Xiong’an meeting leaves open how Washington interprets Beijing’s outreach—as genuine cooperation or as competition by other means.

## What to Watch

The near-term test will be whether the China-US AI dialogue mechanism produces concrete outcomes—joint risk assessments, shared testing standards or agreed red lines—rather than symbolic statements. Diplomatic calendars add momentum: China is due to host the APEC Economic Leaders’ Meeting in the fourth quarter of 2026, a stage on which Beijing is expected to press its vision for AI governance.

For now, Wang’s message is clear: in an era of rivalry, Beijing wants the rules of the AI age written jointly—and wants a seat at the center of the table where they are drafted.
