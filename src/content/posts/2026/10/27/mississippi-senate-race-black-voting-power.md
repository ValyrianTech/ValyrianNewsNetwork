# Mississippi Senate Race Tests Black Voting Power

Mississippi's U.S. Senate race, once left off the national map, has in its final week become a bellwether for two forces shaping the 2026 midterms: the mobilizing power of Black voters and widespread fatigue with President Trump. Democrat Scott Colom, a district attorney, is challenging incumbent Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith in the November 3 election, a contest that [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/27/nx-s1-6018592/mississippi-senate-trump-colom-black-voter-turnout) reports has drawn last-minute millions from both parties after months of neglect.

## The paradox of a deep-red state

On paper, Mississippi looks like fertile ground for a Democratic upset. It has the highest share of Black voters in the country at close to 40%, and it is a rural, agriculture-heavy state that is among the poorest — making higher prices felt more acutely. But Trump won the state by 23 points in 2024, and its politics remain extremely racially polarized, meaning not enough white voters back Democrats for the party to win statewide.

Those fundamentals set a steep climb. The last Democratic senator from Mississippi took office in the early 1980s, and the last successful Black statewide candidate was elected during Reconstruction. Even in the 2018 blue wave, Hyde-Smith won a special election for this seat by roughly seven points.

## Colom's case against his own party

Colom argues the state's Democratic deficit is the product of national neglect rather than voter sentiment. "The reality is, Mississippi has never been a lost cause, we've been a betrayed cause," he told NPR. "The Democratic Party has not been trying to win Mississippi. Let's stop kidding ourselves, right? They spent hundreds of millions of dollars in other states. They spent almost nothing in Mississippi."

That has changed in the closing stretch. Both national parties have poured millions into the race, and high-profile surrogates descended on the state within the final week. Former Vice President Kamala Harris headlined a rally for Colom in his hometown of Columbus on October 22 that drew about a thousand people, where she urged supporters to convert frustration into turnout.

"We've been going through that range of emotions from anger to sadness to depression, all of that for a year and a half," Harris said. "Well guess what? We're 12 days out from an election. And it's time to turn that anger into action."

## Trump counters with a base-first message

A few days later and roughly a hundred miles up the road, Trump, Hyde-Smith and other Mississippi Republicans gathered in Corinth to energize the party base. Speaking ahead of the president, Hyde-Smith framed the race in stark ideological terms: "this is socialism versus capitalism," she said, "Communism versus democracy, and big government versus the free market. It could not be higher stakes."

Trump's visit, he acknowledged, was aimed less at persuasion than at turnout. "Sometimes when you think somebody's doing so well, nobody votes," he said. "That's why I'm here, just get out and vote."

Her campaign manager, Jake Monssen, expressed confidence Mississippians would "once again send Cindy Hyde-Smith to the U.S. Senate," warning voters about "the radical socialist policies being pushed by Scott Colom."

## A proxy fight over Black political power

The race carries weight well beyond Mississippi or control of the Senate. It has become a stand-in for the broader question of Black political power in the South, particularly after a Supreme Court ruling earlier this year rolled back part of the Voting Rights Act, easing challenges to maps that dilute Black voting power. Southern Republican-led states have rushed to redraw congressional maps in its wake, and Mississippi Republicans have signaled a desire to redraw state legislative and judicial races after the midterms.

Colom warned the trend could extend to local elections. "What that tells us is you're trying to take away power from the voters and give it to the leaders," he said. "That's not how democracy is supposed to work. The voters have to pick the leaders, not the leaders pick the voters."

The Senate race cannot be gerrymandered, making it a relatively pure test of statewide coalition-building — and a measure of whether Democratic enthusiasm and elevated Black turnout can close a gap that history and demographics have long kept wide.

## What to watch

All major forecasters still rate the seat as Likely or Solid Republican, and the most recent public polling from a Democratic-aligned firm put Colom at 38% to Hyde-Smith's 44%. Yet organizers see signs of an energized electorate. Ashley Kennedy Shelton, who runs a nonpartisan civic engagement group in Louisiana, told NPR earlier this month that voters across the spectrum are "just tired."

"I think you're going to see voters truly show up," she said. "This doesn't work, and it hasn't been working for quite some time. I think you're going to see it at the ballot box."

Whether that frustration translates into a genuine challenge to Mississippi's red wall will be answered on November 3 — and the result could shape how both parties think about the South for years to come.
