# Belgian Book Sector Warns AI Firms: Books Are Not Free Data

Belgium's Flemish book sector has launched a public campaign against technology companies that train artificial intelligence models on their books without authorisation. Led by the publishers' association Groep Algemene Uitgevers (GAU) and backed by some of the country's best-known authors, the action accuses AI firms of copyright infringement and demands consent, transparency and fair compensation.

"Big AI companies are violating copyright and are essentially stealing the works of authors and publishers," GAU director Maarten Vanhoof told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/26/boekensector-protesteert-tegen-techbedrijven-die-hun-ai-modellen/). The campaign carries the slogan *AI schrijft geen boeken, AI imiteert* — "AI doesn't write books, AI imitates."

## Three Demands, One Empty Book

Behind the campaign is a simple demand structure. Publishers and authors want AI companies to obtain permission before using books, to be transparent about which works have been used to train their models, and to pay a fair fee to those whose work has already been absorbed into datasets.

To make their point, the sector has produced a symbolic campaign book titled *Namaak* ("Counterfeit") that contains nothing but empty pages — a visual statement about what happens when creators lose protection. According to the initiators, the disappearance of the chain of professionals behind a book — from author and editor to publisher and translator — dries up the source itself: fewer authors, fewer new voices and fewer books that say something that has not already been said.

## Authors Say Nothing Was Asked

The campaign has drawn support from a roster of prominent Flemish and Dutch-language writers, including Tom Lanoye, Kristien Hemmerechts, Bart Moeyaert and Lize Spit. Among the most outspoken is historian and author David Van Reybrouck, best known for *Congo: A History* and *Revolusi*.

Van Reybrouck told VRT NWS that 38 of his own titles are being used in AI systems without his permission, including scientific articles from his university years. "They've been stuffed in there and are being used. That's quite something," he said.

He contrasted the practice with conventional reuse, such as when textbook authors request a passage from his work. "I consider that an honour... And then a compensation proposal is usually made. It's often modest, but it is correct. But with AI, I'm not asked anything at all."

Van Reybrouck also dismissed the current legal default, under which creators can only opt out after the fact. "The only thing you can currently do as an artist is an opt-out," he said. "That's like being in a landscape being treated with pesticides and you say: could you please spare my allotment garden?"

## A Global Pattern of Mass Book Buys

The Flemish campaign is the latest flashpoint in a wider international controversy. In August 2026, an investigation by the outlet 404 Media used an Apple AirTag hidden inside a book to trace a large order of roughly 1,000 titles to an Amazon warehouse in Las Vegas, where employees described receiving huge quantities of printed books whose spines were cut off for rapid scanning. Amazon told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/19/amazon-404media-ai-boeken-scannen-vernietigen-chatbot/) that it buys books through commercial channels "to develop and improve the products and services our customers use."

The same VRT reporting noted that AI company Anthropic had bought books en masse through second-hand sellers for model training. Printed books are prized as training data because much of their text is not easily found online, and titles printed before 2022 are guaranteed to have been produced without AI assistance.

## The Regulatory Gap

Much of the legal weight now sits at European level. Since 2 August 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act has required organisations distributing AI-generated content to apply a machine-readable label, with non-compliance risking fines of up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, according to [CivAI](https://www.civai.eu/news/eu-ai-act-requires-transparency-about-ai-generated-content-from-2-august-60).

But transparency about AI-generated *output* is not the same as protection for the authors whose work went into the *input*. Van Reybrouck acknowledged the label rule as "a step in the right direction" that protects the consumer, while adding: "But the protection of the author is left behind."

## What's Next

The sector has called on Flemish Culture Minister Caroline Gennez (Vooruit) to establish a binding legal framework at both Belgian and European level — one that would force AI firms to seek permission, disclose what they consulted and pay creators fairly. No response from Gennez or her office had emerged as the campaign went public.

For now, the scale of the problem remains hard to quantify. Vanhoof conceded that the sector can only observe that companies "steal our content in various ways by scraping websites, buying books and continuing to use them illegally in their systems." The open question for policymakers is whether Europe's emerging AI rules will move from labelling what machines produce to safeguarding the people whose work teaches them to produce at all.
