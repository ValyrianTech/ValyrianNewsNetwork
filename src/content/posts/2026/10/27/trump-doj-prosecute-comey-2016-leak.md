# Trump DOJ Again Moves to Prosecute Comey Over 2016 Leak

The Trump administration has moved once more to prosecute former FBI Director James Comey, this time over a decade-old alleged leak of classified information to a New York Times reporter — an effort Comey's lawyers immediately dismissed as invalid and barred by the statute of limitations. The charges, revealed in newly unsealed court documents on Monday, mark the third federal criminal case brought against Comey since President Donald Trump returned to the White House, per [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/26/nx-s1-6018628/trump-administration-attempting-to-prosecute-former-fbi-director-james-comey-again).

## What the Government Alleges

Federal prosecutors charged Comey with transmission of national defense information and conspiracy to transmit national defense information, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/justice-department/former-fbi-james-comey-charged-third-time-trump-national-security-rcna342963). The underlying allegation dates to an October 2016 New York Times article by reporter Charlie Savage concerning a secret Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court order that required Yahoo to scan emails for messages potentially linked to a foreign terrorist organization.

In a filing unsealed Monday, Special Assistant U.S. Attorney Dustin Farahnak wrote that investigators found evidence Comey "sought to correct misinformation which had appeared in the press by way of Reuters the day before" the Times story was published, and "directed staff to speak to Mr. Savage about specific accuracy concerns related to the Reuters article, and thereby illegally transferred classified information."

## A Dispute Over Legal Timing

The central dispute is procedural. The government filed what is known as a "criminal information" — a charging document filed directly by prosecutors without a judge's approval or a grand jury's probable-cause finding — under seal on October 6, 2026, ten years after the alleged disclosure. The Justice Department said the maneuver was intended to "toll," or pause, the statute of limitations.

"Filing a criminal information to toll the statute of limitations is a widely recognized practice in federal court and has been approved by multiple Circuit Courts of Appeals," a department spokesperson said. "This is an ongoing criminal investigation, and we will not comment further."

Comey's defense contends the opposite. His attorneys argue the charges are "time barred" because the government had ten years to bring the case "by obtaining a grand jury indictment," and that an information "does not suffice." Comey has not been indicted in this case, and prosecutors would still need to seek an indictment before he could be tried unless he waived that right. His defense filed a motion to dismiss roughly ten days ago; it remains pending.

U.S. District Judge Trevor McFadden unsealed the case, noting that the underlying facts "have been subject to considerable press coverage" and that charging documents are ordinarily sealed only to aid a defendant's apprehension.

## A Case Twice Dropped Before

Comey's lawyers note the allegation was thoroughly investigated — and twice declined — years ago. Prosecutor John Durham began investigating the disclosure in early 2017 and wrote a classified memo in December 2018 recommending no criminal charges. In January 2019 the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia opened a second inquiry, code-named "Tropical Vortex," which closed without charges in February 2020.

"Both times the Government determined that no charges should be brought against anyone," Comey attorney Patrick Fitzgerald said in a statement. "Now, ten years after the article was published, the Government filed an 'Information' against Mr. Comey that was not approved by a judge nor issued by a grand jury. It purports to charge Mr. Comey with crimes that he did not commit."

Comey, in a video statement posted to [his Substack](https://jamescomey.substack.com/p/yet-another-case-now), struck a defiant tone. "Well, they're back — again," he said. "It would almost be funny if it weren't such an appalling abuse of the Department of Justice — one that we cannot become numb to. Nothing's changed with me; I'm still innocent, I'm still not afraid of these people — and I still believe in an independent federal judiciary."

## The Third Case in a Year

This is the third federal case against Comey since Trump's second term began. The first, filed in federal court in Virginia, accused him of lying to Congress in 2020 and was dismissed after a judge concluded the prosecutor who brought it was illegally appointed. The second — an indictment returned in the Eastern District of North Carolina on April 28, 2026 — charged that Comey threatened Trump by posting an Instagram photo of seashells arranged to read "86 47." That case is pending, and some legal experts have greeted it with skepticism.

Former federal prosecutor Greg Rosen, now in private practice, disputed the department's characterization of the latest filing as routine. "Filing a placeholder charge the defendant can't be tried on, on the last possible day, under seal, in the third case against the same man, is not routine or 'widely recognized,'" he told NBC News. "It is exactly what it appears to be: vindictive."

## Broader Context and What to Watch

The filing lands amid a series of politically charged investigative actions in the run-up to next week's midterm elections, NPR noted. The same day the charges were unsealed, a federal judge ruled that Comey's daughter, Maurene Comey, had been unlawfully fired from her job as a Justice Department prosecutor in New York and ordered that she be reinstated.

The Comey–Trump rupture dates to 2017, when Comey — appointed FBI director by Barack Obama and a former senior Justice Department official under George W. Bush — resisted Trump's request at a private dinner to pledge personal loyalty, a request he documented in a contemporaneous memo. Trump fired him in May 2017 amid the FBI's Russia investigation, which special counsel Robert Mueller later concluded found Russian interference but insufficient evidence of criminal collaboration with the Trump campaign. Comey's 2018 memoir, "A Higher Loyalty," likened Trump to a mafia don, and Trump has since repeatedly called for Comey's prosecution.

What happens next hinges on two questions. First is whether the court accepts the government's tolling theory; if it does not, the case is time-barred and cannot proceed. Second is whether prosecutors obtain a grand jury indictment, without which Comey cannot be tried on the information alone. For now, Comey remains neither indicted in this case nor silent about it — and the legal confrontation between the former FBI director and the president who fired him shows no sign of ending.
