# How AI Is Transforming Hurricane Forecasts

When the first hurricane of the Atlantic season formed this month, an artificial-intelligence model outperformed the traditional physics-based models at predicting where it would go. The feat marks a turning point for a field that has long relied on supercomputers crunching the equations of atmospheric physics — and it is reshaping how forecasters at the National Hurricane Center (NHC) track and warn about deadly storms.

In early October, Hurricane Isaias made landfall near Destin, Florida, as a Category 2 storm. Conventional forecasting models initially projected it would track further west, toward southeastern Louisiana. But Google DeepMind's AI model called it correctly from the start: the hurricane was heading for the western Florida Panhandle, [NPR/WUSF reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/26/nx-s1-6012820/ai-hurricane-forecast-google-deepmind-isaias).

## A Different Kind of Model

"Google DeepMind, in particular, has really proven itself the last few years as the gold standard model," said Michael Lowry, a longtime meteorologist and hurricane expert in Miami who has advised both the Federal Emergency Management Agency and the NHC. "That model has been, by far and away, the best performing computer model that we've ever seen. Period."

The NHC first began incorporating the Google DeepMind model into its official forecasts in 2025. By the end of that Atlantic season, it was already among the most accurate at predicting a storm's path and strength, according to Brian McNoldy, a hurricane researcher at the University of Miami. That is unusual: new models typically take years to refine after they are introduced.

"It's something we're not used to — to have a model, the new kid on the block, very quickly becoming the best model out there," McNoldy said. "This pretty much came out of the gate in first place, and that's a pretty impressive feat."

The two model types work in fundamentally different ways. Traditional physics-based models ingest real-time atmospheric data — temperature, humidity, pressure and wind shear measured by satellites and aircraft — and solve complex equations to project a storm's path and intensity. AI models rely more on history, identifying patterns by analyzing thousands of past storms and atmospheric observations.

"The AI models don't really know anything about the atmosphere other than the current picture," Lowry said. "Beyond that, it's not leaning on the governing equations of the atmosphere, the physics, to make predictions."

Most of the people designing these models are not even meteorologists, Lowry noted — they are software engineers building neural networks, a set of algorithms modeled on the human brain and trained to find patterns in enormous datasets.

## The 'Black Box' Problem

That approach produces a genuine quandary. Because AI models do not encode atmospheric physics, forecasters cannot fully explain why a forecast turns out right or wrong — a concern that weighs heavily when millions of people depend on hurricane warnings to decide whether to evacuate.

"We don't know why they might get a forecast right. We also don't know why they might get a forecast wrong," Lowry said.

"It makes us a little uncomfortable, because we like to know *why* you're getting the right answer," McNoldy added. "And I feel like we don't — or at least I don't know why."

Not all AI models have performed well. Lowry noted that many unproven weather-forecasting AI models are circulating. The two that have proven most accurate so far are those from Google DeepMind and the U.K.-based European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF).

Independent verification data reinforces the pattern. According to a [Local 10 analysis](https://www.local10.com/weather/hurricane/2026/09/10/the-ai-hurricane-model-that-continues-to-blow-away-the-competition/) of forecast verification shared by former NHC branch chief James Franklin, DeepMind was the most accurate publicly available model for track prediction across the Atlantic, eastern Pacific and central Pacific basins through early September 2026 — outpacing the NHC's own official track forecast by as much as 30% at five-day lead times. It was the second straight year the AI model topped the NHC's human-refined forecasts at most lead times.

## Relying on a 'Blend' of Models

Despite those results, the NHC does not lean on any single model. Forecasters blend several proven systems — both AI and traditional — when assembling official forecasts, which are updated every six hours during active storms.

"Every storm has different forecast challenges," said NHC Director Michael Brennan. "That's why we take that consensus approach and use that blend of the best models."

And it is still humans who interpret the models' output before deciding what to tell the public. When raw model runs swing dramatically from one update to the next, forecasters translate those shifting predictions into clear, actionable guidance.

"That's one thing that the human forecaster provides a lot of, is continuity," Brennan said. "We're using our expertise and our understanding to translate that hurricane forecast into the hazard space, and that's what people really need to pay attention to."

The limits of the AI approach were on display with Isaias. While the DeepMind model topped other guidance in tracking the storm's path, it did not beat the NHC's blended forecast in determining the storm's intensity, or strength. Brennan said his team gives regular feedback to Google DeepMind to help improve the model. Google did not respond to questions from NPR for the story.

## Rapid Advances, but No Replacement

What excites scientists most about AI is how quickly it improves. AI models can be retrained in weeks or months, compared with traditional models updated annually at best.

"They are making advancements in skill and hurricane predictions over the course of a year that would have taken us 10 years, a decade or more, to make," Lowry said.

Brennan noted that forecast improvements are cumulative. It wasn't long ago that forecasters could not predict a storm's strength even a couple of days out. Advances in prediction models and better satellite and aircraft data have changed that: intensity forecast errors are now about half what they were at the time of Hurricane Katrina.

"The future of forecasting has always been changing," Brennan said. "It's never just one thing that's driving those improvements."

Even with AI advancing fast, Lowry said he does not fear that the technology will replace human forecasters.

"For me, I don't feel some sort of existential crisis with AI," he said. "If anything, I think it is a boon for our business."

## What to Watch

The next test will come with each new storm. As AI models continue to be folded into the official forecast blend, the central question is not whether machines will replace human judgment, but how forecasters will harness tools they do not fully understand — and communicate their uncertainty to the public. For now, the consensus is clear: the AI model has earned its seat at the table, even as the people reading its output remain essential to turning a forecast into a warning that saves lives.
