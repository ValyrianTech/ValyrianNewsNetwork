# Zhuque-3 Nears Booster Reflight as Tiangong Eyes Reuse

Chinese commercial launch firm Landspace is targeting a reflight of its recovered Zhuque-3 first stage within months, following a historic vertical landing in August, according to [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/landspace-aims-to-refly-recovered-zhuque-3-booster-within-six-months/). The milestone marks a turning point for China's reusable-rocket ambitions—and it comes as the country's Tiangong space station prepares to hand routine cargo resupply duties to private, reusable vehicles.

The Zhuque-3—named for the "Vermillion Bird" of Chinese mythology—is a two-stage, stainless-steel rocket developed by Landspace, one of a cluster of Chinese startups racing to bring down launch costs through reusability. Its first stage is designed to fly back and land vertically, a capability until recently demonstrated at scale only by SpaceX.

## A Two-Flight Climb

The rocket's path to recovery was not immediate. On its debut flight in December 2025, the vehicle reached orbit successfully but lost its first stage during the landing attempt, [SpaceNews reported](https://spacenews.com/zhuque-3-reaches-orbit-on-test-flight-first-stage-lost-during-landing-attempt/). The setback validated the ascent profile while exposing the difficulty of the return maneuver—precisely the sequence SpaceX spent years perfecting.

Landspace regrouped and set a follow-up orbital launch and recovery attempt. On August 18, 2026, the second Zhuque-3 lifted off from the Gobi Desert and this time brought its booster back for a controlled vertical landing, a first for the company and a significant step for China's commercial sector, per [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/chinas-landspace-recovers-booster-with-second-orbital-launch-of-zhuque-3-rocket/).

With a recovered booster in hand, the company has shifted its focus to reuse: refurbishing the stage and returning it to flight on a compressed timeline. Reaching that goal would move Landspace from demonstrating reusability to proving it economically, the difference between a stunt and an operating launch business.

## Tiangong's Cargo Handoff

Parallel to the rocket work, China's human spaceflight program is rethinking how it keeps the Tiangong station supplied. The station has historically relied on state-run cargo vehicles, but planners are preparing to open routine resupply missions to commercial reusable rockets—with Zhuque-3 among the candidate vehicles.

Also in the mix is the Haolong spaceplane, a reusable cargo craft under development by the Chengdu Aircraft Design and Research Institute, an AVIC subsidiary. Unlike a capsule that burns up on return, a spaceplane is designed to launch on a rocket and land on a runway, offering a recoverable way to bring experiments and equipment back from orbit. Reports suggest it could enter service around 2027.

## Why Reusability Matters

The shift reflects a broader strategy across China's launch industry. Companies including Landspace, Galactic Energy, Space Pioneer, and iSpace are all pursuing reusable rockets and vehicles, aiming to cut per-kilogram costs and raise launch cadence. Those economics matter for ambitious plans such as large satellite constellations and sustained station operations, where frequent, affordable access to orbit is the binding constraint.

For Tiangong, commercial resupply could free state resources for other missions while giving private firms a reliable anchor customer—a dynamic similar to how NASA's commercial cargo program helped seed the U.S. launch industry. Reusable vehicles would also allow cargo to be returned intact, expanding what scientists can send to and recover from the station.

## What to Watch

The immediate test is the Zhuque-3 booster reflight. A successful refurbish-and-relaunch cycle would be the clearest signal yet that China's commercial reusability is maturing from demonstration to routine. Beyond that, watch for concrete timelines on Haolong's debut and for formal contracts assigning commercial rockets to Tiangong cargo runs.

China's reusable-rocket push is no longer a promise on a slide. With a booster recovered and a reflight on the horizon, the question is shifting from whether the country can land a rocket to whether it can fly one again—cheaply, reliably, and often.
