# How Aldi Is Pushing Down US Grocery Prices

Aldi, the German hard-discount grocer that has quietly become the fastest-growing grocery chain in the United States, is putting downward pressure on American food prices as it races toward more than 3,000 locations by the end of 2028—more than any chain but Walmart, [NPR's Planet Money reported](https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2026/10/27/g-s1-149358/how-aldi-is-pushing-down-grocery-prices). The expansion underscores how a roughly 80-year-old retail model, built on cheap store brands and a stripped-down shopping experience, is reshaping competition in a market where the cost of food has become a central economic grievance.

Aldi generated roughly $29 billion in US sales in 2024, more than Trader Joe's, according to figures cited by NPR from Bloomberg. Its prices are reported to match Walmart's or come in slightly cheaper, a finding drawn from comparison shopping by food reporter Deena Shanker.

## A Model Built on Two Tactics

Aldi's low prices are not the product of cutting corners so much as refusing to pay for things shoppers don't strictly need. The company relies on two core tactics, according to the Planet Money analysis.

The first is private-label dominance. More than 80% of Aldi products are its own store brands—look-alikes that go by names such as Benton's Chocolate Sandwich Cookies in place of Oreos and Savoritz Golden Round crackers in place of Ritz. Selling its own brands lets Aldi avoid paying national-brand markups, eliminating a middle layer of cost.

The second tactic is a deliberately limited assortment. Rather than stocking many brands and sizes of each item, Aldi carries only the essentials and typically just one version of each. That enables bulk buying, reduces spoilage and storage needs, and shrinks the shelf and real-estate footprint—and therefore rent. The trade-off is convenience: shoppers may need a second trip elsewhere to complete a grocery list.

Those savings show up in small details. Stores play no in-store music, saving on speakers and licensing fees, and products are left in their cardboard shipping boxes on shelves, saving restocking labor. As Planet Money put it, "the shabbiness is intentional—it's the cost of cheaper groceries."

## Pressuring Competitors

The clearest evidence of Aldi's effect on prices may be what rivals are doing in response. Wegmans and Kroger have both announced defensive price reductions, according to NPR.

The academic record supports the pattern. A study in the [Review of Industrial Organization](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11151-023-09926-w) found that entry by hard-discounters reduced incumbents' markups by about 7.3% relative to markets where no such store opened. While incumbent sales actually rose about 2% within 3–5 kilometers of a new hard discounter—as shoppers split their trips—the net effect on profitability was negative, because markups fell more than sales climbed.

The broader context makes the pressure more notable. From 1960 to 2019, Americans spent a steadily smaller share of their income on food, according to [USDA Economic Research Service](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/100002) data, even as restaurant meals and premium groceries proliferated. Since the pandemic, food has become less affordable—a pain point that has made a discounter's low prices especially attractive and helped fuel Aldi's growth, a pattern first seen during the Great Recession.

## Unbundling the Supermarket

Planet Money frames Aldi's approach as an act of unbundling. Grocery retail evolved by bundling: from separate bakers, butchers and greengrocers into the supermarket under one roof, then into superstores like Walmart, Target and Costco that combine groceries with clothing and electronics. Aldi moves in the opposite direction, stripping away non-essentials and even skipping some grocery-list items—then deliberately locating stores near competing grocers so shoppers can finish their lists elsewhere.

What keeps customers coming back, the analysis suggests, is not just price. Aldi has cultivated a devoted following through its famously chaotic rotating "middle aisle"—stocked with toys, German spätzle and advent calendars of cheese—alongside occasional upscale offerings like well-priced "premium" wine. The flea-market energy creates a loyalty that outlasts economic downturns, with some customers lining up for store openings.

## What It Means for Food Prices

The lesson may be as much about blame as about bargains. If a discounter's growth can pressure competitors to hold prices down, then grocers may not be the primary driver of high food costs. "I think the way Aldi's growth pressured competitors to keep prices low shows that grocers are not the right target of our ire over food prices," the Planet Money analysis concluded.

For now, the story is one of scale. With the Southeastern Grocers acquisition behind it—including the conversion of Winn-Dixie and Harveys stores to the Aldi banner—the company is on track to become the second-largest grocery footprint in the country by store count. Whether that translates into sustained relief for American households will depend on how aggressively rivals respond, and on how much of the broader food system, beyond the grocery aisle, keeps pushing costs upward.
