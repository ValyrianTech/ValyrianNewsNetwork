# China Catalyst Turns Martian Air into Rocket Fuel

Chinese space scientists have developed a catalyst that converts carbon dioxide—which makes up more than 95 percent of the Martian atmosphere—into methanol, a liquid fuel that could one day power rockets, rovers and life-support systems on the Red Planet. The work, from a state-funded laboratory in Hefei, is among the clearest signs yet that Beijing is investing in the fundamental technology needed to send people to Mars and keep them supplied once they arrive.

The team at the Deep Space Exploration Laboratory published its findings in the *Journal of Deep Space Exploration*, and the research was [reported by](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/science/article/3372123/chinese-scientists-create-powerful-catalyst-turn-air-mars-rocket-fuel) the *South China Morning Post* on 27 October 2026.

## Why making fuel on Mars matters

Launching every kilogram of fuel and supplies from Earth is prohibitively expensive, so space agencies increasingly focus on in-situ resource utilization (ISRU)—producing fuel, water, oxygen and building materials from resources already present at the destination. The best-known demonstration to date is NASA's MOXIE experiment on the Perseverance rover, which used solid-oxide electrolysis to split carbon dioxide into breathable oxygen between 2021 and 2023.

The Chinese work takes a different route. Rather than producing oxygen gas, it targets methanol—a storable, transportable liquid that serves both as a fuel and as a chemical feedstock. "Methanol can serve as fuel to generate electricity, provide heating and power Mars rovers, and it can also be used as a chemical building block for making antifreeze, building materials and other products," space resources scientist Zhang Zhe and his colleagues [wrote in the paper](https://jdse.bit.edu.cn/sktcxb/article/doi/10.3724/j.issn.2096-9287.2026.20250134).

## A materials-science advance

Converting carbon dioxide into methanol is not new, but doing it efficiently is hard. Conventional copper-based catalysts suffer from non-uniform active sites and poorly controlled local structures, which produce unwanted carbon monoxide by-products and cap methanol yields.

The Hefei team, led by first author Ling Lili with co-authors Jiang Yawen, Zu Lin and Zhang Zhe, solved this by using a hydroxyl-rich metal-organic framework—MOF-808—as a precisely defined scaffold. They first anchored alkali metals (sodium, potassium or caesium) onto the framework, then introduced copper species to build adjacent dual active sites. The resulting catalysts, dubbed MOF-808-MCu, were then tested for carbon dioxide hydrogenation.

The optimized MOF-808-NaCu catalyst achieved a methanol space-time yield of 245 grams per kilogram of catalyst per hour—roughly an 11-fold improvement over the 22 g·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹ delivered by an alkali-free single-site version. Characterization and theoretical modelling showed that the neighbouring alkali-metal sites strengthen carbon dioxide adsorption and activation near the copper centres, favouring methanol formation while suppressing carbon monoxide.

## Strategic meaning

The study lands as China advances a broader Mars programme. Its Tianwen-1 mission delivered an orbiter, lander and the Zhurong rover to the planet in 2021, and Tianwen-3—a sample-return mission targeted for the late-2028 launch window—aims to bring back at least 500 grams of Martian material around 2031. A dedicated planetary-protection laboratory is planned in Hefei to receive and safely process those samples.

Read alongside those missions, the catalyst research suggests Beijing is thinking beyond flags and footprints. ISRU is the enabling technology for long stays on Mars, and a liquid-fuel pathway that yields rocket propellant, rover power and raw materials would address several logistics problems at once.

## Caveats and what's next

The authors are careful to frame the result as a feasibility study. Producing methanol on Mars from atmospheric carbon dioxide "is technically feasible to a certain extent," the paper concludes—a laboratory proof of concept rather than a flight-ready system.

A significant open question is hydrogen. Methanol synthesis requires hydrogen, which is scarce on the Martian surface; any practical system would need to source it from water ice or processed regolith, adding steps to a full fuel-production chain. Mars-environment testing, reactor scaling and integration with life-support systems also remain to be demonstrated.

Still, the dual-site catalyst design carries value beyond Mars. The same approach—using alkali-metal promoters to sharpen carbon dioxide hydrogenation—could inform terrestrial carbon-capture and "power-to-liquids" technologies on Earth.

For now, the significance is as much strategic as scientific. As the *South China Morning Post* put it, the work is a sign that Beijing is serious about one day sending people to the Red Planet—and about giving them the means to survive there.
