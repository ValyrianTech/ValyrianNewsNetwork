# Borrowing Costs Climb as Mortgage Rates Hit 3-Year High

The cost of borrowing money in the United States has climbed to its highest level in nearly three years, as surging bond yields and a newly hawkish Federal Reserve push up the rates Americans pay on mortgages, car loans, and other consumer credit. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate reached 7.40% for the week ending Oct. 8, up from 7.28% a week earlier and 6.30% a year ago, and the highest reading since mid-November 2023, according to [Realtor.com](https://www.realtor.com/news/trends/mortgage-rates-fed-hikes-today-october-8-2026/).

The squeeze is straining household budgets already worn down by years of elevated inflation, and it is forcing many Americans to shelve major purchases they can no longer finance affordably.

## A Three-Year High, and Still Climbing

Mortgage rates have now risen for seven consecutive weeks, crossing the symbolic 7% threshold in late September for the first time in 19 months. The renewed climb reverses what had looked like a promising start to the year, when rates were drifting lower before the U.S. war with Iran unsettled bond markets and reignited worries about inflation.

As [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/07/nx-s1-5991910/interest-rates-economy-inflation-bonds), borrowing costs are rising across the board because bond markets influence all kinds of consumer credit—from home loans to auto financing and higher education. The 10-year Treasury yield, which helps set rates for mortgages and car loans, averaged 5.28% in the week of Oct. 8 after peaking at 5.34% on Oct. 1, a level not seen since April 2002.

The effect on monthly payments is stark. At the current rate level, a borrower could pay roughly $900 more per month on a median-priced home than they would have under the pandemic-era rates of about 3%, according to NPR. A four-year loan for a used car now carries an interest rate about three percentage points higher than at the start of 2022.

"If you need to borrow, boy, it's really not a good time," John Diamond, senior director of the Center for Tax and Budget Policy at the Baker Institute, told NPR.

## The Fed's Tighter Stance

The climb follows the Federal Reserve's decision in September to raise its benchmark interest rate by a quarter percentage point to a range of 3.75% to 4%—its first hike in more than three years. In its [policy statement](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm), the committee said inflation "remains elevated" and that "today's policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal."

On Oct. 8, Fed Governor Christopher Waller reinforced the message, saying further tightening is likely even if it does not arrive immediately.

"The hikes do not need to come at consecutive meetings," [Waller said](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/waller20261008a.htm) at a Central Bank of Turkey forum in Istanbul. "But they should be in place in an acceptable period of time."

Financial markets put the probability of the Fed holding rates steady at its next meeting at roughly 78%, with a hike at the December meeting considered more likely, according to the CME FedWatch tool cited by Realtor.com.

## Pain for Households, Pressure on Housing

The rate environment is landing hardest on households that need to borrow. Carrie Goldstein, a Cleveland-area homeowner, had hoped to move to a more walkable suburb and buy a new car. But the math stopped her cold.

"It just became more and more disheartening when I did the math," she told NPR. "It's just not in the cards."

Her hesitation reflects a broader pullback. Existing home sales in August were down about 1.2% from a year earlier, according to the National Association of Realtors. In September, 20.8% of listings had a price cut—the highest share in four years—while inventory remains 9.1% below pre-pandemic levels, according to Realtor.com.

"These elevated mortgage rates have the housing market spooked," Joel Berner, a senior economist at Realtor.com, said.

## A Debate Over the Right Medicine

The Fed's rate hikes are intended to cool demand and bring inflation back to the central bank's 2% target, a goal it has missed for more than five years. Annual inflation was 3.4% in August, with gasoline alone accounting for more than a third of the monthly price increase. In his August [Jackson Hole speech](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/warsh20260828a.htm), Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh was blunt about responsibility for the persistent run-up: "The responsibility for 65 months of sustained, elevated inflation sits squarely with the central bank."

But some economists question whether higher rates can address the root causes. Writing before the September decision, Selma Hepp, chief economist at the real estate data firm Cotality, argued that a rate hike "is unlikely to lower gasoline prices, reduce tariff-related costs, or accelerate homebuilding, but it will further dampen housing demand and delay a broader market recovery," as [NPR noted](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/16/nx-s1-5968724/federal-reserve-interest-rates-inflation-economy).

Diamond framed the cumulative strain on families this way: "It's added to higher mortgage payments. Higher student loan payments. Higher prices because of inflation. That's what makes it really painful."

## What to Watch

The Fed has signaled one additional quarter-point increase could come this year, with no hikes forecast for 2027. Much depends on forces beyond the central bank's control—above all, whether an end to the conflict in Iran eases the energy prices that have driven inflation higher.

For now, the direction of borrowing costs points one way. As long as bond yields stay elevated and the Fed keeps the door open to more tightening, households weighing a mortgage or a car loan will likely keep waiting—and the recovery many had hoped for in the housing market will keep slipping further out of reach.
