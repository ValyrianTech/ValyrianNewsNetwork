# Belgium Budget Talks Stall as De Wever's Framework Fails

Belgium's top ministers met for several hours on the evening of Wednesday, 7 October 2026, but left without agreement on a proposed working framework for the federal budget, leaving roughly 10 billion euros in savings unresolved and casting doubt on an unofficial mid-October deadline. Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) had hoped the so-called kernkabinet would at least greenlight a structure within which a concrete budget proposal could be elaborated, but coalition partners remained divided.

According to the [VRT NWS liveblog](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/begrotingsoverleg-tussen-topministers-is-afgelopen-zonder-akkoo~1790599460224/), Wetstraat journalist Michael Van Droogenbroeck said the premier "had hoped that at least a green light could be given for the working framework, but the ministers left without an agreement." He added that the odds of reaching a budget deal by next week are "particularly small."

## An Urgent Warning on the Debt

To press home the gravity of the situation, De Wever summoned Pierre Wunsch, governor of the National Bank of Belgium, and Jean Deboutte, director of the Debt Agency, to brief the vice-premiers. Their message centred on the so-called rentesneeuwbal — the interest snowball, in which the state borrows ever more simply to service the interest on its existing debt.

As [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/begrotingsoverleg-kernkabinet-afgelopen-er-is-nog-geen-millimeter-vooruitgang-geboekt~a9cb0fe2/) reported, the Debt Agency warned the snowball effect could now arrive as early as 2029 — two years sooner than previously forecast — and, if interest rates keep rising, even in 2028, three years early. By 2029 this could add 0.3% of GDP to the deficit, roughly 1.8 billion euros in today's money.

The warning lands against a turbulent market backdrop. Belgium's 10-year long-term interest rate approached 4.4%, its highest level in around 15 years, while the French 10-year rate hovered above 4.9% and the US 10-year stood at 5.35%. Rising inflation fears — driven by higher oil prices linked to the war in Iran — are fuelling expectations that central banks will have to raise policy rates.

## Where the Talks Stand

The kernkabinet, or inner cabinet, has been negotiating since Saturday, 3 October. A first full review of De Wever's budget table on 4 October broke up without agreement, and positions have scarcely moved since. "Not a millimetre of progress has been made," VTM NIEUWS journalist Karel Lattrez was quoted as saying, adding that "we keep going around in circles."

Yet the meeting was not described as a crisis. Vice-Premier Maxime Prevot (Les Engages) called it "a useful meeting on the proposed structure, though positions on it still diverge." Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem (CD&V) said the new table was "certainly a step in the right direction," adding: "Nobody needs to convince me of the necessity of this exercise. I want to keep working on the budget."

Others struck a harder tone. Vice-Premier David Clarinval (MR) said the government had "examined everything in detail" and found "still large differences," describing the negotiations as "still very far from an agreement." An unnamed vice-premier's office put it more bluntly: "We are still nowhere."

## Familiar Sticking Points

The fault lines are well known. The biggest is a proposed VAT reform that would raise the top rate from 21% to 22% and harmonise the lower 6% and 12% rates to 12% — a measure the francophone liberals of MR have declared a hard red line. Planned savings in healthcare divide the coalition's left wing, with Vooruit and Les Engages arguing the cuts go too far.

As [Business AM](https://businessam.be/kernkabinet-snel-uiteen-zonder-akkoord-over-begroting-ondanks-nieuwe-waarschuwing-voor-rentesneeuwbal/) noted, the political divisions remain wide, with MR resisting tax increases while Les Engages and Vooruit consider De Wever's approach too right-leaning. The broader question of whether to raise more revenue from wealthier citizens, and the nullijn — which ministers have yet to meet earlier savings targets — add further friction.

## The Arizona Coalition Under Strain

The talks unfold within the so-called Arizona coalition, which unites N-VA, Vooruit and CD&V on the Flemish side with MR and Les Engages on the francophone side, plus independent Jean-Marie Dedecker. The government holds 81 of 150 seats and took office on 3 February 2025, with De Wever as Belgium's first Flemish-nationalist prime minister.

Despite a Summer Agreement in July 2025 covering pension, labour-market and healthcare reform, the fiscal picture has remained severe. The government agreed in July 2026 to seek an additional 10 billion euros by 2029. Journalists have described Belgium as "one of the poorest pupils of the European budget class," and the coalition has faced large trade-union protests and strikes throughout 2025 and 2026.

The pressure is multi-layered. Over the weekend of 3–4 October, the Brussels regional government separately reached a 2027 budget agreement after two days of difficult talks, aiming to cap its deficit at 719 million euros — a deal immediately criticised by opposition parties.

## What Comes Next

De Wever is due to hold bilateral talks with individual vice-premiers on Thursday, 8 October, before a new kernkabinet meeting on Friday, 9 October to decide whether to launch final-phase negotiations — potentially a weekend conclave.

The unofficial deadline is 13 October, when the prime minister is expected in the Chamber of Representatives to deliver his policy statement, or State of the Union. That date is now widely seen as unlikely to be met. Wetstraat journalist Benedikte Coussement summed up the mood: "The differences are still too large to go all the way to the finish," noting that "pressure from Europe and the financial markets is meanwhile increasing."

With markets sceptical, coalition partners dug in and the interest snowball drawing closer, the coming days will test whether Belgium's government can convert a "useful meeting" into a workable deal — or whether the deadline slips further into autumn.
