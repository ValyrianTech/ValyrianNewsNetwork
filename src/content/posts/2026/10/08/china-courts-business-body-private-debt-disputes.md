# China Courts, Business Body Target Private-Debt Disputes

China's Supreme People's Court and the All-China Federation of Industry and Commerce jointly released a second batch of 10 typical cases on the diversified resolution of private-economy disputes on October 8, with a specific focus on clearing overdue payments owed to enterprises, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261008/8dbee1622a0745aeaa3cb99b6510acb4/c.html).

The release is framed as an implementation of central leadership and State Council directives on settling enterprise arrears and of the Private Economy Promotion Law's provisions protecting private economic organizations' rights to be paid. It aims to guide private enterprises toward voluntary, lawful mediation rather than litigation.

## Context: A Chronic Payment Problem

Late payments to suppliers and contractors have long been a drag on China's smaller firms. Large buyers, including state-owned enterprises, local governments, and major private companies, have repeatedly been cited for delaying payment to small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), straining their cash flow and, in turn, delaying wages and further payments down the supply chain.

The issue has drawn sustained policy attention. The State Council executive meeting of August 21, 2026, deployed further work on clearing enterprise arrears, stressing that big enterprises owed money to SMEs and calling for industry payment rules, reasonable payment periods, and credit-default recognition standards for arrears. The Private Economy Promotion Law, passed by the 14th NPC Standing Committee in April 2025, enshrines fair competition and protections for private economic organizations' payment rights.

## The Cases and the Court + Chamber Model

According to Xinhua, the 10 cases cover sale-of-goods contract disputes arising from overdue payments between upstream and downstream firms in the construction industry, between small and micro building-materials suppliers and medium-sized construction enterprises, and between enterprises operating across provincial lines.

A key feature is the reinforced court-plus-chamber-of-commerce coordinated model. Courts provide professional guidance on the application of legal standards, clarify legal relationships, and set the direction of judicial adjudication. Chambers of commerce, meanwhile, use their familiarity with enterprises and industries to draft workable mediation agreements and lean on industry self-discipline and credit-evaluation systems to encourage voluntary compliance.

Chamber-of-commerce mediation organizations are described as bringing the advantages of low cost, high efficiency, and preserving business relationships. By understanding industry trading customs, payment cycles, and capital-flow patterns, they can diagnose the real cause of a debt and craft a targeted mediation plan, aiming for a win-win outcome in which creditors recover funds, debtors get breathing room, and business ties are repaired.

## Building on an Earlier Batch

The October release is the second such batch. The same two bodies issued the first batch of 10 typical cases on diversified resolution of private-economy disputes on July 31, 2026. The sequential releases suggest a deliberate, step-by-step effort to institutionalize mediation as a primary channel for commercial disputes.

## Analysis: A Signal to the Private Sector

The timing and framing matter. The campaign to clear enterprise arrears sits within a broader push to reassure private entrepreneurs that they can operate in a stable, predictable business environment. By surfacing concrete cases and pairing judicial guidance with industry-based mediation, authorities are signaling both that payment obligations will be enforced and that disputes need not always end in court.

The unfreeze-first-plus-installment-payment and other innovative methods highlighted in the coverage point to a pragmatic approach: keep viable businesses alive and keep supply chains moving, rather than simply extracting payment from a struggling debtor.

## What's Next

How much the case-based guidance changes actual payment behavior remains the open question. Arrears-clearing depends not only on legal standards but on the financial health of buyers and on enforcement at the local level, where incentives have historically been mixed.

Observers will watch for the follow-through: whether more batches of cases are released, how the new credit-default recognition standards are applied in practice, and whether the court-plus-chamber mechanism is extended beyond construction and building materials into other sectors of the private economy.
