# Democrats Hold Clear Edge One Month From Midterms

With less than a month until Election Day on Nov. 3, Democrats hold a clear advantage in the fight for control of Congress, buoyed by record-low approval ratings for President Trump, deep economic discontent and a historic intensity of opposition to the president, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/06/nx-s1-5989694/midterm-elections-control-congress). Democrats are favored to win the House, and the Senate — once considered a long shot — is now within reach.

More than one million ballots have already been cast, and the political compass is pointed firmly in Democrats' direction. "There is no doubt that, right now, they have the wind at their backs," NPR senior political editor Domenico Montanaro wrote.

## The House and Senate Picture

Democrats are favored to take the House with a fairly narrow majority. The [Cook Political Report](https://www.cookpolitical.com/analysis/house/house-overview/gop-track-lose-house-majority-15-race-ratings-shift-towards-dems) forecasts a five-to-15-seat net gain, which would give Democrats roughly 220 to 230 seats — just above the 218 needed for a majority.

The Senate has shifted more dramatically. Six seats have moved in Democrats' direction since June, and independent forecasters now see the chamber as genuinely competitive. According to the [Silver Bulletin](https://www.natesilver.net/p/nate-silver-2026-midterm-election-polls-model), "Democrats are in an excellent position to retake the House and are also favored to retake the Senate." Other models broadly agree, with Democratic odds of winning the Senate estimated between about 70% and 72%.

## A President at the Center of the Storm

Driving the shift is Trump's standing with voters. His approval rating sits at just 37%, according to an [average of the polls](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/polls/trump-approval-rating-polls.html) — tied for the lowest pre-midterm approval in the last half century. The only other president to fall that low was George W. Bush in 2006, when Republicans lost both chambers.

The intensity of opposition is also at record levels. In the latest [NPR/PBS News/Marist poll](https://maristpoll.marist.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/NPR_PBS-News_Marist-Poll_USA-NOS-and-Tables_202609171435.pdf), 53% of respondents said they *strongly* disapprove of the job the president is doing — the highest ever recorded in that survey. That is a troubling sign for Republicans as Trump insists the election be about him.

An [NBC News poll](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/poll-voters-say-trump-hurt-economy-competitive-midterm-election-looms-rcna597868) from September underscored the danger: 55% of voters said Trump's policies have hurt the economy, up from 48% in March, while 56% disapproved of his job performance. Democratic pollster Jeff Horwitt said the president had "achieved his goal of making this election about him at a historic level" — but that the share of voters casting their ballot *in opposition* to Trump is also at a record high.

## The Economy Is the Central Battleground

Economic issues remain the top problem facing the country, and voters are blaming the party in power. An [AP/NORC poll](https://apnorc.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/September-2026-Topline-Final_TRUMP.pdf) released last week found 65% said higher-than-usual prices are more because of Trump's policies than factors outside his control. Just 26% approved of his handling of the economy.

The strain is broad-based. The war with Iran and Trump's tariffs have raised costs on goods and energy, while gas prices have climbed and the Federal Reserve hiked interest rates this fall, making borrowing more expensive. Inflation is outpacing wage growth, and many voters are souring on AI as data centers drive up utility costs.

The economy has become a vulnerability rather than a strength for Republicans. In the NBC News poll, Democrats held a narrow one-point lead on which party voters trust to handle the economy — the third consecutive survey showing the parties roughly even, a striking reversal from the GOP's 21-point advantage just three years ago.

## History and the Enthusiasm Gap

History is unkind to the president's party in midterms. In 18 of the 20 midterms since World War II, the president's party lost House seats — an average of 26 seats, rising to about 35 when the president's approval rating is below 50%. In 2018, the last midterm of Trump's first term, Democrats won 42 seats and control of the House.

The enthusiasm gap reinforces the trend. A CBS poll released Sunday found 84% of Democrats and 82% of independents said they are "definitely" going to vote, compared with 77% of Republicans. On the generic congressional ballot, Democrats hold an average nine-point advantage — and in each of the past seven midterms, the party leading on that measure has made gains.

## Cross-Currents and Republican Hopes

Democratic gains may still be capped. Mid-decade redistricting has insulated some Republicans, there are fewer competitive House seats, and most of those are in districts Trump won in 2024. Republicans also retain a campaign spending edge, and the Democratic Party remains broadly unpopular with voters.

Republican pollster Bill McInturff framed the race as "a classic battle between these numbers and the political geography in which this campaign will be decided." He acknowledged, however, that the issue environment is "tilting substantially towards the Democrats."

Even some Republicans are conceding the momentum. "You've got real momentum on the Democrat side, and if we don't watch out... we may lose in the midterm," Sen. Jim Justice told HuffPost in September. As [The Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/americas/us-politics/trump-polls-senate-midterms-republicans-b3061700.html) reported, GOP candidates across the country have quietly removed references to Trump from their campaign materials.

Trump himself has sent mixed signals, telling an Alabama rally that presidents "don't do well in midterms" while claiming at an Oklahoma event that "the press plays the fake polls all the time." He has repeatedly pointed to a Trump-aligned McLaughlin & Associates poll showing higher approval, though that survey is an outlier that differs from the RealClearPolitics average by about 10 points.

## What to Watch

The Senate map is where the most dramatic movement is occurring. Recent [New York Times/Siena polling](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/03/us/senate-polls-texas-kansas-ohio-alaska-iowa.html) shows Democratic candidates leading in traditionally Republican states, with James Talarico ahead of Ken Paxton in Texas and Mary Peltola up seven points over incumbent Dan Sullivan in Alaska. Competitive races in Michigan, Maine and elsewhere could decide the chamber.

With a month to go, the fundamentals favor Democrats — but the party's gains may not match the scale that similar numbers have produced in past cycles. The final verdict will depend on turnout, geography and whether Republicans can persuade swing voters that Democrats are too extreme to govern. For now, the wind is at Democrats' backs.
