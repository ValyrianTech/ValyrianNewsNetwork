# China Rebuts Human Rights Criticism at UN Committee

China's deputy ambassador to the United Nations delivered a sharp right-of-reply statement on 5 October, rejecting human rights criticisms raised by six countries during the general debate of the General Assembly's Third Committee and accusing them of politicizing human rights and applying double standards.

Ambassador Sun Lei named the United Kingdom, Australia, Japan, Ireland, the Czech Republic and Lithuania, saying their representatives had "made groundless accusations and maliciously slandered China's human rights situation" during the debate in New York, according to the state-run [Xinhua News Agency](https://www.news.cn/world/20261006/712ad9b27d3243bd9b82cdb8843fa51f/c.html).

## Diplomatic Pushback at the UN

The exchange took place at the Third Committee — the General Assembly body responsible for social, humanitarian and cultural affairs, including human rights — rather than the Human Rights Council, a separate Geneva-based body that is frequently confused with it. China's delegation confirmed the timing and venue in a statement published on the [Permanent Mission of the People's Republic of China to the UN](https://un.china-mission.gov.cn/hyyfy/202610/t20261006_12035687.htm).

According to the Chinese mission's account, Sun told the committee that the six countries "consider themselves superior and habitually adopt the pose of a 'human rights lectern,'" adding that their own domestic records left them "in no position to talk about human rights, still less to point fingers at China."

The Third Committee opened its general debate on 1 October as delegates warned of mounting threats to human rights amid wars in Gaza and Ukraine, sanctions, racism and shrinking civic space, [UN Meetings Coverage](https://press.un.org/en/2026/gashc4436.doc.htm) reported.

## Four Lines of Counter-Argument

China structured its rebuttal around four contrasts, pairing claims about its own development record with criticisms of the six nations.

On poverty and development, Sun said China had "completely eliminated absolute poverty," lifting nearly 100 million people out of poverty and forming a middle-income group of more than 400 million. He countered that the United Kingdom has more than 14 million people living below the poverty line — half of them in "extreme poverty" — and that Japan's relative household poverty rate stands at 15%, with child poverty "increasingly prominent."

On governance, Sun said support and satisfaction rates for the Chinese government have exceeded 90% for years, and credited the Hong Kong National Security Law with restoring order in the territory. He said polling shows roughly 75% of Britons are anxious about the future of democracy, and that public trust in government has hit new lows in Australia and the Czech Republic. On ethnic rights, he said policies in Xinjiang and Tibet protect the rights of all groups and cited China's 2024 Ethnic Unity and Progress Promotion Law, while accusing Japan of failing to treat the Ainu and Ryukyuan peoples as equal and pointing to "endless harm" left by colonialism in the UK, Australia and Ireland.

Finally, Sun said the countries "constantly talk about morality" yet remain indifferent to the plight of Palestinians, Iranians and Cubans, accusing them of using human rights as a pretext to interfere in other countries' internal affairs and impose unilateral coercive measures on developing countries.

## A Second Reply Aimed at Japan

Sun delivered a separate, second right-of-reply directed specifically at Japan. According to the Chinese mission, he accused Japan of "turning a blind eye" to its own problems — a wealth gap, racial discrimination, gender violence and elder abuse — and said it had "no standing to point fingers" at others. He invoked the 80th anniversary of the Tokyo Trial, urging Japan to reflect on its history of aggression and to handle legacy issues such as the wartime recruitment of "comfort women" honestly.

## Broader Pattern of Confrontation

The clash is the latest iteration of a long-running pattern at the Third Committee, where Western states raise country-specific concerns about China — often focused on Xinjiang, Hong Kong and Tibet — and Beijing responds with right-of-reply statements and counter-criticism. China has frequently mobilized joint statements from like-minded states to rebut such criticism and to emphasize "dialogue, not confrontation."

In the same session, China's delegate spoke on behalf of 28 Member States, warning that "today's world is plagued by rampant unilateralism and the raging unilateral coercive measures," which he said have "sometimes even life-threatening" consequences, UN Meetings Coverage reported. That framing echoes language used by [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/GuoJi·ZhanLue/2026_10_06_903308.shtml), a Chinese outlet that reported China had advised the countries to "stop fabricating and spreading lies about China."

The broader 81st session has been marked by deep geopolitical divisions. The United States used the committee's opening to call for a leaner, less politicized body, while the European Union warned against treating human rights as "a luxury" and urged strengthening, not weakening, the UN's human rights pillar.

## What to Watch

China's statement signals that Beijing will continue to contest human rights criticism in multilateral forums by turning the spotlight back onto its accusers — a strategy aimed as much at a domestic and developing-world audience as at the six governments it named. Whether the approach shifts votes or dampens Western scrutiny at the Third Committee, which continues its work through late November, is the question that will define this year's session. For now, the exchange underscores how deeply the politics of human rights have become entangled with broader geopolitical rivalries at the United Nations.
