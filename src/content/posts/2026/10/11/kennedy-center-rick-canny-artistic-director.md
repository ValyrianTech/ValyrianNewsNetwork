# Kennedy Center Names Rock Manager as Artistic Director

The Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts has named Rick Canny, a Los Angeles talent manager whose clients have included ZZ Top and former Mötley Crüe drummer Tommy Lee, as its new artistic director — an appointment that has raised eyebrows across the arts world and come despite internal staff complaints about his business practices as a consultant to the institution. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/10/nx-s1-5996934/kennedy-center-rick-canny-new-artistic-director), the news was announced to high-level staff in an internal email sent Thursday, Oct. 8, 2026.

## Context: An Institution Under Strain

The appointment lands at a turbulent moment for the Washington, D.C. venue, which serves as a living memorial to President John F. Kennedy. Since President Trump became chairman of its board in February 2025 and installed a hand-picked slate of trustees, the center has seen donations and ticket sales plummet, a mass exodus of artists who canceled performances in protest of its politicization, and the departure or dismissal of much of its staff.

The center is now mostly closed to the public. Its administration and board claim the institution is on the verge of bankruptcy and have asked a federal court to shutter the building for two years for a roughly $250 million renovation. Trump has threatened to tear down the center if his name is not prominently displayed on it, while a lawsuit brought by Rep. Joyce Beatty (D-Ohio), an ex-officio board member, seeks to block the renaming.

## The Announcement

The email announcing Canny's appointment was sent by Matt Floca, the center's executive director and chief operating officer. In it, Floca wrote that Canny will "advise me and the broader leadership team on artistic strategy, business practices, presenting and talent curation, ticketing and pricing models, media opportunities, and programming sponsorships," according to NPR.

Canny is no stranger to the institution. He has consulted for the Kennedy Center through his firm, Favor the Artist, since shortly after Trump took over as chairman, and has served as creative lead for the Kennedy Center Honors and the Mark Twain Prize. Floca said Canny "will continue in this capacity while serving as the Center's Artistic Director."

Canny's résumé, however, is rooted in the rock and pop music business rather than the classical, opera, ballet, jazz and theater programming for which the Kennedy Center was historically known. As [Artforum](https://www.artforum.com/news/rick-canny-named-artistic-director-of-the-kennedy-center-1234761192/) reported, he has worked variously as a talent buyer, concert promoter, artist-management company vice president and founder of an artist-representative firm. His clients reportedly included Whitesnake and former Creed frontman Scott Stapp.

## Concerns Over Fees and Expertise

Even before the official announcement, Canny's role had drawn internal scrutiny. According to NPR, Sammy Miller, the center's then-senior director of music programming, wrote a memo in March 2026 to Floca and the human resources department raising concerns about fees and expenses Canny had charged. In one month in early 2026, Miller wrote, Canny had billed the center $30,000 in consulting fees; in another month, he charged more than $28,000 in fees and travel expenses, including two first-class round-trip flights between Los Angeles and Washington.

Miller said he had tried to resolve his concerns through the center's general counsel and finance office, but that "both offices appear to be protecting Mr. Canny." The Kennedy Center told [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/09/arts/kennedy-center-trump-rick-canny.html) that the $30,000 figure "did not reflect Mr. Canny's current agreement," without offering specifics, and said his travel expenses had been reviewed. Neither the center nor Canny responded to NPR's requests for comment.

Observers also question whether Canny's background equips him to program at the level the venue once did. Arts administrators across the country have told NPR that booking performances for major performing arts centers is done years in advance — a process demanding deep relationships in the classical, dance and theater worlds. Canny's published professional history shows little experience in those realms, and the website of his firm, Favor the Artist, lists no client roster or company history.

The leadership gap extends beyond Canny. Floca, now the center's top executive, was previously head of facilities and holds a degree in construction management; he has no background in arts administration, programming or fundraising, unlike his peers at comparable venues. The center's previous head of programming lasted just two weeks in January 2026.

## Analysis: A Symbolic Inflection Point

The appointment is about more than one hire. It reflects the broader transformation of an institution once defined by artistic prestige into one shaped by political loyalty and commercial calculation. Critics see a pattern: as the center's traditional programming and donor base have withered, its leadership has increasingly been drawn from outside the arts world and from within Trump's orbit.

The center, for its part, frames the appointment as necessary to its survival. In a statement, it said that "regardless of building closure timelines, the center must continue to curate programming during infrastructure upgrades and deliver a commanding reopening season that brings audiences back and puts the center on stable financial footing." Whether a rock-oriented manager can deliver that remains an open question — as does whether the center's financial claims will hold up in court.

## What's Next

The Beatty lawsuit continues to test the center's plan to close for two years, and the timing of the bankruptcy warnings has drawn suspicion from critics who note they coincided with court deadlines. Canny's appointment also raises questions about whether the venue will pivot toward the "family-friendly," Trump-aligned programming the president has pushed for.

For now, the Kennedy Center remains largely dark, its foyer still shrouded after a September ceiling collapse. Its next artistic chapter will be written by a man whose career was built not in the concert hall, but in the rock arena.
