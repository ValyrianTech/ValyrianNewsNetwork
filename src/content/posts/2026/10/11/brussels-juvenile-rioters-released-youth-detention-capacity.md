# Brussels Frees Juvenile Rioters as Youth Jails Fill Up

Belgian authorities were forced to release several minors detained after rioting on the margins of a major trade-union demonstration in Brussels, because no places were available in youth detention facilities, the Brussels prosecutor's office [announced](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/11/minderjarige-relschoppers-na-betoging-in-brussel-vrijgelaten-doo/) on Sunday. The admissions exposed what officials describe as a structural capacity crisis in the French-speaking juvenile justice system — and prompted an unusually blunt appeal to politicians to "restore the credibility of juvenile justice."

## What Happened

Sixteen French-speaking minors were arrested in the aftermath of Friday's national union march and referred to the Brussels prosecutor. The march itself passed peacefully, but heavy rioting broke out elsewhere in the city: vehicles and street furniture were torched, police were pelted with projectiles, and officers deployed water cannons and tear gas. Police counted roughly 30,000 demonstrators, while unions put the figure at 50,000.

For eight of the detained minors, the prosecutor sought placement in an Open Public Institution for Youth Protection (IPPJ), citing the gravity of the alleged offences. In several cases the juvenile court judge agreed that placement was strictly necessary. But because the relevant institutions had no open places, the decisions could not be executed and the minors were sent home, [BRUZZ reported](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/samenleving/minderjarigen-die-feiten-pleegden-tijdens-de-betoging-vrijgelaten-wegens-plaatsgebrek-2026-10-11).

The prosecutor's office highlighted three cases. One 16-year-old already known to the juvenile court was intercepted near seven ready-to-use Molotov cocktails, which he admitted he intended to carry to the protest. Another 16-year-old was identified on camera footage throwing shared electric e-scooters onto a burning barricade to feed the flames. A 14-year-old was arrested after repeatedly throwing stones and pyrotechnic mortars at security forces, according to [HLN](https://www.hln.be/brussel/minderjarige-verdachten-van-rellen-in-brussel-vrijgelaten-door-plaatsgebrek-in-instellingen-dit-tast-geloofwaardigheid-van-justitie-aan~aacf803e/).

## The Capacity Numbers

The release of the minors is the visible edge of a much larger backlog. According to the prosecutor's statement, 283 French-speaking minors are currently on waiting lists for a place in a closed regime at an IPPJ. A further 44 are waiting for a guidance and supervision trajectory, because the alternatives to placement are also saturated.

The Social Service for Judicial Youth Assistance, which is meant to supervise young offenders, can in some cases only begin its work after days or even weeks. For the minors involved, the gap between a court's decision and any actual intervention leaves a period in which the measure the judge considered necessary simply does not exist.

## 'A Lack of Places May Never Be the Reason for a Release'

The prosecutor's office did not frame the episode as a dispute about the severity of the judicial response. Instead, it pointed to a failure of execution. In a statement cited by [indegazette.be](https://www.indegazette.be/minderjarigen-na-zware-rellen-in-brussel-vrijgelaten-ondanks-beslissing-jeugdrechter-283-jongeren-wachten-op-gesloten-plaatsing/), officials argued that a placement decision which cannot be carried out deprives society, the victims and the minor himself of the educational, protective and structuring measure the judge considered necessary.

"A lack of available places may never be the real reason for a release," the prosecutor's office said. It warned that when minors see that even a juvenile judge's decision is not carried out, it can foster "a sense of impunity and omnipotence" — and that "in this way justice loses credibility."

The statement also stressed that the problem is not confined to detention beds: mobile guidance teams and educational supervision measures are likewise operating with significant waiting times.

## Political Context

The episode lands in a politically charged moment. The rioting followed a national trade-union demonstration, and Interior Minister Bernard Quintin (MR) had said on Friday that the response of police and justice would be "proportionate to the damage caused." The releases suggest that promise has, for now, remained largely unfulfilled. Earlier in the weekend, the police union VSOA had already called for stricter action, arguing that people with bad intentions must be "isolated immediately."

The larger tension is institutional. Youth protection and placement are, in Belgium's federal system, a Community competence. For French-speaking minors, responsibility sits with the Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles and its IPPJ network — not with the federal justice system. The Brussels prosecutor, a federal actor, is thus issuing placement demands whose execution depends on a different level of government.

This is not a new problem. In April 2026, ten minors arrested for serious offences were all released in a single weekend for lack of IPPJ space, prompting an earlier "alarming" statement from Brussels' King's Prosecutor, Julien Moinil. French-Community Minister for Youth Valérie Lescrenier has acknowledged a structural shortage while disputing the prosecutor's framing of the figures. Criminologists, including KU Leuven's Stefaan Pleysier, have argued that extra capacity alone will not solve the issue, because many young people with complex care needs are being directed to community institutions instead of the specialised help they require.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is how many of the eight minors for whom placement was sought were actually released specifically because of the shortage — a figure the prosecutor has not stated precisely. More broadly, the episode renews pressure on French-Community authorities to reconcile their capacity plans with the demands coming out of the courts.

Until that gap closes, the prosecutor's office says, the same scene will keep repeating: a judge orders placement, the institutions have no room, and a young offender walks home. For Belgian policymakers, the test is whether an announced reaction "proportionate to the damage" can be delivered by a system that currently has nowhere to put the people it convicts.
