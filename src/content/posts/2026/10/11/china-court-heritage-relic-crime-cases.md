# China Court, Heritage Agency Release 6 Relic-Crime Cases

China's Supreme People's Court (SPC) and the National Cultural Heritage Administration (NCHA) jointly released six "typical cases" on October 10, 2026, illustrating the lawful punishment of cultural relic crimes, [People's Daily](http://society.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1011/c1008-40812320.html) reported. The cases span illegal tomb excavation, the destruction of a nationally protected ancient road, the resale of a relic pulled from a riverbed, and the illegal "restoration" of looted bronzes. Authorities said the release is meant to guide judicial practice, draw clear legal red lines for the public, and reinforce a nationwide crackdown.

## Context: A Crackdown Stepped Up

According to the SPC, China's relic-security situation remains "complex." Relic crimes, it warned, are becoming increasingly specialized, intelligent, and professionalized, and are gradually spreading onto the internet, with major cases occurring "from time to time." Driven by the prospect of huge profits, offenses such as excavating ancient sites and tombs and stripping carvings from grottoes have become the source of a wider criminal chain that feeds trafficking, smuggling, and concealment.

The release comes amid the "Yunxiao" (Cloud Owl) special campaign, a six-month nationwide crackdown launched on May 15, 2026, by the Ministry of Public Security and the NCHA. As [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260717/46a2b9924979438dbcdcbd73c607a417/c.html) reported, authorities had by mid-July opened more than 170 cases, arrested over 390 suspects, and recovered more than 2,700 relics, including more than 230 precious items. The campaign targets diggings at national and provincial protected sites, theft from state collections, so-called "treasure-hunting" digs, online trafficking, smuggling, and destruction during construction.

## The Six Cases

**Case 1 — Destroying the Qin Straight Road.** Guo Moucai, a coal mine manager, kept mining after heritage authorities issued a stop-work order in 2012, [Sichuan Online](https://focus.scol.com.cn/zgsz/202610/83337040.html) detailed. By 2014 the mine's working area overlapped the core of the Qin Straight Road — a Qin Dynasty artery described as China's first "highway" and a National Key Cultural Relic Protection Unit since 2006 — leaving that section destroyed. A civil public-interest suit led to an order to pay 3.94 million yuan in simulated restoration costs. Guo was convicted of intentionally destroying cultural relics and sentenced in September 2025 to seven years plus a fine, a ruling upheld on appeal.

**Case 2 — Copying tomb-raiding fiction.** Yu Moucai, described as obsessed with tomb-raiding novels, taught himself excavation techniques and bought tools. In late 2023 he and others dug two Eastern Zhou-era tombs within the provincial protection zone of the Guojiamiao tomb complex in Hubei, stealing 23 artifacts — including an inscribed bronze dagger-axe, jade ornaments, and bronze vessels. Of the 23 Spring and Autumn period relics, 22 were Grade-3 or above. Sentenced in March 2026 to 12 years plus a fine as a principal offender, he did not appeal.

**Case 3 — Punishing criminal preparation.** Chen Mouhui was twice recruited to dig the Xidunding site, a Han Dynasty site in Jiangsu, and surveyed it with a probe but did not proceed to a full excavation. Because the act amounted to criminal preparation, and because he surrendered voluntarily, he received a mitigated sentence of three years and four months in June 2022.

**Case 4 — A tomb beneath one's own yard.** Shi Mouxing, a villager living within the Fenghao site — a First-Batch National Key Cultural Relic Protection Unit in Xi'an — found and excavated an ancient tomb while clearing a tree root on his homestead in 2014, taking eight bronzes, all Grade-3 or above. He was sentenced to 15 years in 2018. The court stressed that tombs belong to the state regardless of land ownership, and that illegality does not change because a tomb lies under one's own property.

**Case 5 — Selling a riverbed relic.** While dredging sand in a river in Guangxi in 2011, Tang Mousheng found a bronze chime bell, hid it, and sold it in 2013 for 300,000 yuan. Police seized the bell in 2022; it was authenticated as a Grade-2 Spring and Autumn period "cicada-pattern" bronze yong bell. Convicted of trading in cultural relics under "especially serious" circumstances, he was sentenced to five years plus a fine in 2023, and the 300,000 yuan was confiscated.

**Case 6 — Illegal "restoration" as concealment.** Gao Moulin, who had worked in bronze restoration since around 1998, was given three bronze mirrors to "restore" around 2020, knew they were unearthed relics, and charged 10,000 yuan. The mirrors were Han Dynasty Grade-3 items, and illegal restoration caused losses that cannot be restored. Convicted of concealing criminal proceeds in June 2025, he received a 10-month suspended sentence, forfeited the 10,000 yuan, paid 19,008 yuan in restoration costs, and publicly apologized in national media.

## Analysis: Full-Chain Justice and Restorative Accountability

The six cases are not a random sample. Three are excavation-related, reflecting the SPC's stated priority of striking at "source" offenses that feed the rest of the criminal chain. The SPC said courts apply a "full-chain, full-process, all-elements" standard to sever the sequence it describes as "steal, excavate, traffic, transport, sell." Case 3 pushes liability upstream to criminal preparation; Case 6 pulls it downstream to those who launder looted artifacts through seemingly technical services.

A second thread is a shift in judicial philosophy. The stated approach — "temper justice with mercy, combine punishment and prevention, address symptoms and root causes" — appears in the outcomes themselves. Courts increasingly order offenders to bear restoration costs and to apologize publicly, moving relic justice from pure punishment toward repair and protection.

Several cases also target misconceptions close to ordinary life, evidently to dissolve them. Case 4 rejects the assumption that a tomb under one's own land is one's own to dig. Case 5 rejects the idea that a chance find is free to sell. Case 2 treats popular tomb-raiding fiction not as harmless entertainment but as a documented pathway into serious crime.

## What's Next

The cases land against a strengthened legal framework: China's revised Cultural Relics Protection Law took effect on March 1, 2025, reinforcing that underground, inland-water, and territorial-sea relics belong to the state. They also follow a 2023 joint release of relic-crime cases by the Supreme People's Procuratorate and the NCHA, signaling a sustained multi-agency strategy among courts, prosecutors, heritage officials, and police.

The immediate test is enforcement. The Yunxiao campaign was set to run for six months from May, and authorities have urged the public to report suspected relic crimes promptly to the relevant departments. How aggressively these standards are applied at scale — and whether online trafficking, an explicit concern, proves as tractable as physical dig sites — will determine whether the crackdown's early momentum endures.
