# What Should It Cost to Insure a Home in Wildfire Country?

It is a deceptively simple question, and it sits at the heart of one of the most consequential economic debates in the American West: what should it cost to insure a home in wildfire country? As fires grow more frequent and destructive, homeowners in high-risk regions are confronting sharply rising premiums and shrinking coverage options -- while insurers argue that prices must finally reflect the mounting danger. Reporting from [NPR's Planet Money](https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2026/11/24/g-s1-154477/what-should-it-cost-to-insure-a-home-in-wildfire-country) examines the collision between those two realities, and what it means for where -- and whether -- Americans can continue to live in fire-prone communities.

Some California homeowners have reported premium increases of 800% or more. Others have been dropped entirely, forced onto the state's insurer of last resort, or left to go without coverage altogether. The struggle is not simply about price. It is about whether a market designed to spread risk can survive in a world where that risk is becoming concentrated, correlated, and increasingly severe.

## A Town as a Test Case

The reporting is grounded in Paradise, California -- the town devastated by the 2018 Camp Fire, which destroyed more than 11,000 homes and killed 85 people in one of the deadliest and costliest fires in state history. Paradise has since become a laboratory for a painful question: can a community rebuild to fire-safe standards and still remain insurable?

Heidi Lang, a Paradise homeowner and Camp Fire survivor, described receiving a "Notice of Cancellation" from her insurer, followed by a premium that jumped from roughly $1,000 to about $10,000 a year. "That type of an increase is just catastrophic," she said. "There's no way I could have come up with that."

Her experience is echoed across the region. Dawn Foster, a longtime insurance agent in Paradise, lost 1,100 homes and 352 cars from her book of business in the Camp Fire -- along with six clients' lives. "It's been breaking for years," she said of the market.

## The Moral Hazard Problem

At the center of the story is a concept economists call *moral hazard* -- the idea that insurance can weaken the incentive to reduce risk. If an insurer will pay to rebuild a house after it burns, a homeowner may have less reason to spend heavily on hardening it beforehand, through measures like metal roofs, ember-resistant vents, and defensible space.

But wildfire risk is also a collective problem. A hardened home remains vulnerable if its neighbors do not harden theirs, which weakens the payoff of individual investment. The logical remedy is for insurers to reward risk reduction with discounts -- creating an incentive to harden homes and, ideally, entire communities.

In practice, those discounts are tiny. Foster said she has not seen a single carrier offer more than a 5% discount for home-hardening. One client, she noted, spent $17,000 on retrofits and received a $42 discount.

## When Doing Everything Right Isn't Enough

The gap between mitigation and reward is starkest in Paradise. Jenny Goodlin, who runs the Rebuild Paradise Foundation and organizes Firewise communities, pressed insurers to see the work residents had done. Her question cuts to the core of the crisis: "If the place that is actually doing everything the insurance industry seems to be wanting them to do still can't get insurance, then who can?"

Casey Taylor, owner of the first home certified under the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety's "Wildfire Prepared" program, put it bluntly: "I've done all the things, and I'm on the FAIR Plan at $7,000. All the things insurance companies are asking us to do, we're doing. They're still not covering us. So something has to give here."

## The Regulatory Backstory

The crisis is not solely a story about climate. California's regulatory system, rooted in Proposition 103 -- passed by voters in 1988 -- required state approval for rate increases and, for years, barred insurers from using forward-looking "catastrophe models" to price future risk. Instead, insurers had to set rates using historical losses, effectively pricing tomorrow's danger with yesterday's data.

UC Berkeley economist Nancy Wallace argues this kept premiums artificially low, encouraged building in fire-prone areas, and ultimately destabilized the market. "They destroyed the markets," she told Planet Money.

In late 2024, California moved to change course. As [Insurance Journal reported](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/west/2024/11/19/801694.htm), the state advanced a wildfire catastrophe-modeling regulation intended to let insurers use forward-looking models in exchange for writing more policies in high-risk areas. The measure drew support from industry groups and sharp criticism from Consumer Watchdog, which argued the coverage commitments fell far short of what was promised.

The reform underscores a tension at the heart of the debate: under Proposition 103, insurers may raise rates, but they are not legally required to offer coverage in wildfire-prone areas.

## Is This a Market Failure?

If residents are doing meaningful work that lowers the odds their homes will burn, shouldn't that be reflected in their bills? Or do the small discounts actually reflect insurers' expected savings?

The uncomfortable answer may be that the market is functioning -- just painfully. UC San Diego economist Judson Boomhower told Planet Money that some places may eventually become too risky to insure, but that society will find ways to keep insuring them. The cold reality, he said, is that "one sign that the market has healed will be higher prices."

That logic is cold comfort to families who cannot absorb those prices. The market, in effect, is signaling that some wooded areas are too dangerous to inhabit -- yet millions of people already live there.

## What to Watch

Several questions will define the next chapter. Will the 2024 catastrophe-modeling reform actually bring private insurers back to high-risk areas at affordable prices? Will discounts for home-hardening grow to meaningfully reflect reduced losses? And can the FAIR Plan remain solvent as enrollment surges?

There is also a structural wrinkle. Planet Money's Greg Rosalsky recently moved his own family off the FAIR Plan and into the "non-admitted" market -- a freer-pricing corner of the industry with better coverage but no state backstop if the insurer fails. It is, as he put it, "not ideal." His family, like many others, is hoping the admitted market starts working again.

For now, the question of what it should cost to insure a home in wildfire country remains unresolved -- and the answer will shape not just household budgets, but the map of where Americans can safely build a life.
