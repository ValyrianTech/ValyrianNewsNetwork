# CIA Unclassified System Breached in Cyberattack

An unclassified computer system at the CIA was breached in a cyberattack, according to a person familiar with the incident who spoke to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/cia-cyberattack-unclassified-system-bfa6d3b02e3b0e2779dfb613c900bd77) on condition of anonymity. The breach did not involve any classified information and the issue has been resolved, the person said, though it was not immediately clear what specific information was compromised or who was responsible.

The incident, first reported by The New York Times on Tuesday, is the latest in a string of cyber intrusions and system failures that have rattled confidence in the security of US government networks.

## What Was Affected

The breach hit a system used for open-source intelligence, or OSINT, which involves the collection and analysis of publicly available data from sources including the internet, commercial satellites and private data brokers. Though the material is generally public or semi-public, open-source information can give analysts significant insight on its own or when combined with intelligence gathered through classified spying programs.

[CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/24/politics/cia-odni-hack-unclassified-information), citing four sources, reported that the Office of the Director of National Intelligence was also affected, and that the intrusion hit a system the agencies use for open-source intelligence analysis. "There have been no disruptions to critical capabilities or classified systems," an ODNI official told CNN. The CIA declined to comment.

The [New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/24/us/politics/spy-agencies-computer-network-breaches.html), which first reported the breach, said the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency was also affected.

## The Artex Connection

According to the Times, the intrusion was believed to have been carried out by hackers using an artificial intelligence hacking tool known as Artex. The tool was released earlier this year by a Chinese engineer and has since been linked by cybersecurity researchers to scores of attacks on businesses worldwide, including in Japan and South Korea. It was made publicly available in July and originally designed to aid organizations with cyberdefense, but has been adapted by malicious hackers to carry out attacks.

Investigators do not know who is responsible for the hack, but some initial signs — including the use of Artex — suggest China may be behind it, according to two people familiar with the matter cited by the Times. China's intelligence services and a vast network of contractors have for years been accused by Western officials of targeting US government agencies and businesses with cyberattacks, allegations Beijing routinely denies. The attribution remains unconfirmed.

## A Pattern of Disruption

The CIA breach comes after a series of highly disruptive incidents that have put a spotlight on vulnerabilities inside critical infrastructure, banking systems and the digital tools millions of people use daily.

An internal network the National Security Agency uses to communicate classified information [was offline for multiple days](https://apnews.com/article/nsa-computer-network-down-22dc6be1bf1d1f5f86a7167f016e9526) last week. The NSA described the disruption as "routine maintenance," and spy chief Jay Clayton said the apparent cause was a software update gone wrong rather than a cyberattack. Separately, the [FBI is investigating](https://apnews.com/article/fbi-database-ncic-hack-d066cff87a64a564db0a00172c124972) an apparent hack of the National Crime Information Center, its key national criminal justice database.

Those incidents followed a nationwide ground stop that snarled air travel after air traffic controllers struggled to communicate with pilots. Apple, JPMorgan Chase and Microsoft also had systems go down this month. Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network has said it is investigating a cybersecurity incident involving unauthorized access to a limited subset of its systems, CNN reported.

## Official Response and Analysis

The Trump administration says the recent outages are not part of a coordinated cyberattack. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton has attributed the disruptions largely to the rush to patch and upgrade systems to stay ahead of hackers empowered with sophisticated artificial intelligence tools.

That dynamic cuts both ways. Cybersecurity experts say AI is enabling attackers to find software vulnerabilities at unprecedented scale, prompting a race to patch systems — and sometimes mistakes along the way. Sen. Mark Warner, the Democratic vice chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee, said AI is "making it possible to identify vulnerabilities across enormous numbers of systems at unprecedented speed," according to the AP.

CNN reported that the CIA/ODNI breach does not appear to be related to the FBI database hack, though officials have not ruled out connections to other incidents.

The episode underscores a counterintelligence concern: even "unclassified" OSINT systems aggregate sensitive insight that can be highly revealing when combined with other data, making them valuable targets despite assurances that no classified material was jeopardized.

## What to Watch

Key questions remain unanswered. Investigators have not confirmed who carried out the breach, when exactly it occurred, or what specific information may have been compromised. The difference in how the AP and CNN characterized the incident — the AP source said the issue was resolved, while CNN reported the full extent of the breach was unclear — suggests a fluid and still-developing picture.

If the China-origin indication is confirmed, the incident would escalate into a major state-linked cyber-espionage story. For now, the CIA breach serves as a reminder that the line between "classified" and "unclassified" offers less protection than it may appear — and that the United States' digital defenses are being tested on multiple fronts at once.
