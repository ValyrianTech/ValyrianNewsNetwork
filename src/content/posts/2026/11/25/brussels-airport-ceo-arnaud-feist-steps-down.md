# Brussels Airport CEO Arnaud Feist to step down after 17 years

Arnaud Feist, the long-serving chief executive of Brussels Airport Company, is stepping down after more than 16 years at the helm of Belgium's national airport, the operator confirmed on Wednesday morning. He will remain in post until early 2027 to ensure a smooth handover, with Chief Financial Officer Davy Vreys taking over as interim CEO from 1 January while continuing in his finance role.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/en/2026/11/25/brussels-airport-ceo-arnaud-feist-steps-down-after-17-years-in-t/), the announcement was first reported by daily De Tijd following a board meeting on Tuesday evening. The airport's official press release framed the move as a natural handover, but the reporting around it tells a more complicated story of friction with the airport's new political masters.

## A carefully worded departure

The company's official statement makes no mention of any disagreement. Instead, it casts the decision as a well-timed succession.

"Having steered the airport through a period of major change, laid the foundations for the coming period and with the current strategy now nearing completion, Arnaud Feist has come to the conclusion that this is the right time to hand over the reins," the press release reads.

Feist struck a personal note. "It has always been more than just a job. It is a passion," he said. "It has been an honour to lead Belgium's second-largest economic driver and to help connect people and businesses in our country with the rest of the world. I am proud of what we have built together."

## The conflict narrative

Behind the polished language lies a dispute that the airport has been forced to address directly. According to [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/ceo-arnaud-feist-vertrekt-na-16-jaar-bij-brussels-airport-uit-onvrede-met-vlaamse-overheid~adb16407/), citing De Tijd, Feist's departure is linked to dissatisfaction with the Flemish government, which became the airport company's largest shareholder last year.

The reporting suggests the friction centred on the daily management of the airport rather than a single dispute. After the Flemish government's entry as a shareholder, pressure reportedly grew on the airport's dividend policy, with the regional authority wanting its investment to yield returns and pushing for higher profit distributions. De Tijd also reported that the airport has become more sensitive to Flemish-francophone tensions since the rise of the nationalist N-VA, and that the francophone Feist reportedly became furious the previous Friday and threatened to resign.

Brussels Airport issued a second statement explicitly rejecting that framing. "Feist's decision is not directed against the Flemish government or other shareholders with whom he has worked well all these years," it said, insisting the choice was a personal one he had weighed carefully over recent months. Feist, board chairman Lorin Parys and the cabinet of Flemish Minister-President Matthias Diependaele all declined to respond to De Tijd's questions, leaving the two accounts unreconciled.

As [Bruzz](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/politiek/ceo-arnaud-feist-vertrekt-bij-brussels-airport-uit-onvrede-met-vlaamse-overheid-2026-11-25) reported, the episode has thrust the airport's governance into the spotlight at a delicate moment for the Brussels region.

## A changing of the guard at a strategic asset

The ownership shift is central to the story. The Flemish and federal governments together now hold more than half of Brussels Airport Company's shares, meaning public authorities, not private investors, hold the decisive voice on the board.

"The most influential voice on the board of directors is not the private shareholder, but the government," said Wouter Dewulf, an aviation economist at the University of Antwerp, as quoted by VRT NWS. His remark captures a structural change that predates Feist's exit and is likely to shape his successor's room for manoeuvre.

## The face of the airport

Feist is widely described as the face of Brussels Airport. He took the helm during the 2008-2009 financial crisis and worked to put the company back on its feet. Under his leadership the airport expanded passenger connections, grew cargo volumes at Brucargo and renovated the 1958 departure hall, later reopened as the Sky Hall event space.

The board of directors said he would be remembered for "the leadership, determination and humanity he showed following the terrorist attacks of 22 March 2016, one of the darkest moments in the airport's history." It added that over the past 20 years he helped transform the airport's appearance while building a leading airport operator. More recently he unveiled the Hub 3.0 programme, which envisages expanding the departure and arrival halls and adding a new hotel and office complex.

## What to watch

The immediate focus turns to the search for a permanent successor, which the board has now begun. Vreys will hold the fort as interim CEO while also serving as CFO, concentrating considerable responsibility during a critical period for the Hub 3.0 expansion.

Several questions remain open. Will the next CEO be chosen with an eye to political sensitivities given the government's dominance of the board? How will the leadership change affect the dividend policy and the airport's environmental commitments? And does the company's internal denial fully settle the matter, or is there a genuine governance conflict that could resurface?

For now, Brussels Airport is presenting a calm, orderly transition. Whether the reality matches that picture may only become clear once the new chief executive takes the controls.
