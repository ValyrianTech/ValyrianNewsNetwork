# COP31 Ends in Failure as Fossil Fuel Split Sinks Deal

The COP31 UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, collapsed without an overarching agreement on Sunday after the European Union and a coalition of climate-vulnerable nations refused to accept a final text that made no explicit mention of fossil fuels. Belgium, which had spent the summit's final days pressing alongside some twenty countries to restore a commitment to phase out oil, gas and coal, publicly rejected the proposed "Antalya pact," joining at least 32 countries in blocking consensus in the closing plenary.

The failure makes COP31 only the third UN climate conference in history to end without a cover decision, after The Hague in 2000 and Copenhagen in 2009, and the longest ever — running nearly 50 hours past its scheduled close.

## A retreat from Dubai

The deadlock turned on a single question: how explicitly should the final text address the fuels driving climate change. Three years earlier at COP28 in Dubai, countries had agreed to a landmark "transition away from fossil fuels." But the second draft of the COP31 cover decision, published on 19 November, [excised any mention of fossil fuels entirely](https://www.rtbf.be/article/cop31-a-antalya-la-belgique-et-une-vingtaine-de-pays-haussent-le-ton-pour-reinscrire-la-sortie-du-petrole-et-du-gaz-dans-le-projet-d-accord-11818457), according to RTBF — a step the EU and its allies framed as a dangerous regression.

Belgium's federal climate minister, Jean-Luc Crucke, refused to treat the battle as lost while the text was still in flux. "The final text is not yet approved. It changes hour by hour, and it remains a demand of Europe, a demand of all the countries that are here," he said on 20 November. "So we will fight to the end."

That coalition was broader than Europe alone. France, Panama and the Solomon Islands published an open letter demanding that the final decision cite a "clear transition away from fossil fuels" alongside "immediate clean electrification." Panama's environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, warned bluntly: "We must act immediately, otherwise we will block any text that does not reflect this immediate action."

The presidency, led by Turkey's environment minister Murat Kurum, had instead built the summit around an "electrification" theme — a "35 by 35" target to draw 35% of global final energy consumption from electricity by 2035. Critics noted the text did not specify how that electricity should be generated, leaving room for fossil-fuelled power.

## A three-front breakdown

The collapse was not driven by fossil fuels alone. A second fault line was climate finance: developing countries, led by the African Group, had demanded a firm $120 billion adaptation-finance target by 2035, which the presidency dropped from the text. A third was carbon capture and storage. Oil-producing nations inserted CCS language that the EU and environmental groups saw as a loophole allowing fossil fuels to keep being burned.

When a fourth and supposedly final "Antalya pact" emerged in the early hours of 22 November with only minor changes, the rejection was swift. Speaking for the EU, Irish Climate Minister Darragh O'Brien — Ireland held the rotating EU presidency — did not mince words. "We will not support an agreement that does not even mention fossil fuels, the main cause of climate change," he said, as [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/eu-verwerpt-akkoord-dat-voorligt-we-kunnen-geen-beslissing-ste~1794235749946/). "We will not accept a step back in time. And we will not accept a climate conference that greenwashes fossil fuels."

Crucke was equally blunt. "We must not accept the comedy that Turkey is presenting to us, with more fossil fuels and no language about science," he said. "We must show the courage to say no. The game is over, as far as we are concerned."

One by one, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Belize, Norway and Vanuatu rejected the text. The least developed countries, the UK, South Korea and Japan said consensus had not been reached. COP President Kurum confirmed the deadlock, and the gavel fell in the evening local time — nearly two full days late.

## Reactions: frustration and a call to reform

The recriminations came fast. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he shared "the deep frustration of everyone who had hoped that governments would respond to what the climate crisis requires," calling fossil fuels "the engine of the climate crisis" and warning: "We cannot solve the fire if we keep pouring oil on it."

UNFCCC Executive Secretary Simon Stiell spoke of "scope to improve, modernise and reform" the UN climate process. Former US Vice-President Al Gore went further, calling for a "fundamental reform" of the COP system itself. Greenpeace's delegation head, Jasper Inventor, framed the outcome as double-edged: "We have managed to block an agreement tailor-made for the fossil fuel industry, but the absence of an agreement is very painful for the people at the front line of the climate crisis, such as the island states of the Pacific."

Not everything was lost. Several technical items were adopted, including on the just transition work programme, the global goal on adaptation, carbon markets under Article 6, and long-term finance. Outside the formal cover decision, roughly 80 nations signed onto the "35 by 35" electrification target, and growing coalitions championed "roadmaps" for moving away from fossil fuels.

Belgium itself joined a smaller "coalition of the willing" pledging to draw up a roadmap to transition away from fossil fuels regardless of the summit's outcome. "We have signed a few alliances with countries that do want a phase-out of fossil fuels," Crucke said. "It won't happen in one day, but we have started."

## A process under strain

The failure exposed deeper fractures in the consensus-bound COP model, under which a single objection can block any decision. The unusual Turkey–Australia co-presidency — Turkey hosting while Australian minister Chris Bowen served as "president of the negotiations" — drew criticism, as did both hosts' fossil-fuel ambitions: Turkey unveiled two new oil fields on the eve of the summit and aims to triple output by 2030, while Australia is the world's second-largest coal exporter.

The Belgian climate scientist and former IPCC vice-chair Jean-Pascal van Ypersele called the collapse "symbolic" of a fragmented geopolitical moment — the United States largely absent, China quiet, and the EU ambitious in rhetoric but constrained in practice. Yet he urged patience with the process itself. "I don't think there is really an alternative," he said. "So even now that it is difficult, I think we must continue to support the process."

Le Soir, in [a sharply critical editorial](https://www.lesoir.be/784283/article/2026-11-23/lurgence-climatique-se-heurte-au-mur-de-la-cop31-antalya), invoked a 1936 Winston Churchill speech to describe a world "determined to let itself be carried along by events" — and warned that the indecision carries a mounting cost.

## What to watch

Attention now turns to whether the "coalition of the willing" roadmap amounts to a meaningful framework or a symbolic gesture outside the UN process, and to COP32 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, where momentum must be rebuilt. The unmet $120 billion adaptation-finance target remains a live grievance for developing nations, and calls for structural reform of the consensus rule — from Crucke, Stiell and Gore alike — are unlikely to fade.

For Belgium, the summit ends with its minister walking out of the plenary rather than endorsing a text he said turns its back on science. Whether that stance strengthens the case for reform or deepens the divide will define the road to Addis Ababa.
