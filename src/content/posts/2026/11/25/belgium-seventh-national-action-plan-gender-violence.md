# Belgium Unveils 100+ Measures Against Gender Violence

Belgium on Wednesday presented its seventh National Action Plan against gender-based violence, a sweeping strategy containing more than 100 concrete measures aimed at better protecting victims and tackling violence linked to gender. Federal Minister for Equal Opportunities Rob Beenders (Vooruit) unveiled the plan on the eve of the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/plus-de-100-mesures-concretes-un-nouveau-plan-d-action-national-de-lutte-contre-les-violences-liees-au-genre-11820736).

The plan's central ambition is to sharpen Belgium's response to forms of violence that existing law has struggled to reach — sexual harassment in public spaces, psychological coercive control, and the spread of online misogyny. It is a federal–regional collaboration, drawing together the federal government and the country's federated entities.

## The Scale of the Problem

At the presentation, Beenders recalled the underlying reality: one in six Belgian women aged 18 to 74 has already been a victim of sexual violence. Nearly 4,700 incidents of sexual violence in public space were recorded last year alone, a figure that underpins one of the plan's headline commitments — a new legal framework targeting street harassment.

As [La DH](https://www.dhnet.be/dernieres-depeches/2026/11/25/un-nouveau-plan-daction-national-de-lutte-contre-les-violences-liees-au-genre-devoile-57QXILZZERAQXMBFMTXFCQISMI/) reported, the plan also leans heavily on prevention, emphasizing awareness-raising and the training of "active witnesses" — bystanders equipped to intervene when they see harassment or violence.

## Three Measures in the Spotlight

Although the plan spans more than 100 commitments, the minister detailed three at the presentation, as [BX1](https://bx1.be/categories/news/journee-contre-les-violences-faites-aux-femmes-un-nouveau-plan-daction-national-et-plusieurs-initiatives-a-bruxelles/) reported.

The first is the new legal framework for sexual harassment in public spaces. The second is a study on online masculinism, which will map the phenomenon in Belgium across 2026 and 2027 — a direct response to what the national equality institute has described as the "resurgence of misogynistic discourse." The third concerns coercive control. Beenders proposes studying how to legally protect victims of what French-language coverage calls "terreur intime" — intimate terrorism — a pattern of intimidation and psychological domination that often precedes physical violence between partners.

That focus aligns with mounting evidence that non-physical abuse is both widespread and under-prosecuted. By commissioning a study into legal protection for coercive-control victims, Belgium is signalling interest in following jurisdictions such as the United Kingdom, which criminalized the offence in 2015.

## Expanding Frontline Services

The plan also commits to expanding Belgium's network of Sexual Violence Care Centres (CPVS), which has supported more than 22,500 people since it was established in 2017. The network will grow to 13 establishments by early 2027, backed by a budget of 900,000 euros.

Further measures target persons with disabilities, commission a study on female genital mutilation, and establish an interdisciplinary scientific committee to examine adequate compensation for victims of sexual violence.

The need for such support is visible in Brussels. The capital's bar association reported that the Lawyers Victims Assistance project — which provides free legal consultations to women victims of violence — has received nearly 2,000 requests for help in three years. The service still fields 15 to 20 calls each week, and more than 340 lawyers have received specialist training since the scheme launched in 2023, with over 60 continuing to offer free consultations.

## Building on a Long Institutional Record

The new strategy is the seventh in a series of national action plans. The previous plan, covering 2021-2025, closed at the end of last year, and its final report — coordinated by the Institute for the Equality of Women and Men (IEFH) — was published on 3 March 2026. That assessment struck a cautionary note that clearly shaped its successor. While highlighting meaningful progress in prevention, protection and prosecution, the [IEFH](https://igvm-iefh.belgium.be/fr/actualites/plan-daction-national-de-lutte-contre-les-violences-basees-sur-le-genre-2021-2025) concluded that "efforts must imperatively continue to better prevent and combat gender-based violence, all the more so in a context marked by worrying trends and the resurgence of misogynistic discourse."

The new plan also appears to absorb commitments that had stalled elsewhere. Reporting by [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/771569/article/2026-09-17/le-pack-nouveau-depart-renvoye-au-prochain-plan-contre-les-violences-de-genre) in September indicated that the "pack nouveau départ" — a package of support measures designed to help victims of domestic violence leave an abusive partner — had been deferred and folded into the forthcoming gender-violence plan. That package rests on a widely shared observation: that victims often stay with or return to an abuser not only because of emotional control but because of administrative and financial obstacles — finding housing, arranging schooling for children, activating social rights, securing a lawyer — all while in a state of acute psychological vulnerability.

## What to Watch

Several open questions remain. Only three of the plan's 100-plus measures have been detailed publicly, and the full list has yet to be released. Its success will depend on how effectively the federal government and the federated entities coordinate implementation — a perennial challenge in Belgium's layered institutional system. The 2026-2027 mapping of online masculinism and the exploration of a legal framework for coercive control will be the measures to watch most closely, as both address forms of harm that have historically slipped through the gaps of criminal law and public awareness.

The timing of the announcement is itself deliberate. Belgium released the plan on the eve of the international day dedicated to the issue, alongside a wave of symbolic local initiatives in Brussels — red benches inaugurated in Woluwe-Saint-Lambert, a "Red Shoes" exhibition in Anderlecht, and the European Commission illuminated in red. Those gestures, referencing anti-femicide symbols used worldwide, underscore the plan's dual purpose: to change institutions and to shift public attitudes.
