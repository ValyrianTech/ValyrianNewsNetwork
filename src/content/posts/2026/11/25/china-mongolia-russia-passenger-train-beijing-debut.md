# China-Mongolia-Russia Passenger Train Debuts From Beijing

A new China–Mongolia–Russia international passenger train pulled out of Beijing Railway Station at 07:27 local time on Wednesday, 25 November 2026, carrying its first cohort of travelers on a 2,666-kilometer, roughly 55-hour journey through Ulaanbaatar to Irkutsk in Russia, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/photo/20261125/d8a7601d81bb4012aca176e04512b888/c.html).

The service, designated V3/4 in China and No. 3/4 internationally, runs once per week and is operated under agreements between China Railway and Russian Railways (RZD). Its launch restores a leg of the historic Trans-Mongolian corridor that had been severed for passengers following the February 2020 suspension of the Moscow–Beijing route during the COVID-19 pandemic.

## A Reconfigured Successor to a Historic Route

The new train follows the Trans-Mongolian Railway, the 1956-completed line linking Ulan-Ude in Russia with Erenhot in China via the Mongolian capital. It is a distinct service from the famous K3/4 Beijing–Moscow train that has operated since 1959 — the new train terminates at Irkutsk on the Russian side rather than continuing to the Russian capital, though its numbering echoes that older service.

According to [AK&M Information Agency](https://www.akm.ru/eng/news/russia-and-china-to-launch-beijing-irkutsk-train-via-ulaanbaatar/), RZD and China Railway "agreed to organize the running of international train No. 3/4 Beijing–Irkutsk via the capital of Mongolia, Ulaanbaatar." Russian media framed the launch as the route's return after a six-year hiatus, counting from the 2020 suspension — though analysts note it is best understood as a reconfigured successor rather than a direct reinstatement of the full Moscow service.

The launch fits a broader, staged reopening of cross-border passenger rail between China and Russia. Russia–China links partially resumed in mid-December 2024 with the Suifenhe–Grodekovo train, and in March 2026 RZD began running trains between Zabaikalsk and Manzhouli, with through-carriages organized from Irkutsk and Chita.

## The Journey and Its Logistics

The inaugural timetable places departure from Beijing on Wednesdays at 07:27, arrival in Ulaanbaatar on Thursdays at 14:35, and arrival in Irkutsk on Fridays at 14:55. The return leg leaves Irkutsk on Saturdays at 06:58, reaches Ulaanbaatar on Sundays at 06:38, and arrives back in Beijing on Mondays at 14:35. The first return departure from Irkutsk is scheduled for 28 November.

Intermediate stops include Jiningnan and Erenhot in China; Zamyn-Üüd, Sainshand, Choir, Züünkharaa, Darkhan, and Sükhbaatar in Mongolia; and Naushki, Ulan-Ude, and Slyudyanka in Russia. The train is formed and staffed by Chinese Railways using Chinese-made passenger cars, including standard compartments, deluxe SV cabins, a dining car, and power-generating cars.

One signature feature of the corridor is the gauge break at Erenhot: Russian and Mongolian track uses 1,520 mm broad gauge while China uses 1,435 mm standard gauge, requiring trains to undergo bogie exchange — swapping wheelsets — at the border. Combined with border checks, this helps explain the relatively modest average speed of roughly 48 km/h over the route.

Ticketing remains limited. Per RZD, tickets are sold only at station ticket offices rather than online, with a 60-day advance sales window. In Russia, tickets can be purchased for departures from Russian stations and from Mongolia; in China, for departures from Chinese stations.

## Connectivity With Commercial Caveats

Xinhua framed the launch in terms of regional integration, stating the train "will further promote people-to-people exchanges, economic and trade cooperation, and cultural exchanges among the three countries." That language aligns with Beijing's broader push for regional connectivity and with deepening China–Mongolia–Russia trilateral cooperation on infrastructure and trade.

The commercial significance, however, is more modest than the symbolism. A once-weekly frequency limits capacity and flexibility, and the absence of online cross-border booking reflects still-limited integration. Both factors position the service as much a diplomatic and symbolic gesture as a mass-transit solution.

Tourism offers the clearest near-term upside. The route serves Irkutsk — the gateway to Lake Baikal — Ulaanbaatar and the Mongolian steppe, and northern China, making it attractive to rail-tourism enthusiasts. Russian travel media have highlighted railway fans who waited six years for the connection to return.

## What to Watch

Several questions will determine whether the new service becomes a durable commercial link or remains a chiefly symbolic one. The first is whether frequency increases from weekly to multiple departures per week. The second is whether ticketing migrates online and becomes internationally bookable. The third is whether the route is eventually extended beyond Irkutsk toward Moscow, restoring the full historic K3/4 path.

Passenger load factors over the coming months will offer the clearest signal. For now, the inaugural departure marks a small but tangible step in reknitting a corridor that once connected East Asia to Russia and Europe — and a test of how far three neighbors are prepared to turn connectivity rhetoric into a working railway.
