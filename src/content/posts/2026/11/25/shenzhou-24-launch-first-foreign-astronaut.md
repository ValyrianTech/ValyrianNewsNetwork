# Shenzhou-24 Launches With First Foreign Astronaut

China launched the Shenzhou-24 crewed spacecraft on the morning of November 25, 2026, placing three astronauts into orbit — including the first foreign national ever to fly on a Chinese mission. The Long March 2F Y24 rocket lifted off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in the Gobi Desert at 08:18 Beijing time, and the China Manned Space Agency (CMSA) declared the launch a complete success after the spacecraft separated from the rocket and entered its planned orbit roughly ten minutes later, according to the Observer-affiliated [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/ZhengZhi/2026_11_25_909824.shtml) news report.

The flight marks the 680th launch of the Long March rocket family and the 24th Shenzhou crewed mission, underscoring the steady cadence of a program that now operates its Tiangong space station on a routine, rotating basis.

## A Veteran Commander and Two First-Timers

The crew is led by commander Tang Hongbo, a spaceflight veteran who previously flew on Shenzhou-12 and Shenzhou-17. On this mission he becomes the first Chinese astronaut to visit the Tiangong station three times. He is joined by flight engineer Wu Yi, a third-batch astronaut on his first flight, and payload specialist Muhammad Zeeshan Ali of Pakistan — also a first-time flyer and the first foreign astronaut to visit the Chinese station.

Upon orbital insertion, the spacecraft was to prioritize a 3.5-hour autonomous fast rendezvous and docking, linking up at the forward port of the Tianhe core module to form a three-ship, three-module complex. The crew is scheduled to complete an on-orbit handover with the Shenzhou-23 crew, continue routine station maintenance and inspection, and conduct space science and application experiments. As part of the arrangement, the Pakistani astronaut will return aboard Shenzhou-23 after a short joint flight, while one Shenzhou-23 crew member remains in orbit to complete a one-year residency experiment.

## A Milestone for China–Pakistan Cooperation

Ali's participation stems from a China–Pakistan cooperation agreement under which he was selected in April 2026 as one of the first cohort of foreign astronauts in China's crewed space program. In a profile published by [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/ZhengZhi/2026_11_24_909740.shtml) and sourced from China National Radio, Ali recounted that the most challenging part of his training was neither the centrifuge nor sea-survival exercises, but the Chinese language itself.

With the help of his crewmates, he mastered the technical Chinese terminology needed for normal and emergency operating procedures. "I am very confident that I will be the one to lift China–Pakistan friendship to the height of space," he said. Carrying a Pakistani science payload, he will focus on a "green micro-factory" experiment studying how microorganisms — particularly cyanobacteria — behave under microgravity.

The CMSA said preparations for the joint flight emphasized three areas: professional skills training, crew coordination, and language instruction focused on Chinese operational commands and terminology. Ali, who previously worked in human resources before being selected as an astronaut, described the mission as a responsibility to billions of people in both countries, and said that from space Earth appears without borders — a "community with a shared future for mankind."

## The Messaging From Beijing

The launch coincided with a commemorative feature published by the state news agency Xinhua, revisiting President Xi Jinping's longstanding attention to the space sector. The piece, produced by Xinhua's New Media Center, recalled that Xi has held three "space–Earth calls" with astronauts in orbit and called on the public to "cheer on China's space program" on the day of the Shenzhou-24 launch, according to the [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/leaders/20261125/f21fdee7686045188134a3038be43842/c.html) report. It is a review of established messages rather than a newly issued congratulatory telegram.

## What to Watch

The immediate test will be the fast rendezvous and docking, which had not yet been confirmed in initial reports. Beyond the technical milestone, the flight carries a symbolic weight: by flying a foreign astronaut for the first time, China is presenting its station as a platform for international scientific cooperation, framed within its broader South–South partnership narrative. How far that opening extends — and whether more foreign astronauts follow — will be a key indicator of how China intends to position Tiangong in the years ahead.
