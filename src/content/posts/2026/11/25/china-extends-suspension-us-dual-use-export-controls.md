# China Extends Suspension of US Dual-Use Export Controls

China's Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) has extended the suspension of a key clause in its 2024 dual-use export control measures targeting the United States, keeping a pause on near-blanket restrictions for critical minerals such as gallium, germanium, antimony and graphite. According to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20261125/e7f24d2d7bb6404988950976ba03df6c/c.html), the suspension of the second clause of MOFCOM Announcement No. 46 of 2024 will run from 28 November 2026 to 27 January 2027.

The move is the latest in a rolling series of suspensions designed to keep Beijing's most visible economic countermeasures against Washington in abeyance — and to preserve the fragile trade truce the two sides struck in late 2025.

## What Is Being Suspended

The measure at issue, Announcement No. 46 of 2024, was issued on 3 December 2024 as a response to US export restrictions. It contained two clauses. The first prohibits the export of dual-use items to US military users or for military end-uses, and it remains in force. The second clause — the one now paused — stated that exports to the US of dual-use items related to gallium, germanium, antimony and superhard materials would "in principle" not be permitted, while graphite exports would face stricter end-user and end-use review.

Crucially, the suspension does not remove these goods from control. As [legal analysis by Shanghai Landi (Shenzhen) law firm's customs and tax team](https://www.163.com/dy/article/KFU5JG5I05562JTQ.html) explained, the minerals remain on China's Dual-Use Items Export Control List, and exports still require a MOFCOM licence. What changes is the review standard: the presumption of denial and stricter scrutiny are replaced by the ordinary case-by-case licensing process that applies to dual-use items generally.

In short, the goods stay controlled, but the near-blanket ban on US-bound shipments is lifted while the suspension holds.

## A Chain of Rolling Suspensions

The suspension is not a new concession so much as an extension of an existing one. The sequence began with the late-October 2025 Kuala Lumpur economic and trade consultations, held after a leaders' summit in Busan, where Washington and Beijing agreed a one-year truce package.

As [Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/realtime/china/story20251109-7790874) reported, China had announced on 9 November 2025 that it would suspend the second clause of Announcement No. 46 from that date until 27 November 2026. The latest notice, signed on 23 November 2026 and published two days later, bridges the gap left when that earlier suspension lapses — rolling the pause forward by roughly two months, to the end of January 2027.

The Landi analysis described the earlier suspension as a direct result of the Kuala Lumpur consultations. The same political logic appears to underpin the current extension.

## Why It Matters

Gallium, germanium, antimony, graphite and superhard materials are critical inputs for semiconductors, defence electronics, batteries and advanced manufacturing. China dominates global processing of several of these, giving Beijing meaningful leverage in the technology rivalry — and making controls on these minerals among its most potent economic tools.

The timing is significant. The Kuala Lumpur truce did not resolve fundamental disputes between the two economies; it bought time. China's decision to hold its October 2025 countermeasures in abeyance was a core deliverable of that bargain, and extending the pause signals continued adherence to it. The quiet, procedural tone of the notice — devoid of political rhetoric — is consistent with Beijing's pattern of implementing truce commitments without fanfare.

## Analysis: A Fine-Tuning, Not a Dismantling

The practical easing is limited. Because the first clause remains in force and the minerals stay on the control list, the change is confined to commercial US-bound shipments of these specific materials, under normal licensing. It is a fine-tuning of China's export-control arsenal rather than a dismantling of it.

The short horizon is telling. A roughly two-month window, rather than a fresh one-year pause, preserves maximum flexibility and hints that the broader truce may face review points in early 2027. The underlying bans remain on the books and are merely held in abeyance — a design that allows Beijing to switch controls back on if relations deteriorate.

By keeping the minerals controlled and the military end-use ban intact, China retains its bargaining chip. The suspension is reversible by design, and the "pause" framing is itself a negotiating instrument.

## What to Watch

Several questions remain open. It is unclear whether Washington has reciprocated with matching extensions — for instance, of its own suspension of the "50% penetration rule" or tariff pauses — beyond their separate expiry dates. Nor is it confirmed whether the parallel suspensions of China's October 2025 measures on rare earths, lithium batteries and artificial graphite anode materials, which were paused until 10 November 2026, have been separately extended.

The central question is whether the current suspension will be extended again before 27 January 2027 or allowed to lapse. For now, the notice signals that Beijing intends to keep the détente alive — on its own terms and on a short leash.
