# Liège Customs Step Up Counterfeit Watch Before Black Friday

Ahead of Black Friday, customs officers at Liège Airport have stepped up their vigilance against counterfeit and non-compliant products arriving in parcels during the year-end online shopping peak, [RTBF reports](https://www.rtbf.be/article/black-friday-a-liege-airport-les-douaniers-redoublent-de-vigilance-face-aux-contrefacons-et-aux-produits-non-conformes-11820822). The intensified checks target the surge in e-commerce shipments that accompanies the commercial kickoff of the holiday shopping season, which this year falls on Friday 27 November 2026.

## A Hub at the Heart of the Parcel Flood

The stakes are considerable. Liège-Bierset has become one of Europe's leading e-commerce gateways, a hub [submerged by some 1.4 billion products, most of them from China](https://www.lemonde.fr/economie/article/2025/09/25/l-aeroport-de-liege-symbole-des-exces-de-la-mondialisation-les-colis-nous-ecrasent-et-cela-ne-fait-qu-empirer_6642890_3234.html), according to reporting by Le Monde. In the airport's customs warehouses, crates and parcels marked "Made in China" are stacked floor to ceiling — a volume that makes the site a natural chokepoint for goods entering Europe from overseas.

That position reflects a broader shift in global commerce. As RTBF's Caroline Adam reported, based on a reportage by journalist Thomas Michiels, customs officers now confront a torrent of small parcels rather than traditional bulk freight. At Liège-Bierset, roughly a thousand counterfeiting cases are opened every month, Le Monde noted, forcing officers to rely on risk analysis to decide which shipments to inspect.

## Counterfeits That Carry Real Risks

Customs officer Julien Richard described the product categories that most frequently raise flags. "When it comes to counterfeits, we see an enormous number of products like pairs of shoes, bags, backpacks, but also toys where certain brands are counterfeited," he said. Crucially, he noted, the problem goes beyond intellectual property: counterfeit goods often coincide with safety non-compliance.

"Beyond the risk of the brand being counterfeited, there is also a risk to product safety, because we find that counterfeiting goes hand in hand with non-compliance and therefore a risk for the end consumer," Richard explained.

When officers suspect a problem, they apply a "stop function" that triggers a processing delay but is reserved strictly for shipments where non-compliance is suspected. The most damaging consequence for a buyer, Richard said, is simple: "He will not receive his parcel. So in addition to a possible delay regarding a Christmas gift, he will not receive his merchandise."

## Cosmetics: A Category of Concern

The Black Friday period brings a particular surge in cosmetics orders — and with it, a particularly high rate of violations. Kristian Vanderwaeren, administrator-general of Belgium's Customs and Excise, was blunt in his assessment.

"Almost all cosmetics that come from China and enter here through Liège are non-compliant," he said. "And that is problematic. Cosmetics are something we put on our bodies. We really need to make sure there are no elements that are dangerous to public health." The inspections reveal infraction rates of around 80% in that category, according to Vanderwaeren.

Toward Christmas, the flagged categories shift. Toys become a priority because of safety risks, as do luminaires and lighting products, which fall into the electrical and electronic goods category and show numerous non-conformities.

## How the Controls Work

Belgian customs follow a structured process. "We do a risk analysis, and on the basis of that risk analysis, we stop [the parcel]," Vanderwaeren explained. "If we see there are problems, at that point we contact the SPF Public Health to help us. They have the specialists to say whether it is problematic or not. And if it is problematic, at that point we halt the goods and we also destroy them."

The Federal Public Service for Public Health thus plays an essential role, providing the specialist assessment that determines whether a shipment poses a genuine health hazard. Confirmed dangerous products are halted and destroyed rather than returned to circulation.

## A Tiny Fraction, a Residual Risk

The scale of the inspection effort is dwarfed by the volume of goods. Only about 0.006% of parcels are checked by Belgian customs, RTBF reported — a figure that underscores the reliance on targeted, intelligence-driven selection rather than blanket screening. In practice, this means enforcement depends heavily on accurate risk profiling to catch the shipments most likely to endanger consumers.

## What Consumers Can Do

Customs officials used the Black Friday spotlight to urge caution. They invited consumers to verify the merchandise they order online and to buy only from reliable, trustworthy websites — advice that carries particular weight for cosmetics and electronics from unfamiliar sellers.

Richard's message to shoppers was direct: "We can only invite end consumers to check the merchandise they order on the internet, to use reliable sites."

## What to Watch

As the holiday shopping season moves into full swing, the coming weeks will test the balance between facilitation and enforcement at one of Europe's busiest parcel gateways. The cosmetics wave of Black Friday will give way to the toys and lighting products of December — and with them, a fresh set of risks that customs officers will be watching closely. For consumers, the lesson is straightforward: a bargain that never arrives, or arrives unsafe, is no bargain at all.
