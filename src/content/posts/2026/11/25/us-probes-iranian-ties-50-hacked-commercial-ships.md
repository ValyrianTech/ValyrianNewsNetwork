# US Probes Iranian Ties to 50 Hacked Commercial Ships

The U.S. government has intelligence suggesting that as many as 50 commercial shipping vessels were recently compromised by a series of coordinated cyberattacks, activity federal officials are investigating as potentially linked to Iran, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/24/nx-s1-6052994/hack-commercial-vessels-iran). The breaches, first reported by The Wall Street Journal, mark a potential expansion of the U.S.–Iran conflict from military operations into cyberspace and international maritime trade.

## What Investigators Know

The compromised vessels include crude oil tankers, liquefied petroleum gas carriers, and ships carrying chemicals and other hazardous cargo. According to a U.S. official familiar with the matter who was not authorized to speak on the record, the affected ships operated near the U.S., Europe and Asia. Russia is also being considered as a possible source of the attacks.

Crucially, investigators have not established Iranian responsibility. A Trump administration official told the [Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/politics/national-security/iran-us-cyberattack-suspect-fed78942) that investigators found no evidence of Iranian involvement and characterized four of the attacks as minor. The suspicion of Iran is treated as an investigative lead, not proof.

The attackers exploited known vulnerabilities in Fortinet FortiGate devices — firewalls and virtual private networks widely used in the maritime industry — to gain a foothold, then pivoted from ships' information technology systems to the operational technology that controls physical processes. The U.S. Coast Guard and FBI warned of exactly this risk in a [joint cybersecurity bulletin](https://www.uscg.mil/Portals/0/Images/cyber/Maritime%20Cyber%20Alert%2002-26%20(joint%20with%20FBI)_TLP%20CLEAR.pdf) issued in September, noting that "threat actors routinely scan for specific, unpatched vulnerabilities to gain a foothold."

## A New Paradigm at Sea

According to a U.S. official, hackers “could have impacted the systems on board that control propulsion, steering, navigation, and cargo management,” though no confirmed instance of such physical manipulation has been disclosed. In at least one case, an affected vessel was an LNG tanker, raising safety concerns given the need to keep such cargo at controlled temperatures.

Crews in some cases noticed something was wrong through email disruptions, an inability to log in to accounts, or, in at least one case, an extended communications blackout. Some vessels received bomb threats, ransom demands and threatening messages, including warnings in Farsi telling operators not to transport American products.

“It's a totally new paradigm that these tankers are networked in this way,” the U.S. official said. A former Navy official, speaking anonymously, told NPR that “we had been tracking the possibility to exploit and take control of [operational technology] systems on Navy ships and Merchant [Marine] ships for a long time, and now this is coming to fruition.”

## The Response on the Water

The U.S. Coast Guard, working with the FBI, has deployed specialized cyber-protection teams to board and inspect vessels before they enter U.S. ports. At least four vessels approaching U.S. ports were inspected in recent weeks. In August, the two agencies boarded two foreign-flagged commercial vessels in the Gulf of Mexico after indications their networks had been compromised.

“The captain, crew, and shore-side corporate staff were critical partners in helping to ensure the threats were mitigated,” the FBI and Coast Guard said in a joint statement. Both agencies said there are currently no reports of operational disruptions, vessel instability, physical danger to crews, or environmental impacts.

## Analysis: A Blended Threat

The central tension in the story is suspicion versus proof. Iran is the lead suspect given the maritime confrontation, Farsi-language threats and prior Iranian cyber activity — including attacks on U.S. water treatment systems and refrigeration systems at grocery stores on U.S. military bases. But the possibility of a false-flag operation or an opportunistic criminal actor has not been ruled out.

The severity of the campaign is also disputed. One U.S. official describes potentially serious operational-technology exposure, while a Trump administration official downplays the attacks as minor. Independent shipping analyst Lars Jensen has cautioned that catastrophic physical outcomes — remotely causing an explosion or steering a ship into a bridge — are unlikely because vessels retain manual controls and overrides. The more immediate risks, he suggested, are operational disruption, delays, ransom demands and costly supply-chain interruptions.

The vulnerability itself is telling: the attacks exploited a known, patchable flaw, suggesting basic cyber-hygiene failures across the maritime industry rather than sophisticated zero-day exploitation. “These are not sophisticated attacks,” the U.S. official said. “This is a known vulnerability that could be mitigated with some pretty basic cyber hygiene.”

## What to Watch

The investigation matters well beyond U.S. waters: roughly one-fifth of global petroleum liquids consumption normally passes through the Strait of Hormuz, making maritime cyber risk a global energy-security concern — especially for Gulf logistics hubs in the UAE, Qatar and Saudi Arabia.

Looking ahead, [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/mena/cyber-threats-targeting-50-ships-spark-fears-of-escalating-us-iran-conflict-in-maritime-trade-1.500758663) identifies key watchpoints: whether the FBI and Coast Guard publicly establish links to Iran or Russia; whether more vessels report intrusions, bomb threats or ransom demands; and whether Washington expands sanctions or maritime cyber operations.

For now, officials are bracing for a busy winter shipping season. “People take the marine transportation system for granted until there's a problem and their stuff doesn't get there by Christmas,” the U.S. official said. “I wish more Americans knew how complex this system is.”
