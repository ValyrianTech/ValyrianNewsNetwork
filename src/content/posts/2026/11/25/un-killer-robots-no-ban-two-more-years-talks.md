# UN Talks End Without Killer Robot Ban, Just Two More Years

Five days of United Nations negotiations over "killer robots" ended early on 21 November without an international ban on lethal autonomous weapons systems, with the 128 participating countries instead agreeing to keep talking for another two years, according [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/21/vn-bereiken-overeenstemming-tijdens-conferentie-over-killerrobot/).

The conference in Geneva concluded with countries agreeing to "begin the elaboration of an international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons systems," according to conference chair Julien Thöni. That instrument is meant to be complete by 2028. For now, there are no concrete agreements on how such weapons may be used.

## What Was at Stake

Lethal autonomous weapons systems are weapons that use artificial intelligence to independently select and attack targets—without a human giving the final order to fire. Roughly 100 countries had pushed for negotiations on a legally binding ban on fully autonomous systems, arguing that machines should not be empowered to make life-and-death decisions.

But the states that actually possess the technology, including the United States and Russia, opposed binding commitments. That divide mirrors long-standing patterns in arms control, where the countries furthest ahead in a given capability tend to resist constraints on it.

The talks took place under the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, a treaty signed by 128 countries. Because its decisions must be approved by consensus, a single country can block an agreement—and any terms that emerge must be acceptable to every party in the room. That structure, critics say, effectively hands a veto to the very states least inclined to accept limits.

## Disappointment From Campaigners

The outcome drew sharp criticism from civil society. The NGO coalition Stop Killer Robots described the result as a "watered-down mandate" that does not genuinely clear the path to a legally binding agreement. Human Rights Watch called the outcome "disappointing."

Brazilian ambassador Frederico Meyer, one of the voices pressing for stricter rules, voiced frustration at the pace of the process. "Technology evolves faster than diplomacy," he said. "Diplomats are very slow."

The result also fell short of the ambitions of UN Secretary-General António Guterres, who had hoped that formal negotiations on an international agreement would begin in 2026. That milestone was not reached.

## What Comes Next

Over the next two years, the work will focus on a narrower question: which autonomous weapons can be prohibited under international humanitarian law, and which may remain permitted but subject to rules governing their use. That framing shifts the debate from whether to ban the technology outright toward where the line between lawful and unlawful autonomy should be drawn.

The core difficulty is that the technology is advancing faster than the diplomatic machinery designed to govern it. Consensus-based forums move at the speed of their most reluctant member, while autonomous systems are being developed and fielded in real time. The gap is precisely what Meyer pointed to—and precisely what the two-year extension does not close.

For now, nearly 100 countries that wanted a binding ban will have to settle for a mandate to keep drafting. Whether the process produces meaningful constraints by 2028, or simply another round of extended talks, will depend on whether the technology-holding states can be moved to accept limits they have so far rejected.

The question of accountability remains open: when a machine selects a target on its own, who answers under international law? The next two years of talks are meant to begin addressing it—but the Geneva outcome shows how slowly that answer is likely to arrive.
