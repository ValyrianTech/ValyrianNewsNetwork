# Mississippi Gulf Plane Crash Kills Family of Six

A single-engine Cessna 210 crashed into the Gulf of Mexico off the coast of Mississippi on Tuesday afternoon, killing all six people aboard — three adults and three boys — the U.S. Coast Guard confirmed. The victims were members of an extended family from Fort Myers, Florida, returning home from a Thanksgiving-season fishing trip.

Divers from the Mississippi Department of Marine Resources recovered the bodies from inside the aircraft's fuselage after the plane went down between Pascagoula, Mississippi, and Horn Island, part of the Gulf Islands National Seashore, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/plane-down-gulf-of-mexico-a1dfde1b2e73ef5862a4786db9644046).

## A family lost at the start of the holidays

Authorities identified the victims through Jackson County Coroner Bruce Lynd. The three adults were the pilot, 50-year-old Brent Stokes; his sister, 43-year-old Arlyn Knox; and her husband, 43-year-old Michael Knox. The three boys were Brent's 16-year-old son, Hunter Stokes, and Arlyn and Michael's sons, 12-year-old Charles Holden Knox and 10-year-old Truett Knox.

In a statement, the Stokes and Knox families said the six died "doing what they loved" — celebrating the start of the holiday season together.

"They died doing what they loved: celebrating the start of a wonderful holiday season with a fishing trip together, before heading home to be with the rest of our family," the family said, according to [WLOX News](https://www.wlox.com/2026/11/25/heartbreaking-outcome-6-killed-plane-crash-near-horn-island-coast-guard-says/).

## How the crash unfolded

The flight departed New Orleans Lakefront Airport en route to Page Field in Fort Myers, Florida, where the family was headed to rejoin relatives for the holiday. The air traffic control regional tower in Mobile, Alabama, lost contact with the aircraft at about 3:45 p.m. local time, the Coast Guard said.

Responders searched for hours and initially found only three life jackets, a life raft and the plane's cabin floating on the water. First responders at the scene reported debris "floating every which direction," local dispatch audio captured.

The Federal Aviation Administration reported that the plane was descending at roughly 10,000 feet per minute when contact was lost — an extraordinary rate that aviation specialists associate with a catastrophic, near-vertical event rather than a gradual loss of control.

Cmdr. Trevor Blount, the Coast Guard's search and rescue mission coordinator for Sector Mobile, called the outcome heartbreaking.

"While this is not the outcome any of us had hoped for, we are grateful for the strong interagency coordination that enabled us to swiftly locate those aboard the aircraft and provide closure to their families," Blount said.

Joe Spraggins, executive director of the Mississippi Department of Marine Resources, said recovering the victims was "a solemn responsibility" carried out "with the utmost care, professionalism, and respect." Multiple agencies assisted, including the Pascagoula Police Department, Harrison County divers, a U.S. Air Force C-130 aircrew and the FBI.

## Investigation and a wider safety question

The National Transportation Safety Board is leading the investigation, with support from the FAA. The inquiry is expected to examine why the aircraft descended so rapidly — weighing possibilities that include a loss of control, an in-flight breakup, engine failure or pilot incapacitation. Weather conditions, the pilot's experience and medical history, and the plane's maintenance records will all be reviewed; radar and flight-tracking data may prove crucial, since a piston single of this vintage typically carries no flight data recorder.

The crash came just weeks after another deadly U.S. accident. On Nov. 3, a Learjet 60 business jet nosedived into the ground in south Florida, killing all nine aboard in a crash caught on bystander video, [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/25/mississippi-plane-crash-gulf-mexico) reported.

Taken together, the two tragedies have renewed attention on air safety, particularly for small private aircraft, which statistically account for the vast majority of U.S. aviation fatalities. Single-engine planes like the Cessna 210 offer little redundancy: a catastrophic structural or engine failure, or a sudden loss of control, leaves pilots with no backup.

## What's next

For the Gulf Coast community, the priority in the days ahead is mourning a family struck down at the outset of the holiday season. The families have asked for privacy while they grieve and arrange services. For federal investigators, the task is slower: determining what caused a routine holiday flight to end in the water between Pascagoula and Horn Island. Whether the crash, following the Learjet accident, prompts renewed scrutiny of small-aircraft safety is a question now hanging over Washington as much as the Gulf Coast.
