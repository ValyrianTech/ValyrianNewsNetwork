# COP31 Ends Without Deal on Political Decisions

The COP31 United Nations climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, closed on 22 November without consensus on an overarching political "cover decision," after negotiations ran nearly 50 hours past their scheduled end—making it the longest climate conference in history. Delegates did adopt a range of decisions on just transition, adaptation, climate finance and carbon markets, but the collapse of the proposed "Antalya Pact" laid bare deep divisions over how—and how quickly—the world should move away from fossil fuels.

The failure, [reported](https://www.news.cn/world/20261123/dd34253be20e45e793580b9e6ab3a67b/c.html) by Xinhua, reflects continued disagreement among nations over the pace and financing of global climate action. After two weeks of marathon talks, the summit managed to advance parts of the multilateral process but could not bridge the gap on its central political statement.

## A Summit of Two Halves

The conference, held under a joint "dual presidency" of Turkey and Australia, was always going to be unusual. The host dispute between the two countries had dragged on for three years before a deal at COP30 in Belém resolved it: the summit would be held in Antalya, with Turkish Environment Minister Murat Kürüm serving as COP president and Australia's climate minister, Chris Bowen, acting as "president of negotiations."

According to [Carbon Brief's comprehensive account](https://www.carbonbrief.org/cop31-key-outcomes-agreed-at-the-un-climate-talks-in-antalya), the presidencies had invested heavily in delivering a cover decision—an overarching political statement intended to respond to the imminent overshoot of the 1.5C warming threshold and to champion an electrification agenda. But the final "Antalya Pact" was rejected in the closing plenary by at least 32 countries, including the EU, the UK, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Belize, Norway and Vanuatu. The 44-member Least Developed Countries bloc, along with the UK, South Korea and Japan, registered that consensus had not been reached.

## The Fossil Fuel Flashpoint

The central objection was that the final draft did not explicitly name fossil fuels—what the EU called "the key driver of climate change"—while including controversial provisions on carbon capture and storage (CCS).

"It fails to meet the moment," Ireland's environment minister, Darragh O'Brien, said on behalf of the EU, as [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/22/cop31-climate-talks-set-to-end-in-deep-division-as-key-deal-rejected). "We will not endorse a cover decision or an Antalya pact that does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change." His intervention drew applause from the hall.

A second major dispute concerned adaptation finance. Developing countries had sought a specific $120 billion target, which was ultimately dropped from the text. The UK told the closing plenary that the outcome was "not commensurate with the urgency" of the crisis, calling it "the lowest common denominator."

## A Split Verdict

Not every delegation opposed the pact. Saudi Arabia, China, Pakistan and Ghana did not object to the deal, though several expressed reservations. Ghana, speaking for the African group, said it could support the decision despite misgivings; Pakistan warned against selective ambition, telling the conference that "climate action is not a menu from which we can select only what suits us."

The contrast between these positions and the official Chinese reaction is instructive. Li Gao, who headed China's delegation as vice minister of ecology and environment, said China was "deeply disappointed" with the outcome, according to Xinhua. He urged the world not to lose faith in multilateralism: "Global climate governance has never been an easy path. Upholding multilateralism and unity and cooperation is the only way out to address climate change, a common challenge for all humanity."

Kürüm, for his part, said the presidency had "done our utmost" but recognized there was no consensus. "It is clear that there are parties in this hall who oppose this text," he said. "Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted."

## What Was Agreed

Despite the failure of the cover decision, the summit did not end in total collapse. Delegates adopted decisions on the just transition work programme, the global goal on adaptation, the climate finance work programme, carbon markets under Article 6, loss and damage, gender, and agriculture and food security. Because consensus was not reached, however, the draft Antalya Pact and its many elements carry no formal standing.

Beyond the negotiating rooms, a growing coalition of nations championed "roadmaps" for transitioning away from fossil fuels, and around 70 to 80 countries signed up to the presidency's "35 by 35" goal—securing 35% of final energy consumption from electricity by 2035.

## A Failure of Leadership?

The disappointment was sharp and widespread. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was "deeply disappointed" that COP31 had ended without consensus, adding: "There may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science."

Ani Dasgupta, president of the World Resources Institute, argued in a [statement](https://www.wri.org/news/statement-hollow-outcome-cop31-failure-political-leadership) that the "hollow outcome at COP31 was a failure of political leadership, not a failure of the Paris Agreement." He warned that "when narrow national interests override collective responsibility to confront the climate crisis, the entire world pays the price."

Others were blunter still. Mohamed Adow of Power Shift Africa called it "the worst COP ever," accusing the EU of hypocrisy for demanding fossil fuel roadmaps from others while expanding fossil fuels at home. Mary Robinson, the former president of Ireland, lamented the lack of urgency and said the co-presidents had not "really been stepping up."

## What's Next

The collapse of the cover decision has revived calls to reform the consensus-based UN climate process. UNFCCC Executive Secretary Simon Stiell acknowledged there was "scope to improve, modernise and reform" the system, while emphasizing that many agenda items had passed with "real progress and concrete results."

Attention now turns to COP32, which will be held in Ethiopia in 2027. The Antalya outcome leaves open fundamental questions: whether the "transition away from fossil fuels" language first agreed in the 2023 UAE Consensus can be revived and given teeth, and whether wealthy nations will commit to the adaptation finance that vulnerable countries say is essential. For now, the science and the politics remain badly out of step—and the world's most consequential negotiating forum has adjourned without a clear answer.
