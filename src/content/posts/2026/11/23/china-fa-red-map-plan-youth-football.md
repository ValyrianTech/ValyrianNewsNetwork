# China's FA Unveils 'Red Map Plan' to Rebuild Youth Football

The Chinese Football Association (CFA) formally launched its "Red Map Plan" (红图计划) at an event in Beijing on Monday, a comprehensive top-level technical framework intended to unify the country's youth development philosophy, playing style, training methods, and talent pathways. The plan, described as the guiding technical document for all national youth football work, is framed by the CFA as a decade-spanning effort to correct systemic problems and rebuild the foundation of Chinese football, as [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34538301) reported.

The release marks the culmination of a multi-year project led by CFA technical director Chris Van Puyvelde, a Belgian coach, with a preface written by CFA president Song Kai. According to the [CFA's official website](https://www.thecfa.cn/qtxw/20261123/38491.html), the plan weaves five cultivation pillars and seven development stages into a single framework, connecting youth football to the senior national team through what it calls a "talent pyramid."

## What the plan sets out to fix

The Red Map Plan directly confronts long-standing weaknesses in Chinese youth football: development philosophies that were never unified, pathways that were poorly connected, a mismatch between how players train and how they compete, and fragmented evaluation standards. To address these, the plan organizes its core text into four sections and eleven chapters covering theory, practice, science and technology support, and promotion, with a closing chapter envisioning the future.

The framework is built around answering three fundamental questions — what kind of players to develop, by what pathway to develop them, and in what manner they should play matches. It is anchored by "four priorities": development over results, learning over winning, quality over quantity, and potential over premature selection. Five cultivation pillars — technical, tactical, physical, psychological, and social — underpin player development, while a seven-stage pathway maps a player's progress from childhood through to the elite level.

The age structure is split into a foundation phase for ages 6 to 11, a development phase for 12 to 16, and an elite competition phase for 17 to 21. In his preface, Song framed the sequence as honing basic skills in childhood, developing technique under duels in the teenage years, and converting potential into ready-to-play ability by the early twenties.

## A shift toward 'technical football'

At the heart of the plan is a philosophical pivot away from a results-first, physically oriented approach toward what the CFA calls "technical football." It explicitly opposes the simple long-ball style and instead calls for players who can control the ball under pressure, escape in tight spaces, pass precisely at high speed, and create chances at decisive moments.

Van Puyvelde, the plan's principal architect, emphasized that China should forge its own identity rather than imitate others. "China does not need to copy other football countries; it needs to form its own football characteristics; it needs to cultivate players who are intelligent, skilled, and responsible — able to read the game, dare to make decisions, and shoulder responsibility," he said, as [Zhibo8](https://news.zhibo8.com/zuqiu/2026-11-23/6b03af22e244cnative.htm) reported.

He outlined the transformations the plan is meant to deliver: from coaching that goes its own way to a unified philosophy and football language; from results-first to development-first; from treating children as "little adults" to age-appropriate, player-centered training; from favoring early-maturing players to patiently supporting late developers; and from a disconnect between training and competition to coordinated education, competition, and talent identification.

## Long-termism and the pyramid

Song positioned the plan as a corrective to the turbulence that has long plagued Chinese football, stressing that sustained progress depends on consistency rather than repeated reinvention. "The height of the national team always depends on the thickness of the youth training pyramid base," he said. "If Chinese football wants to rise steadily and continuously, the most fundamental, reliable, and enduring support is a continuous, echeloned stream of high-quality young talent."

He added that the CFA would maintain strategic resolve: "We are not afraid of a slow start; we are afraid of repeated turmoil. We are not afraid of short-term difficulties; we are afraid of a wavering direction."

The plan leans on China's existing five-tier youth training system to build a nationally connected execution network and proposes a restructured, layered competition system so that matches serve growth, selection, and training. A science and technology section introduces an "observe–benchmark–transform–train–evaluate" video-based learning model, a sports science and player data platform, and lifelong player growth files intended to reduce subjective bias in evaluation. A strategic roadmap covering 2025 to 2035 is divided into three phases: consolidating the foundation, promoting and expanding, and competitive uplift.

## A growing talent pool, and familiar skepticism

The plan arrives against a backdrop of measured progress. At the CFA's member congress in January, Song reported that registered youth players had grown nearly 25 percent over two years to about 109,200, while registered youth coaches — including futsal — reached 126,000, according to the [Japan-China Press](https://www.jpchinapress.com/static/content/WH/2026-01-21/1463576063505470390.html). The National Football Youth Training Center was established in November 2025, networking with five regional centers.

Yet Song has been candid about the scale of the challenge, acknowledging that Chinese football's "comprehensive, systemic backwardness" has not yet been reversed and that the senior national team's competitiveness remains insufficient.

Public reaction on Zhibo8 reflected that tension between hope and hard-earned caution. Many commenters welcomed the plan's scientific framing — "these slogans are far more scientific than before," one wrote — but the dominant theme was a demand for follow-through. "Execution is the key," one reader posted, while another warned of the perennial fear that "each new leader brings a new philosophy," calling instead for continuity across leadership changes. Others raised the cost of youth football for ordinary families and the difficulty of balancing academics with the sport.

## What to watch

The Red Map Plan's ambitions will be tested not by its 254-page text but by whether it survives the leadership changes and short-term pressures that have undone past reform efforts. The coming years will reveal whether the unified philosophy reaches grassroots coaches, whether the restructured competition calendar gives young players meaningful matches, and whether the talent pyramid produces the steady stream of senior players the plan envisions. For a country that has announced ambitious football reforms before, the measure of this one will be patience — and persistence.
