# COP31 Ends Without a Deal as Fossil Fuel Fight Sinks Talks

The COP31 UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, closed on Sunday 22 November without a final agreement, after negotiators failed to bridge an unbridgeable divide over whether to name fossil fuels as the primary driver of global warming. It is the first time UN climate talks have failed to adopt an overarching deal since Copenhagen in 2009, and it caps the longest COP on record, running two days past its scheduled Friday finish.

Nearly 200 countries and territories had gathered under an unusual joint presidency shared by Turkey and Australia. In the end, the conference adopted a range of technical and procedural decisions on just transition, mitigation, adaptation and finance, but could not agree the overarching political declaration — the so-called "Antalya pact" — that was meant to be the summit's centrepiece, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20261123/dd34253be20e45e793580b9e6ab3a67b/c.html).

## The dispute that broke the summit

The fault line was a single phrase. An early draft had called for "accelerated action to phase out fossil fuels" and urged an end to inefficient fossil fuel subsidies. By the final texts, that language had been stripped out. The last drafts instead asked countries to "recall" the 2023 Dubai decision — which did commit nations to "transitioning away from fossil fuels" — "in its entirety," without ever naming fossil fuels directly.

That compromise proved unacceptable to a coalition determined to keep the words in the text and to a bloc of oil producers determined to keep them out. The European Union, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Vanuatu, Norway and Belize were among those that rejected the final draft, while Saudi Arabia and other major oil exporters had lobbied hard to keep any fossil-fuel reference out, according to observers cited by [France 24](https://www.france24.com/en/environment/20261121-latest-cop31-deal-sidesteps-fossil-fuels-amid-growing-divisions).

"It is unconscionable that we would have a climate act that makes no reference to fossil fuel, no reference to transitioning away from fossil fuels," said Ireland's climate minister, Darragh O'Brien, speaking for the EU. "We will not accept negotiating backward in time, and we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuel."

On Friday, the scheduled final day, a coalition led by France and Panama and drawing in countries from Europe, Africa, Latin America and the Pacific had already rejected the draft as too weak, demanding explicit fossil-fuel language and stronger climate finance commitments. Panama's environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, put it bluntly: "We cannot continue to be held hostage by petroleum producers."

## A presidency at the breaking point

COP31 president Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister, announced at the closing plenary that there was no agreement and declined to advance any new text. "There is nothing to do, we did all that we could," he said. "There will be no new text from now on." He later left the stage in the middle of speeches being delivered by country representatives.

Australia's climate and energy minister, Chris Bowen, who served as president of negotiations under the joint presidency, called the outcome "very disappointing" and attributed the breakdown to "unbridgeable red lines" over how a fossil-fuel transition was mentioned. "I would have been happy with transition away from fossil fuels being in the text, but that was not going to get support," he said, adding that the geopolitical environment coming into the summit was "the most complicated and fraught in living memory."

## A world already overshooting 1.5C

The failure lands at a particularly fraught moment. COP31 was the first summit since the UN's environmental body confirmed in October that global temperature rise is set to exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius — the threshold countries pledged at Paris to try not to breach. Current national plans, if fully implemented, put the world on track for roughly 2.5 degrees of warming, down from pre-Paris projections of more than 4 degrees.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres voiced the frustration of many. "I share the very profound frustration of all those who expected governments to rise to the moment the climate crisis demands," he said. "But this setback does not change the facts. There may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science." He added: "Those who profit from the destruction of our planet must never dictate the terms of the fight to save it. Fossil fuels are driving the climate crisis."

The United States sent no representatives for the second consecutive year, following the Trump administration's withdrawal from the 2015 Paris Agreement — a further blow to momentum, as [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/un-cop31-climate-conference-ends-no-agreement-fossil-fuel-debate-standstill/) reported.

## Carbon capture and the finance gap

The final drafts were not empty of substance. They included proposed targets to capture billions of tons of carbon dioxide annually from the atmosphere by 2035 using carbon-removal technology, and a Turkish-backed goal to expand electricity's share of final world energy consumption to 35% by 2035, from around 23% today. More than 90 countries backed the electrification target, and it was not among the deal-breakers.

But critics saw the carbon-removal language as a sop to fossil-fuel interests — a licence to keep pumping oil. Saudi Arabia's delegation, which supported the carbon-removal provision, said at the closing plenary that it was surprised by resistance to a technology it said offered a concrete solution. Developing countries, meanwhile, criticised the drafts for insufficient new climate finance for adaptation: the text called for tripling such finance by 2035 but did not specify a baseline.

## Did the process collapse, or just fracture?

The picture is more nuanced than the word "collapse" suggests. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/23/cop31-left-chris-bowen-stuck-between-petrostates-and-climate-reality-but-the-failure-to-say-the-f-word-is-not-his-alone) noted, the talks did not actually break apart: many less controversial decisions were passed, and the failure of a political declaration does not erase obligations agreed at previous COPs or dismantle the technical and legal frameworks governing multilateral climate action.

Yet the failure has sharpened questions about the COP process itself. Because decisions require unanimous consent, a single nation can block a deal — a structural vulnerability that former US vice-president Al Gore cited in calling for reform, describing it as "an outrage" that "a handful of petrostates have veto power over any mention of fossil fuels." Belgium's climate minister, Jean-Luc Crucke, framed it differently: "It's not the end of multilateralism, but there has to be courage to say no."

Some campaigners pointed at Australia's role, with Denise Cauchi of Climate Action Network Australia arguing it had "failed to broker an agreement" and "caved" to carbon-capture lobbyists. But Pacific ministers, for whom the breach of 1.5C is existential, pushed back. Fiji's climate change minister, Lynda Tabuya, addressed Bowen directly in the final plenary: "The failure here is not yours to carry. It is the responsibility of all parties."

## What comes next

Attention now turns to whether the loss of a political declaration will slow the energy transition on the ground — and to the alternatives emerging outside the formal UN process. Some nations are pursuing voluntary pacts, including the Santa Marta process coalition working to drive a transition away from coal, oil and gas, while others are setting up their own agreements.

China's delegation head, Li Gao, expressed deep disappointment but urged countries not to lose faith in multilateralism, saying mutual recrimination does not build trust and that solidarity and cooperation remain the only path to addressing a challenge shared by all humanity.

The next summit, COP32, is scheduled for 2027 in Ethiopia — where the same question, whether the world's climate architecture can name the fuel at the heart of the crisis, will return to the table.
