# Van Gogh's Blooming Chestnut Trees Sells for Record €173 Million

Vincent van Gogh's *Blooming Chestnut Trees* sold at Sotheby's in New York on the evening of Wednesday, 18 November 2026 for $190.4 million — approximately €173 million including buyer's premium — setting a new auction record for the artist, [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/19/schilderij-van-vincent-van-gogh-bloeiende-kastanjebomen-geve/) reported. The result, the fourth-highest price ever paid for an artwork at auction, came only a day after the sale appeared on the brink of collapse over competing claims about the painting's Nazi-era provenance.

## A record for the artist

The hammer came down at $165 million, with fees lifting the final figure to $190.4 million. According to [Artnet News](https://news.artnet.com/market/a-van-gogh-painting-sells-record-190-4m-million-in-new-york-2838742), the entire affair lasted roughly three minutes: auctioneer Oliver Barker opened bidding at $135 million, the price climbed in $5 million increments, then stalled at $165 million before the gavel fell. The underbidder was a collector on the phone with Julian Dawes, Christie's head of Impressionist and modern art for the Americas.

The buyer was New York art dealer and gallerist Bill Acquavella, of Acquavella Galleries, who confirmed his purchase to Artnet News but did not disclose whether he was bidding on behalf of a client. His past clients have included collectors Steve Wynn and Steven A. Cohen.

The sale smashed Van Gogh's previous auction record: $117.2 million, paid in 2022 at Christie's New York for the 1888 *Verger avec cyprès* from Paul Allen's collection. VRT places that earlier benchmark at roughly €107 million — the same sale, expressed in euros.

"It was the top lot of a dedicated, 12-lot sale, 'Legendary: The Blaquier-Arrieta Collection,'" Artnet reported, adding that Sotheby's guaranteed the entire group and that the Van Gogh was backed by an irrevocable bid, ensuring it would sell regardless of the outcome. The sale was the top lot of a $2 billion auction season.

## The painting and its history

*Blooming Chestnut Trees* — *Châtaigniers en fleurs* — is dated to 1890, painted in Auvers-sur-Oise, the small village near Paris where Van Gogh moved from the South of France. Artnet dates its creation to around 24 May 1890, roughly two months before the artist died by suicide on 29 July of the same year.

The landscape shows a cluster of large trees at the centre of the village. In the foreground, three women in colourful clothing are seen from behind, walking in the shade of the trees; in the opposite direction, a man in blue work clothes descends the street.

Sotheby's said the work reflected the culmination of Van Gogh's brief, fast-moving career. "Van Gogh's time in Auvers represented the culmination of a brief and fast-paced career of only 10 years," the auction house wrote in its essay on the painting. "Painted quickly with magnificent brushwork and an exuberant, expressive range of colors, it reflected past achievements and set out a path for new work to come with fresh motifs and hues of the North." According to Sotheby's, the artist created 74 paintings and more than 50 drawings during his final months in Auvers.

The painting had not been publicly exhibited in 60 years. It had been held for roughly half a century by the Blaquier-Arrieta family, part of a celebrated Impressionist collection amassed by Nelly Arrieta de Blaquier and her husband Carlos Pedro Blaquier, whose fortune derived from the Argentine sugar-manufacturing giant Ledesma. The couple began collecting in the 1960s and acquired the Van Gogh from Wildenstein & Co. in 1975.

## Nearly derailed by a provenance dispute

The sale was very nearly called off. In the days before the auction, heirs of the German Jewish publisher Samuel Fischer came forward to argue that the painting had been sold under duress tied to Nazi persecution via an Amsterdam gallery in 1936.

Fischer, a celebrated German publisher, had acquired the painting in 1909. After his death, the work passed within his family in 1934. [NOS](https://nos.nl/artikel/2639532-veiling-van-gogh-schilderij-op-losse-schroeven-werk-mogelijk-onder-druk-nazi-s-verkocht), citing The New York Times, reported that the heirs' lawyer wrote to Sotheby's that the painting was sold in Amsterdam in 1936 — before the Second World War — as the Fischer family's publishing business suffered financial losses under pressure from the Nazi regime, which had held power in Germany since 1933. The heirs argued that the family had been forced to sell under the economic and political conditions the Nazis created, and demanded that the work be withdrawn from auction.

Sotheby's said it had investigated the matter and concluded that the work had not been sold under such duress. "Sotheby's takes provenance and restitution issues very seriously and has conducted extensive research into the ownership history of *Châtaigniers en fleurs*," the auction house said. "We are aware of the concerns recently raised by representatives of Fischer's heirs and have consulted with them about the history of the painting."

On the eve of the auction, on Tuesday 17 November, Sotheby's announced that an agreement had been reached between the painting's current owners — the Blaquier-Arrieta family, who had held the work since 1975 — and all legal claimants of Samuel Fischer, clearing the way for the sale to proceed. The details of the agreement were not disclosed.

## What the sale tells us

The record price is a reminder that the very top of the art market remains governed by scarcity and trophy value as much as by aesthetics. A late Van Gogh — painted in the artist's final, feverish months and unseen publicly for six decades — is about as scarce as blue-chip art gets, and Sotheby's structured the auction to guarantee a result regardless of the room.

The provenance dispute is equally telling. In recent years, restitution claims for artworks that Jewish collectors were forced to relinquish during the Nazi period have returned to the fore, and many such cases are now settled out of court through mutual agreements rather than lengthy litigation. That pattern — private resolution followed by a public sale, with the terms kept confidential — has become a familiar feature of the high-end market, even as it leaves the historical record only partly illuminated.

That a masterpiece can move from the brink of withdrawal to a record price in the space of 24 hours is a measure of how quickly the commercial machinery of the art world can absorb, and then neutralise, a historical controversy. Whether that is a satisfactory way to reckon with the past is a question the price tag does not answer.

## What's next

With the sale complete, attention turns to the painting's next chapter — and to whether the buyer or any future owner will lend it for public exhibition, ending a six-decade absence from view. The record also resets the benchmark against which other Van Goghs will now be valued, and adds fresh weight to the broader debate over how restitution claims and market incentives interact at the very top of the auction world.
