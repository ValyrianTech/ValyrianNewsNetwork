# No Ban on Killer Robots, Just Two More Years of Talks

After five days of negotiations in Geneva, the more than 120 member states of a key UN weapons convention ended their talks on Saturday without agreeing to ban lethal autonomous weapons systems — the machines critics call "killer robots" — settling instead on two more years of consultations. The outcome leaves the central question of how to control weapons that can select and kill targets without human input unresolved, even as the underlying technology advances.

The agreement commits states to "elaborate" a possible accord on such systems by 2028. It stops well short of the legally binding rules sought by UN Secretary-General António Guterres, many governments and human-rights advocates, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/un-drones-robots-un-lethal-ai-weapons-ebe057d2a47cde53100970c8f648e7fa), which reported that the world body's own disarmament chief described the result as a "glass half full or half empty" scenario.

## A Consensus Rule and a Diluted Mandate

The talks unfolded inside the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), a framework ratified by 128 countries that for decades has addressed the human cost of landmines, incendiary munitions and explosive remnants of war. Because the CCW makes decisions by consensus, any single country can thwart an agreement — and any deal tends to reflect the lowest common denominator.

That structure proved decisive. Nearly 100 countries had backed negotiations toward a binding treaty, and seven states — Austria, Belgium, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand, Panama and Brazil — formally proposed launching that process. But major military powers, chiefly Russia and the United States (with Israel named by some outlets), pressed for looser language, and the final text conspicuously avoids the word "negotiate."

According to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/21/nations-agree-to-draft-rules-for-killer-robots-at-arms-control-summit), the compromise asks states to "elaborate" on possible rules rather than commit to drafting a binding instrument. [JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/11/un-arms-conference-falls-short-of-treaty-mandate-on-autonomous-weapons-due-to-us-and-russia-opposition/) reported that the conference fell short of a treaty mandate chiefly because of opposition from the United States and Russia.

## Voices of Frustration

The gap between ambition and outcome drew sharp criticism. "Technology is moving faster than diplomacy. Diplomats are very slow," said Brazilian Ambassador Frederico Meyer, whose country was among those pushing for stricter rules.

Activists were blunter. Nicole van Rooijen, executive director of the Stop Killer Robots campaign, warned that "delaying the creation of a legally binding instrument puts civilians and humanity at risk," noting that autonomous weapons are already being used in combat, including systems that apply artificial intelligence to choose targets. Max Tegmark, an MIT machine-learning specialist who founded the Future of Life Institute, said states had agreed it was sufficient to "just talk about the issue, for the 14th year, while these weapons enable atrocities."

The International Committee of the Red Cross stressed the unpredictability of such systems compared with conventional weapons. "For years, the ICRC and many others have urged states to ensure that humans retain control over the use of force and that life-and-death decisions are never handed over to machines," it said in a statement.

Guterres, who leaves office at the end of the year, had made the issue a priority. Opening the conference on 16 November, he urged delegates to "seize this moment" and "take action to prevent the use and spread of these merciless weapons," as [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/11/1168810) reported. His goal of launching formal negotiations in 2026 was not met — a missed deadline that removes a prominent advocate from the fray just as the process enters its most delicate phase.

## What the Delay Means

Advocates say armed drones capable of independently striking targets have already appeared in select conflicts, though not yet at scale. Each year without rules narrows the window to shape norms before autonomous weapons become commonplace and harder to regulate.

The two-year window will now focus largely on determining which autonomous weapons can be banned outright under international humanitarian law and which may remain permitted but regulated. UN High Representative for Disarmament Affairs Izumi Nakamitsu, while calling the outcome a "significant achievement," urged countries to begin work almost immediately, "as early as Monday," to build the "strongest possible instrument."

Conference chair Julien Thöni, a Swiss diplomat, framed the result as proof that "even in today's deeply divided world, multilateral diplomacy can still deliver concrete results." Critics counter that the result is less a result than a postponement.

## What to Watch

The coming consultations will test whether the majority that favors binding rules can convert its numbers into leverage against a handful of holdouts. Several dynamics bear watching: whether the push moves to other forums where consensus is not required, how fast autonomous systems are fielded in active conflicts, and whether the next US administration and other powers soften their resistance as public pressure grows.

For now, the world's answer to weapons that can decide whom to kill is not a prohibition but a promise to keep talking — until 2028, at the earliest.
