# Will Your Thanksgiving Meal Cost More This Year? It Depends

As Americans prepare for Thanksgiving 2026, the question hanging over the grocery aisle is deceptively simple: will the holiday feast cost more than last year? Two of the most closely watched food-price trackers reach opposite conclusions—and the gap between them reveals as much about how households shop as it does about the economy.

Grocery costs have risen nearly 22% over the past five years, [according to federal data](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CUSR0000SAF11). Wars in Ukraine and Iran, the Trump administration's tariffs and severe weather across the globe have all pushed food prices higher, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/23/nx-s1-6044207/thanksgiving-meal-costs).

## Two Trackers, Two Answers

The American Farm Bureau Federation estimates the cost of a classic Thanksgiving dinner is up more than 6% compared with last year, with staples like cranberries and dinner rolls driving the increase—the first annual rise since 2022.

The [Wells Fargo Agri-Food Institute](https://www.wellsfargo.com/com/insights/agri-food-intelligence/thanksgiving-food-report/) counters that the average holiday food bill will come in slightly cheaper than a year ago. Its analysis puts food for 10 at $78.93, down 0.4% from 2025, with six of the ten traditional items costing less—including turkey, down 0.5%, and cranberries, down 6.6%.

The divergence is largely methodological. The Farm Bureau prices a fixed basket of holiday staples without assuming sales, while Wells Fargo emphasizes that promotions, store brands and disciplined shopping can substantially lower real-world bills.

"But you have to be very disciplined," said Wells Fargo chief agricultural economist Michael Swanson. "You get the turkey at a great price, but then you might throw a bunch of stuff in the cart you didn't look at that carefully and find out you spent more money than you intended to."

## The Pressure Is in the Glass, Not on the Plate

Wells Fargo found that the biggest source of upward price pressure isn't the food itself. Wine, beer and sparkling water add $48.61 to the celebration—38% of the expanded basket—even as shoppers pull back on alcohol. Sparkling water prices alone jumped 6.4%, the largest increase in the holiday basket.

That nuance matters for households budgeting the whole meal rather than just the turkey. A cheaper plate can still yield a higher checkout total once drinks and impulse purchases are added.

## Shoppers Are Rethinking the Holiday

Financial anxiety is reshaping how Americans celebrate. In a recent LendingTree survey, 53% of respondents said inflation was affecting their holiday plans or how much they expected to spend on the meal.

"They're doing everything from changing their food choices to couponing more and even cutting back on guests," said Matt Schulz, LendingTree's chief consumer finance analyst.

The same survey found a notable drop in hosting: 51% of people expect to have company over this year, down from 59% last year.

"Nobody wants to cut back on something as meaningful as a gathering of family and friends on Thanksgiving because it costs too much," Schulz said, "but that's what a lot of people are facing right now."

## Retailers Lean Into Deep Discounts

Major grocers are competing aggressively for holiday shoppers, using steeply discounted meal bundles—sometimes at a loss—as traffic drivers. Walmart, Aldi, Amazon and others are offering complete holiday meals for families at low, prominently advertised price points, [Axios reported](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/19/walmart-costco-thanksgiving-deals-dinner-kroger-aldi).

That promotional environment supports the "cheaper with discipline" thesis. But it also means headline prices and actual savings depend heavily on where—and how carefully—a household shops.

## What to Watch

The competing estimates may frustrate anyone looking for a single number, but they capture a genuine divide in American kitchens: the list price of Thanksgiving versus what a careful shopper actually pays.

For many families, the decision is less about whether to celebrate than how. As Philadelphia retiree Andre Andrews told NPR, prices are "too high, so much higher"—yet he still plans to smoke a turkey and a roast for the holiday. "I can tell you this: I know it was more than it was last year," he said.

That sentiment—sticker shock met with determined tradition—may be the truest measure of Thanksgiving 2026. The headline number may be lower or higher, but the holiday, most Americans seem to agree, is worth the cost.
