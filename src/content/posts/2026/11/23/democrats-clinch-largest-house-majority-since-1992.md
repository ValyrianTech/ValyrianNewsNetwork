# Democrats Clinch Largest House Majority Since 1992

Democrats have secured at least 258 seats in the U.S. House of Representatives, the party's largest general-election majority in more than three decades, after Democrat Chuck Hubbard defeated Republican Rep. Virginia Foxx in North Carolina's 5th Congressional District. The milestone, reached Sunday and reported by [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/democrats-house-majority-election-2026-congress-721096a4d9ba2cd68683692361798cdb), caps a sweeping midterm performance that also delivered Democrats a 56-seat Senate majority — and hands the party control of both chambers of Congress alongside a Republican president.

Democrats have gained 44 House seats from where they stood on Election Day, four more than the 40 they picked up in Trump's first midterm in 2018. Republicans hold at least 173 seats in the minority, one of their lowest tallies in decades. Four House races remain uncalled: one each in Minnesota and Tennessee, and two in Louisiana heading to a December runoff.

## The clinching race

Hubbard's victory came after Foxx requested a recount, which state law permits when the margin is under 1%. He led by 330 votes out of roughly 287,000 cast, with 99% counted, according to [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c6vg29g7dlyno). Foxx, who has represented the district since 2005, conceded and offered a graceful statement, calling the peaceful transition of office "one of the true hallmarks of our system of government."

"I think the American people are just sick of what's going on and they wanted a change. They voted for change," Hubbard told the BBC after his victory, pledging to work alongside fellow lawmakers to hold President Donald Trump accountable. Hubbard, like many Democrats running this cycle, focused on the rising cost of living amid the war with Iran — a top issue for voters.

## A rebuke, and a mandate

The headline total places the Democrats' majority above any the party won in elections since Bill Clinton's first victory in 1992 (258 seats) and Barack Obama's in 2008 (257), though it remains below the Franklin D. Roosevelt-era majorities that exceeded 300. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries of New York celebrated the figure, writing on social media that Democrats had "just secured the largest majority any party in Congress has held this century," [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/22/house-democrats-historic-sweep) reported.

The numbers underscore the scale of Democratic wins, which spanned from the strongest Republican states to the Midwest and East Coast, as voters angry over high prices and the Iran war — and concerned about corruption — made clear their discontent with Trump. Democrats also flipped seats in South Carolina, Texas, Kansas, Alaska and Iowa.

"This is a historic moment for our country. This is one of the largest classes we have seen," said progressive leader Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez of New York at the Capitol, calling the big numbers "a mandate for change."

## Opportunities — and pressure

The wide margin gives the new leadership far more room to maneuver than Republicans had. Outgoing Speaker Mike Johnson struggled to keep the House functioning for two years with a narrow single-digit majority, meaning a few GOP lawmakers could derail the agenda. Jeffries, who is poised to make history as the first Black speaker of the House if elected on Jan. 3, 2027, will be able to lose dozens of Democrats on any vote and still reach the 218 needed for passage.

That advantage also creates intense pressure to deliver. Democrats are preparing a 100-day agenda focused on affordability, including swift votes to reverse Republican Medicaid cuts and restore Affordable Care Act tax credits that Republicans allowed to expire. They have also vowed to marshal subpoena powers and launch a flurry of oversight investigations into Trump, his family's finances and the administration — a standard congressional check on presidential power that the White House has warned could amount to "blatant criminality." Jeffries has not ruled out pursuing a third impeachment of Trump.

"Like, hell yeah, we should feel pressure," said Democratic Rep. Jason Crow of Colorado, a former Army Ranger. "There are people dying in this country and people losing their healthcare, losing their livelihoods, losing their farms, being terrorized from ICE. If you don't feel a sense of urgency and pressure in this job, and a desperation to deliver for folks, then you don't belong under this Capitol dome."

## Balancing affordability and accountability

Within the caucus, some are urging discipline in how Democrats wield their new power. Rep. Tom Suozzi, a centrist New York Democrat and accountant, said the party should focus 75% of its time on affordability issues and 25% on accountability — tying every investigation back to voters' pocketbooks.

"Everything should be tied back to the people — and how it's affecting the people," Suozzi said.

Others emphasized the breadth of the new caucus. "We're not a monolith — people come to the caucus with different ideas — it's OK," said Rep. Troy Carter of Louisiana, who is expected to lead the Congressional Black Caucus.

On the Republican side, Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio was elected by colleagues as minority leader after Johnson declined to seek the post. "Over the next two years, House Republicans will fight for American families," Jordan said. Party high-water marks remain far off: House Republicans won 247 seats in 2014 and 230 in 1994, with a 1920 peak of 301.

## What's next

The new Congress convenes on Jan. 3, 2027, when Jeffries is expected to be elected Speaker. Even with both chambers in hand, Democrats face limits: Trump retains veto power, and he has threatened to use it and warned he does not want to spend two years "vetoing everything." The remaining Louisiana races will be settled in December runoffs.

For now, the 258-seat tally marks a striking reversal of fortune — and a test of whether Democrats can convert an emphatic electoral mandate into governing results before the pressure Crow described becomes the defining story of their majority.
