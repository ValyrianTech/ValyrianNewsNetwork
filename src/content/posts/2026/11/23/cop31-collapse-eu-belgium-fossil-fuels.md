# COP31 collapses as EU rejects deal without fossil fuels

The COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, ended without an overarching agreement on Sunday, after the European Union, Belgium and a broad coalition of nations rejected a final draft that made no mention of fossil fuels. It was only the third time in the history of the UN climate process that a conference closed without a deal, and it came after negotiations ran roughly two days into overtime, making it the longest COP ever held.

The breaking point was a final text that omitted any explicit commitment to phasing out oil, gas and coal. The EU, alongside the UK, Panama and vulnerable Pacific island states such as Vanuatu and the Marshall Islands, formally objected, according to [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/cop31-collapse-countries-reject-deal-without-mention-of-transition-away-from-fossil-fuels-297269). Belgium, which had pushed hard for the phase-out language earlier in the summit, joined the objection.

## A fight foretold

The collapse did not come without warning. On 20 November, as the scheduled final day began, Belgium and around twenty like-minded countries raised the alarm that the phase-out commitment had disappeared from the Turkish presidency's draft, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/cop31-a-antalya-la-belgique-et-une-vingtaine-de-pays-haussent-le-ton-pour-reinscrire-la-sortie-du-petrole-et-du-gaz-dans-le-projet-d-accord-11818457). Belgium's federal climate minister, Jean-Luc Crucke, struck a defiant tone as the negotiations entered their final stretch.

"The final text is not yet approved. It changes hour by hour, and it remains a demand of Europe, of all the countries here. It goes well beyond Europe. So we will fight to the end," Crucke said at the time.

That push echoes a commitment first made at COP28 in Dubai in 2023, when countries agreed for the first time to "transition away from fossil fuels." Its erosion in later texts has become a recurring flashpoint, and COP31 forced the question into the open. An initial draft had called for "accelerated action to phase out fossil fuels," but that language was removed in subsequent versions, [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/un-cop31-climate-conference-ends-no-agreement-fossil-fuel-debate-standstill/) reported.

## The EU draws a red line

By the final plenary on Sunday, the EU had made clear it would not sign a text that ignored the root cause of climate change. Speaking for the EU presidency, Ireland's climate minister Darragh O'Brien delivered the bloc's rejection in unequivocal terms.

"We will not endorse a cover decision or an Antalya pact that does not even mention fossil fuel, which is the key driver of climate change," O'Brien said. "And we will not accept negotiating backward in time, and we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuel."

Belgium's Crucke, who had spent days pressing for stronger language, announced he was leaving Antalya as the deal slipped away, said in [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/eu-verwerpt-akkoord-dat-voorligt-we-kunnen-geen-beslissing-ste~1794235749946/) that the outcome was a step backward. "We had a difficult year with heat and floods. We cannot accept the comedy Turkey is presenting us, with more fossil fuels and no language about science," he said. "The game is over, as far as we are concerned."

VRT later issued a correction: Crucke departed, but the Belgian negotiating team remained on the ground. Belgium holds no direct vote in the plenary, where only the EU speaks, but it carries weight in shaping the European position.

## A summit that ran off the rails

COP31 was billed by its co-presidents, Turkey and Australia, as an "implementation COP," but it delivered on few of its own goals. Beyond fossil fuels, the summit stumbled on climate finance: developing countries and Saudi Arabia pressed for US$120 billion a year by 2035 in adaptation finance, a figure that was ultimately dropped from the text, as The Conversation reported. Adaptation needs in the Global South alone are estimated at around US$500 billion a year.

The summit's flagship electrification target, known as "35x35" — electricity supplying 35% of global final energy consumption by 2035 — was downgraded to merely one voluntary option among several, supported as a pledge by 90 countries. Carbon capture and storage was inserted into the text at the urging of oil producers, a provision the EU and Panama opposed as a means of prolonging fossil fuel use.

COP31 President Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister, confirmed that no consensus could be found and that the decisions had not been adopted. "We did everything we could. In the end, we did not impose this text on anyone," Kurum said. "There are parties in the hall that oppose this text, and we see that there is no agreement on it. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted." Under COP rules, a single nation's objection can derail a deal.

## Reaction: frustration and resolve

UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was "deeply disappointed" by the outcome, while insisting the science remained unambiguous. "This setback does not change the facts. There may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science," he said, warning that fossil-fuel interests were using tobacco-industry tactics to delay action. "We cannot solve the fire if we keep pouring oil on it."

Panama's environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, gave voice to the frustration of the coalition that held firm. "We cannot continue to be held hostage by petroleum producers," he said. "The atmosphere does not negotiate, science does not negotiate either. The planet cannot afford to wait any longer."

Environmental groups and Belgian politicians laid the blame squarely at the door of oil-producing nations. Greenpeace's delegation head, Jasper Inventor, said the summit "should have put an end to fossil fuels, instead of promoting them and extending their lifespan," adding that the absence of a deal was "very painful for the people on the front line of the climate crisis." Groen chair Aimen Horch said it was right that European negotiators "pulled the plug in Antalya," while Flemish climate minister Melissa Depraetere called the omission of fossil fuels from the text "a step backwards."

For its part, the fossil-fuel sector federation Energia offered a muted verdict. "No one has won," said Jean-Benoît Schrans, who criticized the EU for refusing to recognize carbon capture technology.

## What the failure means

The failure is the first COP to end without a deal since Copenhagen in 2009, and only the third ever, after The Hague in 2000. It also marks the first outing of the summit's unusual co-presidency, in which Turkey hosted and presided formally while Australia led the negotiations — an arrangement that drew scepticism before the summit and is now likely to face scrutiny. Australia's climate minister and co-president, Chris Bowen, said talks broke down over "unbridgeable red lines" in what he called "the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory," CBS News reported.

The backdrop was unforgiving. The United States sent no representatives for the second consecutive year, following the Trump administration's withdrawal from the Paris Agreement, leaving the EU and China as the most influential players. China accepted the final text while noting room for improvement; Russia and Saudi Arabia led the resistance to fossil-fuel language.

Yet the collapse may also accelerate a shift already under way. Belgium announced it would join a coalition developing a roadmap to phase out fossil fuels, part of a growing movement of "coalitions of the willing" that seek to advance the transition outside the consensus-bound COP structure — the same impulse behind the Santa Marta process. Crucke said the COP itself must continue, but its workings cannot.

"A process where a few countries can do everything to block an agreement — that cannot continue," he said. "We have signed a few alliances with countries that do want a phase-out of fossil fuels. It will not happen in a single day, but we have started."

As negotiators left Antalya, the question hanging over the process was whether a system built to act on the science can still summon the will to do so. For now, the answer from Antalya was no — and the next major test, COP32, awaits in Addis Ababa next year.
