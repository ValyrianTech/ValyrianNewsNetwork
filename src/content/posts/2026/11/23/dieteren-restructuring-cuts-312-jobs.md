# D'Ieteren Confirms Restructuring, Cuts 312 Jobs

D'Ieteren Automotive, the Belgian importer and distributor of Volkswagen Group vehicles, confirmed on Monday that its "Transformation Plan 2026-2030" will eliminate 312 jobs — 32 fewer than the 344 positions originally announced in September. According to [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/auto-importeur-dieteren-bevestigt-herstructurering-en-schrapt-312-banen~aaa1c599/), the revision follows the legal information and consultation phase with the trade unions.

The cuts, which affect roughly 10.6% of the company's 2,945 full-time-equivalent workforce, mark one of the most significant restructurings at a company that has operated continuously in Belgium since 1805.

## Context: A Two-Century-Old Company Under Pressure

D'Ieteren is something of a Belgian institution. Founded by Jean-Joseph D'Ieteren as a coachbuilder in 1805, the group has grown into a multi-billion-euro business that imports and distributes all Volkswagen Group brands in Belgium — including Volkswagen, Audi, SEAT and Škoda — alongside several emerging and exclusive marques. It also owns the car-sharing platform Poppy and the bicycle chain Lucien.

But the market on which the company built its fortune is changing. When D'Ieteren first announced the plan in September, it said up to 344 positions could be lost, with the cuts falling mainly on white-collar staff (around 236) alongside about 100 blue-collar workers. The news shocked unions. "We knew something was brewing, because the market is turbulent, but we were shocked by the scale of the restructuring," Jean-Paul Sellekaerts of the ABVV/FGTB union said at the time, as [reported](https://www.hln.be/brussel/344-banen-op-de-helling-bij-volkswagen-importeur-dieteren-automotive-vakbond-geschrokken-van-de-omvang~a94e9184/) by HLN.

## A Rocky Consultation

The period between September and November was not smooth. After a first meeting on 11 September, union representatives complained that management's answers were insufficient and that not all employers were present. Corinne Martin of the Christian union CNE said the unions felt they were not being taken seriously, [HLN reported](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/vakbond-ontevreden-over-antwoorden-directie-dieteren-automotive-we-hebben-de-indruk-dat-het-licht-opgenomen-wordt~a508823c0/).

That friction appears to have produced at least a partial result: the reduction from 344 to 312 positions suggests the Wet-Renault procedure — Belgium's legal framework for collective dismissals — had some effect on the final figure. Discussions over accompanying social measures, including severance and redeployment support, are still ongoing.

"Management wants to continue a constructive dialogue in order to reach an agreement as soon as possible and to limit the uncertainty for the employees concerned as much as possible," the company said.

## The Structural Argument

D'Ieteren is careful to frame the cuts as a response to permanent shifts rather than a temporary downturn. The company points to the decarbonisation of the vehicle fleet, the impact of Belgium's mandatory mobility budget from 2027, and AI-accelerated digitalisation. It also cites intensifying global competition and a structural market decline that has been under way since the COVID-19 pandemic.

As CEO Denis Gorteman put it in September: "The shift in the car market is happening faster than anticipated." The company says some activities, functions and structures "no longer match the expectations of the market."

The plan includes closing certain garages and branches, merging functions and reorganising teams, with a particular impact on the bodywork division including the Zaventem site. The headquarters in Elsene (Brussels) is also affected.

## From Cars to Mobility

At the heart of the plan is a strategic pivot. D'Ieteren is shifting its business model from classic new-car sales toward mobility services and long-term customer relationships, investing in digitalisation and alternative transport — including Poppy and Lucien.

The bet is that the future of the automotive business lies in services rather than vehicle volume. That has implications well beyond the 312 employees directly affected: it signals a different skill profile for the company's remaining workforce, and a different relationship with customers who may no longer simply buy a car every few years.

The policy backdrop matters here. Belgium's mobility budget, set to become mandatory from 2027, allows employees to trade a company car for a mobility allowance — a direct hit to a core part of D'Ieteren's traditional revenue. The restructuring therefore connects corporate strategy to national fiscal and mobility policy in ways that will play out over years, not months.

## A Sector Under Strain

The D'Ieteren cuts are not an isolated event. The broader Volkswagen Group has been weighing its own massive restructuring, with reports of up to 120,000 jobs threatened at group level, as [HLN has reported](https://www.hln.be/buitenland/volkswagen-overweegt-grootste-herstructurering-ooit-tot-120-000-banen-bedreigd~abf589c3/). Across Europe, automakers are under pressure from the electric-vehicle transition, Chinese competition and softer consumer demand — a squeeze that has followed billions in EV investment that consumer demand has not matched.

The announcement also lands on a sensitive day in Belgium: a national day of action, with thousands protesting the policies of the De Wever government amid a wider political and budget crisis. The D'Ieteren cuts add another data point to a climate of labour unrest and economic anxiety.

## What's Next

The immediate focus is the conclusion of negotiations over accompanying measures for affected workers. Three questions will shape what follows: whether the 312 figure holds or is revised again; how deeply branch and garage closures affect local employment; and whether the pain spreads across Belgium's wider auto retail and garage sector.

For a company that has survived more than two centuries by adapting, the transformation plan is a wager that it can do so again. The 312 jobs are the price of that wager — and a reminder that even Belgium's most storied businesses are not immune to the forces remaking the car industry.
