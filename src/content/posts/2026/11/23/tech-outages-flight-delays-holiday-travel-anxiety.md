# Tech Outages, Flight Delays Feed Holiday Travel Anxiety

A rare nationwide aviation ground stop, a string of airline and tech outages, and the highest airfares in a decade are converging just as millions of Americans prepare to fly for Thanksgiving, feeding a national mood of unease about the systems that keep daily life moving. The disruptions have raised fresh questions about the resilience of the country's aging air-traffic infrastructure and the digital networks that underpin modern travel.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/americans-air-travel-disruptions-holiday-43fd1d097a7ebcb8c1c583952bb9efef), the busy holiday week "effectively begins Friday," and this year it arrives with a new ingredient: concerns about the possibility of disruption in the wake of a ground stop that was the longest since the September 11, 2001, attacks — and a separate airline outage a few days later.

## A Ground Stop Unlike Any in 25 Years

The trouble began on Friday, November 13, when a communications failure at air-traffic control centers forced the Federal Aviation Administration to halt departures nationwide for roughly four hours and 25 minutes. Flights already in the air were allowed to continue, and some departures were cleared case by case. The effects rippled outward: European departures bound for the US were briefly suspended at the FAA's request, Canada's air-traffic agency held arrivals at Toronto and Montreal, and Mexico's aviation authority held US-bound aircraft.

The net impact was severe. Nearly 8,000 flights were canceled and more than 10,000 delayed across the country, with thousands more cancellations spilling into the following days as airlines worked to reposition aircraft and crews. Officials attributed the failure to newly installed routers that flooded an FAA communications network with connection attempts, overloading older equipment across a majority of air-route traffic control centers. Authorities said there was no indication of a cyberattack or malicious activity.

The episode thrust the state of US air-traffic infrastructure into the political spotlight. The copper-wire system it relies on dates to the 1960s, and a costly modernization effort has been underway for about 25 years. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, appearing on NBC's *Meet the Press*, declined to apologize, saying he could not "apologize for what was passed off to us" and could not assign blame to a single party "because it's all of them." Officials have asked Congress for $50 billion to overhaul air-traffic control.

## When Everything Seems to Break at Once

The ground stop was only one entry in a season of outages. Days earlier, a global Microsoft disruption had scrambled email and office software for millions worldwide. The following Monday, United Airlines reported an IT outage affecting departing flights, and JPMorgan Chase grappled with transaction slowdowns that it said were not the result of a cyberattack. Just before the holiday, Delta Air Lines suffered a failure in its automated aircraft-weight-calculation system that caused more than 1,000 delays on Sunday, with about 7% of its flights still lagging on Monday morning, according to flight-tracking data.

For many travelers, the cumulative effect has been a nagging sense that the nation's digital foundations are more fragile than they once seemed — a feeling amplified by unrelated scares and a steady stream of online speculation.

"For a lot of people, especially as they're paying attention and because it's in the news where they're tying together all of the IT-based infrastructure challenges we're having ... I think more and more people are now getting anxious," said Tracey Evers, an energy executive from Raleigh, North Carolina, who was weighing whether to fly to the Caribbean to see her mother.

Maxine Gustafson, a physician assistant from Minneapolis, put the worry more bluntly: "It makes you think, like, is this a cyberattack? We don't have control over a lot of this stuff." Chuck Amasio, a traveler in Newark, looked at the bigger picture: "We talk about how wars aren't done with bombs anymore. They shut down airspace or a power grid. They can cripple an economy."

## Costlier Flights, Yet Still Flying

Anxiety has not translated into empty planes. The AAA travel group forecast that 81.5 million people would travel at least 50 miles over Thanksgiving — a slight 0.1% decline from last year that nonetheless ends three consecutive years of record post-pandemic travel. Roughly 6 million people are expected to fly during the November 24–30 stretch. AAA attributed the marginal dip largely to higher costs.

Those costs loom large. Roundtrip domestic Thanksgiving airfares were up about 39% from a year earlier, with Christmas fares up roughly 40% — the highest in a decade, according to fare tracker Hopper. AAA pegged the average Thanksgiving roundtrip booked through its service at about $800, some 17% more than last year. Jet fuel prices, which surged during the US war with Iran, are a key driver, and regular gasoline averaged $3.82 a gallon in the lead-up to the holiday, potentially the most expensive Thanksgiving fuel on record.

"After three years of record highs, the number of travelers is slightly lower partly because of higher travel costs this year. But travelers won't notice much of a difference," said Stacey Barber, vice president of AAA Travel. Company spokesperson Aixa Diaz added that "travelers are resilient, and from what AAA has seen in the past, it takes a lot to keep holiday travelers home."

## A Commodity Taken for Granted

Analysts say the persistence of air travel despite the chaos reveals something deeper about it: it has become essential infrastructure rather than a luxury.

"Air travel now is indispensable for a modern economy ... and frankly, it is taken for granted," said Bob van der Linden, acting chair of the aeronautics department at the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum. "It's a commodity. There's nothing romantic about it anymore. But in a way, that's a clear sign of its success."

Dan Bubb, a professor at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, who teaches commercial aviation history, agreed. "For the most part, air travel still remains strong," he said. People "might be unhappy. They might grumble, but they are still going to do it."

Miami traveler Michelle Jolene captured the weariness of frequent flyers. "I literally haven't had a normal flight in so long and I fly a lot," she said. "So I'm like, just, okay, here's another thing." For those with no choice but to fly, she said, there's little to do but carry on.

## What to Watch

Forecasters are also warning of stormy weather on both coasts, with rain and wind threatening the East Coast and coastal flood risks on the West — conditions that could complicate the holiday travel crunch further. New York City's law limits full-size parade balloons when sustained winds exceed 23 mph, a reminder of how weather and scheduling can collide at scale.

For the coming week, the key questions are whether the FAA can prevent a recurrence of the router failure, whether sporadic airline IT glitches flare up again on the busiest flying days, and whether rising fares — driven by geopolitical energy shocks — begin to erode demand. For now, the evidence suggests Americans' appetite for flight remains stubbornly intact, even as their confidence in the systems beneath it is tested.
