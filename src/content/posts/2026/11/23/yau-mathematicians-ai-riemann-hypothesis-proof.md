# Yau, Fields Medalists Weigh an AI Proof of Riemann

Three leading mathematicians, including Fields Medalist Yau Shing-Tung, have offered their first extended assessment of an AI-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, calling the result credible and potentially epochal while insisting that human understanding—not machine verification—remains the real prize. The comments came in a joint interview published by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34537212) on 23 November 2026, days after a mathematician at the AI company Anthropic claimed the breakthrough.

## A claim unlike any before

On 20 November, Levent Alpöge, a 34-year-old mathematician and researcher at Anthropic, announced that working with the company's Claude model he had found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis—the 1859 conjecture about the distribution of prime numbers that remains among the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems. He said extending the method also resolved the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.

The announcement did not take the form of a conventional paper. Alpöge released a four-page explanation alongside 14 compressed archives totaling roughly 17 million lines of Lean formal-verification code, according to a [China Science Daily account](https://news.sciencenet.cn/htmlnews/2026/11/575152.shtm). The natural-language proof generated by Claude reportedly runs beyond 250,000 pages and was not published because of its length. Alpöge said the work took about six weeks.

The timing drew immediate scrutiny: Anthropic completed a large Nasdaq listing on 19 November, the day before the announcement. Alpöge acknowledged the awkwardness, telling [BBC News Chinese](https://www.bbc.com/zhongwen/articles/cwe85gr487dno/simp) that "the timing was just awful" and that a cynical reading was "wrong."

## Why formal verification is not the same as acceptance

Within about 24 hours, the Lean and Mathlib developer communities issued a joint statement confirming the code verified, saying they were "fully confident Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct." As [ScienceNet reported](https://news.sciencenet.cn/htmlnews/2026/11/575149.shtm), the communities confirmed four criteria: that the theorem statement matched Mathlib's standard definition of the hypothesis, that the code compiled identically across dozens of independent runs, that independent tools confirmed the proven object was indeed the Riemann Hypothesis, and that the logic passed the official Lean kernel plus multiple third-party checkers.

The caveats are significant. The certified object is the Mathlib version of the Riemann Hypothesis; the full Generalized Riemann Hypothesis has not been verified through this path. And formal verification is not peer review. The Clay Institute still lists the problem as unsolved, and its rules require formal publication plus at least two years of community scrutiny before any award consideration.

## "Mathematics has entered a new era"

Speaking at the Riemann Bicentenary Academic Symposium in Shenzhen, Yau did not hedge. "Undoubtedly this is a great contribution to number theory; you could say mathematics has entered a new era," he said. He argued there is little need to over-question the proof's credibility, noting that step-by-step machine verification sets a standard "even higher than the experimental verification standard for many major physics discoveries."

James Maynard, a 2022 Fields Medalist at Oxford, put his confidence at "98% or 99%," drawing on both the Lean checks and his familiarity with Alpöge's prior work. If it holds, he said, it will be "one of the great achievements in the history of mathematics." Still, he cautioned that a few additional checks remain.

Xu Wenqiang, an associate professor at Tsinghua's Yau Mathematical Sciences Center, focused on the downstream effects: many results previously conditional on the Riemann Hypothesis now become unconditional proofs, and conjectures such as Goldbach and the twin-prime problem may move closer to resolution.

## Understanding, not verification, is the hard part

The three mathematicians converged on a point that formal verification cannot settle. Machine-checkable correctness, Maynard said, is only the starting point—"what really matters is human understanding." Grasping why the conjecture holds could yield a wholly new view of the primes and open fresh research directions.

That is complicated by sheer scale. Reading 250,000 pages is impractical. Maynard proposed that AI first compress the material to a human-manageable size, after which mathematicians could map the overall framework—perhaps producing a readable full version within a few years. Yau estimated the community could fully understand and distil the proof within five years, and expressed confidence it could soon be rewritten in human terms because "the methods AI used are all methods humans have thought of."

A telling parallel emerged the same day. A separate ~940-page manuscript on the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, written without AI over 12 years by Stanford-trained mathematician Alessandro Masullo, was posted by American Mathematical Society president Ravi Vakil. Within about eight hours, Vakil updated that several people had used AI models to find doubtful points in that human proof. AI, in other words, is now involved both in proving and in checking.

## Who gets the credit—and who gets the compute

Yau likened AI to the microscope, telescope and computer: scientific progress has always depended on better tools, and tools do not topple a discipline's foundations. On authorship, he argued the key credit usually belongs to "the person directing the AI," much as the Nobel Prize for the Higgs boson went to the theorists who proposed the idea rather than the vast experimental teams.

Maynard rejected the framing that "AI proved this problem." AI, he said, ran "the last short stretch of the race," while the hardest work was done by mathematicians over decades. Xu predicted that mathematics will shift from over-emphasizing "the first proof" toward a more plural evaluation—rewarding deep understanding of machine proofs, extension of derived theory, and the posing of key new questions.

Yau's sharpest concern was not credit but access. High-performance AI research compute, he noted, is concentrated in a handful of US companies, with some resources restricted for Chinese scholars. He called for open, equitable AI research tools and said correcting the imbalance must begin at the national level. He also framed the moment as a strategic opening: China's mathematics still trails the international frontier, but a paradigm shift is a window to "overtake on the curve," and young scholars are best placed to seize it.

## What to watch

The immediate test is digestion. Terence Tao warned that understanding the proof will take considerable time—though "at least this time there is a human author to communicate with." The full GRH remains unverified through Lean. Traditional peer review, and the two-year clock the Clay Institute requires, have barely begun.

A group of mathematics scholars has already pushed back, arguing in a joint letter that AI companies touting major solutions to showcase commercial products is "harmful to the mathematical sciences." That debate—over hype, credit and the future of the discipline—may prove as consequential as the proof itself. For now, the mathematicians' verdict is striking in its balance: the answer may belong to the machine, but the meaning, they insist, still belongs to us.
