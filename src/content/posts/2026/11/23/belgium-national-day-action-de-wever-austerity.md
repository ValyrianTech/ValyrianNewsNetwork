# Belgians March Against De Wever Austerity in 11th Protest

Thousands of demonstrators converged on Brussels on Monday for a national day of action against the austerity policies of Prime Minister Bart De Wever's government, marching from Brussels-North station to Brussels-South in a largely peaceful protest that coincided with a nationwide strike disrupting air travel, public transport and postal services. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/nationale-betoging-van-vakbonden-trekt-door-brussel-er-mag-nie~1795406722704/), it was at least the eleventh national demonstration staged by Belgium's three main trade unions since the so-called "Arizona" coalition took office.

The protest unfolded without reported incidents for much of the morning, as [HLN reported](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/voorlopig-geen-incidenten-bij-nationale-actiedag-met-duizenden-betogers-tegen-beleid-van-regering-de-wever-politie-aanwezig-met-waterkanon~af72f38b/), though police deployed in large numbers with water cannons as a precaution following serious violence that marred the previous national march on 9 October.

## A Broad Coalition Against Budget Cuts

The demonstration was organised by the three national unions — the Christian ACV, the socialist ABVV and the liberal Synova (formerly ACLVB) — joined by a wide civil-society coalition including health insurance funds, anti-poverty groups, environmental organisations, students and pensioners. Organisers announced the action in October, framing it as a society-wide mobilisation rather than a narrowly worker-focused dispute.

At the pre-march speeches, union leaders trained their fire on the government's budget consolidation. "PM De Wever, look at the ordinary man and woman. Give them perspective. Don't ignore them," ACV chair Ann Vermorgen said, arguing that "the budget bill is being placed unilaterally on workers, families, pensioners, benefit recipients and civil servants."

ABVV chair Bert Engelaar struck a sharper tone, telling the crowd that "it's always the same people who get the bitter chocolate," adding that "workers get bitter chocolate, the rich get pralines with champagne." Synova chair Gert Truyens urged politicians to "listen to the people… listen to our proposals," warning that they were becoming "a bit detached from the world."

The unions say they accept the need for reform but reject savings imposed on ordinary people, instead demanding a fair contribution from large wealth and capital — pointing to alternatives worth up to €21 billion through closing tax loopholes, tackling fraud and reforming management companies.

## The Political Backdrop

The march came as the federal budget crisis deepened. On the eve of the protest, coalition partner Vooruit — the coalition's only left-leaning party — refused to accept De Wever's latest budget proposal. The inner cabinet ended without agreement, and the prime minister travelled to the King without offering his resignation.

PS chair Paul Magnette, who joined the march, backed Vooruit's resistance and warned that further "index jumps" — skipping the automatic indexation of wages and benefits — would be "catastrophic" for the country. "PM De Wever would do better to walk along with the protesters once to listen to them," he said. "Many people are up to their necks in water."

## Workers Left Behind by Bankruptcy

Among the marchers was a delegation of former workers from the chemical company Vynova in Tessenderlo-Ham, which filed for bankruptcy earlier this month with more than €275 million in debts, costing hundreds of jobs. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/23/ex-medewerkers-vynova-in-tessenderlo-doen-mee-aan-betoging-in-br/), the workers carried a banner demanding an "ICIG law" — a proposed legal framework, modelled on the Renault law, to stop investment funds from extracting profits for years and then abandoning companies without responsibility.

"It cannot be that more than 300 million euros was taken out of the company over seven years, and that the company is then left behind for everyone to pay for," said ABVV's Yannick Urbain.

## Economic Cost and a Growing Backlash

The strike wave caused significant disruption. All regular flights at Belgian airports were cancelled between 07:00 and 21:00 because of a strike by air-traffic controller Skeyes, while Flemish bus and tram operator De Lijn ran at roughly 60 percent service and Brussels' MIVB operated only two of its four metro lines. Postal service was also affected, with one in three Walloon postal workers on strike.

Business groups pushed back sharply. Voka CEO Frank Beckx called the action day "the summit of surrealism," complaining that "as almost always, the airports are the victim of union actions they have nothing to do with," and noting that more than 1,300 jobs had been lost in ten days. Employers' organisation Unizo called the recurring strikes "totally irresponsible" and called for a revision of the 2002 agreement governing industrial action.

## What's Next

The eleventh national march underscores how much of the De Wever government's agenda remains contested on the streets as well as at the negotiating table. With Vooruit holding out on the budget and the unions vowing to keep up pressure, the coming days will test whether the coalition can close its fiscal gap without triggering further confrontation — and whether a reform of bankruptcy protection for workers gains traction in parliament.
