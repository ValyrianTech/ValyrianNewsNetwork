# Trump to Address a DOJ Reshaped by Pursuit of Adversaries

President Donald Trump is set to condemn California Gov. Gavin Newsom's clemency for the state's death row inmates in a speech at the Justice Department on Monday — his second address in less than two years at an agency that critics say has been transformed into an instrument of his political retribution. According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-justice-department-speech-blanche-5fff1865550ea7699667120234f2ebd4), the White House framed the address in the building's ceremonial Great Hall as a defense of "American families" and a rebuke of Newsom's decision to commute the sentences of more than 500 inmates to life without parole.

But the central question hanging over the event is whether the speech will double as a platform for the president to again encourage the prosecution of his political opponents — and to revisit years-old grievances about a department that investigated him over the past decade.

## A Department Under a Giant Portrait

Trump's influence over the Justice Department is now embodied, literally, by a giant banner bearing his face that hangs from the building's exterior — a symbol that critics cite as evidence of the erosion of the institution's historic independence. The speech comes as the department faces intensifying scrutiny over that independence, with Attorney General Todd Blanche, Trump's former personal defense lawyer, steering an agency increasingly aligned with the White House agenda.

Blanche, who was elevated from deputy attorney general to the top job in April after Trump ousted Pam Bondi, has been unapologetic in his view that the president is entitled to exert his will on the department. Bondi struggled to satisfy the president's demands for retribution and endured bipartisan criticism over her handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files. According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/justice-department-congress-fbi-trump-e121c4420d0010b9ba7e8003407d304a), the department has operated as an extension of the White House — pursuing investigations into Trump's enemies, firing waves of career employees and defending legally dubious positions rejected by courts.

## The Newsom Clemency Fight

The stated purpose of Monday's address centers on Newsom, the Democratic governor who frequently trades barbs with Trump and is considering a presidential run. Earlier this month, Newsom said he would change more than 500 death sentences in his state to life in prison without the possibility of parole, a decision he described as among the most difficult of his career.

"I take this action because I firmly believe that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values, undermines the foundations of our legal system, and diminishes each of us," Newsom said in a public letter. "As Governor, I alone bear responsibility for this decision and its impact, just as I alone would bear responsibility for each execution."

As [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/california-death-row-newsom-commute-sentences-dd8a949162b3325edfaa381952ff2466) reported, Newsom moved to grant clemency to 548 people on California's death row — 529 men and 19 women. Because the state constitution requires the California Supreme Court's approval for anyone convicted of a felony more than once, the court would have to approve 382 of those commutations, roughly 70%. According to the Death Penalty Information Center, it is the largest mass clemency in American history; if all are approved, it would reduce the U.S. death row population by nearly 30%.

The move drew sharp condemnation from victims' groups and prosecutors, setting up the emotional backdrop the White House appears eager to amplify. The California District Attorneys Association warned it would inflict additional pain on victims' relatives, and individual district attorneys called it an abuse of power.

## A Pattern of Retribution — and Setbacks

Monday's address echoes Trump's first Justice Department speech in March 2025, when he used an event meant to rally support for his policies on violent crime, drugs and illegal immigration to air a litany of complaints about the criminal cases he faced under President Joe Biden — including charges of hoarding classified documents at Mar-a-Lago and conspiring to overturn the 2020 election. He vowed retribution for what he called the "lies and abuses that have occurred within these walls" and pledged to expose the "egregious crimes and severe misconduct" of his foes.

Since then, the department has accelerated efforts to investigate his enemies but has encountered multiple setbacks, with cases either thrown out by judges or spurned by grand juries that refused to issue indictments. In the weeks before this month's midterms, the department unsuccessfully sought an indictment against former White House aide Cassidy Hutchinson, accelerated an investigation into Rep. Ilhan Omar, filed a fresh criminal case against former FBI Director James Comey and charged two ex-FBI officials in a complaint swiftly dismissed by a judge.

## A New Political Landscape

The speech unfolds against the backdrop of sweeping Democratic gains in the Nov. 3 midterm elections, which gave Democrats control of Congress and teed up possible House and Senate investigations into everything from the mass firings of agents and prosecutors who investigated Trump to a series of politically charged investigations that have been rejected by grand juries and judges alike.

Blanche has signaled the department will not be cowed. "We will not back down. This will not slow us down. This will not stop us," he said in a Fox News interview. "President Trump has two years remaining in this administration, and you better believe that we're going to use it effectively."

Yet the coming confrontation may prove difficult to resolve. "It's a standoff, and I don't know what the answer to that is yet," said Stan Brand, a veteran Washington lawyer and former House general counsel. "I don't know if the Congress knows."

## What to Watch

Whether Monday's speech stays within the White House's stated frame of crime and family — or veers into naming individuals for prosecution — will shape how the next phase of the Trump-Blanche Justice Department unfolds. Democrats now hold the gavel and intend to investigate the department's politicization, but they lack foolproof enforcement tools: subpoenas are hard to enforce, and criminal contempt referrals go to the very department under investigation.

For now, the address is a reminder of how thoroughly the lines between the White House and the Justice Department have blurred — and how central the pursuit of Trump's adversaries has become to the institution's public identity. What happens after the Great Hall speech, and whether courts and a newly empowered Congress check that pursuit, is the story that will define the months ahead.
