# Christian Nationalists Move to Supplant Trump's MAGA

IRVING, Texas — Christian nationalists who once celebrated Donald Trump's return to the White House as a victory for their movement now see the Republican Party's recent election losses as an opportunity to supplant his "Make America Great Again" coalition with an explicitly theocratic vision, [the Associated Press reported](https://apnews.com/article/trump-webbon-christian-nationalist-maga-theocracy-republicans-9c5e34308609af945f4e738d03c30257).

The push crystallized at a three-day conference near Dallas titled "Christ is King: America After Trump," where white nationalist streamer Nick Fuentes was the surprise guest and pastor-podcaster Joel Webbon served as master of ceremonies. Organizers said more than 1,000 people attended the gathering at the Irving Convention Center, which featured speakers including conspiracy theorist Alex Jones and right-wing YouTube comedian JP Sears.

## A movement on the political fringe

Webbon's movement, which seeks a theocratic government and a patriarchal society, remains on the political fringe, and no elected officials appeared on stage. Webbon said conservative leaders won't give him the time of day "because they want their base to not even know I exist."

Yet scholars caution against dismissing the effort. "We can clearly say they have been gaining power," said Julie Ingersoll, a professor of religious studies at the University of North Florida who studies Christian nationalism. "I wouldn't dismiss them."

"This isn't your next-door neighbor's Christianity," Ingersoll added. "They want America to be a Christian nation in a very specific kind of way. And that's going to be exclusively for Christians, mostly for white Christians."

In Webbon's stated vision, the United States is largely made up of white Christians, the Apostles' Creed serves as the preamble to the Constitution, at least 50 million people are deported, interracial marriage is considered "not normal," and Jews, Muslims, Hindus and women are denied the right to vote or hold public office.

"We're done with equality," Webbon declared from the stage. "Christianity is American. Islam is not, Hinduism is not, Judaism is not."

## A vacuum in MAGA's wake

The conference followed a historic Republican collapse in the November midterms, in which Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. Fuentes framed the rout as a rejection of Trump personally rather than of his political project.

"This is like the death of Josef Stalin," Fuentes said, his voice reverberating through a dark convention hall. "And there is nothing that will fill the void right now."

That void is precisely what Webbon aims to occupy. According to [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/15/politics/christian-nationalists-nick-fuentes-trump), he outlined a four-phase plan: phase one, destabilizing MAGA, which he described as already complete; phase two, building political machinery such as door-knockers, donors and phone banks; phase three, winning elected office, "ideally the presidency," but at minimum "a couple governors or some Senate races"; and phase four, placing allies in "serious positions of influence."

Fuentes, who leads the "groyper" movement, said the midterms represented "a rejection of Donald Trump" but "not a rejection of Trump-ism," and pointed to the Democratic Socialists of America as a tactical model. "100,000 groypers doesn't flip the country 50% in favor of our ideology, but 100,000 groypers can do 100 million phone calls," he said, according to CNN.

Both men acknowledged they are in discussions with unnamed people considering a 2028 presidential run, recognizing that association with the movement could be a political liability.

## An opening rooted in the GOP's evangelical dependence

Analysts say the movement is exploiting a genuine structural opening. White evangelical Christians remained the Republican Party's most loyal bloc in the midterms even as Democrats swept to victory, [CNN's Ronald Brownstein wrote](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/22/politics/white-evangelical-christians-republicans-midterms), deepening the GOP's reliance on a denomination that has shrunk from roughly 27% of the U.S. population in the 1990s to about 13% today.

Roughly 82% of white evangelicals backed Republicans, matching Trump's 2024 margin, while Democrats posted historic gains among the large majority of voters who are not evangelical. After the election, according to Progressive strategist Michael Podhorzer, about two-thirds of House Republicans will represent evangelical-heavy districts, and just five of the GOP's remaining 44 senators represent states with below-average evangelical populations.

That dynamic cuts both ways for Christian nationalists. The GOP's dependence on a shrinking base gives the movement motive and opportunity to compete for those voters — but its explicit exclusions make it broadly unelectable, a paradox mainstream strategists see as politically self-defeating if adopted wholesale.

## Disappointment with Trump runs deep

There was little goodwill toward the president at the conference. Sears, the comedian, drew a show of hands from attendees who felt they had been fooled by Trump's promises to dismantle the "deep state" and root out corruption.

"He's going to dismantle the deep state, he is going to root out the corruption in the system," Sears said, mimicking the 2024 pitch. "And here we are, he is the corruption."

The White House did not respond to a request for comment.

Some Republicans, notably Sen. Ted Cruz of Texas, have warned about growing antisemitism on the political right, and even some attendees seemed uncomfortable with the movement's racial determinism. Webbon insists his worldview prioritizes religion over race, but scholars say the distinction makes little practical difference. "It doesn't have to just be white folk," said Michael Emerson, a Rice University sociologist. "But you've got to support ultimately that white folks are in the dominant."

## What to watch

The coming months will test whether the movement can convert conference energy into electoral infrastructure. Key questions include whether any 2028 candidate openly courts this faction, whether mainstream Republicans actively marginalize it, and whether the organizers' promised "phase two" produces real candidate recruitment and fundraising.

Webbon is candid about the gap between his ambitions and his reach, but he believes the boundaries of acceptable politics can be shifted. His stated goal, in a phrase, is to "break containment" — to pull once-unthinkable positions toward the mainstream of a party now searching for its post-Trump identity.
