# Belgian Fuel Prices Fall as Petrol Set for Seventh Drop

Petrol prices at Belgian filling stations will fall from Tuesday 24 November, the Federal Public Service for Economy announced on Monday, handing motorists a modest saving as a prolonged energy crisis finally eases at the pump. Petrol 95 (E10) will drop 3.7 cents to a maximum of €1.782 per litre, while petrol 98 (E5) will fall 3.9 cents to €1.874 per litre, [Le Soir reported](https://www.lesoir.be/784322/article/2026-11-23/les-prix-la-pompe-en-baisse-partir-de-mardi).

It marks the seventh consecutive weekly decline in petrol prices, taking them to their lowest level since mid-March 2026. The relief, however, is partial: road diesel (B7) is left unchanged at €2.274 per litre, and heating oil rises slightly as winter sets in.

## A small saving at the pump

For drivers, the change is tangible but modest. A 50-litre fill of E10 will cost about €1.85 less than under the previous maximum, and the same fill of E5 about €1.95 less. Belgium sets official maximum prices for petroleum products, meaning stations may charge less but never more than the published ceiling.

The adjustment was confirmed in the official tariff published by the [FOD Economie](https://economie.fgov.be/nl/themas/energie/energieprijzen/maximumprijzen/officieel-tarief-van-de) — Tariff No. 2026/225, valid from 24 November 2026. All quoted prices include VAT.

According to Le Soir, the price of petrol has been falling steadily for just over a month, after it peaked at more than €2.10 per litre in mid-October. Heating oil, by contrast, is on the rise, edging up 1.4 cents, with gasoil for heating applications capped at €1.3719 per litre for orders under 2,000 litres.

## The tail end of a price shock

The softening follows one of the sharpest fuel-price spikes in recent European memory. As [HLN detailed](https://www.hln.be/economie/koude-doet-europese-gasprijs-stijgen-maar-benzine-daalt-tot-laagste-prijs-sinds-midden-maart~a634e96a/), the war in the Middle East that broke out at the end of February sent fuel prices climbing sharply from early March. Diesel (B7) reached a record maximum of €2.51 per litre in late October, and petrol 95 peaked at €2.105 while petrol 98 neared €2.30.

The conflict disrupted flows through the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow passage through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil and gas supply once moved. That shock has left deep marks well beyond the petrol pump, pushing European gas prices sharply higher and, according to HLN, driving Flemish household energy bills to multi-year highs.

The recent run of declines reflects easing international oil quotations rather than any policy intervention. Belgian maximum prices are recalculated mechanically from world market prices for petroleum products and their bio-components, alongside periodic indexation of storage costs, distribution margins and the PROMAZ soil-remediation levy.

## Why Belgium's model draws attention

Belgium and Luxembourg operate a distinctive system of fixed maximum prices, unlike fully liberalised markets elsewhere. That model has drawn fresh interest abroad: HLN noted that Germany plans to introduce maximum prices for diesel and petrol from 2027, explicitly following the Belgian and Luxembourg example, initially for three months.

Yet the broader energy picture remains strained. On the same day the petrol cut was announced, cold weather across Europe pushed the benchmark TTF gas price up nearly 9% to more than €96 per megawatt-hour, and the International Energy Agency has warned that the Middle East crisis poses the greatest energy security threat on record, surpassing the oil shocks of the 1970s.

## What to watch

For motorists, Tuesday brings a welcome few euros of relief and a seventh straight week of cheaper petrol. But the divergence within the announcement tells the fuller story: diesel is paused at a historically elevated level, and heating oil is rising just as households brace for winter.

Whether petrol continues to slide will depend less on Brussels than on international markets and the trajectory of the Middle East conflict — a reminder that Belgium's maximum-price system can soften the edges of a global shock, but cannot insulate consumers from it.
