# COP31 Summit Ends Without a Political Deal

The COP31 UN climate summit closed in Antalya, Türkiye, on 22 November without adopting its central political package, leaving the future of key climate workstreams unresolved after two weeks of negotiations and more than 30 hours of overtime. Delegates failed to reach consensus on the proposed "Antalya Pact"—the cover decisions of the COP and the CMA—after a final plenary in which several parties formally objected to the text, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/world/20261123/dd34253be20e45e793580b9e6ab3a67b/c.html), the summit's primary reporting source.

The collapse marked a setback for multilateral climate diplomacy and capped a year of sobering scientific warnings, with the world now on course to overshoot the 1.5°C warming limit.

## What happened in the final hours

The conference was scheduled to close on 20 November but ran two days long, becoming the longest-running UN climate summit on record. Through the weekend, the Turkish presidency held closed-door consultations on finance, adaptation, mitigation and the cover decisions, and revised texts circulated into Sunday morning.

When the plenary reconvened on the afternoon of 22 November, additional outcomes were adopted—including on the Global Goal on Adaptation, the Adaptation Fund and a Just Transition Work Programme. But when COP31 President Murat Kurum, Türkiye's environment minister, presented the overarching "Antalya Pact," objections from several delegations prevented consensus.

"I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted," Kurum said, as [reported](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/11/1168855) by UN News. Per Xinhua, he added that some countries had fixated on a particular word or phrasing and brought climate-unrelated issues into the discussion, obstructing progress.

## Three fault lines blocked a deal

The deadlock centered on three interrelated disputes. The first was the response to the science: compromise drafts acknowledged that warming is now set to exceed 1.5°C and leaned on expanded carbon-removal technologies, an approach critics dismissed as "false solutions." The second was fossil fuels: the final text dropped any explicit reference to transitioning away from coal, oil and gas, undoing language first agreed at COP28 in Dubai in 2023. The third was finance, with developing countries pressing for stronger commitments on adaptation and loss and damage.

The European Union objected that the text failed to "meet the moment" and said it could not accept an omission of fossil fuel language, calling it "unconscionable." Panama argued that governments have a responsibility "not to negotiate the minimum threshold, but to act." The Marshall Islands said accepting the text would amount to a betrayal of its people. On the other side, Saudi Arabia, speaking for the League of Arab States, defended references to carbon capture and storage, rejecting claims of greenwashing, while the Russian Federation warned that supply-side measures could cause energy shortages and poverty.

Ghana, for the African Group, acknowledged shortcomings on finance, loss and damage and the just transition—which it said received "a single generic sentence"—but said the group would not stand in the way of consensus.

## UN leaders: no ambiguity in the science

UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was deeply disappointed by the outcome but insisted the failure did not alter the realities of the climate crisis.

"There may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science," Guterres said. "The take-home message from COP31 is clear: the world must do much more to confront the climate crisis—and it must do it now." He warned that temperatures continue to break records, impacts are intensifying and emissions remain dangerously high. "We can't put out the fire while pouring fuel on it," he said.

UN Climate Change Executive Secretary Simon Stiell, addressing the closing plenary, [acknowledged the frustration](https://unfccc.int/news/un-climate-change-executive-secretary-simon-stiell-remarks-at-the-closing-plenary-of-cop31) of delegates. "The world cannot afford a repeat of this moment," he said, urging countries to learn lessons from Antalya ahead of COP32.

## China voices disappointment, defends multilateralism

Li Gao, head of the Chinese delegation and Vice Minister of Ecology and Environment, said China was deeply disappointed as a party that had worked hard for the summit's success. But he argued that confidence in the multilateral process should not be lost, and that mutual recrimination does not help build trust. Global climate governance, he said, has never been a smooth path, and upholding multilateralism and unity is the only way forward—remarks carried by Xinhua.

The summit itself operated under an unprecedented "dual presidency" model, with Türkiye leading the COP Presidency and Action Agenda while Australia guided the formal negotiations as Presidency of Negotiations. Nearly 200 countries and territories took part, drawing more than 90,000 registered on-site participants.

## Partial wins, structural damage

Despite the political failure, agreement was reached on a just transition work programme and a set of COP31 Action Agenda initiatives on electrification, resilience and investment. Stiell pointed to these as real-world progress, noting that around half the global economy is accelerating electrification.

Yet the collapse of the cover decision carried collateral damage. Parties also failed to adopt a decision on the continuation of the Mitigation Ambition and Implementation Work Programme (MWP), whose mandate was meant to be settled at this session. Its future now stands unclear, weakening the mitigation architecture of the process.

## What's next

Attention shifts to COP32, to be hosted by Ethiopia in Addis Ababa in 2027. Delegations there will confront the same unanswered questions: how to accelerate the transition away from fossil fuels, how to mobilize climate finance for developing nations, and how to keep the 1.5°C goal within reach despite an unavoidable overshoot.

The context is not encouraging. The United States formally notified its withdrawal from the UNFCCC in February 2026, with exit effective in February 2027. The UN Environment Programme has concluded that exceeding 1.5°C is now unavoidable, with the task being to minimize the magnitude and duration of the overshoot. Stiell framed COP32 as a vital stepping stone, saying the second Global Stocktake must "build on the first—not step back."

The fight, he said, continues. Whether the UNFCCC process can deliver at the pace the science demands remains the open question Antalya leaves behind.
