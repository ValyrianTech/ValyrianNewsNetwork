# Trump, Xi, Putin Hold Rare Nuclear Talks, No Breakthroughs

The leaders of the world's three largest nuclear powers met together for the first time in history on Thursday, gathering at a villa in the Chinese tech hub of Shenzhen to discuss reducing nuclear dangers—but the roughly 70-minute session produced far more symbolism than substance. U.S. President Donald Trump, China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin emerged with a broad "peace declaration" and a pledge to keep talking, yet no concrete arms-control agreements.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-xi-putin-nuclear-ukraine-iran-a04dc76ed8e786b087e2c1ea9598783a), the talks on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit were billed by Trump as a potential diplomatic triumph. Instead, the trio settled on a far narrower agenda: reforming the United Nations, refining more fossil fuels and curbing the reach of the International Criminal Court.

## What the Declaration Said—and What It Avoided

The joint "U.S.-Russia-China Heads of State Declaration: Toward Peace" affirmed that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought," and committed the three powers to "conduct a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons." It also called for unspecified UN reforms and asserted that state officials should enjoy immunity from prosecution by the International Criminal Court, opposing what the signatories described as "jurisdiction abuse."

Conspicuously absent from the written declaration was any direct mention of Russia's war in Ukraine or the U.S.-led war with Iran—the two active conflicts that most acutely touch all three powers. The omission underscored the gap between the summit's lofty framing and its limited deliverables.

Trump, who had sought to flatter both leaders as his friends ahead of the meeting, framed the gathering in historic terms. "We are really making history," he said. "The United States, Russia and China are uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on Earth and also to lessen the risk of those weapons being used."

Xi cast the meeting as a model of great-power cooperation. "History tells us that when major powers live in harmony, the world is at peace, and when they engage in confrontation the world is thrown into turmoil," he said, adding that "as long as dialogue does not stop, doors remain open, there is hope for peace." Putin described the talks as "open and candid," saying the three nations are "united not only by our status as victors in World War II, but, most importantly, by our shared responsibility for maintaining international peace and security."

## A Nuclear Arms-Control Vacuum

The summit unfolded against a stark backdrop: the last U.S.-Russia nuclear arms pact, New START, expired in February 2026, removing binding caps on the two largest arsenals for the first time in more than half a century. Trump rejected Russia's call to extend it for a year, demanding a "new, improved and modernized" deal that would include China.

Yet Beijing's incentives to accept limits are limited. According to the Pentagon's annual report, China's nuclear stockpile stood in the "low 600s" through 2024 and is on track to exceed 1,000 warheads by 2030, while the U.S. and Russia each hold more than 5,000. The summit's modest outcome—an agreement to *talk*—was itself notable, given analysts had doubted China would engage at all.

"This statement is far from meaningful arms control. But it's still a reasonable down payment on stability," said Erin D. Dumbacher of the Council on Foreign Relations. Daryl Kimball, executive director of the Arms Control Association, was more skeptical: "We need more than diplomatic theater and photo opportunities between Xi, Putin and Trump. And one meeting will not likely produce dramatic breakthroughs."

Secretary of State Marco Rubio defended the engagement, noting the three powers "possess the overwhelming majority of nuclear weapons on this planet" and arguing that the idea they "should not engage on a regular basis is not just absurd, it's dangerous."

## Optics and the Question of Who Won

The summit's imagery carried weight beyond its written output. Xi greeted Air Force One on the tarmac—an unusual gesture—and hosted the leaders at Kylin Villa, positioning China at the center of a reordered global hierarchy. Analysts largely agreed that Beijing was the chief beneficiary of the symbolism.

"Xi's clearest diplomatic gain in Shenzhen was bringing Trump and Putin to the same table," said Yaqi Li, a Chinese foreign policy researcher at the ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute. Ruby Osman of the Tony Blair Institute for Global Change put it more bluntly: "Beijing wanted to be seen as the adult in the room."

The gathering also offered Putin a measure of rehabilitation on the world stage, signaling that Russia remains a top-tier power despite Western sanctions and an ICC arrest warrant against him. For U.S. allies, the picture was less reassuring. Rush Doshi of the Council on Foreign Relations warned that for many, "the image was a suggestion that the world increasingly revolves around Beijing's power and preferences," adding it "was unsettling because it suggested that Washington will prioritize engagement with the other great powers over that with its own partners."

## The Wars Left Unaddressed

After the trilateral talks, Trump dialed into a Berlin meeting between U.S. envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, alongside more than 40 European leaders supporting Ukraine. Trump told the gathering he had spoken to Xi and Putin about a peaceful end to the Ukraine war. Yet the summit's own declaration made no reference to the conflict, and Zelenskyy has voiced concern that a trilateral format could become a venue for decisions taken behind Kyiv's back.

Edgard Kagan, a former U.S. ambassador now at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, said the meeting suited Putin's purposes: it was "very much about signaling a desire to talk to the president and talk to Chairman Xi in a way that suggests that they're making decisions on Ukraine, which obviously the Ukrainians and the Europeans don't like."

## What's Next

Trump's post-summit messaging muddied the picture. A day after agreeing to talks on nuclear proliferation, he told a White House press conference that China is building nuclear weapons at a "hot and heavy" pace and that "they'll catch us within 10 years," according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-xi-china-nuclear-weapons-286391edaacc7e8544dc0dc023e253fb).

The next test comes at the G20 summit in Miami in December, where Trump and Xi are scheduled to meet again. As Craig Singleton of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies put it, "Xi treated Trump with respect in Shenzhen. He will test his resolve in Miami next month, when the bill for Beijing's trade commitments comes due." Whether the Shenzhen dialogue on nuclear risks matures into a formal process—and whether China will accept any limits at all—remains the central open question left by a summit that, for now, delivered a photograph more than a framework.
