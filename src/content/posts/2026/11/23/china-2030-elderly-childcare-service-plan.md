# China Sets 2030 Care Targets for Elderly and Children

China has released an implementation plan to build a service system for the elderly and children, setting hard targets for 2030 as the country grapples with rapid population aging and persistently low fertility. The plan, approved by the State Council and issued jointly by the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC), the Ministry of Civil Affairs, the National Health Commission and the Ministry of Education, was announced by [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261123/b4229c255dfe40bba8e7730552a01685/c.html) on 23 November 2026.

The initiative centers on the "one elderly, one young" (一老一小) framework — a signature concept in China's livelihood policy referring to the twin care burdens facing families who must support aging parents while raising young children.

## Context: A Demographic Squeeze

The plan arrives at a pivotal moment. As of the end of 2024, China had about 310 million people aged 60 and over and 220 million aged 65 and over — roughly 22% and 15.6% of the population respectively, according to figures cited by [China Youth Daily](https://news.cyol.com/gb/articles/2025-03/25/content_4w5WjbFWl7.html). That places the country in what officials call a "moderate aging stage."

Beijing has framed the coming decade as a critical window for responding to demographic change. With births declining and single-child families shouldering heavier care obligations, officials have increasingly described eldercare and childcare as inseparable, family-level pressures rather than separate policy silos — the "anxiety over eldercare, difficulty in childcare" (养老愁、托育难) that policymakers now cite explicitly.

## What the Plan Commits To

The plan sets several measurable goals to be reached by 2030:

- **Nursing-type beds** as a share of elderly-care institution beds to reach **73%**.
- **Community elderly-care service institutions and facilities coverage** to reach **70%**.
- **Enrollment of children under three in childcare** to rise by **6 percentage points** compared with 2025.

By that deadline, the plan aims to have basically formed a "universal, affordable" care supply pattern — covering urban and rural areas, rationally distributed, affordably priced and quality-assured — as the Xinhua report described.

The document organizes its measures around four pillars: optimizing resource allocation (aligning eldercare facility planning with population size, structure and migration trends), improving service quality (service standards, pricing mechanisms and smart-technology adoption), perfecting management models (shifting facilities toward populous counties and villages and exploring urban-rural elderly-care consortiums), and strengthening safeguards in funding, land, talent and supervision.

## Building on Existing Investment

The plan operationalizes commitments already sketched in the 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030). It also follows concrete funding moves disclosed earlier this month. At a 10 November NDRC press conference, Huang Weiru, deputy director of the commission's social development department, said 2026 had already seen **14 billion yuan (140亿元)** of central budget investment allocated to build or expand elderly-care and childcare facilities, as [reported](https://news.ifeng.com/c/8xwRtkdgNrG) by Observer network via ifeng.

That funding supported the construction of more than 100,000 nursing-type elderly-care beds and more than 30,000 childcare slots, Huang said. Ultra-long special treasury bonds were also used to help public elderly-care institutions renew about 110,000 units of equipment.

"For many families, caring for the elderly on one side and children on the other is a real, tangible pressure," Huang said. "Doing everything possible to solve their urgent difficulties is the top priority of our livelihood protection and improvement work now and for the period ahead."

Huang also outlined the plan's 13 task measures, noting that on the childcare side the focus would be on achieving full prefecture-level coverage of comprehensive childcare service centers and encouraging employers to run their own childcare.

## Analysis: A Hybrid, Quality-Focused Shift

Two features distinguish this plan from earlier, facility-heavy approaches. First, it explicitly pairs "hard investment" in buildings and equipment with "soft construction" — standards, service quality, pricing and management models — a more balanced supply-and-quality strategy. Second, it targets the urban-rural gap directly, calling for facilities and services to shift toward populous counties and villages, and for mobile elderly-care services in mountainous, pastoral and rural areas.

The plan also embraces market and technology tools: it promotes smart technology in care, employer-run childcare, public-private operating models and chain or branded operation — a hybrid state-market approach rather than pure public provision.

The stakes are substantial. With 310 million elderly citizens and a shrinking younger cohort, China's care economy is becoming both a social stability priority and an economic growth area. The government has projected the so-called silver economy will rise from roughly 6% to 9% of GDP by 2035, according to comments from the Ministry of Civil Affairs reported by China Youth Daily.

## What's Next

The test now lies in implementation. Reaching 73% nursing-bed share and 70% community coverage by 2030 will require sustained fiscal commitment and coordination across four ministries and every level of local government. The plan calls on regions to fold the effort into local economic, spatial and livelihood planning, and to conduct regular effectiveness evaluations.

For Chinese families, the measure of success will be simpler: whether affordable, quality care is genuinely within reach — at the doorstep, and without leaving the familiar surroundings of home.
