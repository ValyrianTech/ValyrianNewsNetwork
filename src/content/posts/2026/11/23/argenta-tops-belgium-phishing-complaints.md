# Argenta Tops Belgium's Phishing Complaints List

Belgian retail bank Argenta receives more phishing complaints relative to its market share than any other bank in the country, according to an exclusive investigation by HLN's research desk. The findings, [published by HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/exclusief-jobstudenten-met-toegang-tot-alle-rekeningen-en-een-onderbemande-fraudedienst-waarom-argenta-de-meeste-phishingklachten-krijgt~a04aeabf/) on 23 November, tie the problem to two internal practices: student workers with access to all customer accounts, and a fraud department that operates with too few staff.

The revelations have quickly escalated from a consumer-protection story into a political confrontation, with Belgium's consumer minister demanding that every bank publish its phishing figures.

## What the investigation found

According to HLN, an internal report compares the absolute number of phishing complaints against each bank's market share, and Argenta comes out worst on that measure. High-placed sources in the banking sector confirmed to HLN's investigative unit that most complaints indeed originate from Argenta customers.

The two structural causes identified are striking precisely because they point to internal governance rather than customer error. Student workers employed by the bank reportedly have access to all accounts, while the fraud unit is described as understaffed, meaning cases move slowly and victims wait longer for answers.

A concrete case illustrates the human cost. Marc Van Doren, 57, from Heffen, lost EUR 730 after scammers posed as Argenta employees, HLN reported. The story was among the outlet's most-read and most-shared articles of the day, drawing 81 reader reactions on the main piece alone.

## A yawning gap in case-handling times

The most politically charged detail is speed. Argenta takes more than 100 days to handle a phishing dossier, against an industry commitment to process and personally respond within 15 days. Consumption Minister Rob Beenders (Vooruit) called that discrepancy "unacceptable."

"Argenta's taking more than a hundred days is unacceptable," he said, according to [HLN's follow-up report](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/minister-beenders-eist-transparantie-na-onthullingen-over-argenta-bij-hln-ik-wil-exact-weten-hoeveel-phishinggevallen-elke-bank-heeft~a991af07/).

Argenta disputed the characterization in a video response, insisting the backlog is a thing of the past. "In the past we had delays there, but not today," the bank said. "Today we no longer have a backlog."

## Beenders demands sector-wide transparency

Beenders is now taking the fight to the banking federation Febelfin. On Wednesday he plans to arrive with a list of demands, seeking total transparency from all banks on how they handle phishing and online fraud.

"I want to know how many phishing cases each bank has," the minister said. He also wants banks to publish audits of their security — provided they contain no sensitive information criminals could exploit — as well as complaint figures. On reimbursement, he argued customers have a right to know how many victims are repaid, under what conditions, and how often a bank ignores an Ombudsfin ruling that it should pay.

"Customers don't know whether they are going to a bank that performs better or worse in that area," Beenders said. "Banks must be able to say in detail what they do if you become a phishing victim tomorrow."

The political pressure builds on a broader dispute over who bears the loss when a customer is tricked. In June 2026, an Antwerp summary-proceedings judge ruled that banks must reimburse phishing victims immediately, before pursuing the customer for gross negligence. Beenders has accused banks of ignoring the law and court rulings, a charge Febelfin denies, as [Gazet van Antwerpen reported](https://www.gva.be/binnenland/banken-leggen-geen-wetten-of-rechterlijke-uitspraken-naast-zich-neer-rond-phishing/155265875.html).

## The banks push back

The sector is not accepting the framing quietly. Febelfin points out that banks have invested roughly EUR 100 million in the fight against online fraud in recent years, and argues that security is a shared responsibility.

"Phishing is a story inflated by some," Febelfin's chief legal officer Michael Anseeuw said, according to [De Tijd](https://www.tijd.be/ondernemen/banken/banken-slaan-terug-na-kritiek-op-fraudebeleid-phishing-is-een-door-sommigen-opgeklopt-verhaal/10655596.html). "Banks have been professionalizing their fraud-fighting for years." He stressed that telecom operators, technology companies and customers themselves are part of the equation. "If a customer opens the door by giving out codes, no system can stop it," he said.

That defense is a legitimate counterpoint but sits awkwardly against the Argenta-specific allegations. If student workers genuinely have access to all accounts and the fraud unit is understaffed, the problem looks less like a customer who left the door open and more like a house with a weak lock.

## A backdrop of mounting losses

Belgians lost roughly EUR 93 million to phishing in the year before a joint action plan was unveiled in July 2026, [Het Belang van Limburg reported](https://www.hbvl.be/politiek/beenders-en-banken-trekken-ten-strijde-tegen-phishing-minder-comfort-voor-meer-beveiliging/158372056.html). That 15-point plan — agreed between Febelfin and Beenders — introduced a EUR 5,000 daily transfer limit, a four-hour cooling-down period for limit increases, a "slow banking" option, a larger role for the Itsme identity app, an expanded Fraudstop emergency number, and a commitment to handle fraud dossiers within 15 bank-working days. Most measures take effect at the end of 2026 or early 2027.

"If we want to structurally reduce online fraud, we must fight fraud where it originates and effectively prosecute criminals," Febelfin CEO Karel Baert said at the time.

## What to watch

The Argenta story matters because it turns a diffuse, often invisible crime into a question of institutional accountability. Until now, per-bank phishing volumes and repayment rates were not public. Beenders' demand would change that, giving customers a basis for comparing banks on something more meaningful than interest rates.

The immediate test comes Wednesday, when the minister meets Febelfin. The key questions: will banks agree to publish phishing case numbers, repayment rates and Ombudsfin compliance? And will Argenta face supervisory or reputational consequences from the FSMA or the National Bank of Belgium?

For now, the bank insists it has fixed its backlog. The complaint data, and the minister's demands, suggest the public — and the regulator — will want proof.
