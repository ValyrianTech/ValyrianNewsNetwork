# China MFA Rebukes UK Police Over Border Detentions

China's Foreign Ministry has publicly accused British border police of "arbitrary and excessive enforcement," saying it has received multiple reports of Chinese citizens being detained for long periods, questioned, and having their phones and electronic devices seized when entering or leaving the United Kingdom.

Speaking at a routine press conference on 27 October 2026, ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said Beijing had lodged a formal diplomatic protest (严正交涉) and expressed "strong dissatisfaction and serious concern" over the treatment of its nationals, according to the [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20261027/381c087884c54e2c80872f9ceecf6861/c.html) wire service. The escalation followed a consular advisory issued by the Chinese Embassy in London eight days earlier.

## What Beijing Says Happened

According to Guo, the Foreign Ministry and the embassy have "recently received multiple reports of Chinese citizens being detained and questioned for long periods by UK border enforcement officers, and of their mobile phones and other electronic devices being seized."

The full press conference transcript published by the [Foreign Ministry](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/fyrbt_673021/202610/t20261027_12047706.shtml) shows the issue was raised by a Xinhua reporter, who asked the spokesperson to elaborate on an embassy travel notice issued in the preceding days. Guo's response framed the incidents as a pattern rather than isolated cases, and urged Chinese nationals planning to travel to the UK to read the embassy's advisory carefully "to avoid inconvenience and losses."

## The Embassy's Advisory and the Legal Dispute

The primary document behind the dispute is a consular notice the Chinese Embassy in London published on 19 October 2026, titled "Advisory: Chinese Citizens Should Note UK Entry-Exit Checks." In it, the embassy said that "multiple cases" had occurred of Chinese citizens being held and questioned at the border and having electronic devices confiscated.

Crucially, the [embassy advisory](https://gb.china-embassy.gov.cn/lsfw_137163/lsbhyxz/lstx/202610/t20261019_12043053.htm) specifies who was affected: members of business and economic delegations visiting the UK, people travelling privately to visit family, and ordinary tourists. That detail matters, because it casts the issue as one affecting everyday travellers rather than officials or diplomats.

The embassy also identified the legal basis British officers cited for their actions: the UK's Counter-Terrorism and Border Security Act 2019. That statute's [Schedule 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2019/3/schedule/3/enacted) grants "examining officers" at ports broad powers to stop, question, search and detain people entering or leaving the country to determine whether they are "concerned in hostile activity." The powers can be exercised without a warrant and, under the schedule's terms, "whether or not there are grounds for suspecting" hostile activity. The legislation also provides for the examination and retention of property, including electronic devices.

## A Two-Step Diplomatic Signal

The sequence of events suggests a deliberate escalation rather than a spontaneous complaint. The embassy issued its advisory on 19 October, and the [China Daily](https://cn.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202610/27/WS6ae05553e4b09a165c791cf7.html) republication of the 27 October statement shows the message being carried across state media.

The shift in language is telling. On 19 October the embassy spoke of "serious concern." By 27 October, the Foreign Ministry had added "strong dissatisfaction" and the pointed characterization of British policing as "arbitrary and excessive." In Chinese diplomatic practice, that progression—from a consular notice to a formal spokesperson statement—signals that Beijing wants the issue registered publicly, not merely through private channels.

Both the embassy and the ministry directed travellers to read the advisory and published consular hotlines, including the ministry's global emergency line and the embassy's consular-protection number in London.

## Analysis: Context and Open Questions

The complaint lands during a period of sustained friction between London and Beijing. The two governments have been managing disputes over espionage allegations and the future of China's large planned embassy compound in London, even as Britain has sought to keep trade and engagement channels open.

Beijing has increasingly used consular travel advisories as a public instrument of diplomatic pressure, deploying similar notices in past years over entry scrutiny and device searches in other countries. That pattern makes the UK complaint as much a political signal as a consular-protection measure.

Several important questions remain unanswered. There has been no public British response in the sources reviewed, and Beijing has not disclosed the number of cases, the individuals involved, the dates, or the ports of entry—limitations that make independent verification difficult. The UK's use of Schedule 3 powers has itself been contested domestically by journalists and civil-liberties groups, who argue the powers are broad and applied with little transparency; that controversy is not unique to Chinese nationals.

The reciprocity dimension is also delicate. China imposes stringent entry procedures of its own, a point Western governments have raised in the past, and Beijing's framing here focuses narrowly on how its citizens are treated abroad.

## What to Watch

The next developments will turn on whether the UK Home Office or Foreign Office issues a public reply, and on whether Beijing escalates the advisory into a formal travel caution affecting tourism and business travel. Also worth watching is whether reciprocal measures against British nationals entering China follow, and whether specific, verifiable cases emerge that can be independently confirmed. For now, the dispute stands as a carefully calibrated public complaint—one that tests how far a consular grievance can be pushed as a diplomatic instrument.
