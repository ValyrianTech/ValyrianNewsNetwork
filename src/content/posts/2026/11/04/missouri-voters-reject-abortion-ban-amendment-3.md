# Missouri Voters Reject Bid to Reimpose Abortion Ban

Missouri voters on Tuesday decisively rejected a ballot proposal that would have reimposed an abortion ban and written restrictions on transgender care for minors into the state constitution, handing abortion-rights advocates a major victory and reaffirming a 2024 vote that had already lifted one of the nation's strictest bans.

The Associated Press called the race for rejection. With about 98.5% of precincts reporting, the measure — placed on the ballot by the Republican-led legislature and again numbered Amendment 3 — failed by roughly 44.5% to 55.5%. The result preserves current law allowing abortion until fetal viability, subject to certain restrictions.

## What the Measure Would Have Done

The proposal would have repealed the 2024 constitutional amendment Missourians approved to protect reproductive rights, and replaced it with an abortion ban permitting exceptions for medical emergencies, fetal anomalies, and rape or incest within the first 12 weeks of gestation. It also would have constitutionally prohibited gender-transition surgeries, puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones for minors.

As [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/03/g-s1-151063/missouri-abortion-ban-transgender) reported, the defeat was a major victory for the state's abortion rights supporters and signaled voter support for transgender rights as well. Critics noted the transgender-care provision was largely redundant: Missouri already bans gender-affirming surgery for minors, and bars puberty blockers and hormones through August 2027 — meaning the amendment's chief new effect would have been permanence.

## A Familiar Playbook, Repackaged

The vote marked the second time in two years that Missouri Republicans tried to restrict abortion at the ballot box. In 2024, voters approved a citizen-led Amendment 3 by 51.6% to 48.4% — a margin of roughly 48,000 votes — making Missouri the first state to overturn an abortion ban by popular vote. This year's legislature placed a measure with the same number on the ballot, but with the opposite effect.

According to [ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/missouri-abortion-gender-transition-minors-amendment-3), supporters leaned heavily on the transgender-care provision in their messaging. The pro-amendment committee Her Health, Her Future, whose treasurer is First Lady Claudia Kehoe, ran ads and a website declaring that children are not lab experiments, largely avoiding the word abortion.

Republican State Auditor Scott Fitzpatrick had explained the strategy bluntly: he said the abortion issue is much closer to a 50/50 matter in the state, as evidenced by 2024, whereas the issue of transgender medical procedures is probably more like an 80/20 issue.

Opponents framed the bundling as ballot candy — a popular-sounding restriction meant to make an unpopular abortion ban more palatable. Their coalition, Stop the Ban, received more than $26 million in large donations since the start of the year, compared with about $6.7 million for the pro-amendment campaign, NPR reported.

## Fights Over Ballot Language

The campaign was shadowed by a prolonged legal battle over how the measure was described to voters. A judge ordered the legislature's summary rewritten after finding it unfair. A state appeals court later rewrote language from Republican Secretary of State Denny Hoskins to lead with the fact that the amendment would repeal the 2024 voter-approved amendment protecting reproductive rights.

Michael Wolff, a former Missouri Supreme Court chief justice advising the opposition, said the original summary appeared intended to deceive people into thinking that they were going to protect women having abortions. Hoskins, who publicly endorsed the amendment, defended his role, telling ProPublica that he could not all of a sudden erase his conservative views of the past 16 years in the Missouri Legislature.

## What's Next

Missouri is one of four states that had abortion on the ballot in 2026, and the outcome underscores how abortion remains a potent electoral force even when packaged with other measures. With abortion law now a state-by-state patchwork following the Supreme Court's 2022 Dobbs decision, the result is likely to be read nationally as a warning to lawmakers seeking to curtail reproductive rights through the ballot.

For Missouri, the vote leaves in place the reproductive-rights protections voters approved two years ago — and raises the question of whether Republican leaders will try a third time.
