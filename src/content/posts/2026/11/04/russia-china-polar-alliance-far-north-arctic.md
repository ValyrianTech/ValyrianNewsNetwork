# Russia and China Build a Polar Alliance in the Far North

Russia and China are deepening a strategic partnership in the Arctic, pairing Russian ports and geography with Chinese capital, shipping demand and technology to build what both call a "Polar Silk Road" along the emerging Northern Sea Route. The rapprochement, detailed in a recent [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719) geopolitical analysis originally produced by Czech broadcaster CT24, is alarming Western capitals and pushing the European Union to sharpen its own Arctic strategy.

## A partnership built on ports and shipping

The latest signal came in October, when Nikolai Patrushev, an adviser to President Vladimir Putin and chairman of Russia's Maritime Board, made a six-day visit to China. According to [Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/), discussions focused on linking China's Maritime Silk Road with Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor, developing Russian ports, exchanging container-terminal automation technology and conducting joint maritime research. Patrushev also floated cooperation on shipbuilding and maritime education.

The economic logic runs in both directions. Russia wants Chinese companies involved in the Northern Sea Route to move more oil and gas to Asian markets and to escape Western sanctions imposed over its war in Ukraine. China, for its part, is seeking an alternative maritime route that reduces its dependence on the Strait of Malacca, while giving Chinese firms access to Russian Arctic resources and infrastructure.

In its 2018 Arctic strategy, Beijing already described itself as a "near-Arctic state" with legitimate interests in shipping, resource extraction, scientific research and regional governance. "China aspires to the status of a global superpower. One of its attributes is dominance, or at least a presence, in geopolitically important areas of the world, and the Arctic is indisputably one of them," Vlastislav Bříza, a security expert at Charles University in Prague, told CT24.

## The lure of the Northern Sea Route

Climate change is the engine behind the shift. Melting ice is opening shipping lanes that were previously inaccessible, while the Arctic seabed sharpens appetites for resources. According to the US Geological Survey, the region holds nearly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and 13% of its undiscovered oil.

The Northern Sea Route dramatically shortens the journey between Europe and East Asia. A trip from Shanghai to Hamburg takes roughly 18 days via the Arctic, compared with 30 to 35 days through the Suez Canal. As [Modern Diplomacy](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/10/19/russia-ramps-up-arctic-oil-exports-as-global-shipping-crisis-disrupts-trade-routes/), citing Reuters, reported, Russia's oil exports through the route rose nearly 50% in 2026, with around 20 million barrels delivered to Asia between July and mid-October. Gazprom Neft plans to increase its shipments by about 35% this year, and Putin has said freight volumes could double to 70 million tonnes annually by the end of the decade.

The route's rise is being accelerated by disruption elsewhere. With tanker freight rates at record highs due to conflicts in Iran and Ukraine, Asian refiners are seeking alternatives to costly American crude. Despite the fees for icebreaker assistance, the Arctic route has become cheaper than paths through the Red Sea — and for Moscow, it offers a way to export its oil while avoiding the restrictions it faces in European waters.

## Western alarm and the militarisation of the North

None of this has gone unnoticed in Washington or European capitals. The United States already treats the Arctic as an arena of intensifying strategic competition, and the US Coast Guard reported five China-affiliated research vessels operating in or near the US Arctic in 2025. Western governments accuse Beijing of using research stations for military purposes — a charge China denies.

Thomas Nilsson, head of Swedish military intelligence, warned that China will "use" Russia to gain a foothold in the Far North. Moscow, which once viewed China's growing Arctic influence with suspicion, now depends on Beijing to finance its war effort through energy sales and to supply military materiel and dual-use goods such as microchips.

Russia is also steadily militarising the region. It controls roughly 53% of the Arctic coastline and has reopened Soviet-era bases, deploying advanced coastal defence systems, climate-adapted air defences and fighter aircraft armed with hypersonic missiles. "Russia is expanding its military presence in the North. Moscow has reopened Soviet bases and is testing new weapons in the region," EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said, warning that drone incursions, GPS jamming and sabotage of undersea cables are undermining Arctic security.

NATO's challenge, analysts argue, is less about geography than capability. Seven of the eight Arctic Council member states are now NATO members following Finland's and Sweden's accession, giving the Alliance strategic depth on its northern flank. But the Polish outlet Defence24 noted that the United States has only two ageing operational icebreakers, while Russia has more than forty, including powerful Arktika-class nuclear vessels. "Without the physical capability to operate in ice-covered waters, Washington's declarations in favour of freedom of navigation in the Arctic remain mere diplomatic notes," it wrote.

## Europe's response

The EU unveiled its own Arctic strategy in October 2026, built around four priorities: strengthening security and resilience, combating climate change, and promoting prosperity and opportunities for Arctic communities. "The Arctic has changed dramatically, and the whole world has changed, especially since Russia's aggression against Ukraine," said Jozef Síkela, the EU commissioner who prepared the strategy. "The Arctic is warming up to four times faster than the global average and has become the object of genuine geopolitical rivalry."

The plan calls for strengthening Europe's icebreaker fleet — the bloc currently has only about ten such ships — and intensifying surveillance of Russia's "shadow fleet," whose movements are increasingly hard to track. A new Centre for Maritime Security would extend monitoring of that fleet beyond the Mediterranean and Baltic to the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean, though officials say it will not be operational immediately and must first be discussed with member states.

Other measures appear aimed at reducing dependence on Washington. The EU envisions a "sovereign and secure" subsea cable between Greenland and the European continent, part of a broader pan-Arctic project linking Canada, Japan and South Korea, and the IRIS² satellite constellation to guarantee Arctic connectivity in competition with Elon Musk's Starlink. Brussels also insists that oil, coal and gas "must stay in the ground" — a point of divergence with the Trump administration, which has loosened environmental rules and wants to maximise extraction in the US Arctic.

## What to watch

The Russo-Chinese Arctic partnership is real but still incomplete. Asia Times cautioned that the Patrushev proposals "are still largely unimplemented plans," and that Arctic shipping "won't take over Asia-Europe routes any time soon" given ice, high insurance premiums, thin infrastructure and a short operating season. Traffic through Suez remains far larger than Arctic volumes.

Yet the direction is clear. In August, Chinese shipping company Sea Legend launched the first regular weekly container service across the Arctic, realising a long-held ambition, while South Korea and India have shown interest in the icy shortcut. The coming years will test whether Russian geography and Chinese capital can turn an ambitious vision into a working logistics system — and whether the West can muster the icebreakers, satellites and solidarity to keep pace in a region where the tectonic plates of geopolitics are colliding.
