# Democrats Ride Midterm Wave as Gerrymandering Fails and Jeffries Poised to Make History

Democrats won control of both the House and Senate in Tuesday night's midterm elections, overcoming an aggressive Republican redistricting campaign that President Donald Trump had personally championed. With Democrats set to take the House majority, Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries is now poised to become the first Black speaker of the House in U.S. history, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/04/g-s1-150030/hakeem-jeffries-speaker-of-the-house-midterms).

## A Strategy That Backfired

Republicans had braced for losses and, at Trump's urging, took the extraordinary step of redrawing congressional maps in the middle of the decade. The goal was to insulate the party's narrow House majority from an expected Democratic surge.

It did not work. Among the sweeping Democratic victories were wins in several districts Republicans had redrawn explicitly to favor themselves, [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/04/nx-s1-6028588/redistricting-efforts-failed-republicans). In Florida, multiple Democrats won in redrawn districts. In North Carolina, Democratic Rep. Don Davis held his 1st District seat even though the new map would have favored Trump by nearly 12 points. And in Alabama, Rep. Shomari Figures held a redrawn district that was tilted even more heavily toward the GOP.

The pattern extended to Texas, the first state to redistrict at Trump's urging, where several redrawn districts nonetheless fell to Democrats. The results suggest the national wave simply overwhelmed the structural advantage the new maps were designed to create.

## A Referendum on the President

Analysts framed the outcome as a decisive rebuke of the president. According to [NPR's election analysis](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/04/nx-s1-6022216/midterms-democrats-trump-republicans-analysis), a striking 62% of voters disapproved of Trump's job performance, and three-quarters said they were angry or dissatisfied with the country's direction. A plurality named the economy as their top issue, and two-thirds said Trump's policies had made economic conditions worse.

"This is a change election, and we understand as House Democrats that we need to deliver dramatic change," Jeffries said on election night. "Things are broken. We need a Congress that puts the American people first."

The Democratic coalition also showed signs of rebuilding. Latinos, who had drifted toward Trump in 2024, swung sharply back toward Democrats, a shift analysts attributed in part to dissatisfaction with aggressive deportation tactics. Roughly 60% of voters said they disapproved of Immigration and Customs Enforcement, including 68% of Hispanic respondents.

## The Arc of a Historic Rise

Jeffries' elevation to speaker would cap a nearly 14-year House tenure. First elected in 2013 to represent parts of Brooklyn, he rose from a rank-and-file member focused on criminal justice reform to a top lieutenant under former Speaker Nancy Pelosi, and then to minority leader in 2022 — becoming the first Black politician to lead either party in the House or Senate.

His record includes work on regulating stop-and-frisk searches as a New York state assemblyman and leading the bipartisan First Step Act, which reduced sentences for some drug crimes during Trump's first term. As speaker, he would be second in the presidential line of succession, after the vice president. The House is expected to hold its floor vote for speaker when the new Congress convenes on Jan. 3.

## What Comes Next

Democrats now face the challenge of governing under divided government for the final two years of Trump's term. They will lack a filibuster-proof Senate majority and are unlikely to find a willing negotiating partner in the White House or congressional Republicans. Even before the night ended, Trump posted an image of a red hat emblazoned with the word "VETO" on his social media platform.

That dynamic carries risk. Democrats were handed the keys to Congress, and voters will expect results — but the party's own favorability stood at just 47%, only modestly better than Republicans' 41%, according to the voter poll. The tension between base expectations and legislative reality will be one of the defining storylines of the new Congress.

For now, though, the night belonged to a party that overcame the map — and to a leader on the verge of a historic first.
