# China Says in Contact Over Possible China-US-Russia APEC Meeting

China's Foreign Ministry said on Wednesday that it is "maintaining communication with the relevant parties" regarding a possible trilateral meeting of the Chinese, Russian and American leaders — but stopped short of confirming that any such summit will take place.

The remark, delivered by spokesperson Mao Ning at the ministry's regular press conference in Beijing, came in response to a question from an Agence France-Presse reporter asking China to confirm a *Financial Times* report that Beijing, Moscow and Washington were working to arrange a meeting between Presidents Xi Jinping, Vladimir Putin and Donald Trump on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen later this month, according to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34403955).

"China is maintaining communication with the relevant parties regarding a China-Russia-US leaders' meeting," Mao said, in a carefully hedged formulation that acknowledged active coordination while leaving the format, timing and agenda of any gathering unconfirmed.

## A subtle shift in language

The response marks a noticeable change in tone from the ministry's earlier position. As recently as September 29, spokesperson Guo Jiakun said China had "no information to provide at present" when asked about the Kremlin's suggestion that the Shenzhen summit offered a good opportunity for a trilateral. On October 20, Mao gave the same answer when asked about Trump's positive stance toward such a meeting.

The move from a flat "no information" to "maintaining communication" suggests discussions are live but no agreement has been finalized — a classic piece of diplomatic hedging that neither confirms nor denies a summit while preserving room to maneuver.

## Why all three capitals are circling the idea

The trilateral concept has been floated repeatedly in recent months. Moscow has been its most active proponent. Kremlin foreign policy aide Yuri Ushakov first raised the idea publicly on September 1, saying Xi and Putin had discussed the possibility. At the Valdai Discussion Club on October 1, Putin expressed "full support" for a trilateral but said whether it happens "mainly depends on host China," noting that an agenda must first be agreed.

On November 3, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said there was a "general understanding" among participants in the potential meeting that it would be "appropriate and highly useful."

Washington, too, has signaled openness. Trump told reporters on October 19 that he would be willing to hold three-way arms-control talks with Xi and Putin, framing the idea around nuclear weapons and denuclearization while adding that the leaders could discuss "other things like trade, too." He has confirmed he will attend the APEC summit.

China, as the 2026 APEC host, controls both the agenda and the logistics of the leaders' meeting, scheduled for November 18–19 in Shenzhen. That hosting role is a central reason both Moscow and Washington have publicly deferred to Beijing on whether — and how — the gathering would take shape.

## Realistic expectations

Analysts broadly expect that if a trilateral does happen, it would more likely produce an informal exchange of positions than a binding agreement. Likely topics include Ukraine and European security, arms control and nuclear issues, Iran, energy, the global economy and the Korean Peninsula.

Beijing is also weighing its own interests. A trilateral would hand China a diplomatic highlight for its Shenzhen hosting duties, but Chinese officials are wary of being drawn into Ukraine mediation and appear to prefer avoiding a leading role before a settlement is in sight. Commentators have floated comparisons to the 1945 Yalta conference, though regional analysts dismiss the analogy, arguing that a three-way meeting would yield symbolic statements on regional stability rather than any order-reshaping arrangement.

The idea faces constraints on other fronts as well. Reporting cited by [the Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/73c1d1cc-6bb2-43a6-8532-7823e5c1a40b) has noted skepticism in some European and Japanese capitals about a possible informal "great-power condominium," and questions remain about whether the White House staff fully supports the format. Some observers expect Washington to expand Trump's trip into a broader Asia tour — adding stops in South Korea and Japan — to avoid the impression of a China-only visit.

## What to watch

The next two weeks will be decisive. A formal announcement from either China's Foreign Ministry or the White House before November 18 would clarify whether the trilateral is on, and in what format — a full three-way summit or a more limited pull-aside on the margins.

The key unanswered questions remain whether the meeting will be confirmed at all, whether Ukraine will be substantively discussed, and whether Beijing is willing to convert its hosting leverage into a concrete diplomatic deliverable. For now, China's carefully worded answer signals only that the door is open — not that anyone has walked through it.
