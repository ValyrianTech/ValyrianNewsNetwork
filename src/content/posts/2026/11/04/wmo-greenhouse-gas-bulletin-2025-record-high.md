# Greenhouse Gases Hit Record Highs, WMO Warns

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases reached unprecedented highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization said, warning that the planet is now locked into decades of additional warming. In its annual [Greenhouse Gas Bulletin](https://wmo.int/resources/publication-series/greenhouse-gas-bulletin/no-22), released on 27 October 2026, the UN agency reported that carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide all set new records — the latest signal that global efforts to curb emissions have yet to bend the curve.

## A Record Year, Again

The globally averaged surface concentration of carbon dioxide hit 426.8 parts per million in 2025, or 153% of the pre-industrial baseline of 1750, according to the [WMO press release](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high). Methane reached 1,948 parts per billion — 267% of the 1750 level — while nitrous oxide climbed to 339.1 ppb, or 126% above pre-industrial times.

CO2 remains the dominant driver, accounting for roughly two-thirds of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases. Its growth rate has tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from an average of about 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year over the decade from 2011 to 2020. Between 2024 and 2025, the concentration rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024.

"Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies."

Barrett stressed that the past 11 years have been the warmest on record and that the trend is set to continue. "CO2 has a very long life-time and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries."

## Why the Slight Slowdown Isn't Good News

The more modest 2024–2025 increase offered a rare note of encouragement — but one that experts were quick to qualify. WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed it to the recovery of land carbon sinks and reduced fire emissions in 2025, following the strong 2023/2024 El Niño that had dried vegetation and fueled wildfires.

"However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," Tarasova said. "We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026 because of fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño which will impact land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024."

A central point of the Bulletin is the distinction between concentrations and emissions. The report measures the accumulated stock of gas in the atmosphere, not new annual emissions. About half of the CO2 emitted each year remains in the atmosphere, while the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans. Even if emissions were frozen tomorrow, the CO2 already aloft would keep trapping heat.

The scale of the shift is stark. When the Bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 level was 377.5 ppm — some 49 ppm lower than today. NOAA's Annual Greenhouse Gas Index shows that radiative forcing from long-lived greenhouse gases rose 56% between 1990 and 2025, with CO2 responsible for about 81% of that increase.

## The Hidden Risks of the Energy Transition

The Bulletin flagged two less abundant but potent trace gases tied to the shift toward cleaner energy. Sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), used as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, has an atmospheric lifetime of more than 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO2. Its increase in 2025 was a record high.

Molecular hydrogen (H2) is not itself a greenhouse gas, but it influences atmospheric chemistry and has a strong indirect warming effect. Its concentration rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024, and leakage from production facilities, pipelines, storage tanks and fuel cells is a growing concern.

Both gases illustrate that decarbonization carries its own climate risks if not carefully managed. "Careful monitoring of the composition of Earth's atmosphere is essential to detect, understand and prevent unintended consequences of these changes," the WMO said.

## What It Means for COP31

The Bulletin is timed as scientific input to the COP31 UN climate summit, which opens in Antalya, Türkiye, on 9 November and runs through 20 November 2026. Negotiators there will seek to turn climate commitments into concrete action — a task made harder by the widening gap between rising concentrations and the steep cuts scientists say are needed.

As [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779336/article/2026-10-27/une-nouvelle-annee-un-nouveau-record-la-concentration-gaz-effet-de-serre-atteint) reported, relaying the AFP wire, the UN agency's findings underscore how far the world remains from stabilizing the climate. The research context points to estimates that emissions would need to fall by roughly 6% by 2035 to be consistent with stated goals.

## What to Watch

Two things will shape the next data point. First, the projected El Niño is likely to weaken land carbon sinks again, pushing 2026 concentrations higher and complicating any apparent improvement in the growth rate. Second, the resilience of ocean CO2 uptake is increasingly in question as the climate warms.

For now, the message from Geneva is unambiguous: every year of inaction adds to a burden that will persist for generations. The record set in 2025 will not stand for long.
