# Wang Yi Urges China-US Cooperation on AI Governance

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi called on China and the United States to jointly develop and govern artificial intelligence, arguing that the world's two leading AI powers have both the capacity and the responsibility to keep the technology under human control. The remarks, delivered during a meeting in Xiong'an New Area in Hebei province, signal Beijing's push for bilateral engagement on emerging AI rules at a moment of intense technological competition between the two countries.

According to the [Chinese Foreign Ministry](https://www.fmprc.gov.cn/wjbzhd/202610/t20261023_12046093.shtml), Wang made the comments on October 23 during a meeting with John L. Thornton, co-chair of the Board of Trustees of the Asia Society, and Thomas L. Friedman, a New York Times columnist. The two Americans were in China for the 2026 Global Governance Forum (Xiong'an), held October 23–25 and hosted by the Chinese People's Institute of Foreign Affairs.

## A Call for Shared Rules

Wang framed AI as a shared challenge that demands cooperation rather than only risk mitigation. "As first-tier AI countries, China and the U.S. have the capacity and the responsibility to jointly develop and manage AI well, ensuring its development remains under human control and benefits the people," he said, per the official readout. "We must not only guard against risks but also carry out cooperation, jointly discussing globally applicable rules and standards, so that AI serves to promote common prosperity and safeguard common security."

The foreign minister said China advocates that AI develop in what he described as an "inclusive, beneficial and good-oriented manner." He also invoked the Global Governance Initiative that President Xi Jinping proposed in September 2025, describing it as an important public good widely supported by the international community, and cast the Xiong'an forum as a venue for building consensus on reforming the global governance system.

## The American Delegation's Message

Per the same readout, Thornton and Friedman congratulated Xi on his recent successful visit to the United States and said that the core of a constructive strategic-stability relationship is stability — and that the greatest risk to that stability is the malicious use of AI. They expressed hope that the two sides would deepen cooperation on the issue and provide an important public good to the world. They also noted international attention on China's upcoming hosting of the APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting and voiced hope that Beijing would use the occasion to play a unique and positive role in addressing global challenges.

## Friedman's Firsthand Account

In a [New York Times opinion column](https://cn.nytimes.com/opinion/20261103/us-china-ai-manufacturing-exports-weapons/) recounting the meeting, Friedman wrote that he was struck by Wang's explicit endorsement of a multilateral AI safety mechanism. "In the AI era, global stability will increasingly require a relationship of assured mutual collaboration between the world's two AI superpowers," Friedman wrote.

He argued that Beijing may now be more eager than President Donald Trump to discuss an AI regulatory framework, noting that Trump told the United Nations in September that the U.S. "rejects any attempt to build a globalist plan to control AI." Friedman predicted that 2027 could be the year both countries fully grasp the need for coordinated AI model controls, though he cautioned that progress may require a large but not apocalyptic AI-driven incident to force cooperation.

The columnist also pointed to shared anxieties about malicious actors — criminals, scammers and hackers empowered by cheap AI and drones — as a rare area of convergent interest. He cited China's State Security Minister Chen Yixin, who warned in September 2026 that emerging AI technology poses a "direct threat" to China's political, institutional and ideological security and cautioned about "low-cost, batch-scale" malicious use of generative AI.

## A Broader Governance Push

Wang's remarks extend a wider Chinese effort to shape global AI norms. In July 2026, China launched the Shanghai-based World AI Cooperation Organization with 29 founding member countries — an intergovernmental body positioning Beijing as a convenor of global AI rules outside the United Nations system, as [Caixin Global reported](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-07-17/china-launches-shanghai-based-ai-governance-body-with-29-founding-nations-102465524.html).

That multilateral track runs in parallel with tightening domestic controls. In late October 2026, China issued draft rules giving authorities new powers to track or restrict exports of certain advanced AI systems, and in early November it unveiled an 18-point plan to protect workers from AI-related job losses. The dual approach — openness abroad, control at home — illustrates how Beijing is trying to position itself as both a global rule-shaper and a guardian of domestic stability.

## Analysis: Converging Interests, Diverging Systems

The Wang intervention comes as Washington and Beijing remain strategic competitors on AI, with the U.S. leading on frontier models and China leveraging open-weight models and its manufacturing base. Commentary has likened the dynamic to a Cold War-style prisoner's dilemma, in which fear of losing competitive edge keeps both sides accelerating. Wang's appeal attempts to reframe that race around a shared interest in preventing catastrophic misuse.

Yet significant obstacles remain. The two governments define "harmful" AI differently, reflecting divergent political systems — a gap Friedman highlighted as central to how each side approaches cooperation. Trump's stated rejection of a "globalist" AI control plan contrasts with what Friedman describes as Beijing's relative eagerness to discuss a regulatory framework. No direct U.S. government response to Wang's specific remarks was immediately available.

## What's Next

Attention now turns to whether Wang's overture translates into concrete bilateral engagement. China's upcoming hosting of the APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting offers a potential stage for further signals, while the question of whether Washington will reciprocate remains open. For now, the foreign minister's remarks mark one of Beijing's clearest public calls for the two AI superpowers to co-author the rules of the road — a proposal whose reception in Washington will shape the next phase of the AI rivalry.
