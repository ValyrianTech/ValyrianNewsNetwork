# Belgium's Federal Crisis Deepens as De Wever Resigns

Belgium has been plunged into a deepening federal political crisis after Prime Minister Bart De Wever formally submitted his government's resignation to King Filip, following the collapse of negotiations over the 2027 federal budget. The King is holding the resignation in suspense and is consulting the leaders of the coalition parties over several days before deciding whether to accept it — a decision expected to crystallise when he receives De Wever again on Friday.

## A resignation held in suspense

The drama began in the small hours of Monday 2 November, when the federal inner cabinet — the *kernkabinet* — broke up without a budget agreement. De Wever, leader of the Flemish nationalist N-VA, travelled to the Royal Palace that morning and spoke with the King for more than an hour and a half before offering his resignation, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/short/bart-de-wever-a-bien-remis-sa-demission-au-roi-6782). He made no statement to the press.

Rather than accept the resignation outright, the Palace opted for the standard Belgian constitutional device of holding the decision "in deliberation." The King said he regretted the absence of an agreement and warned that the country could not afford a prolonged political crisis, calling on all parties to put the general interest and a willingness to compromise first. His consultations were spread across the week: Les Engagés and CD&V on Tuesday, Vooruit and MR on Wednesday, N-VA on Thursday, before a fresh audience with the Prime Minister at 10:30 on Friday.

## Wednesday: the day of the "difficult clients"

Wednesday's audiences were the most closely watched, because they involved the two parties that VRT NWS journalists had dubbed "the difficult clients" of the budget talks — the Flemish socialists of Vooruit and the Francophone liberals of MR.

After an audience lasting roughly one hour and twenty minutes, Vooruit chairman Conner Rousseau emerged to confirm that his party still wants a deal — but on its own terms. "We want an agreement, but it must be just and everyone must do their part," he said, [according to VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/vooruitvoorzitter-rousseau-bevestigt-dat-hij-akkoord-wil-na-ond~1793247532380/). He declined to elaborate further, telling reporters he had informed the King that he would say little more publicly. "We're going to get back to work, now," he added — a deliberately restrained tone that appeared designed to avoid inflaming an already tense standoff.

MR chairman Georges-Louis Bouchez, by contrast, offered no comment at all after his roughly one-hour audience, keeping the press at arm's length. Earlier in the week he had struck a more combative note on social media, arguing that the budget was "a purely economic problem" and that, with inflation high and growth weak, public spending simply had to fall. He had also pledged restraint during the King's consultations so as not to jeopardise a possible deal.

## The €10 billion fault line

The crisis is, at its root, a budget crisis. The coalition must find roughly €10 billion of consolidation for 2027 against a backdrop of high public debt, growth of only about 0.6 percent a year, and rapidly rising social and ageing costs.

The central fault line runs between those who want extra revenue from large fortunes and those who insist only spending cuts can reassure the markets. Les Engagés and Vooruit have pressed for contributions from "the broadest shoulders," including a reduction in tax breaks — notably €70 million in benefits for professional footballers. MR and, to a degree, N-VA argue the overall tax burden is already too high and that expenditure must come down.

The divisions have grown personal. Maxime Prévot, the Les Engagés vice-premier, pointedly blamed "Les Francs Borains" — a reference to the football club chaired by Bouchez — for blocking the football tax measure. CD&V chairman Sammy Mahdi arrived at the Palace on Tuesday visibly furious, saying there was "no margin for games" and warning that Belgium was drifting towards "Greek-style" conditions. Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem, also of CD&V, echoed the warning in an interview with VRT's *Terzake*, cautioning that "we are closer to Greek conditions than we think."

## The emergency-budget spectre

With no full 2027 budget in sight, Belgium is preparing to run on "provisional twelfths" — a mechanism under which the state may spend one-twelfth of the previous year's expenditure each month. It would be the second consecutive year of such an emergency regime, after an agreement in late November 2025 forced roughly three months of provisional twelfths at the start of 2026. Van Peteghem has said he is preparing the mechanism again, calling it "particularly regrettable," while De Wever had only weeks earlier described a repeat as "not an option."

Markets have so far stayed calm, but the pressure is real. Belgium's ten-year borrowing rate stood at about 4.38 percent on Monday, its highest in fourteen years. On Tuesday the OECD again urged Belgium to put its public finances in order, and business leaders warned of the cost of instability. "This political crisis comes at a particularly bad moment," said Frank Beckx of the employers' federation Voka, noting that industrial capacity utilisation is at 73 percent and that construction is struggling.

## A parallel flashpoint: the home-visits bill

As if to underline that parliamentary business continues even amid the crisis, the Chamber is still scheduled to vote on Thursday 5 November on a controversial "home visits" bill. The legislation, championed by Asylum and Migration Minister Anneleen Van Bossuyt (N-VA), would allow police, with prior authorisation from an investigating judge, to enter a private home to arrest an irregular foreign national who refuses removal and "poses a danger to public order or national security."

Les Engagés, historically opposed to a similar 2017 proposal, are expected to back the text out of government solidarity despite internal unease, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/visites-domiciliaires-malgre-la-crise-au-sein-de-l-arizona-les-engages-devraient-voter-en-faveur-du-texte-controverse-ce-jeudi-11810436). The bill has drawn fierce criticism from the judiciary, human-rights groups and the opposition, and the Brussels Parliament passed a resolution against it in October. More than 55 demonstrations are planned across Belgium on Wednesday, and Ecolo MP Claire Hugon Lecharlier has demanded the vote be postponed, arguing it would be "politically irresponsible" to carry on as if nothing had happened. Chamber President Peter De Roover has insisted the House remains master of its own agenda.

## What happens next

Everything now hinges on Friday, when the King receives De Wever once more. He may accept the resignation — pushing Belgium towards early elections and a formation process that could take six to eight months — or send the parties back to the negotiating table in the hope that a budget deal can still be salvaged.

The political class is hedging. Prévot put the odds of new elections at "50/50," while Van Peteghem argued that a fresh ballot would solve nothing and merely prolong the uncertainty. The opposition is circling: PS leader Paul Magnette declared his party would not "help a dying government out of trouble," and Vlaams Belang called the impasse a "personal political failure" of the Prime Minister.

For ordinary Belgians, the mood is weary. Voters interviewed by VRT NWS described the parties as "a bunch of children" and accused them of putting party interest above the public good. Whether the King can force a compromise from a coalition that has spent the week trading barbs remains the central question — and the answer, one way or another, is due by Friday.
