# Ex-China Eastern Chairman Liu Shaoyong Sentenced to 14 Years

A court in northeastern China has sentenced Liu Shaoyong, the former chairman of China Eastern Air Holding Company, to 14 years in prison for accepting more than 67.5 million yuan (about $9.5 million) in bribes over a two-decade career at the helm of two of the country's largest state airlines, according to state media.

The Shenyang Intermediate People's Court in Liaoning Province handed down the first-instance verdict on November 3, [The Paper reported](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34395921). Alongside the prison term, the court imposed a fine of 4 million yuan and ordered the confiscation of all illicit proceeds and their accrued interest, to be turned over to the state treasury. The [official Xinhua News Agency](https://www.xinhuanet.com/20261103/ba2f6cdb7d9f4919a63cb990ab2427b8/c.html) carried an identical account of the ruling.

The case marks one of the most prominent convictions yet in Beijing's sustained anti-corruption drive targeting senior executives of state-owned enterprises (SOEs), and it brings down a figure once celebrated for rescuing China Eastern during the 2008 global financial crisis.

## What the Court Found

According to the verdict, between 2002 and 2023 Liu exploited his positions to provide benefits to companies and individuals in matters of enterprise operation, project contracting, and route management, accepting property worth more than 67.5 million yuan in return.

The court traced the misconduct across a succession of senior roles: general manager of China Eastern Airlines Co.; general manager of China Southern Air Holding Co. and chairman of China Southern Airlines Co.; and finally general manager and chairman of China Eastern Air Holding Co. while also serving as chairman of its listed carrier.

Judges described the bribery sum as "particularly huge" — a legal threshold that ordinarily points toward the harshest penalties. Yet the court opted for leniency, citing several mitigating factors: the offenses included uncompleted, or attempted, bribery; after being brought to justice Liu truthfully confessed and proactively disclosed most of the bribery facts that investigators did not already know; he pleaded guilty and expressed remorse; and he actively returned illicit gains, with all funds, property, and interest fully recovered.

The trial itself opened on July 24, 2026, at the same Shenyang court. Prosecutors presented evidence, the defense cross-examined it, and Liu made a final statement in which he pleaded guilty and expressed remorse before more than 30 observers, including national legislators, political advisers, and members of the public.

## From Star Pilot to Fallen Executive

Liu's trajectory made his downfall a notable one. Born in November 1958 in Luoshan County, Henan Province, he joined the civil aviation industry in 1978 as a pilot and rose to become a special-grade pilot, the highest rating in China's aviation system. He moved into management in the mid-1990s and, by December 2000, was appointed general manager of China Eastern Airlines Co. Just two years later he was named a deputy director of the Civil Aviation Administration of China, reaching vice-ministerial rank before the age of 44.

In 2004 he took the helm of China Southern Air Holding Co., and in December 2008 he returned to China Eastern in what state media long described as a rescue mission amid the global financial crisis. There he presided over what was billed as a "golden decade" of consecutive annual profits, a record that cemented his reputation as one of the industry's most capable operators, [Jiemian News recalled](https://m.jiemian.com/article/13840289.html) in its coverage of his expulsion from the Party.

He formally became chairman and Party secretary of China Eastern Air Holding Co. in December 2016 and stepped down in July 2022.

## The Anti-Corruption Arc

The case followed the standard pathway for SOE corruption in China: a disciplinary review by the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection (CCDI), an investigation by the National Supervisory Commission, indictment by prosecutors, and a designated public trial.

Liu was placed under investigation on June 28, 2025, [Caixin reported](https://companies.caixin.com/2025-06-28/102335935.html), a move that stunned an industry in which he was known as a perennial "firefighter" brought in to steady struggling carriers. On January 4, 2026 — the first working day of the new year — the CCDI announced his "double expulsion" from the Party and from public office, alongside three other senior cadres, a piece of timing that underlined the campaign's symbolic continuity.

The disciplinary findings went considerably broader than the criminal charge. The CCDI said Liu had "lost his ideals and convictions," resisted organizational review, and engaged in superstitious activities; accepted banned banquets and golf outings; took money to help others secure employment; and used his influence to benefit relatives' business dealings, including in private equity, while improperly holding shares in unlisted companies. It accused him of "trading power for money" through project contracting, flight management, and aircraft leasing. The notice also invoked a familiar formula, stating that his conduct did not abate after the 18th Party Congress of 2012 — the line Beijing uses to signal that misconduct continued despite years of warnings.

## Analysis: Why 14 Years

At roughly 67.5 million yuan, Liu's bribe total is large in absolute terms but moderate compared with the biggest "tiger" cases, some of which have involved hundreds of millions of yuan and drawn life sentences or suspended death penalties. A 14-year term is broadly consistent with Chinese sentencing patterns for defendants of this scale who cooperate fully, confess, and return all assets.

The verdict's explicit emphasis on "attempted bribery" and complete asset recovery provided the legal basis for a sentence below the maximum. That structure is procedurally routine under Chinese law, but it also serves a communicative purpose: rewarding cooperation while still delivering a substantial punishment.

The broader significance lies in the target. Liu was not a peripheral figure but a celebrated, vice-ministerial-level operator who led two of China's "big three" airlines. His conviction reinforces the message that no reputation, however decorated, insulates a senior executive from scrutiny — a theme Beijing has pressed hard in its post-2022 wave of SOE cases.

## What to Watch

The verdict is a first-instance ruling, leaving open the possibility of an appeal; none had been reported as of the announcement. Also unreported are the identities and fate of any bribe-payers or co-conspirators, and the precise disposition of recovered assets — details that in comparable cases have sometimes emerged later through follow-on prosecutions.

For China Eastern, the case closes a chapter on a leadership era that ended in 2022, when Wang Zhiqing took over the chairmanship. For the wider anti-corruption campaign, it offers another data point on how Beijing is handling the sprawling, high-profile cases that have come to define its relationship with the state corporate sector — and a reminder that the reckoning for even the most storied careers can arrive years after the applause has faded.
