# Chinese Cities Open Government Toilets to the Public

A growing number of Chinese localities are moving beyond holiday-only trials and permanently opening the internal toilets of government offices, public institutions and businesses to the general public, part of a low-cost push to fill gaps in urban public services. According to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34397296), the trend spans regions from Anhui and Zhejiang to Xinjiang and Guangdong, as authorities look to squeeze more use out of existing facilities rather than build new ones.

The shift reflects a broader rethinking of how cities deliver basic amenities — treating idle internal resources as a shared public good rather than a private convenience.

## From Building New to Using What Exists

The practice builds on China's national "toilet revolution," launched around 2015–2017 with an initial focus on tourist sites and rural areas. That campaign aimed to dramatically expand and upgrade public and rural sanitation facilities. The current wave represents a further stage: instead of building, authorities are opening doors that were previously closed to the public.

Building new public toilets is expensive, slow and often hampered by site constraints in dense urban areas. Opening existing internal toilets, officials argue, densifies the service network quickly and at far lower cost — a practical response to the perennial urban frustration of not being able to find a toilet when one is needed.

## Four Regions, One Approach

In Hefei, the capital of Anhui province, the city's urban management department announced on October 29 that street-front shopping malls, enterprises and public institutions in the Hefei High-tech Zone would open their internal restrooms to the public. Uniform "Social Public Toilet Open to the Public" signs are posted at entrances, and all directly managed toilets have been geo-tagged on Amap, Baidu Maps and Didi so residents can find the nearest facility with one-click navigation.

What began with a first batch of 22 signed-up facilities has now expanded to 140 participating units. To ease the added cleaning and maintenance burden, the district distributes disinfection and consumable supplies to participants and integrates them into a unified "city-friendly shared environment" system. As The Paper put it, turning "internal facilities" into "public resources" is not only an effective tapping of existing potential but also a beneficial attempt at co-governed, shared urban management.

In Huzhou, Zhejiang province, the city's environmental sanitation center has continued deepening the opening of internal toilets at government institutions, communities and state-owned enterprises. As of July, 227 units across the city had opened their facilities to the public, according to The Paper. The sanitation management center itself was the initiating unit of the shared-toilet action and among the first demonstration windows, long serving sanitation workers, passing residents and tourists. Residents and visitors can use the "Huzhou Public Toilets" WeChat mini-program to search all 227 shared toilets, view locations and opening hours, and navigate directly.

In Wusu, Xinjiang, authorities launched a "Toilet Alliance" to address an uneven distribution and shortage of public toilets. Fifty-one street-front party and government organs, supermarkets, hospitals and other units opened their internal toilets free of charge, with signs reading "This unit's toilet is open to the public" hung at entrances. Compared with building new public toilets — described as time-consuming, costly and constrained by available sites — the alliance revitalizes existing resources, improves quality and efficiency at low cost, and quickly fills the public-service gap.

Local resident Zhang Zhiyong told Wusu media that finding a toilet used to be troublesome when out and about. "Now that all units' toilets are open to the public, it's very convenient," he said.

In Luhe County, part of Shanwei in Guangdong province, authorities have converted internal restrooms previously dispersed across government units, villages, communities and businesses into publicly accessible resources. The county has cumulatively opened more than 480 shared toilets of various types. In May, the county government office described a "whole-area resource sharing" model that breaks down usage barriers between government units and the public, and between businesses and non-consuming visitors — turning toilets from "independent resources" into "public welfare."

Key sites have been equipped with hand soap, toilet paper and other amenities, meeting management requirements of "no standing water on floors, no odor indoors, no damaged facilities, no cleaning dead zones," with regular inspections and maintenance.

## Policy Momentum Builds

The trend has been formalized in local policy. In October 2025, Shanghai's greening and city appearance administration, together with the city's commerce, transport, and culture and tourism commissions, issued [official guidance](https://lhsr.sh.gov.cn/srgl/20251117/88ac0879-2a73-405a-856c-9fd90441299f.html) directing districts to encourage social toilets to open wherever conditions allow — "应开尽开," or open wherever possible. The policy targets high-footfall areas such as major commercial districts, large malls, tourist sites and transport hubs, as well as blind spots in public-toilet coverage, and offers pilot subsidies, facility-renovation assistance and management guidance.

Earlier still, Suqian in Jiangsu province formed a "Toilet Opening Alliance" with more than 230 units, achieving near-blanket availability by distance — reportedly within 100 meters of commercial and recreation areas, 300 meters of residential zones and 500 meters along main roads, [China Jiangsu Net reported](https://jsnews.jschina.com.cn/jsyw/202411/t20241120_s673d1641e4b06023c1904ee9.shtml) in late 2024.

## Challenges and What to Watch

The approach is not without friction. Opening internal facilities adds cleaning, maintenance and supply costs for participating units, which local governments address through supplies, subsidies and recognition. Hefei, for instance, coupled its distribution of sanitation supplies with efforts to shift units from "passive response" to "active participation." Privacy and security concerns for government buildings, and maintaining consistent hygiene standards, are the natural follow-up questions that will shape how far the model spreads.

The shift from holiday-only trials to routine, permanent opening signals the institutionalization of the practice. It aligns with national "people's city" governance messaging — the idea that urban services should be built around residents' needs — and is likely to feature in broader urban-services planning.

For now, the message from Huzhou captures the ambition: from government compounds to community sites, from central urban areas to county townships, each open restroom door, in the words of local officials, displays a city's inclusiveness and warmth. Whether that warmth can be sustained as maintenance costs mount will determine if the open-door policy becomes a permanent fixture of Chinese urban life.
