# Brussels Flight Path Health Risk Grows Under Route Crucke

A growing number of Brussels residents living beneath the approach path to Brussels Airport are having their health put at risk, according to an [RTBF investigation](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-sante-de-toujours-plus-de-bruxellois-survoles-mise-en-danger-mais-quel-est-le-probleme-de-brussels-airport-11798700) published on 23 October 2026. The probe centres on the controversial RNP-07L approach route — widely dubbed "Route Crucke" — introduced in July 2025, which has shifted the noise and pollution burden onto densely populated, less affluent neighbourhoods of north-west Brussels. An estimated 450,000 residents are now affected, and residents say the unrelenting overflights are taking a measurable toll on their health.

## A Route That Changed the Map

Until July 2025, runway 07L was used only residually and was considered dangerous by Skeyes, Belgium's air traffic controller. When works on other runways forced landings onto 07L during easterly winds, Skeyes flagged "urgent safety concerns" with the existing VOR approach — warning in a February letter to the minister that pilots executing a tight turn in descent were sometimes dropping below authorised safety thresholds.

Its solution was an RNAV (RNP) approach — a GPS-guided straight-line trajectory that eliminates the turn but shifts the entire flight path toward central Brussels. Federal Mobility Minister Jean-Luc Crucke (Les Engagés) validated the change on Skeyes' advice. What Skeyes did not emphasise, RTBF notes, is that European rules require runways to be *equipped* with the system, not that the route be *used*. A study commissioned by the minister from consultancy CGX now estimates that more than 450,000 Brussels residents are impacted by 07L — well above the 271,483 figure in an earlier ULB study, whose author, geographer Frédéric Dobruszkes, concedes the CGX model is "closer to reality."

## A Burden Borne by Poorer Neighbourhoods

The overflown zones — Dilbeek, Berchem-Sainte-Agathe, Ganshoren, Molenbeek, Koekelberg and Jette — are dense and relatively disadvantaged. Dobruszkes, who for two decades has overlaid flight paths with population density and unemployment data, argues that wealthier areas mount legal challenges far more effectively. "It's easier to overfly many poor people than few rich ones," he said, raising the sensitive question of whether the pattern amounts to "social racism" — though he stresses he lacks evidence of intent.

Crucke rejected the charge forcefully: "This idea is false, absolutely false!" He countered that Brussels Airport "supports more than 80,000 families" as Belgium's second economic engine.

## What the Residents Report

For Frédérik, a Molenbeek artist who captured videos of aircraft passing just above his home, the strain is already physical. "All the doctors I consulted told me my body reacts as if it were in a state of war," he said. "Every two minutes. It's unlivable!" On days when easterly winds push traffic onto 07L, he compares the frequency of aircraft to the city's buses — "if only we had a stop to get on," he remarked bitterly.

Medical authorities have long warned that repeated exposure to significant noise raises the risk of hypertension and cardiovascular problems, a link recognised by the World Health Organization. The RTBF investigation documents residents across the overflown communes describing disrupted sleep and chronic stress long after a given overflight has passed.

## Why 07L? The Capacity Question

Runway usage has flipped dramatically. In 2024, runway 01 handled 8,157 landings versus 3,184 on 07L. In 2025, as the RNAV approach took hold after July, that became 13,366 landings on 01 against 6,078 on 07L. In the first nine months of 2026, runway 01 handled only 5,957 against 9,189 on 07L — the first time 07L has been used more than 01.

RTBF points to a structural explanation. Runway 01 crosses all other runways, and because aircraft can never cross, using it cuts airport capacity from 67 movements per hour (with 07L) to 54 (with 01) — a roughly 25% reduction. That is hard to sustain given Brussels Airport's plan to grow traffic by more than 75% by 2040, from 200,000 movements in 2025 to more than 350,000. Skeyes and Brussels Airport declined all interview requests. Crucke insisted capacity is not Skeyes' concern: "Their only preoccupation must be safety," he said — while acknowledging the controller retains latitude over runway choice and should now be held to account for it.

## Measures With Limited Impact

On 1 October 2026, Crucke presented a package of measures, but only two took effect immediately on 07L: aligning aircraft at 4,000 feet instead of 3,000 feet, and continuous rather than stepped descent. Other measures — raising the wind threshold from 7 to 8 knots (matching Charleroi), cutting wind-change anticipation from three to four hours down to under 30 minutes, and tighter gust handling — remain under study.

Acoustician Philippe Brux, CEO of the consultancy Modyva, was candid about the limits. Continuous descent already occurs in 80% of landings, he noted, and the overall gain would be just 1 to 2 decibels. "It's not much, but it's something." Experts estimate the measures will cut landings on 07L and 01 by only around 5%.

According to [BRUZZ](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/politiek/vliegroute-rnp-07l-stilzwijgend-behouden-voor-crucke-niets-doen-het-makkelijkst-2026-11-02), the RNP-07L "test phase" formally ended on 31 October 2026, yet the route has been retained in Belgium's aeronautical information publication (AIP) without an end date. The minister's spokesperson confirmed the route is maintained because Skeyes presented no alternative. Lies Ryckeboer of the residents' group Free Air 4 Brussels put it bluntly: "For Minister Crucke, doing nothing is easiest."

## The Political Fault Line

Reducing overflight over Brussels means increasing it over Flanders — something the Flemish government, the airport's main shareholder, calls "inimaginable." Flemish Budget Minister Ben Weyts warned that raising the wind threshold "will only make things worse... It's really no longer possible." Crucke, for his part, insists he has the power to change the wind norm unilaterally and need not ask anyone — but expects the Flemish side may respond with a conflict-of-interest procedure. On 23 October, the Flemish government duly invoked a conflict of interest against the federal measures; Brussels called the move "unfounded" and wants the matter heard at the Comité de concertation on 16 November 2026.

Meanwhile, legal action is mounting. The Brussels Region has filed a cease-and-desist action against the Belgian State over 07L, joined by nine communes, with a hearing set for 11 January 2027. Separately, 42 families from Dilbeek, Lennik and Roosdaal are suing the Belgian State, a case opened to Brussels residents that will be pleaded on 8 February 2027. No binding ruling is expected before 2027.

## What to Watch

The central unresolved tension is whether the airport's growth ambitions are structurally compatible with protecting residents' health. The Comité de concertation on 16 November will test whether the wind-norm change survives Flemish resistance. A new air navigation law is targeted for the first quarter of 2027, and the minister has asked Skeyes and the air transport directorate to study an alternation between the current straight route and a slightly curved variant using GPS. For the roughly 450,000 people below the flight path, the question is whether any of it arrives soon enough.
