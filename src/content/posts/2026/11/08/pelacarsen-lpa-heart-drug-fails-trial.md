# Heart Drug Pelacarsen Fails, a Blow to Lp(a) Approach

An experimental heart drug that doctors had hoped would become the next major advance in preventing heart attacks and strokes has failed a pivotal test, researchers reported Saturday. The medicine, called pelacarsen, sharply lowered a genetically determined particle in the blood long linked to cardiovascular risk — yet it did not protect patients in one of the most closely watched trials in cardiology.

"I was shocked. I had high hopes for success," said Steven Nissen, a cardiologist at the Cleveland Clinic who led the study, in an interview with [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/07/nx-s1-6026379/lpa-drug-lipoproteina-heart-attack). "Cardiovascular disease is still the leading cause of death in developed countries. And so we really were hoping to find additional therapies that would reduce risk."

## A Target With High Hopes

The trial aimed at patients who seem to do everything right. They keep cholesterol and blood pressure low, eat well, maintain a healthy weight and exercise regularly — yet many still suffer heart attacks and strokes. A large share carry elevated blood levels of lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), a cholesterol-carrying particle thought to raise heart disease risk.

"About 20% of the global population have elevated levels. And that's about a billion people. So it's a common disorder," Nissen said.

Until recently, doctors had no drugs that could lower Lp(a). Pelacarsen, developed by Ionis Pharmaceuticals and licensed to Novartis, is a once-monthly injection designed to suppress production of the particle. In the Lp(a)HORIZON trial, researchers gave 8,350 adults with established heart disease either the drug or a placebo. According to the [ClinicalTrials.gov record](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04023552), the Phase 3 study enrolled 8,323 participants and was completed in July 2026.

## Dramatic Reduction, No Benefit

Pelacarsen did what it was designed to do: it suppressed Lp(a) levels by about 78%. But after five years, that steep drop did not translate into fewer cardiovascular complications.

"Very disappointing, terribly disappointing," Nissen said. He presented the first details at an American Heart Association meeting in Chicago on Saturday, with fuller specifics published in the [New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2610159) and NEJM Evidence.

Novartis, which sponsored the study, said the research "provides meaningful evidence" about the relationship between Lp(a) and heart disease and may "help inform future approaches to cardiovascular management." The company added that progress in science depends on pursuing challenging questions and that such studies "play a crucial role in moving the field forward, even if the results are not what we had hoped for."

The failure is stunning because expectations were so high. "I think it's a big deal because the expectation was so high that this would end up being positive," said Harlan Krumholz, a Yale University cardiologist. "And so it was really jarring to see this."

## What Went Wrong?

Researchers are now trying to explain the disappointment, and several hypotheses have emerged.

One possibility is that high Lp(a) does not actually cause heart attacks and strokes but is instead a marker for something else that drives risk. Another is timing. "Maybe you need to treat longer. You may need to treat earlier, before people have advanced hardening of the arteries," said Roger Blumenthal, a cardiologist at Johns Hopkins University.

A third is the patient population itself. "Maybe a higher Lp(a) should have been required for entry into the trial," said David Maron, a Stanford University cardiologist and president of the American Society for Preventive Cardiology. Other experts noted that patients were already on optimized standard care, making any incremental benefit harder to detect.

It is also possible the problem is specific to pelacarsen — that another Lp(a)-lowering drug might succeed where this one did not.

## A Class Under Scrutiny

Several other drugmakers, including Amgen and Eli Lilly, are testing alternative Lp(a)-lowering medicines using different technologies, and results are expected over the next few years. The outcome has weighed on the whole field, lowering confidence across a class of drugs that analysts had once projected could generate billions of dollars in annual sales.

Yet cardiologists caution that the science of Lp(a) is far from settled. "The take-home message is the story's not over," said Karol Watson, a cardiologist at the University of California, Los Angeles. "It's kind of just starting."

## What to Watch

For the roughly one billion people worldwide with elevated Lp(a), the result offers no immediate new option — no targeted treatment for the condition is yet approved. The next milestones will be the readouts from rival programs at Amgen and Eli Lilly, which could either revive the Lp(a) hypothesis or confirm that lowering the particle alone is not enough. Either way, pelacarsen's failure has reshaped one of cardiology's most anticipated quests: turning a powerful biomarker into a therapy that actually saves lives.
