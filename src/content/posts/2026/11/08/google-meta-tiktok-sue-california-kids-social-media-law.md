# Google, Meta and TikTok Sue to Block California Kids Law

Google, Meta and TikTok filed separate federal lawsuits on November 4 seeking to block a California law that bars addictive social media features for children under 16, setting up a high-stakes collision between child online-safety regulation and the First Amendment. The suits, first reported by [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/news/12108703/google-meta-and-tiktok-sue-to-block-california-law-curbing-addictive-social-media-for-kids), target Assembly Bill 1709, which is scheduled to take effect January 1, 2027.

## What the Law Does

Signed by Gov. Gavin Newsom in September 2026, AB 1709 prohibits social media platforms from offering users under 16 "psychologically exploitative" or addictive features—specifically personalized, algorithmic feeds based on a user's history and profile, as well as autoplay for videos. Platforms that keep those features for minors would be required to delete the affected accounts.

According to [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/05/meta-google-and-tiktok-sue-california-ag-over-teen-social-media-law/), noncompliant platforms could face fines of up to $50,000 per affected user under 16. The measure was authored by Assemblymember Josh Lowenthal (D-Long Beach) as part of a broader child online-safety package.

## Where the Suits Landed

Google, a unit of Alphabet, filed in federal court in San Jose; Meta sued in San Francisco; and TikTok, owned by China's ByteDance, filed in Los Angeles. California Attorney General Rob Bonta—whose office enforces the law—is the sole defendant. The companies seek a declaratory judgment that AB 1709 violates the First Amendment and is preempted by Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, along with an order barring its enforcement.

Bonta's office said it is reviewing the filings. "Our office is committed to defending California's commonsense laws to protect kids online," it told KQED.

## The Companies' Argument

The platforms contend the law substitutes the state's judgment for that of parents and compels them to alter how they curate and present content—an act they characterize as protected editorial expression.

"We are challenging this law because it violates the First Amendment by letting the government dictate how ideas are organized, displayed, or shared with Californians," a Meta spokesperson told KQED. "Under the law, we would be forced to either remove features that keep teens' experiences age-appropriate, or cut thousands of California teens off from family, friends, and their communities."

A TikTok spokesperson called AB 1709 "a blatantly unconstitutional restriction on free expression that erodes the long-standing and protected practice of curating content based on user preferences." Google spokesperson José Castañeda said parents are best positioned to decide, noting that autoplay on YouTube is off by default for minors and that parents can disable Shorts access for teens.

## Pushback From Defenders and Critics

Lowenthal dismissed the industry's framing outright. "Nobody in their right mind believes the platforms are using free speech as a form of consumer protection," he said, arguing that the companies' "actual goal" is "to mine and monetize our children's focus and attention."

The law also drew objections from an unexpected quarter: civil-liberties advocates. The Electronic Frontier Foundation opposed the bill, arguing it mandates greater data collection and functions as a de facto ban on social media for teens. "We all deserve a better internet and not a smaller one," said Rindala Alajaji, the EFF's associate director of state affairs.

Eric Goldman, a Santa Clara University School of Law professor, told KQED the measure veers toward censorship, citing restrictions on how publishers present content, mandatory age authentication for all users, and requirements that platforms terminate accounts—which he said removes users' ability to publish their own content.

## What's Next

The litigation arrives amid mounting pressure on the platforms. Meta agreed in August to pay up to $18 billion and limit teen use of Facebook and Instagram in settlements with nearly all U.S. states, and the companies face thousands of lawsuits alleging they addicted children. Internationally, Australia banned social media for under-16s in December 2025, while France's top court struck down a similar law in August citing free-expression concerns.

With a January 1 effective date approaching, the central question is whether a federal judge will grant an injunction before the law takes effect. The outcome could shape not just California's rules but the template for a growing wave of state-level child online-safety laws enacted in the absence of federal action.
