# U.S. Adds 165,000 Jobs as Long-Term Jobless Rate Climbs

The U.S. economy added 165,000 jobs in October 2026, far exceeding expectations and signaling a resilient labor market, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Friday. The unemployment rate fell to 4.1% from 4.2%, but beneath the strong headline, the share of Americans out of work for 27 weeks or longer climbed to its highest level since late 2021.

The gain of 165,000 nonfarm payroll jobs was more than double the roughly 65,000 economists had forecast. The BLS also revised August and September figures upward by a combined 84,000 jobs, lifting September's initially weak reading from +29,000 to +60,000. Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, called the report [a "blowout"](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/article/a-blowout-us-added-165000-jobs-in-october-trouncing-expectations-135651705.html) on social media.

## Where the Jobs Came From

Hiring was concentrated in a handful of sectors. Health care, manufacturing, and business services drove the gains, while warehouse and transportation added 25,000 positions. Restaurants and construction, by contrast, shed jobs. Labor force participation edged up to 61.9% from 61.8%, a modest but encouraging sign that more Americans are re-entering the workforce.

The [BLS report](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/empsit.pdf) also showed that layoffs and discharges fell to their lowest volume in more than a year, reinforcing the picture of a "low-hire, low-fire" labor market in which employers are reluctant to cut staff but slow to expand.

## The Warning Sign Beneath the Headline

Despite the strong hiring, the average duration of unemployment rose to 27.1 weeks, and about 2 million people — 27.6% of all unemployed workers — had been jobless for 27 weeks or more. As [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/06/long-term-unemployment-october-jobs-report.html), that is the highest share of long-term unemployment since late 2021.

"This is a series that can be noisy from month to month. But if you look over a longer time horizon, there is no mistake in the trend," said Cory Stahle, an economist at Indeed. "Now, the average unemployed person takes longer to find a job than their benefits would last. That is a really key point of this."

Researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond have [documented](https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-25) how joblessness can become self-perpetuating. "Time spent unemployed may itself reduce the probability of finding a job, perhaps because skills depreciate, networks thin or employers interpret long unemployment durations as a negative signal," they wrote.

## Wages Lag Behind Prices

Average hourly wages rose 3.1% year-over-year — an improvement over the prior month but likely still trailing inflation, which reached 3.7% in September, driven partly by a jump in energy prices tied to the war with Iran. That gap has squeezed household budgets and helped fuel the cost-of-living frustration that reshaped the midterm elections.

"Workers are losing ground right now," said Cory Stahle of Indeed. "The longer it wears on, the more and more they're going to feel that."

The dissonance between strong data and lived experience is a challenge for policymakers. "It's difficult to separate what's happening to you or the people around you from the big picture," said Elizabeth Renter, lead economist at Life360. "When your LinkedIn is filled with layoffs and your social circle features a few folks who have been job seeking for several months, positive narratives about the labor market can seem jarring."

## What's Next

Attention now turns to the Federal Reserve, which held its benchmark interest rate steady on Oct. 28 but faces mounting pressure as inflation remains above target. Investors increasingly expect a rate hike at the Fed's December meeting — a move that could cool hiring just as long-term unemployment is already a concern.

The paradox of the October report — strong payrolls alongside deepening joblessness — underscores a labor market that is resilient on paper but uneven in practice. For the millions of Americans caught in extended job searches, the headline numbers offer little comfort. How the Fed balances inflation control against a still-fragile employment picture will shape the economy into 2027.
