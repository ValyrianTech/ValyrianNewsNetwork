# Berkshire Profit Falls 28% as Abel Ramps Buybacks

Berkshire Hathaway reported a 28% decline in third-quarter net profit on Saturday, even as new chief executive Greg Abel accelerated share repurchases to a record and kept the conglomerate a net seller of stocks. Net income attributable to shareholders fell to $22.11 billion from $30.80 billion a year earlier, dragged down by a sharp drop in investment gains, according to the company's [quarterly filing](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/qtrly/3rdqtr26.pdf).

The result offers an early verdict on the post-Buffett era at one of the world's most closely watched companies — a quarter in which Abel put Berkshire's enormous cash pile to work more aggressively than his legendary predecessor, with mixed early results.

## What the numbers show

Revenue rose 11.2% to $105.65 billion, the second straight quarter of double-digit growth. But the headline profit figure was distorted by the accounting treatment of Berkshire's sprawling stock portfolio. Investment gains fell nearly 46% to $9.41 billion from $17.31 billion a year earlier, a swing that alone explains most of the net-profit decline.

Berkshire has long urged investors to look past that volatility. As [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34429766) reported, the company reiterated its standard warning that "the amount of investment gains/losses in any given quarter is usually meaningless" and can produce earnings figures "extremely misleading to investors who have little or no knowledge of accounting rules."

By the metric Berkshire prefers — operating profit, which excludes portfolio swings — earnings fell 5.8% to $12.705 billion from $13.485 billion, [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/07/berkshire-hathaway-q3-2026-earnings-buybacks.html). The culprit was insurance. Underwriting profit tumbled 43% to $1.352 billion, and insurance investment income slipped 14% to $2.734 billion, pulling overall insurance profit down about 26%.

At Geico, pretax profit fell roughly 45% as the loss ratio climbed to 76.6% from 71.5%. The auto insurer raised advertising spending and chose to charge less for policies to win back customers lost during a multi-year push to tighten underwriting, according to [Reuters](https://www.detroitnews.com/story/business/2026/11/07/berkshire-hathaway-accelerates-stock-buybacks-operating-profit-falls/92659895007/).

The picture was brighter elsewhere. Railroad unit BNSF posted after-tax profit of $1.729 billion, up about 19%, helped by fuel surcharges and higher volumes. Berkshire Hathaway Energy earned $1.612 billion, up 8%, and manufacturing, service and retail profit rose 12% to $4.042 billion. Aerospace supplier Precision Castparts lifted pretax profit 30%.

## A record buyback, and a seller of stocks

The quarter's most striking move was on the buyback front. Berkshire repurchased about $9.6 billion of its own stock in July through September — its largest single quarter ever, surpassing the roughly $9.3 billion spent in the third quarter of 2020, according to [Yahoo Finance's 24/7 Wall St.](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/warren-buffett-berkshire-just-spent-125803586.html). The average purchase price was about $503.68 per Class B share.

That brought buybacks for the first nine months of 2026 to roughly $14.4 billion, and Berkshire bought back about $2 billion more in the first four weeks of October, Reuters reported. The acceleration is a clear signal: management sees Berkshire's own shares as the best available use of capital at current valuations. Under company rules, the CEO approves repurchases after consulting the chairman, and only when the stock trades below intrinsic value.

At the same time, Berkshire was a net seller of about $426 million of stocks in the quarter — a reversal from the first half of the year, when it was a net buyer of more than $11 billion. That combination — buying its own stock aggressively while selling others — suggests the conglomerate finds few attractive external opportunities at today's prices.

## The AI bet behind the drag

A key driver of the investment-loss swing was Berkshire's push into artificial intelligence under Abel. In June, Alphabet launched an $80 billion equity raise that included a $10 billion strategic investment from Berkshire: $5 billion in Class A shares at $351.81 each and $5 billion in Class C shares at $348.20 each.

Since then, Alphabet's A and C shares have fallen roughly 24% to 25%, now trading below Berkshire's purchase price, as enthusiasm for the AI trade has cooled, [ZeroHedge noted](https://www.zerohedge.com/markets/berkshires-post-buffett-era-gets-rocky-start-ai-bets-stumble) in a commentary piece. Abel has also taken a 4% stake, worth $930 million, in Italian technology group Bending Spoons, and spent $6.8 billion in July to acquire homebuilder Taylor Morrison.

The pivot marks a break from Warren Buffett's long-standing caution toward technology. Buffett, 96, stepped down as CEO at the end of 2025 after six decades at the helm; the July-September quarter was Abel's third as chief executive. Buffett became chairman emeritus in September, and the board elected his son Howard Buffett as chairman.

Berkshire's equity portfolio remains highly concentrated. Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America and Coca-Cola together account for about 65% of the portfolio's fair value, which stood at $335.5 billion as of September 30.

## A defensive winner amid the AI selloff

The results landed during a sharp rotation out of technology and AI stocks. The S&P 500 fell 5.5% over the past month, while Berkshire's Class A shares rose more than 12% and are up about 13% year to date, outpacing the index by more than five percentage points, CNBC reported.

That outperformance makes the record buyback all the more notable: Berkshire was repurchasing its own stock even as the shares touched record highs, implying management believes intrinsic value remains well above the purchase price. It also suggests investors are treating the value-heavy conglomerate as a defensive haven as fears of an AI bubble mount.

## Cash, and questions about definitions

The quarter also highlighted how Berkshire's headline cash figures can be read in different ways. Total cash and short-term Treasury holdings rose 3% to $377.435 billion from $365.514 billion at the end of the second quarter, CNBC reported. Reuters, using a narrower definition of cash and equivalents, put the figure at $356.9 billion.

Yet excluding rail and utility cash along with Treasury-bill payables, operating cash was $352.3 billion — down 1.9% from the prior quarter. And the total remains below the $380.2 billion peak reached at the end of March. Buffett spent years building one of the largest cash reserves in corporate America, more than doubling it from roughly $150 billion at the end of 2021 to more than $370 billion by late 2025.

Berkshire said "considerable uncertainty" remains about the outcomes of economic and geopolitical events, including tariffs and wars.

## What to watch

The quarter is only the first real test of Abel's stewardship, and it raises the central question of his tenure: can he deploy Berkshire's still-enormous cash pile more boldly than Buffett without sacrificing the discipline that built it?

The early scorecard is mixed. The record buyback and Berkshire's relative outperformance signal confidence in the company's own value. But the Alphabet losses show that Abel's tilt toward AI carries new risks. Geico's deteriorating underwriting will be a key watch point in the fourth quarter, as will any further moves on the AI front.

If the AI trade recovers, Berkshire's $10 billion entry into Alphabet — struck below current prices — could yet look prescient, echoing Buffett's patient, long-horizon approach. If the unwind deepens, it could become a defining loss of the new era. For now, Berkshire remains what it has long been: a defensive giant sitting on a mountain of cash, cautiously testing how to spend it.
