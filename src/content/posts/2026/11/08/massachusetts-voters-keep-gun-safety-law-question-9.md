# Massachusetts Voters Keep Sweeping Gun Safety Law

Massachusetts voters on Tuesday approved Question 9, rejecting a gun-rights effort to repeal the state's sweeping 2024 firearms law and keeping the measure in effect, according to [WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/11/04/question-9-gun-repeal-election-results), citing an Associated Press projection. Roughly 68% of voters statewide backed retention of the statute — Chapter 135, signed by Gov. Maura Healey in 2024.

Because Question 9 was a veto referendum, the ballot was inverted: a "yes" vote kept the law, while a "no" vote would have repealed it — an arrangement that confused some voters at the polls.

## How the vote unfolded

The law, which Healey called the state's "most significant gun safety legislation in a decade" when she [signed it in July 2024](https://www.wbur.org/news/2024/07/25/governor-healey-gun-law-massachusetts), had already been in effect for two years. The result caps a hard-fought campaign that began months after the law passed, when gun-rights advocates [launched a signature drive](https://www.wbur.org/news/2024/08/12/gun-law-massachusetts-proposed-referendum) to put the repeal question before voters. Organizers gathered well over the roughly 78,700 verified signatures needed to certify the measure ultimately for the 2026 ballot.

The pro-law side was backed by Healey, state and federal lawmakers and a coalition of labor unions. The repeal effort drew support from gun-rights groups, firearms retailers, firing ranges and local gun clubs, which argued the law is confusing, burdens hunting traditions and places undue strain on local police departments.

"This victory belongs to the people of Massachusetts, who through their votes said a resounding yes to protecting this legislation and keeping our communities safe from gun violence," said Ruth Zakarin, chair of Yes on 9 for a Safe Massachusetts.

## What the law does

The statute touched nearly every corner of the state's firearms rules. It required gun owners to register their firearms with the state, with a deadline for grandfathered assault-style weapons falling just before the election. It barred firearms from polling places and made it a crime to carry weapons in government buildings.

The law expanded so-called red-flag provisions, allowing healthcare providers and school administrators to petition courts to have firearms temporarily removed from people deemed a threat to themselves or others. It widened the definition of "assault-style firearm," added regulations for devices that let semi-automatic weapons fire like automatic ones, and targeted so-called "ghost guns" — untraceable firearms built from kits or 3D-printed parts — by requiring serial numbers. It also mandated new material in firearm safety courses, including live-fire training and instruction on safe storage and suicide prevention.

## Why it matters

Massachusetts already has some of the nation's lowest rates of gun deaths and tightest regulations. Supporters argued the law protects Bay Staters — from others and from themselves; in 2024, more than half of all firearm deaths in the state were suicides, according to federal data cited in the reporting.

The referendum was the first time any state put a post-*Bruen* gun package to a popular vote, and voters' approval hands a clear win to gun-safety advocates at a moment when the issue is largely fought in legislatures and courtrooms.

## What's next

The ballot result does not end the fight. Gun-rights advocates continue to challenge the law in federal court on Second Amendment grounds, with the lawsuits ongoing and no injunction yet granted against Chapter 135. As WBUR noted, the courts could ultimately return a different decision than the voters did. With the law now upheld at the ballot box, litigation remains the repeal side's most likely path forward.
