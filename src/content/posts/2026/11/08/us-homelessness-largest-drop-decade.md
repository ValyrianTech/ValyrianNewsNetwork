# U.S. Homelessness Falls by Largest Margin in Over a Decade

Homelessness across the United States fell by its largest margin in more than a decade last year, according to new federal data, with the number of people living without housing on a single night dropping to roughly 698,000. The decline — reported by the Department of Housing and Urban Development on Friday — marks the second consecutive year of improvement, but it has quickly become a political battleground, with the Trump administration and leading housing advocates offering sharply different explanations for the drop and conflicting forecasts for what comes next.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/07/nx-s1-6033491/homelessness-in-the-u-s-saw-its-largest-drop-in-more-than-a-decade-last-year), the 2026 nationwide Point-in-Time count recorded 698,425 people homeless on a single night in January, a decline of more than 6% — roughly 49,000 fewer people than the prior year. It is the largest single-year decrease on record and follows a similar drop reported for 2025, which HUD described as the first year-over-year decline since 2016.

## The Numbers Behind the Decline

The federal count, conducted annually in January by local Continuums of Care and widely considered an undercount, tallied 745,652 people in January 2025 and 698,425 in January 2026. In its [2026 release](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-090), HUD reported the year-over-year change as a 6.3% decrease, while a separate [release covering the 2025 count](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037) had described a 3% decline the year before.

An independent analysis commissioned by the National Alliance to End Homelessness (NAEH), authored by Dr. Jared Schachner of the University of Southern California, estimates the 2026 count dropped 6.7% between 2025 and 2026 — closely tracking the federal figures. That analysis found a 7.9% decrease in sheltered homelessness, a 4.9% decrease in unsheltered homelessness, and a 7.8% decrease in veteran homelessness. Chronic homelessness, however, rose by about 1%, even as other categories fell.

Both the federal and independent analyses agree on a central point: nearly 700,000 people remain homeless, a figure significantly higher than before the COVID-19 pandemic. HUD notes homelessness has increased 27% since 2013, the year it began measuring from the start of "Housing First" policy, and 28% from 2022 to 2025 under the prior administration.

## Competing Explanations for a Contested Trend

The question of what drove the decline is where the consensus breaks down.

In his statement announcing the data, HUD Secretary Scott Turner credited the administration's enforcement and policy agenda. "President Trump's actions to secure the border, get criminal illegals off our streets, and restore safety to our communities drove a historic decrease in homelessness," he said. Turner added that "after a decade of failed Housing First policies, homelessness remains near a historic high," and that the agency is "rejecting the addiction-fueled depravity seen from LA's Skid Row to New York's 'Boulevard of Broken Dreams' and moving to Treatment First to restore lives of self-sufficiency."

Allies of the administration point to immigration shifts as a key driver. Devon Kurtz, public safety policy director at the Cicero Institute, told reporters that New York, Chicago, Boston, and Denver saw surges in asylum seekers in recent years and "that's also the four cities that have driven half of the drop most recently."

The NAEH's analysis, by contrast, attributes the improvement largely to housing and support programs that predate the current administration. "It's good news that more people are getting housed, and that the programs that are funded by Congress are working," said Ann Oliva, the group's CEO. Speaking on the prior year's data, Oliva had argued that "so much of the progress reflected in the 2025 count is due to targeted housing and service resources that were available in 2024 to rehouse people" — including the Emergency Housing Voucher program and new funds for rural and unsheltered homelessness. "Unfortunately, the Trump Administration has largely deprioritized these tools and worked to dismantle the very systems that drove these reductions," she said.

Jesse Rabinowitz of the National Homelessness Law Center made a similar argument when the earlier figures were released, attributing the decline to investments made under the previous administration and accusing the current one of "doing everything they can to backtrack on this progress."

## Housing First vs. Treatment First

At the heart of the dispute is a long-running policy debate over how to house people who are homeless. "Housing First" — the prevailing federal approach since 2013 — places people in permanent housing without preconditions such as sobriety, then offers services voluntarily. "Treatment First," which the Trump administration is now pushing, makes participation in addiction or mental health treatment a condition of housing.

The administration has sought to redirect several billion dollars in homelessness spending toward short-term housing tied to mandated treatment. Those changes have so far been blocked by courts, and California is among 19 states that have sued to stop them. In July 2025, the president signed an executive order titled "Ending Crime and Disorder on America's Streets," empowering HUD to address substance abuse — a signal of the administration's intended shift.

Advocates argue the veteran figures illustrate the case for Housing First. Homelessness among veterans fell more than 7% last year and nearly 60% since 2010. "Investments in programs like that are much closer to the scale of need for veterans than for any other population experiencing homelessness," Oliva said.

## Other Factors Behind the Drop

Beyond the political framing, analysts cite several practical contributors. Softening rental markets — with rents down from pandemic peaks — have made housing more attainable. Declines in disaster-affected areas such as western North Carolina and Hawaii, as federal aid reached previously unhoused residents, also contributed. And a wave of encampment crackdowns, enabled by the Supreme Court's 2024 Grants Pass v. Johnson ruling allowing cities to ban public camping, has changed the visibility and distribution of unsheltered homelessness.

## A Dispute Over Timing and Presentation

The release itself became a flashpoint. The NAEH criticized HUD for issuing its announcement late on a Friday before close of business, describing it as "hastily drafted" and "speculative," and noted the agency did not post the full report or subpopulation data. The group also observed that HUD's 2026 release scrubbed references to gender and replaced "people experiencing homelessness" with "homeless persons" — part of what the NAEH characterized as the administration's broader reframing of the issue.

The timing placed the NAEH's independent analysis in direct competition with the federal announcement, ensuring that the public received two narratives about the same underlying data at nearly the same moment.

## What to Watch

While the headline number is unambiguously positive — fewer people living without shelter is, on its face, good news — both sides agree the picture is fragile. Nearly 700,000 people remain homeless, chronic homelessness is edging upward, and the legal fight over the administration's Treatment First overhaul is unresolved.

Three things will shape the next count. First, whether courts allow the redirect of homelessness funds toward treatment-linked housing, or whether the 19-state lawsuit halts it. Second, whether the housing and voucher programs credited with driving the decline continue to be funded at current levels. And third, whether softening rental markets persist or reverse.

For now, the data offer a rare point of agreement in a deeply divided policy debate: the trend is moving in the right direction, even as the arguments over why — and what to do next — remain as contested as ever.
