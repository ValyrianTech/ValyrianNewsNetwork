# US Public Schools Face Massive Enrollment Drop

Public schools across the United States are facing a sustained and accelerating decline in student enrollment, driven by the convergence of historically low birth rates and sharply reduced immigration. For the first time in nearly a century, both forces are pulling in the same direction, forcing districts to consolidate and close campuses and threatening billions of dollars in funding, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/06/nx-s1-5971283/public-school-enrollment-ice-deportation-birthrate).

The problem is most acute in districts with large immigrant communities, where federal deportation campaigns have accelerated a decline that demographers had long seen coming. "We were predicting a 2% drop in enrollment. It was double that," said Kelly Gonez, a board member in the Los Angeles Unified School District (LAUSD), the second largest in the country.

## A Demographic Cliff, Then an Accelerant

The decline predates any single policy. The population of children under 18 began falling nationwide after 2010, dropping by roughly 2.1 million kids through 2025, according to U.S. Census data cited by NPR. Enrollment in public schools peaked in 2019 and fell by roughly 1.5 million students as of the 2024 school year, according to the U.S. Department of Education.

Based on data gathered before the current immigration crackdown began, federal researchers project enrollment will decline by at least another 2 million students by 2030 — a roughly 7% national decline in about a decade.

What changed recently is the second force. "Birth rates here in Los Angeles County have been declining for decades, which means fewer children enrolled in our schools," Gonez said. After federal officials weakened restrictions that had kept ICE agents away from schools, "many of our families immediately went into hiding."

"The U.S. isn't alone in facing the challenges of demographic decline," said Thomas Dee, an economist who specializes in education policy at Stanford University. "To stop or slow immigration really exacerbates those problems. Communities are now confronting very painful decisions about closing schools."

## The Scale of the Loss

A [study by the Migration Policy Institute](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/publication/us-deportations-second-trump) found as many as 1.9 million people left the U.S. during the first 18 months of the administration's second term. ICE is now arresting as many as 50,000 people each month.

A June report by two education think tanks, Bellwether and WestEd, warned that public schools have entered an era of "sustained" enrollment declines already reshaping districts in 40 states. The analysis identified "a sustained drop in birth rates" as the primary driver, followed by a "national slowdown in immigration," and predicted a loss of $11 billion a year in public school funding.

The effects are visible from coast to coast. In Texas, the number of kids in classrooms plunged statewide last year by more than 76,000 students — the first non-pandemic decline in nearly four decades, [the Texas Tribune reported](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/05/11/texas-public-schools-see-historic-enrollment-drop/). The vast majority of that drop came among Hispanic students and the children of immigrant families.

In Florida, home to a large community of Haitian refugees who were stripped of temporary protected status in July, the state's two largest districts — [Miami-Dade](https://www.wlrn.org/light/education/2026-08-21/miami-dade-county-schools-shrunk-by-about-19-000-students-at-start-of-school-year-data-shows) and [Broward](https://www.wlrn.org/education/2026-08-10/first-day-of-school-in-broward-sees-enrollment-drop-by-11-000-students) — lost roughly 30,000 children combined in a single year.

In California, state officials predicted public schools could lose another 710,000 students over the next decade "if current trends in fertility and migration hold," according to a state forecast cited by NPR.

## Empty Classrooms and Empty Streets

Beyond the budget math, the decline is reshaping daily life in affected communities. When Dee studied public schools in communities hit by ICE raids in California's Central Valley in 2025, he found student daily absences spiked more than 20%.

At Santee Education Complex, a high school in South Los Angeles, an ICE agent opened fire in October 2025. No children or school employees were injured, but teacher Guadalupe Cardona believes the fear that followed caused enrollment to plummet. "In my school alone, we had over 200 students that didn't show up on the first day of school," she said. "We lost two teachers at the beginning of the school year because of it."

Guadalupe Pineda, a parent who works in a shop just down the street, said the fear has hurt the broader community. "There's definitely less sales, less traffic, less everything," she said.

The disruption is not limited to foreign-born children. According to [Brookings Institution estimates](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-many-children-are-affected-by-parental-immigrant-detention/), as many as 4.6 million school-age kids — including many U.S. citizens — live in homes with family members vulnerable to deportation. Vanessa Salazar, a U.S.-born senior in LAUSD, dropped out after her mother was detained and deported to Mexico. "It really threw me off balance," she said. "Coming home without my mother there to be my anchor, really affected my performance in school."

## A Political Divide Over the Numbers

The White House frames the trend differently. Spokeswoman Lauren Bis said deportations "make American classrooms less crowded" and pointed to an administration post stating that the total foreign-born population declined 2.9 million from January 2025 to July 2026. That figure originates from the Center for Immigration Studies, a restrictionist think tank analyzing Census survey data — a methodology some independent analysts have questioned.

Advocacy groups that support sharply reduced immigration view the trend as a necessary correction. "We don't guarantee the school system X number of kids," said Ira Mehlman of the Federation for American Immigration Reform. "That is not the job of our immigration system."

## What to Watch

Other factors have also driven enrollment losses, including the COVID-19 pandemic and a shift toward private and homeschool learning. But the Department of Education projects private schools, too, will lose more than a million students by the end of this decade — evidence that the demographic decline runs deeper than any single policy.

With LAUSD now facing a second school year in a row with 4% enrollment declines, the district announced a citywide effort in September aimed at identifying campuses to close. Gonez expects the cuts to be deep. "When your district is roughly half the size of student population it was 20 years ago, it's hard to find a sustainable path to keep campuses open," she said.

How far the decline goes — and how quickly — will depend on how long immigration enforcement remains at current levels and whether birth rates recover. For now, districts across the country are planning for fewer students, fewer teachers and fewer schools.
