# World Media Summit Pushes Global AI Media Standards

Around 500 delegates from roughly 330 media outlets, think tanks and international organisations in 112 countries gathered in Beijing for the 7th World Media Summit, which opened on November 7 and launched a plan to set shared global standards for artificial intelligence in newsrooms, [Xinhua reported](https://www.news.cn/world/20261108/5d1a62087498411ebad452d84da0fadb/c.html). The summit, held under the theme "Media's Mission and Cooperative Action amid Intelligent Transformation," adopted a concluding document, the Beijing Consensus, and unveiled what organisers called the world's first bionic humanoid robot news anchor.

## Context

Hosted by Xinhua, China's state news agency, the World Media Summit was initiated in 2009 and has become one of the flagship platforms through which Beijing convenes global media leaders — a venue distinct from Western-dominated bodies such as the World Editors Forum or the International Press Institute. The 2021 edition, held virtually, drew a congratulatory letter from President Xi Jinping that organisers again cited this year as a guiding principle: media should "strive to be communicators who connect the hearts of the people" and contribute to "building a community with a shared future for mankind."

The "global communication governance" framing used to describe this year's event is a recurring element of Chinese official discourse, tied to Beijing's broader push for what it calls a fairer international order and to its AI governance initiatives. The gathering should be read as presenting Beijing's preferred narrative on how the world's press should handle artificial intelligence — less a neutral record of open debate than an advocacy account, with participants' remarks selected by the host to align with Chinese priorities.

## Key Developments

Xinhua President Fu Hua opened the summit by positioning media as the forward edge of international exchange, describing the Beijing gathering as "a grand event for the world's press, and a microcosm of carrying forward the common values of humanity." The programme combined an opening ceremony, a high-level dialogue of international mainstream media, a plenary session and sub-forums, alongside cultural visits to the Temple of Heaven, the "Robot World" exhibition and the 798 Art District.

Three tangible outputs anchored the event. On the morning of November 7 at the Great Hall of the People, organisers launched the Global Media AI Application Standards Co-construction Plan, with 22 Chinese and foreign institutions named as the first batch of partners. The summit also adopted the Beijing Consensus, which commits participants to promoting inclusive and responsible AI application and to resisting disinformation and false reporting. And a think-tank report, the "AI+Media Global Development Annual Blue Book (2026)," was released to share what organisers described as authoritative industry knowledge.

The robot anchor — unveiled at a pre-summit forum on AI and Chinese-style modernisation on November 5 — delivered the first news bulletin presented by a bionic humanoid anchor, a symbolic showcase of Chinese leadership in "AI plus media" applications.

## Ethical Debate at the Core

Beneath the showcase lay a genuine and unresolved debate about journalism's place in an AI-driven newsroom. Reuters President Paul Bascobert said that in an era of profound AI-driven change it is essential for media leaders to convene to discuss the future, providing opportunities to learn from one another and share successful experience.

Several executives converged on the principle that humans must remain in control. Andrey Kondrashov, Director General of Russia's TASS, argued that AI must be used responsibly and ethically, with final decision-making power in editorial workflows remaining "firmly in human hands." AFP Chairman and CEO Fabrice Fries made a sharper point about dependency, arguing that AI depends on journalism far more than journalism depends on AI — and that if trustworthy journalism weakens, AI's own reliability will weaken with it.

Xinhua Editor-in-Chief Lü Yansong framed professional judgement as irreplaceable, saying that a journalist's insight into history, perception of the era and observation of society are core competencies "no algorithm can replace." Poland's Marek Traczyk, president of the Polish Media Association, called on reporters to hold to higher professional standards and to verify facts to protect accuracy and objectivity in the AI age.

## A Global South Push

A recurring theme was the demand from developing-country media for AI to narrow rather than widen the gap between rich and poor newsrooms. Viasen Sobramoney, CEO of South Africa's Independent Online, said media leaders must ensure AI becomes "a bridge rather than a barrier" — a force for inclusion rather than exclusion, and for common progress rather than deepening inequality.

Wong Chun Wai, chairman of Malaysia's Bernama, urged participants to focus on sharing knowledge, technology and training resources with developing-country media, to build multilingual AI, to protect cultural diversity and to let the Global South "speak with a louder voice." Diana Ávila Morales, a YouTube content creator, said the AI-enabled newsroom practices on show had strong appeal for Global South media and argued that cooperation among them should widen.

Mohammed Al-Yami, Director General of the Union of OIC News Agencies, cast media as a force that brings peoples closer and a tool for promoting peaceful development and mutual understanding among civilisations. Pratyush Ranjan, editor of India's PTI, stressed that each civilisation has its own historical traditions, languages and values, but that the shared pursuit of peace, dignity and a better future binds them together.

## Analysis

The summit's most consequential element is the standards plan, not the rhetoric. By assembling 22 founding partners around a "co-construction" framework outside Western-led standards bodies, China is attempting to shape how AI is governed in newsrooms worldwide — and to do so at a moment when rules are still being written. The Beijing Consensus's language on resisting disinformation also carries a dual edge: it aligns with universal concerns about false information, but the definition of what counts as disinformation remains unaddressed.

The event also served broader diplomatic purposes. [Yonhap reported](https://cn.yna.co.kr/view/ACK20261104001800881) that a Korean Central News Agency delegation led by Vice President Pak Kwang-chol travelled to China to attend the summit, part of a marked uptick in high-level exchanges between Pyongyang and Beijing following reciprocal visits by Kim Jong Un and Xi Jinping. Media summits, in this framing, are instruments of regional diplomacy as much as industry gatherings.

The gaps matter. Organisers did not publish the full list of the 22 founding partners, the Beijing Consensus was summarised rather than reproduced, and the mix of Western versus Global South participants was not broken down. Those omissions limit outside assessment of how broad the new coalition really is.

## What's Next

With the summit running through November 9, attention turns to implementation. The test of the standards plan will be whether its 22 partners produce concrete, transparent rules that developing-country newsrooms can actually adopt — and whether the framework accommodates the professional and ethical concerns raised by Western and Global South executives alike. For now, the summit has established Beijing as a convenor of global media governance. Whether it becomes a rule-maker is the open question.
