# Sabena Bankruptcy Nears Closure After 25 Years

Twenty-five years after Sabena became the first national airline in the world to file for bankruptcy, the liquidation of Belgium's former flag carrier is finally approaching its end. The trustee overseeing the case, Christian Van Buggenhout, said the proceedings could "really close next year," [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/faillissement-sabena-na-25-jaar-nu-echt-bijna-afgehandeld-zegt/), capping one of the longest and most consequential corporate wind-downs in Belgian legal history.

Sabena's collapse, declared by the Brussels commercial court on 7 November 2001, was the largest bankruptcy Belgium had ever seen. More than 7,500 people lost their jobs, and the airline's failure became a symbol of the fragility that gripped European aviation after the September 11 attacks and the meltdown of its key shareholder, Swissair.

## A Slow, Deliberate Unwinding

Rather than sell off assets in a rapid fire-sale, Van Buggenhout — appointed curator in November 2001 — chose a riskier, more patient approach: keeping parts of the business running and restructuring them before selling. The strategy paid off handsomely for creditors.

At the time of the bankruptcy, the declared debt exceeded €3 billion. After hundreds of legal proceedings contesting inflated creditor claims, that figure was reduced to just over €1 billion. Under a fast-liquidation scenario, only about €190 million would have been available for payout. Through the decision to continue and restructure operations, roughly €1.05 billion became available instead.

The clearest illustration is Sabena's baggage-handling unit, worth about €3 million at the moment of bankruptcy. "We continued the company and sold it for 55 million euros," Van Buggenhout said. The trustee has described the approach as a duty to go the full distance: "You have to drain the cup to the bottom."

## What Has Been Recovered

Much of the recovery has already reached those owed money. Some €500 million in severance payments to staff has been fully paid. Suppliers, who are owed €600 million in total, have received €250 million so far, leaving €350 million outstanding.

The liquidation also carries an unusual international dimension rooted in Sabena's historical African operations. A parcel of land in Congo remains a "problem file" now before Belgium's Court of Cassation, and a separate procedure in Switzerland may yet yield additional money. Van Buggenhout spent about four years personally in Congo, where Sabena's Hotel Memling was operated for nearly 25 years before being sold.

"I think we have been a very good 'paterfamilias' — a careful steward — for our creditors," he said.

## Why It Took a Quarter-Century

The sheer scale of the collapse explains much of the delay. Sabena's downfall was tightly bound to Swissair, whose own financial troubles triggered the Belgian carrier's sudden grounding. Untangling cross-border claims, aircraft leases, and contested creditor filings across multiple jurisdictions consumed years of legal work.

The case is now studied as a textbook example of value-maximizing administration rather than a rapid disposal — an approach that dramatically increased what creditors ultimately recovered, even as it stretched the process across a generation.

## What to Watch

The remaining hurdles are narrow but consequential. The Congo land dispute before the Court of Cassation and the Swiss procedure are the last significant open items. If those resolve as the trustee expects, the Sabena file — opened in the shadow of a national crisis in 2001 — could at last be closed in 2027.

For the thousands of former employees, suppliers, and creditors who have waited a quarter-century, the closure would mark the formal end of a chapter that has long outlasted the airline itself.
