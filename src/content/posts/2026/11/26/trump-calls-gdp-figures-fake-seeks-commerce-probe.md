# Trump Calls Pre-Election GDP Figures 'Fake' and Demands Commerce Department Probe

President Donald Trump on Thanksgiving Day accused his own Commerce Department of deliberately understating economic growth before the midterm elections and demanded an investigation, escalating a long-running campaign to discredit federal data that does not align with his narrative. The complaint centers on a routine statistical revision issued the day before, in which the government raised its estimate of third-quarter growth to 4.3% annualized from an earlier 3.4%.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-gdp-economy-midterms-4fd800b44005ae11f950f96ff62f876e), the president said in a Truth Social post that the original figures were a "massive, and purposeful, error" and tied them to Republican losses in the Nov. 3 vote that handed Democrats control of both chambers of Congress.

"It was just revealed that the REAL Numbers on the Economy, published before the Election, were FAKE, and a correction was issued yesterday by the people in the Government Agency that does these numbers," Trump wrote. "Why wasn't the correct number issued before the Election? I am asking for an immediate investigation of this situation."

The Commerce Department did not immediately respond to a request for comment; the federal government was largely closed for the holiday.

## A Routine Revision, Not a Correction

The revision at the heart of the dispute is an ordinary part of how U.S. economic statistics are produced. The Commerce Department's Bureau of Economic Analysis issues gross domestic product estimates in three successive rounds — advance, second and third — as more complete source data becomes available. The second estimate, released Wednesday, pegged third-quarter growth at 4.3%, up from October's initial 3.4% reading. A third estimate is scheduled for next month.

[The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/administration/6244608-trump-revised-gdp-data-probe/) reported that the BEA releases these updates on a pre-scheduled basis without regard to the political calendar, part of a process designed to improve accuracy rather than to reflect election timing. The White House offered no evidence that the revision was politically motivated.

The same BEA release that Trump celebrated also contained news he did not mention: the personal consumption expenditures price index, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, accelerated to 3.8% annual in October, well above the central bank's 2% target.

## A Familiar Playbook

The episode fits a pattern that AP has documented extensively. Trump has repeatedly sought to undermine data that contradicts his claims of a historic boom — from limiting COVID-19 testing in 2020 so the outbreak looked less severe, to the 2019 "Sharpie" hurricane map, to firing the Bureau of Labor Statistics commissioner in August 2025 after a weak jobs report he called "phony" and "rigged."

As [AP News detailed](https://apnews.com/article/trump-playbook-discredit-unfavorable-data-jobs-5138de0a2abfb45546fe40d70bcca346) in its review of that pattern, economists warn the strategy carries real risks. "Our democratic system and the strength of our private economy depend on the honest flow of information about our economy, our government and our society," said Douglas Elmendorf, a Harvard professor and former director of the Congressional Budget Office. "The Trump administration is trying to suppress honest analysis."

Jed Kolko, a senior fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics, has stressed that revisions are a feature of sound statistics, not a flaw. "Revisions solve the tension between timeliness and accuracy," he said. "We want timely data because policymakers and businesses and investors need to make decisions with the best data that's available, but we also want accuracy."

## Why It Matters

Politics-free data underpins trillions of dollars in investment decisions, Federal Reserve policymaking and the public's ability to hold government accountable. Trump's allegation — coming as the economy grows briskly but inflation stays elevated — arrives at a delicate moment: the Fed is weighing a possible rate hike at its Dec. 8-9 meeting, and markets have been volatile.

The political backdrop sharpens the stakes. Voters delivered a rebuke to Republicans in the midterms amid anger over affordability; according to the AP Voter Poll, 76% described economic conditions as "not so good" or "poor," and about two-thirds said Trump's own policies fueled inflation. With Democrats set to take both chambers in January, the incoming House majority is expected to pursue aggressive oversight of the administration.

## What to Watch

Whether any formal investigation materializes — and how the Commerce Department responds once the holiday recess ends — will be an early test of how the administration handles its own statistical agencies under divided government. For now, the episode leaves a familiar question hanging: when the numbers help the president, they are proof of a booming economy; when they complicate his story, they are suspect. The credibility of the institutions that produce them is caught in between.
