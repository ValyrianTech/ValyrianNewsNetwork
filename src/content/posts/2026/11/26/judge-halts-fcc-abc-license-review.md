# Judge Halts FCC Review of ABC Licenses

A federal judge on Wednesday blocked the Federal Communications Commission from conducting an early review of ABC's broadcast licenses, ruling that the Trump administration was likely violating the network's free speech rights. The decision, handed down by U.S. District Judge Loren AliKhan in Washington, D.C., marks a sharp rebuke of efforts to use the FCC's regulatory power against a major news organization over its editorial content, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/fcc-abc-ruling-editorial-content-f04f6b2b1a17fc10f2da11e4fd83a9df).

AliKhan granted ABC's request for a temporary restraining order and a preliminary injunction, finding that the network was "likely to succeed" on its First Amendment retaliation claim. Her assessment was blunt.

"This is not a close case," she wrote.

## The Ruling at a Glance

The injunction bars the FCC from taking any further action in its early license renewal proceeding against eight ABC-owned stations. AliKhan concluded that ABC had detailed "a campaign of retaliation against them in response to their exercise of their First Amendment rights," which on its own constitutes an "irreparable injury."

She was careful to acknowledge the limits on her order. "No one doubts that the Commission may enforce the law in the manner it sees fit," AliKhan wrote. "But in doing so, Defendants may not retaliate against or punish Plaintiffs and other broadcast license holders for disfavored speech."

The judge also rejected the FCC's bid to dismiss the lawsuit on jurisdictional grounds, concluding that ABC's First Amendment retaliation claim fell outside the statutory review process set up in the Communications Act — a process that directs such challenges to the federal appellate court and could have added months of delay.

As [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2026/11/abc-fcc-trump-first-amendment-lawsuit-1237158218/) reported, she split her decision: while blocking the license review, she denied ABC's broader request for an injunction against any FCC action "to coerce or threaten" the network, ruling that such relief could not be issued for something "merely feared."

## How the Dispute Began

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr, a Trump ally, ordered the early review of the eight stations' licenses in April 2026 — shortly after a joke by ABC late-night host Jimmy Kimmel infuriated Trump, who called for Kimmel's firing. The first of the licenses was not set to expire until 2028.

ABC and its parent company, Disney, sued the FCC and Carr in August 2026, alleging the agency violated the First Amendment by retaliating against programming decisions the administration dislikes. The FCC countered that the early renewal was tied to its inability to obtain documents from the network as part of an investigation into its diversity, equity and inclusion practices. Government lawyers urged the court to dismiss the case, arguing that "to foreclose the Commission from using all the tools at its disposal to investigate whether Plaintiffs are meeting their statutory and regulatory duties would seriously harm both the government and the public."

AliKhan did not find the agency's explanation credible. She pointed to concrete moves ABC made out of fear of losing its licenses — placing James Talarico's guest appearance on "Jimmy Kimmel Live" on YouTube, outside the FCC's authority, and livestreaming Trump's July primetime speech out of concern about retaliation.

"These alterations to Plaintiffs' editorial decision making showcase how the Commission's early license renewal proceedings have already curtailed Plaintiffs' protected activity," she wrote. "That an objectively reasonable broadcaster faced with the same existential threat would follow suit is beyond debate."

She also drew a direct line between the president's rhetoric and the regulator's actions. "Chairman Carr has taken President Trump's rhetoric as his marching orders," AliKhan wrote, noting that in the same week he was tapped to lead the FCC, Carr had lauded "license revocation" as a "remedy" to "deter this kind of conduct."

## A Divided Reaction

ABC News welcomed the decision. "Today's decision is an important victory for the First Amendment," a spokesperson said. "We thank the court for its careful consideration."

The FCC signaled it would keep fighting, calling the ruling "wrong on the facts and wrong on the law" and vowing to "prevail in full on this matter." The agency said it would "continue to use our lawful authority to hold broadcasters accountable to their public interest obligations."

Anna Gomez, the sole Democrat on the FCC, offered a sharply different view, saying the agency had "weaponized its licensing authority to retaliate against broadcasters for exercising their right to free expression" and that the court had now "put a check on that conduct."

Bruce Brown, president of the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, called the ruling "a big win not only for ABC, but for all broadcasters," describing it as "a novel case forced on ABC because of the administration's punitive actions."

## What Comes Next

The ruling is preliminary, not a final judgment on the merits, and the case will proceed. Given the FCC's vow to "prevail in full," an appeal is widely anticipated.

The ABC case is one of several legal skirmishes between the Trump administration and news outlets — including The Associated Press — over their coverage and access to the executive branch since Trump returned to the White House nearly two years ago. ABC is the first major media conglomerate to challenge the FCC's wave of investigations, and the outcome could shape how far regulators can go in pressuring broadcasters over what they air.

For now, the immediate message from the court is unambiguous: the government may regulate broadcasters, but it may not punish them for speech it disfavors.
