# Army Detonates Five Tons of Lead Explosives in Iowa

The U.S. Army on Wednesday carried out a controlled detonation of more than 60 barrels — roughly five tons — of degraded, highly unstable lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, ending a weeks-long standoff over lead dust, public health and how the military communicated with the communities in the blast's path.

The explosion went off at approximately 9 a.m. local time, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/25/nx-s1-6052338/army-detonation-lead-explosives-iowa), capping a disposal operation that had been delayed three times amid lawsuits by environmental groups and last-minute court intervention.

## A Blast Framed as the Only Safe Option

Within about two hours of the detonation, Army officials declared the operation a complete success. Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley of the U.S. Army Sustainment Command said overhead coverage showed all lead azide barrels were "definitively removed," with no secondary explosions, no "kickout," and no infrastructure damage. He said the visible burst was largely the result of more than 3,600 gallons of aqueous foam used to suppress the blast.

"I believe, absolutely, that we reduced, as close to zero as is absolutely humanly possible, the risk to the health and safety of the people of Middletown and the extended communities throughout Iowa," Shirley said.

Federal and state officials quickly amplified the message. FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton said preliminary evidence from the Environmental Protection Agency and other sources showed a "0.0 detection level of lead in the atmosphere" after the blast. Missouri Gov. Mike Kehoe said initial analysis indicated the mitigation measures "appear to have been successful and the explosion was well-contained." President Donald Trump, in a post on Truth Social, said early reports showed the Army's mitigation efforts "have far exceeded expectations."

Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds called the outcome "a best-case scenario for the residents of Middletown and its surrounding communities."

## Why the Army Chose to Detonate

The material at the center of the operation was lead azide, an explosive used to ignite munitions since World War I. It had been stored for decades at the Middletown plant, a facility that opened during World War II and became the Army's main site for packing heavy artillery. In recent years, crystals formed inside the containers, making the chemical acutely sensitive to movement and temperature change.

According to officials, chemical treatment was ruled out because the munitions had degraded to the point where it was too dangerous to move them — or even open their containers — without risking an accidental detonation. Col. Aaron Teller, commander of the 71st Explosive Ordnance Disposal Group, said a significant accumulation of crystallized lead azide on barrel exteriors and lids prevented crews from opening or moving them.

The Army argued that a planned detonation allowed authorities to pick favorable weather, limit dust spread, and monitor it — versus an uncontrolled explosion that could occur without warning.

## The Controversy Over a Safer Alternative

But the decision was far from uncontested. Records obtained by [Iowa Public Radio](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/environment/2026-11-24/army-blast-unstable-lead-azide-contractor-chemical-treatment-plan) reveal that the Army had a chemical-neutralization contractor, MuniRem Environmental, lined up and that a multi-agency panel had recommended neutralization as the safest option. The Army selected detonation instead on May 8, 2026 — just four days after MuniRem submitted its detailed treatment proposal — raising unanswered questions about what changed and how the Army assessed the alternative.

"Based on my experience and knowledge, I believe that chemical neutralization remains a viable option for safely opening the drums," MuniRem CEO Valentine Nzengung told Iowa Public Radio.

The episode drew sharp, bipartisan criticism from Iowa officials who said the emergency was a self-inflicted product of years of poor storage and inadequate communication.

"At a time when trust in our institutions is already low, the military owes the people it serves more transparency, not less," said U.S. Sen. Joni Ernst, a Republican. "These materials should never have been allowed to get to this point, and Iowans are right to be frustrated."

Her colleague, Sen. Chuck Grassley, warned that the situation had "turned into political finger pointing from all sides," adding that "this is a life-or-death situation and the government has a responsibility to protect precious American lives."

Iowa Gov.-elect Rob Sand, a Democrat, said that "because of the decisions that have already been made over the course of months, if not years, no good options exist and the situation is more dangerous and unpredictable than it ever should have been."

## The Health Stakes

The detonation carried genuine public-health risk. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says there is no safe level of lead exposure, and children and pregnant people face the greatest danger because lead is a neurotoxin that can cause learning disabilities and developmental harm.

The Army's dispersion maps modeled a lead-dust plume traveling southwest over an eight-hour timeline, with surface deposition scenarios of 5 and 100 micrograms per square foot — the EPA's indoor hazard standards are 5 micrograms per square foot for floors and 10 for windowsills. As [Iowa Public Radio reported](https://www.iowapublicradio.org/environment/2026-11-19/army-lead-detonation-public-health-safety-risks), earlier Army maps modeled a plume that could extend more than 100 miles, and the USDA estimated that approximately 350 farms could be affected based on the projected path.

FEMA recommended that residents in Middletown evacuate and those within 5 to 20 miles southwest of the site — including parts of northern Missouri — remain indoors with windows and doors closed. Some residents voluntarily evacuated.

The Army built earthen berms, misting systems, and additional water tanks around the site to capture harmful particles, and more than 1,200 EPA samples were collected before the blast.

## A Pattern of Concern Over Follow-Through

Critics focused less on the blast itself than on what comes next. Purdue University professor Andrew Whelton, who has studied environmental disaster planning, told NPR he was concerned about follow-through with toxic sampling and the cleanup that disasters like this require, citing the East Palestine chemical spill and the Los Angeles wildfires as examples where government has not tracked long-term toxic exposure well.

He also faulted the planning and timing. "...the timing should have been planned better. Because that didn't happen, because the messaging was just so poor, you had anxiety, you had stress, you had businesses planning to shut down," he said.

Public health experts have noted they could not find examples of this type of lead azide detonation happening in the U.S. before — making outcomes hard to predict, and raising the worry that it becomes a template for future disposals elsewhere. Lead is "persistent"; it does not decompose, so cleanup means reducing concentration and limiting exposure. Washed-off lead can migrate into soil.

## What's Next

Attention now turns to the aftermath: whether post-blast soil, water and forage testing detects elevated lead; how an extensive cleanup and remediation effort will be carried out and who will pay for it; and how compensation claims will be handled. Residents with property damage — broken windows, cracked foundations — can file claims under the Federal Tort Claims Act and Military Claims Act, though the process does not cover environmental or health impacts from lead exposure.

Multiple Iowa officials and environmental groups are pressing for investigations into how the situation degraded and why communication lagged. The Army has also ordered a thorough examination of every storage facility in its inventory.

As of the latest reporting, no official all-clear had been given for evacuated residents to return; they were to be contacted by phone. For Middletown and the communities downwind, the immediate danger has passed — but the questions about how it was allowed to reach this point, and what it leaves behind, have only begun.
