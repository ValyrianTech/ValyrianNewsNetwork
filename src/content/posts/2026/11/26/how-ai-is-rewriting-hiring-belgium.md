# How AI Is Rewriting Hiring in Belgium

Forget the newspaper ad and the CV handed across a desk. In Belgium, artificial intelligence now sits on both sides of the hiring table — helping candidates polish their applications and helping employers screen, rank and interview them. An immersive report from [RTBF's "Les Clés" podcast](https://www.rtbf.be/article/entretien-par-whatsapp-disparition-des-cv-comment-l-ia-modifie-le-recrutement-en-belgique-11821025) pulls back the curtain on two very different Belgian employers to show how far the transformation has gone — and where the questions of fairness and transparency are only beginning to be asked.

The contrast is striking. At supermarket giant Carrefour, the CV has been scrapped altogether for store roles. At insurer Ethias, human recruiters remain firmly in charge, with AI cast as an assistant rather than a gatekeeper. Together, the two cases map the widening spectrum of AI adoption in Belgian human resources.

## At Carrefour, a chatbot does the first interview

Carrefour Belgium has decided it no longer needs a curriculum vitae for cashiers, shelf-restockers and other store positions. Instead, candidates' first contact is a WhatsApp chatbot that openly announces itself as an AI agent.

"For store employees, we decided to no longer ask for the CV," **Tristan De Wree**, Carrefour Belgium's head of recruitment and internal mobility, told RTBF. "The skills needed to work in a store don't show in a CV; they show during an interview, because it's about human contact. Experience could be a plus, but it's not necessary for us."

Transparency is built into the design, De Wree insists. "We state from the start that it's an AI agent. We want to be transparent, we don't want to deceive people by saying it's really a recruiter."

The process unfolds in three stages. First, the chatbot asks practical questions — such as whether the candidate is available every day between 6 a.m. and 8 p.m., and until 9 p.m. on Fridays — plus open situational questions. Next comes an online personality and skills test, including logic puzzles. Only at the third stage does a human appear: a one-hour video interview with a recruiter. Even there, AI assists by recording the session, taking notes, flagging questions the recruiter may have missed, and drafting roughly 85% of the report. The recruiter finalizes the document and makes the final call.

Speed is the point. "In retail there's heavy competition in Belgium; we all target the same profiles," De Wree said. "Whoever is first to invite the candidate to interview is probably the one who wins the candidate."

The human toll is real, if indirect. No HR staff were dismissed because of the automated process, according to the report, but through unreplaced departures and internal moves, Carrefour's HR department shrank from 14 employees to 5.

## At Ethias, a more cautious middle path

Ethias, which hires around 100 people a year and often needs specialized profiles, has taken a slower route. Its human HR team remains central, with AI used to analyze CVs, match open vacancies against the company's history of applications, and send automatic replies to candidates.

"All these deployments obviously cost organizations money and aren't always the most profitable investment," said **Julien Balistreri**, Ethias's chief people and organization officer. "Tools are seen as decision support… But the recruiter does not delegate responsibility to the technology. Final responsibility remains human."

Yet AI is already changing whom Ethias looks for. "What matters more than a candidate's immediate expertise is their potential for growth," Balistreri said. A commissioned study found that 50% of the insurer's occupations will be strongly impacted by AI within five years — some possibly becoming obsolete. Rather than plan mass layoffs, Ethias is rethinking the contracts it offers, weighing fixed-term arrangements, outsourcing and subcontracting for roles likely to be reshaped.

## A mainstream practice — with thin guardrails

These are not outliers. According to [RTBF reporting from February 2026](https://www.rtbf.be/article/recruter-et-trier-les-cv-avec-l-ia-de-plus-en-plus-utilise-par-les-entreprises-mais-a-encadrer-pour-eviter-les-derives-11675655), three-quarters of Belgian companies already use AI for tasks such as analyzing and screening CVs and selecting candidates. At rail infrastructure manager Infrabel, which receives more than 22,000 applications a year, AI helps screen CVs so candidates get faster feedback and recruiters can focus on human contact. Interim agency Randstad has gone further, creating an ethics committee to vet which AI tools it uses and how.

But governance lags far behind adoption. Drawing on the Belgian Institute for Equality of Women and Men, RTBF reported that fewer than one in five recruitment agencies have put measures in place to govern AI use in selection. "That's obviously very little," said the institute's spokesperson, **Véronique De Baets**, warning that recruitment firms must pair AI with control and transparency measures.

The institute's own 2026 study, ["Recrutement et sélection à l'ère de l'IA"](https://igvm-iefh.belgium.be/fr/documentation/recrutement-et-selection-lere-de-lia), combined qualitative interviews, a survey of 416 professionals and five focus groups. It found that while gender bias is the most likely risk, bias linked to ethnicity and age is widely underestimated — and it issued recommendations for public authorities, organizations and recruitment professionals.

## The bias trap: why "neutral" data isn't enough

Experts warn that transparency about *being* AI is not the same as transparency about *how* decisions are made. Recruitment AI systems are classified as "high-risk" under the EU AI Act, imposing obligations around human oversight, data governance and bias monitoring.

Yet the deeper problem is structural. As [research published via RTBF and The Conversation](https://www.rtbf.be/article/quand-les-algorithmes-de-recrutement-renforcent-les-biais-que-l-on-voulait-reduire-11788754) explains, removing sensitive variables like sex or ethnicity does not eliminate discrimination. AI ranking systems learn from historical data that already reflect inequality, creating feedback loops: a small early advantage compounds into greater visibility, which generates more data reinforcing that advantage. "Neutral" proxies — the number of completed assignments, accumulated experience — can systematically disadvantage women, young professionals and women from ethnic minorities.

That insight complicates both models. Carrefour's "skills over CV" approach may lower credential barriers, but it replaces them with algorithmic personality and logic tests whose own fairness is contested. Ethias's matching of vacancies to past applications risks inheriting whatever patterns the past contains.

## Where it could go next

Both recruiters are already eyeing more powerful tools. De Wree is intrigued by AI that could infer personality from non-verbal communication — body language — saying such systems already exist. Balistreri follows predictive-behavior tools, floating a hypothetical example: could four consecutive Monday-morning absences signal a resignation within three to six months, prompting an earlier conversation between an employee and their manager?

Such ambitions — profiling personality or predicting behavior — would meet heightened scrutiny under the EU AI Act and Europe's data-protection rules.

For Belgium, the coming years will test whether speed can coexist with fairness. The technology is already embedded in the hiring pipeline; the governance, so far, is not. The question is no longer whether AI will shape who gets hired, but whether companies and regulators can ensure it does so transparently and equitably — and whether candidates who are rejected by an algorithm will have a human to turn to.
