# Sichuan School Told to Scrap Rule on Dialect at Break

Education authorities in Yibin, Sichuan province, have ordered a local school to immediately repeal an "improper regulation" after students were disciplined — photographed and made to write self-criticisms — for speaking a regional dialect during break time, a case that has reignited debate over how far schools should go in promoting Standard Mandarin.

The Cuiping District Education and Sports Bureau issued its ruling late on November 25, acknowledging management failures at Tanghu School and announcing a package of corrective and disciplinary measures, according to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34559692), which republished the official notice.

## What happened

Students at Tanghu School who spoke the local Sichuan dialect with classmates during break were photographed by teachers as evidence and deemed to have violated school discipline, according to a commentary published by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34558623). The students were required to write 500-character self-criticisms and attend a "moral education mini-class."

The matter surfaced through a complaint filed on the "Sichuan Public Voice" platform, a provincial government-affiliated channel for public grievances, before spreading across social media and being picked up by national outlets, prompting official scrutiny.

## The official response

The bureau said a working group conducted a special verification at the school. It found that Tanghu School was implementing the Law on the National Common Language and Script — which requires Mandarin in classrooms and advocates its use on campus — but did so with "mechanistic and simplistic management methods." It also found the school had enforced appearance and dress rules in a "dogmatic and inappropriate manner."

The bureau ordered a three-part correction: the school must immediately repeal the improper regulation, prohibit photographing students for disciplinary purposes, delete photographs already taken, and stop treating such situations as violations. It also summoned the school's principal, ordered a profound self-examination of its management practices, and issued public criticism and admonishment talks to the teachers involved under rules governing teacher professional conduct. Finally, the school was told to revise any non-compliant content in its management rules.

"Grasp well the scale and warmth of education management, and effectively safeguard students' legitimate rights and interests," the bureau said in its notice, pledging a district-wide inspection to draw lessons from the case and urge all schools to govern by the rules.

## Why it matters

The episode touches a long-running tension in China between the state promotion of Standard Mandarin and the protection of regional linguistic heritage. Sichuanese is a major Sinitic variety with a strong regional cultural identity, and attempts to suppress it — especially in informal settings such as school breaks — tend to draw rapid public criticism.

The Paper's commentary captured the core objection: speaking Mandarin in class is understandable, it argued, but break time is free activity time, and "advocating" cannot become a disguised prohibition. Dialects and Mandarin "are not opposed to each other," it added, warning against letting the hometown dialect become "something that needs to be corrected."

The bureau's language — criticizing "mechanistic and simplistic" enforcement — reflects a broader official concern in Chinese education about schools over-applying rules rigidly in ways that harm students' rights and invite public backlash.

## What to watch

The bureau has promised to inspect schools across the district, raising the question of whether similar rules exist elsewhere. It remains unclear how many students were affected and whether the photographs have been fully deleted as ordered. Also unresolved is whether the sanctions against the teachers — public criticism and admonishment talks — represent the full extent of accountability.

The case is likely to reinforce a national conversation about whether promoting Mandarin on campus can coexist with respecting students' freedom to use their dialects in informal settings — a question that, on this evidence, is far from settled.
