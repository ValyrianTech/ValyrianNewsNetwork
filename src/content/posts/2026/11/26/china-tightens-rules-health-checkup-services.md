# China Tightens Rules on Health Checkup Services

China's National Health Commission (NHC) has issued a formal notice directing health authorities and checkup institutions nationwide to standardize and improve health checkup services, targeting a sprawling and unevenly regulated screening market. The notice, dated 23 November and released publicly on 26 November, was published in full by [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261126/e7886c2e4e4447199ec1f2323e1e1e11/c.html), the state news agency, alongside an official interpretation.

## What the notice requires

The directive, formally numbered as NHC General Office Medical Administration Letter No. 368 of 2026, is addressed to health commissions across all provinces, autonomous regions, municipalities, and the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps. It is organized around four pillars: standardizing the construction and management of checkup institutions, optimizing and expanding services, strengthening technical guidance, and tightening day-to-day supervision.

On institutional standards, checkup providers must comply with the Regulation on the Administration of Medical Institutions, equipping themselves with required sites, facilities, equipment, and staff. They must also establish systems for protecting examinee privacy, verifying procedures, discussing difficult reports, registering and following up on high-risk abnormal results, and handling patient rescue and referrals.

## Consumer protections at the core

Perhaps the most consequential provisions target consumer grievances that have long dogged the industry. The notice states that institutions must "strictly follow the principles of informed consent and voluntariness" and "must not provide explicitly prohibited or clearly meaningless examination items, nor force examinees to accept checkup items." This directly answers widespread complaints about aggressive up-selling and unnecessary add-on packages, particularly at private providers.

The notice also calls on institutions to recommend items appropriate to a person's age, sex, and pre-examination consultation, to issue reports promptly, and to clearly flag and explain abnormal results in plain language. Institutions are encouraged to explore a risk-grading management system for checkup results and to set up dedicated health checkup consultation clinics offering report interpretation, health education, and treatment guidance.

## Cracking down on fraud

The supervision section gives the directive teeth. Health authorities are instructed to combine institutional verification, routine oversight, and random inspections, and to promote information technology and intelligent supervision to improve efficiency. Crucially, the notice calls for severe punishment of illegal activities, "including carrying out health checkups in violation of regulations, practicing beyond one's scope, and falsifying health checkup reports."

According to the [official interpretation](https://www.news.cn/20261126/c1d1f923cfa049faaf25f30d1178fe97/c.html) released by the NHC's Medical Administration Department, the notice is intended to implement the deployment of the National Health and Health Work Conference, strengthen institutional management, safeguard the public's right to health, and promote the healthy development of the checkup industry.

## Integration with primary care

The directive also links checkups to China's broader chronic-disease strategy. When checkups identify patients with hypertension, Type 2 diabetes, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, institutions must guide them to primary-level facilities, such as township hospitals, village clinics, and community health centers, to receive chronic disease management through basic public health services. The notice further encourages "Internet + Health Checkup" initiatives and closer connections between checkups and clinical care, including specialist appointment bookings for those in need.

## Scope and context

The interpretation clarifies what counts as a "health checkup" under the notice, defining it as a medical examination to understand a person's health status and detect early disease signals and health risks. It explicitly excludes occupational health examinations, employee health checks, statutory special exams for school enrollment, military recruitment, and marriage registration, as well as basic public health service checkups, pre-pregnancy screening, and specialized disease screening or census programs.

The notice operationalizes an earlier framework: the NHC's October 2025 "Adult Health Checkup Item Recommendation Guidelines," which shifted the industry from fixed packages toward a "1+X" model of a basic package plus personalized add-ons based on a pre-examination questionnaire.

## What to watch

The notice sets national direction but leaves enforcement to provincial and local health authorities, and it includes no numeric targets or public enforcement data. The key questions ahead are how aggressively provinces will act against non-compliant providers, whether the risk-grading system for results will be standardized nationwide, and how compliance will be measured among the many private standalone checkup centers that dominate the market. For a sector long criticized for opaque pricing and hard-sell tactics, the notice marks a clear shift from standard-setting toward enforcement.
