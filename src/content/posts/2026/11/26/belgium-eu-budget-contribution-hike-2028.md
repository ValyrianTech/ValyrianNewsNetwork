# Belgium Faces €1.5 Billion EU Bill Hike From 2028

Belgium could be asked to pay roughly €1.5 billion more per year into the European Union budget starting in 2028, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/25/moet-belgie-vanaf-2028-per-jaar-1-5-miljard-euro-meer-bijdragen/). The projection hinges on the outcome of negotiations over the EU's next seven-year budget, which the bloc's 27 heads of state and government began debating at a special summit in Brussels on 26 November. The potential increase lands at an awkward moment for Prime Minister Bart De Wever, whose government is already hunting for around €10 billion in domestic savings.

## A Seven-Year Budget, Settled All at Once

The EU agrees its long-term spending plan — the Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF) — every seven years. The current framework runs from 2021 to 2027 and is worth €1,250 billion; the next one covers 2028 to 2034. By fixing the whole envelope in one go, member states avoid an annual fight over the EU budget.

The starting point was ambitious. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen tabled her first proposal roughly two years ago: just under €2,000 billion, well above the current framework. As the [Council of the EU](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/nl/policies/the-eu-s-long-term-budget-2028-2034/) details, that proposal amounted to almost €2 trillion, equivalent to 1.26% of the EU's average gross national income (GNI) for 2028–2034, and restructured spending into four headings while introducing a new National and Regional Partnership Plan (NRPP) system and a European Competitiveness Fund.

The proposal drew swift criticism, notably from Germany, which demanded cuts of hundreds of billions. On Saturday 21 November, European Council President António Costa trimmed 10% off the Commission's figure, presenting a revised negotiating box of €1,780 billion — the number now on the table.

## Ukraine Adds a Second Front

A large second block of the negotiations concerns support for Ukraine. In December last year, the EU agreed to lend Kyiv €90 billion — a solution reached only after De Wever fought hard against using frozen Russian assets held at Brussels-based clearing house Euroclear.

With the war dragging on, Ukraine needs more. The Commission now proposes a second €45 billion loan, enough to fund Kyiv through the end of 2027. If leaders approve it, the EU would owe an estimated €55 billion in interest over 2028–2034. On top of that sits a proposal for a further €100 billion in support for 2028–2034 — as loans or grants, that remains undecided, and whether it will suffice is even less certain.

## Rich Countries Push Back

Every member state arrives in Brussels armed with spreadsheets, trying to draw as much as possible from the EU budget while contributing as little as possible. Rich net contributors such as Germany and the Netherlands argue that €1,780 billion is still too high, since they pay in more than they receive back.

Belgium shares that instinct. De Wever told parliament this week that his country also wants a lower contribution, though where exactly Belgium believes savings should fall is less clear. Under Costa's proposal, the agriculture and cohesion budgets are already relatively smaller than in 2021–2027. Belgium opposes further cuts to farming, and a group of 17 countries is resisting reductions in both agricultural and regional aid. Belgium would rather see more money flow to the European fund meant to strengthen competitiveness.

## Where the €1.5 Billion Comes From

The numbers facing De Wever are concrete. In 2021–2027, Belgium's contribution averaged €4.25 billion a year. Under Costa's proposal, that would rise to €5.5 billion a year — an increase of €1.25 billion annually. Adding Belgium's share of the interest costs on the Ukraine loans (€250 million a year) brings the total increase to €1.5 billion a year, or more than €10.5 billion across the 2028–2034 period.

That figure is not fixed. Belgium is fighting to preserve a lucrative customs arrangement. The Port of Antwerp takes in enormous volumes of goods from outside the EU, meaning Belgium collects substantial import duties and passes them on to Brussels. It currently keeps 25% — roughly €750 million a year — as a collection fee. The Commission wants to cut that to 10%, as reflected in Costa's proposal. Belgium, alongside the Netherlands and its Port of Rotterdam, wants to keep the 25%. If they succeed, officials calculate Belgium's contribution would fall by €600 million a year.

## A Bigger Budget That Is Actually Smaller

On paper, the leap from €1,250 billion to €1,780 billion looks dramatic — a rise of €530 billion. Measured against the size of the European economy, however, the increase is barely noticeable. €1,250 billion equals about 1% of EU GDP for 2021–2027, while €1,780 billion equals about 1.07% of estimated GDP for 2028–2034, since GDP itself grows through inflation and economic expansion.

Part of the nominal rise is legacy debt. The EU borrowed to fund post-COVID recovery through NextGenerationEU, and that money must now be repaid. Repayments plus interest account for more than €150 billion. Exclude them, and the EU budget comes to just 0.97% of GDP — the lowest level ever.

Yet member states must find billions extra for their own priorities: rising interest bills, pensions and defence. That leaves little room to give Europe more. "The EU budget has never fallen so low," VRT noted.

The European Parliament, meanwhile, demands an increase to €2,200 billion, and no MFF can be approved without its consent. Its negotiators argue that a euro spent through the EU budget delivers more value than a euro spent separately by each member state.

## Searching for New Revenue

With member states reluctant to pay more, Costa and von der Leyen have looked for fresh income. Costa proposes an EU levy on the turnover that large digital players such as Google and Meta book in Europe, expected to raise €5 billion a year, alongside a share of tobacco excise duties and revenues from auctioning emissions allowances. Together, all new revenue sources would be expected to bring in around €60 billion a year.

The problem, as VRT put it, is that "there is not a single new European tax that everyone answers with an emphatic yes to." As the Council of the EU confirms, the Commission proposes five new types of own resources — emissions trading revenues, a carbon border adjustment mechanism share, a tobacco excise share, an e-waste levy and a corporate contribution from large companies — designed to generate an additional €58.2 billion per year and ease pressure on national budgets. None has yet won unanimous backing.

## What's Next

Reaching agreement this week looks unlikely; a fresh attempt is expected in December. Leaders want a political deal before the end of 2026 so that legislation can be adopted in 2027 and the budget can start on 1 January 2028. For Belgium, the stakes are twofold: fending off an EU bill that could swell by €1.5 billion a year while shielding its customs rebate, and doing so as it wrestles with its own budget crisis. Both are reminders that in the EU's budget arithmetic, the headline billions tell only part of the story.
