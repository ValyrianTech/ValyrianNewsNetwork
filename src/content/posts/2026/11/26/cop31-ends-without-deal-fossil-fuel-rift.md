# COP31 Ends Without a Deal as Fossil Fuel Rift Sinks Pact

The COP31 UN climate summit closed in Antalya, Türkiye, on Sunday, 22 November, without adopting an overarching political agreement after the EU, the UK and a coalition of climate-vulnerable nations rejected a final draft that failed to mention fossil fuels at all. The talks, scheduled to end on 20 November, overran by more than 44 hours — making Antalya the longest UN climate summit on record — before COP31 President Murat Kurum confirmed that consensus could not be reached.

"We recognise that there is no consensus on this document. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted," Kurum, Türkiye's environment minister, told the closing plenary, according to [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/11/1168855).

## A Collapse Rooted in Fossil Fuels

The central obstacle was language on fossil fuels. More than 130 countries demanded an explicit restatement of the COP28 Dubai pledge to "transition away from fossil fuels," but the final draft omitted any direct reference, recalling the UAE Consensus only "in its entirety" — a weaker reference-by-reminder, as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/22/cop31-climate-talks-set-to-end-in-deep-division-as-key-deal-rejected) reported. Saudi Arabia and Russia resisted stronger wording on coal, oil and gas.

The draft also included provisions to expand carbon capture and storage (CCS) to "gigatonne level by 2035," which critics argued would perpetuate fossil fuel use. Saudi Arabia defended CCS on behalf of the Arab League, rejecting suggestions that the technology amounted to greenwashing.

Speaking for the EU, Ireland's Environment Minister Darragh O'Brien said the proposed "Antalya Pact" failed to meet the moment. "We will not endorse a cover decision or an Antalya Pact that does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change," he said. His intervention drew applause, and was followed by objections from Panama, the Marshall Islands and Norway.

## Finance Gap Left Unresolved

Climate finance proved an equally intractable divide. An early draft named $120 billion per year by 2035 for adaptation finance, but the figure was dropped and never restored. The final text only "recalled" Article 9.1 of the Paris Agreement, citing UNEP's estimate that developing countries need roughly $305 billion per year for adaptation through 2035 — with no new numbers or baseline attached.

Ghana's environment minister, Zanetor Agyeman-Rawlings, captured the frustration of many developing nations, saying: "We didn't come here to beg." India, speaking for the Like-Minded Developing Countries bloc, pressed the equity question, asking whose historical emissions had exhausted the global carbon budget.

Notably, the split did not follow a simple rich-versus-poor line. Climate-vulnerable developing countries objected alongside the EU, while the developing-country LMDC bloc said it was prepared to move forward despite reservations.

## What Survived — and What Fell

The summit did not collapse entirely. A series of technical and procedural decisions were adopted separately and still stand, including on the "just transition," the Green Climate Fund, and the COP31 Action Agenda on electrification, resilience and investment. Earlier agreements, including the Paris Agreement and the 2023 UAE Consensus, remain in force.

But several anticipated outcomes fell with the failed pact: a two-year work programme on mountains and glaciers, the Antalya–Addis Ababa road map on adaptation finance, a high-level dialogue on overshooting 1.5°C, and a report on measuring the pledge to triple adaptation finance.

UN Climate Change Executive Secretary Simon Stiell acknowledged the disappointment while pointing to partial progress. "The world cannot afford a repeat of this moment," he said in [closing remarks](https://unfccc.int/news/un-climate-change-executive-secretary-simon-stiell-remarks-at-the-closing-plenary-of-cop31), while noting real gains on a just transition and the Action Agenda.

## Deep Disappointment, Divergent Framing

UN Secretary-General António Guterres voiced the sharpest response. "There may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science," he said, warning that current national plans still put the world on course for roughly 2.6°C of warming. "We can't put out the fire while pouring fuel on it," he added, describing fossil fuels as the primary driver of warming.

Reactions diverged sharply. Bill Hare of Climate Analytics called it "a real line in the sand moment," with countries "standing up to fossil fuel interests and saying no." Mohamed Adow of Power Shift Africa branded it "the worst COP ever," accusing Europe of hypocrisy.

China's delegation head, Vice Minister of Ecology and Environment Li Gao, expressed deep disappointment but said the failure should not erode confidence in multilateralism, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20261123/dd34253be20e45e793580b9e6ab3a67b/c.html). He argued that recriminations do not build trust and that global climate governance "has never been a smooth path."

## What's Next

Attention now shifts to COP32 in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in November 2027, preceded by mid-year talks in Bonn in June 2027. Delegates will confront many of the same unanswered questions — how to accelerate the transition from fossil fuels and how to mobilize finance for developing countries.

The Antalya failure also revived calls to reform the consensus-based UN climate process, under which any determined group can block an outcome. The closest historical parallel is the 2009 Copenhagen summit, where countries only "took note" of an accord rather than adopting it. Whether Antalya becomes a similar turning point — or a warning — will depend on whether governments can convert the science's clarity into political will before they meet again.
