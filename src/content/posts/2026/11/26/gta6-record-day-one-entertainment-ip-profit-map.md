# GTA6's $3.8B Day One Rewrites the IP Profit Map

On November 19, 2026, Grand Theft Auto VI launched and racked up an estimated US$3.79 billion (about RMB 25.4 billion) in first-day retail sales, publisher Take-Two Interactive announced — a record haul for any entertainment product debut and more than the lifetime global box office of any film ever made. The milestone, reported by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34548215) on November 25, has become a lens for a bigger question: how do successful entertainment franchises actually make their money, and where does China fit in that map?

## A Single Day That Outearns a Blockbuster's Lifetime

The scale is hard to overstate. According to The Paper's data-desk analysis, GTA6's one-day take was roughly ten times previous single-day entertainment records. *Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows* opened to about US$220 million in its first day; the Floyd Mayweather–Manny Pacquiao fight generated roughly US$400 million in U.S. pay-per-view alone. Even *Avengers: Endgame*, the highest-grossing film in history, earned about RMB 19.8 billion (roughly US$2.9 billion) over its entire global run — less than 80% of what GTA6 collected in 24 hours.

The launch was no sudden accident. Pre-orders opened on June 25, 2026, and according to [Sina Finance](https://finance.sina.com.cn/tech/discovery/2026-11-21/doc-inizmwan6885629.shtml), a record 32.5 million copies had been sold before release. [Sohu](https://www.sohu.com/a/1104488647_122348420) noted that GTA6's first day already exceeded its predecessor's first three days combined — about 4.7 times GTA5's opening-day revenue of roughly US$800 million.

Rockstar Games confirmed the figures and the record-setting 99 Metacritic score, with studio president Sam Houser thanking players worldwide, as [reported](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34526420) by Red Star News via The Paper.

## Why Games Out-Earn Film: The Retention Machine

The gap between gaming and film is structural, not accidental. Box-office revenue is largely collected within weeks of release; games keep earning long after launch.

GTA5, released in 2013, still drew an average of about 122,000 concurrent Steam players over the past year — 2.2 times its first-year PC figures, per The Paper's analysis. Take-Two's fiscal 2026 results show that "recurring consumer spending" — virtual currency, add-on content, and in-game purchases — accounted for 78% of the company's full-year net revenue. Research firm MoffettNathanson estimates GTA5 has generated about US$10.78 billion in cumulative revenue as of March 2026, a figure [Sina Finance](https://finance.sina.com.cn/tech/roll/2026-11-24/doc-inizvzia8904597.shtml) and others have cited in framing the launch as the largest debut of any creative work in entertainment history.

That live-service model explains why one launch can rival the entire theatrical economy of a major studio — and why the comparison to film may grow starker over time.

## The IP Business-Model Spectrum

GTA's money still comes mostly from inside its game world. Hello Kitty's, by contrast, can appear almost anywhere — on stationery, clothes, cosmetics, even aircraft and hotel rooms. As the IP holder, Sanrio does not make every product itself; it licenses the character to other companies.

By contrast, Pop Mart, which earns from character IP too, keeps most of the value chain for itself. The Paper's analysis of company filings found striking differences in licensing revenue as a share of total income for the fiscal year ending March 2026: Toei Animation, owner of *One Piece* and *Dragon Ball*, drew 51.7% of revenue from outbound licensing; Sanrio reached 49.7%; Tsuburaya Productions, home of Ultraman, reached 44.5%. Pop Mart, by comparison, reported 2025 revenue of about RMB 37.1 billion but only about RMB 71 million in licensing fees.

That restraint is deliberate. Pop Mart founder Wang Ning said in a 2026 interview that the company could license IP to other theme parks but "we want the capability to operate a park ourselves," and revealed the firm paused some external partnerships after LABUBU took off. In his view, good partnerships help an IP grow; bad ones can wear it down.

## Where the Margins Actually Sit

Which model is more profitable? A 2025 Guosen Securities research note cited by The Paper maps the IP-toy supply chain: mature-IP licensing can carry gross margins of roughly 70% to 90%; developing products around one's own IP exceeds 60%; developing products under third-party licenses runs about 40% to 60%; and contract manufacturing earns just 10% to 15%.

For a mature IP, the same rights can be reused across many manufacturers. A character moves from toys to clothing to food packaging, and the rights holder earns new licensing income at each step — the mechanism that keeps multiplying an IP's commercial value. But consumer affection has cycles, and no IP is guaranteed a steady profit. Tsuburaya's licensing revenue from China roughly halved year-on-year in the fiscal year ending March 2026, dragged down by weaker card and building-block toy sales.

## China's IP Export Push

China is increasingly an IP exporter, not just a market. According to a count by Leopard News based on company filings and public data cited by The Paper, as of August 2026 seven of the world's 20 highest-earning IPs were Chinese — five games and two trendy toys.

The momentum is strongest in games. China's self-developed games earned US$18.14 billion overseas in the first three quarters of 2026, close to 90% of full-year 2025 revenue, per the China Audio-video and Digital Publishing Association. The United States, Japan, and South Korea are the top three markets, together contributing more than half of overseas revenue. *Genshin Impact* is the showcase: Sensor Tower put its overseas mobile player spending at US$2.5 billion within two years of launch, and its traditional-opera-inspired song "Divine Damsel of Devastation" has drawn nearly 13 million views on YouTube's official English channel.

The broader market favors gaming too. PwC's Global Entertainment & Media Outlook puts China's 2025 gaming and esports market at about US$76.5 billion — nearly ten times the country's box office. Beyond mobile games, *Black Myth: Wukong* became a global AAA phenomenon, and Pop Mart's Americas revenue reached RMB 6.81 billion in 2025, up 748.4% year-on-year.

Yet the export story remains fragmented. Wang Yilan, deputy director of the Film Industry Research Department at the China Film Art Research Center, told Beijing Daily that China's games and web novels already lead film and television overseas, but "promotion of various cultural products abroad still goes its own way, without forming a combination punch." She urged a "broad culture" export mindset and full IP-chain planning so that a single hit can be converted into lasting industry value.

## What's Next

The GTA6 record will be hard to match in the near term, but the deeper story is the business model behind it: continuous monetization, a diverse licensing spectrum, and a widening global market for cultural franchises. For China, the question is whether its breakout hits — in games, toys, and beyond — can be stitched into coordinated franchises rather than isolated successes. As *Black Myth: Wukong* producer Feng Ji told CCTV, the goal is to make it fun first, so players will later feel what lies behind it. Players need not read *Journey to the West* to start the pilgrimage — but having started, they may want to read the original and see the ancient buildings for themselves. The next chapter of the IP profit map may depend on how well China turns that impulse into an industry.
