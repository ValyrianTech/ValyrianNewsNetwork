# FCC Clears SpaceX for 1 Million Orbital Data Centers

The Trump administration on Wednesday authorized SpaceX to deploy up to 1 million satellites designed to function as data centers in orbit, approving an audacious goal of Elon Musk's rocket company while drawing sharp skepticism and alarm from scientists, astronomers and rival operators.

According to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/spacex-data-center-elon-musk-b9897fe06f851ffaf92fd304ae69075c), the Federal Communications Commission lauded its decision as a historic first in the global race for artificial intelligence. The one million "orbital compute modules" would form a constellation called "Starmind," processing massive amounts of data for use back on Earth and, in the agency's framing, bringing humanity closer to harnessing "the full power of the sun."

"America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence," FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said in a statement, adding that the move "opens a bold new frontier."

Musk welcomed the decision on social media, calling it a great asset to the country and the world. But critics argue the approval leapfrogs the technical, environmental and safety questions that have shadowed the project since it was proposed.

## A Constellation Built for Compute

SpaceX's plan, filed in late January 2026 alongside its acquisition of the AI company xAI, envisions satellites that process AI workloads in orbit using near-constant solar energy. Proponents say that capturing the sun's power could ease pressure on strained terrestrial power grids and reduce demand for sprawling, water-hungry computing warehouses on Earth.

The FCC's [authorization order](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-1273A1.pdf) requires SpaceX to deploy at least one authorized satellite by 2033, then ramp toward full deployment by 2040. SpaceX President and COO Gwynne Shotwell has said launches could begin as early as 2027, with manufacturing planned at facilities in Texas. The agency said SpaceX's constellation is consistent with its orbital debris rules and that deploying the satellites "serves the public interest."

## The Trouble With Heat — and Junk

Space is cold, but it is also a vacuum, trapping heat inside objects much like a thermos. Adam Becker, an astrophysicist and author, called the prospect of a million data-center satellites "a fantasy."

"It's not going to happen," he said Wednesday. He noted there are "basic physical limits" to radiating heat away, and that although distributing the load across many smaller satellites could theoretically help, it would be expensive and difficult.

Becker also warned of a cascading risk. "You could end up with a cascading set of space junk collisions," he said, noting such crashes could create "a cloud of debris" in low Earth orbit — where many weather and telecommunications satellites operate.

The FCC acknowledged it received a "substantial number" of public comments, including petitions to deny the request, but maintained it had dismissed topics outside its authority, such as environmental concerns. In its ruling, the agency did not require further environmental review under federal law, a decision at the heart of the mounting backlash.

## An Astronomical Backlash

The American Astronomical Society, satellite communications company Viasat and Amazon-owned Kuiper Systems were among those urging the regulator to deny the application. In a [statement](https://aas.org/press/spacex-data-center-fcc-approval), the AAS said it was "disheartened and dismayed" by the decision, warning that the constellation would undermine decades of federal investment in observatories and "the ability of astronomers and the public to enjoy and study the night sky."

"The proposed constellation involves larger (and therefore brighter) and more numerous objects," the AAS said, noting that "these satellites would therefore be visible as extremely bright objects for longer periods in the night sky." Becker echoed the point: "It also is pollution of the night sky to the naked eye."

Environmental advocates went further. In a [statement](https://earthjustice.org/press/2026/fcc-approves-spacex-request-to-launch-one-million-data-centers-into-space), Earthjustice senior attorney Jan Hasselman accused the agency of sidestepping the law and releasing the decision on the eve of a holiday to soften scrutiny. "It is dismaying that the FCC has ignored the law so blatantly and tried to mask the fact with a holiday filing," he said.

## Why the Decision Matters

The approval is the first of its kind for an orbital data center constellation, and it signals a policy shift toward treating space as critical AI infrastructure rather than purely a communications medium. Carr has explicitly framed the decision as part of extending America's lead in superintelligence, elevating the matter from a technical curiosity to a question of national competitiveness.

Yet the agency also declined to weigh in on whether the business is feasible at all, reasoning that doing so would substitute the government's judgment for that of private enterprise. That stance leaves the hardest questions — heat dissipation at scale, the absence of an in-orbit repair crew, five-year satellite lifespans, and the sheer scale of launching a million objects — unresolved.

Musk is not alone in the orbital-compute race. Rival operators including Starcloud and Blue Origin have filed their own ambitious constellations, and Google has begun testing AI chips in orbit. The FCC has said it intends to rule on other applications "expeditiously," suggesting Wednesday's decision is less an endpoint than an opening salvo.

## What's Next

SpaceX's launch targets begin in 2027, with incremental deployment milestones stretching to 2040. In the meantime, environmental groups are weighing legal challenges, and astronomers are pressing for stronger review before any orbital data center licenses advance. The coming years will test whether the promise of computing beyond Earth can be squared with the physics, the night sky and the safety of the orbits that satellites — and the people who depend on them — share.

"It's a way of telling the others we will keep these low launch costs for myself," Eurospace research director Pierre Lionnet said of the dynamic, according to an earlier [AP explainer](https://apnews.com/article/elon-musk-orbital-ai-data-centers-xai-spacex-92bc8ad95593bf3b5b801ddf36427194). "It's a kind of powerplay."
