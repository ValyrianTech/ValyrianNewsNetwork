# China's Top Courts Move to Criminalize Doping Cheats

China's Supreme People's Court and Supreme People's Procuratorate on 26 November jointly issued a judicial interpretation aimed at strictly punishing doping-related crimes, sharpening the legal tools available to prosecutors and closing loopholes that had left parts of the doping supply chain beyond clear criminal reach. The interpretation, formally numbered SPC/SPP Legal Interpretation No. 20 [2026], takes effect on 1 December and replaces an earlier 2019 version.

The measure was adopted by the Supreme People's Court Adjudication Committee on 14 September and by the Supreme People's Procuratorate's 14th Procuratorial Committee on 30 October, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/legal/20261126/14c0a85715e047e1a1683da1d945bda9/c.html), which published the full text. It runs to 14 articles and is designed to operationalise the standalone crime of "impairing anti-doping management" (妨害兴奋剂管理罪), added to China's Criminal Law by Amendment (XI) in 2021.

## Why It Matters

The interpretation gives prosecutors concrete definitions for a crime that had, until now, lacked clear thresholds. When lawmakers created the offence in 2021, they set a penalty of up to three years' imprisonment plus a fine for inducing, instigating or deceiving athletes into using doping at major domestic or international competitions — but they left key terms such as "serious circumstances" undefined. The 2026 interpretation fills that gap.

The move also lands two years after the 2019 interpretation, which had worked by shoehorning doping conduct into existing offences such as smuggling, illegal business operation and food-safety crimes. The new text keeps that surrounding net while building a dedicated, chain-wide enforcement framework.

## What the Rules Actually Say

Under Article 1, prosecutors can treat doping offences as "serious" — and therefore criminal — where the conduct is repeated or targets multiple athletes, where the offender re-offends within two years of a prior administrative or industry penalty, where an athlete's physical or mental health is seriously harmed, or where the conduct targets minor or disabled athletes. Illegal gains of 50,000 yuan (about $7,000) or more, or egregious social impact, also qualify.

Article 10 defines "organising" — which carries heavier penalties under the second paragraph of the underlying offence — as planning, leading or directing three or more athletes to use doping.

Beyond elite competition, the interpretation reaches into education and public service. Article 3 brings the organisation of doping in physical or fitness tests tied to national exams — including university admissions and civil-service recruitment — under the crime of "organising cheating." Article 2 allows coaches and sports-training staff who administer doping to minors or people with disabilities outside major competitions to be charged with abusing a person under their guardianship or care.

A key deterrent provision appears in Article 9. An athlete who uses doping after qualifying for a major competition has committed the offence even if they never actually take part. "Whether an athlete actually competes does not affect the determination of the crime," the interpretation states.

The smuggling and supply side is covered in Articles 4 through 7. Importing or exporting prohibited doping-catalogue substances, or restricted substances such as anabolic agents and peptide hormones without a licence, can constitute smuggling of goods prohibited from import or export. Companies can also be fined, with liability extending to responsible managers. Producing or selling food or medicine containing doping substances may be prosecuted under food-safety or counterfeit-drug statutes.

Article 13 sets up a referral mechanism — often described as the link between criminal and administrative enforcement (行刑衔接) — under which conduct that does not meet the criminal threshold but breaches regulations is passed to the relevant authority for administrative sanction or official discipline.

## The Policy Backdrop

The interpretation is the judicial complement to a policy push that has gathered pace through 2026. At the National Anti-Doping Work Conference in Beijing on 20 July, General Administration of Sport Director Gao Zhidan called for a "clean national team anti-doping ecosystem" and for ensuring "zero occurrence" of doping problems at major events such as the Aichi-Nagoya Asian Games and the Los Angeles Olympics, according to the [General Administration of Sport](https://www.sport.gov.cn/n20001099/n20001302/n20067029/n20067581/c29783719/content.html).

Significantly, Gao also urged the authorities to "further solidify anti-doping governance in key weak-link areas: social sports, youth sports and student sports" — a focus the interpretation echoes by reaching beyond elite competition into schools, fitness tests and grassroots sport. He tied the effort to the "15th Five-Year Plan" period and a "deepening governance year" campaign.

## Analysis: A Deterrence-First Posture

As [CCTV](https://news.cctv.com/2026/11/26/ARTIvvBmzxXV1yeXRrMizHRN261126.shtml) reported, the Two Highs framed the interpretation as providing "strong judicial safeguards for deepening anti-doping governance and building a sports powerhouse." The phrase signals that the measure serves a dual purpose: cleaning up domestic sport and projecting a credible anti-doping image internationally.

The timing is unlikely to be coincidental. With the Asian Games under way and the Los Angeles Olympics two years out, China has an interest in demonstrating that its legal system can punish doping as a serious crime rather than a regulatory slip. Past episodes — including contamination cases that drew scrutiny from the World Anti-Doping Agency — have made the credibility of China's anti-doping regime a matter of international attention.

The focus on coaches, trainers and the athlete's entourage is a notable shift. Under the new text, the people around an athlete face sharper liability, particularly where minors or people with disabilities are involved. That reflects a broader understanding that doping is rarely a solitary act; it typically involves a supply chain and a support network.

[The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34562509), citing CCTV News, highlighted that the interpretation "refines the criminalisation standards" for the doping offence — a technical-sounding phrase with practical consequences. Clearer thresholds mean more predictable prosecutions and fewer cases slipping through on definitional ambiguity.

[China National Radio](https://news.cnr.cn/native/gd/kx/20261126/t20261126_527883653.shtml) reported the same core provisions, noting that the interpretation will guide local courts and procuratorates in applying the law uniformly.

## What to Watch

The clearest test will be enforcement volume. If prosecutions under the doping offence visibly rise in 2027 — especially in youth, school and grassroots sport — the interpretation will have changed behaviour, not just the statute book. Whether the criminal-to-administrative referral mechanism produces real follow-up sanctions will be another indicator.

Several questions remain open. The interpretation leaves terms such as "egregious social impact" and "serious harm to health" to be proven case by case, and how courts will handle the interaction with international anti-doping standards and cross-border smuggling cases is not yet clear. Whether enforcement reaches the commercial supplement and pharmaceutical supply chains that feed doping will also be closely watched.

For now, the message from Beijing is unambiguous: doping is no longer treated as a rule-book violation to be handled by sports officials alone. From 1 December, it sits squarely within the reach of China's criminal law.
