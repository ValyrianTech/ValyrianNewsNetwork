# Black Friday carts fill with necessities, not just gifts

This Black Friday, Americans are shopping with a different instinct: stock up, not just splurge. Shoppers are tossing toiletries, cleaning supplies and basic clothing into their carts alongside the flat-screen TVs and Lego sets, a shift analysts say reflects persistent inflation and a new reliance on artificial intelligence to hunt for value. Online spending is running hot—but a growing share of it is going toward things people need, not just what they want.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/black-friday-shopping-ai-essentials-5e542ffd1f94f24e4ffb79d412223544), shoppers are "stocking up on discounted shampoo while grabbing a flat-screen TV and several Lego sets for under the tree." The mix tells a story of a budget-conscious consumer navigating a strange economic moment defined by lackluster confidence, stubbornly high prices and a willingness to keep spending anyway.

## Online spending beats expectations

Early holiday numbers suggest the consumer is far from tapped out. U.S. consumers spent $82.5 billion online from Nov. 1 to Nov. 22, a 9.1% jump over the same stretch last year, according to Adobe Digital Insights. Adobe projects full-season online sales will grow 6.7% to $275.1 billion, while the National Retail Federation expects 4.1% sales growth across stores and online, totaling about $1.01 trillion.

The critical caveat: roughly half of that growth is expected to come from higher prices rather than shoppers buying more. But this year differs from recent ones in a key way. Salesforce reports that November discounts are 8% bigger than last year, pushing average selling prices of physical goods 1% below last November's levels.

"In the last several years, a lot of the (sales) gains have been from higher prices," Caila Schwartz, a retail and consumer insights leader at Salesforce, told AP. "This is not what we are seeing this year."

## Necessities move into the basket

The defining behavior this season is what might be called the mixed basket. Adobe data show online sales of personal hygiene products up 40% from Nov. 1 to Nov. 22 compared with the October average, household cleaning products up 33%, and clothing basics up 27%.

"Usually, when you think of holiday shopping, it's premium handbags and jewelry and those types of items, but we're fast seeing shoppers also use this season to stock up on the essentials," said Vivek Pandya, director of digital insights at Adobe.

Schwartz put it more plainly: "It's not just a moment where shoppers are buying gifts. They're also buying things that they need, and so that's going to help drive up a lot of the spend."

## Why costs keep biting

The frugal mood has roots in a persistent cost-of-living squeeze. As [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/10/nx-s1-6034518/inflation-consumer-prices-economy-gas-prices) reported, consumer prices rose 0.4% in October from the prior month—a bigger-than-forecast increase, with housing accounting for roughly a third and grocery prices up sharply. Because last year's shutdown idled government price-checkers, the report lacked its usual year-over-year reading; the Cleveland Fed estimated prices were up about 3.7% over the year, outpacing average wage gains of 3.1%. In other words, the typical worker is losing buying power.

Fuel costs tied to the U.S. war with Iran remain elevated, with gasoline still about a dollar a gallon higher than when the conflict began—though the national average has dipped below $4. Diesel, at $6.15 a gallon, feeds into freight and imported-goods costs.

## AI becomes a spending accelerant

If inflation is making shoppers more cautious, AI is making them more efficient—and, evidence suggests, more free-spending. Traffic to retail sites from links in AI-powered tools more than doubled this month compared with November 2025, according to Adobe.

Retail giants report that AI-assisted shoppers fill bigger baskets. Walmart CEO John Furner said customers using the company's Sparky assistant spent 40% more per order than those who didn't, while Amazon CEO Andy Jassy said U.S. shoppers using Alexa spend 50% more than non-users.

## The bottom line

Black Friday remains the year's biggest day for physical store traffic, with TVs expected to average 23% off on Friday versus just 11% during the first 22 days of November. But its role is shifting—from the season's starting gun to the deep-discount finale of a promotions calendar that now stretches across all of November.

The year's spending will hinge on a central tension: falling gas prices and resilient consumer demand on one side, elevated inflation and a Federal Reserve that may yet raise rates on the other. For now, Americans are doing what they've done all year—keeping spending, but with an eye firmly on value.
