# As Holiday Costs Rise, Cannabis Is Getting Cheaper

This Thanksgiving, Americans are paying more for nearly everything—travel, turkeys, and the wine on the family table. But one commodity purchased at unusually high volumes this week is getting cheaper: legal cannabis. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/25/nx-s1-6046173/cannabis-marijuana-prices-retailers-discounts), the days before Thanksgiving have become one of the cannabis industry's biggest sales events of the year, with dispensaries offering steep discounts even as they struggle through a revenue decline.

Known in the industry as "Green Wednesday," the day before the holiday now rivals the stoner holiday 4/20 for the most sales and highest foot traffic of the year. [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/petersu/2026/11/21/green-wednesday-now-outsells-black-friday-at-dispensaries/) reports that sales run about double a normal Wednesday, with transactions jumping more than 80% as shoppers stock up. Seed-to-sale tracking firm BioTrack expects roughly $147 million in nationwide sales for the holiday in 2026.

## A Ritual Built on Discounts

The rise of Green Wednesday reflects how thoroughly cannabis has been folded into the family-centered holiday. At The Forest, a dispensary in Baltimore, shoppers this week stocked up on gummies for Friendsgiving, THC-infused beverages for curious in-laws, and pre-rolls for what has become a recognized cultural ritual: the "cousin walk."

"You know, you take a little breaky-poo with your cousins, and we live in the country, so we just walk, usually before dinner," said Kelley Vinton, 36, a medical marijuana patient and regular customer. "We smoke a joint, and we get nice and high, and get some munchies and get ready for dinner."

The store's screens advertised 25% off edibles, 30% off for medical patients, and an extra $5 off for bringing in canned food for a holiday drive. Yet the discounts are a symptom of something less festive than they appear.

## Cheap Weed, Strained Operators

At roughly $30 billion, the U.S. legal cannabis market spans 24 states and Washington, D.C., according to Whitney Economics. But in 2025 the industry recorded its first annual revenue decline since Colorado and Washington began recreational sales in 2014—falling to $29.1 billion from $30.1 billion the year before.

"It's forced a race to the bottom," said Beau Whitney, founder and chief economist at Whitney Economics. "What this is resulting in is great prices for the consumer, they're spending less and getting the same or more, so that's good for them on an affordability perspective. But it's hurting operators."

The root cause, Whitney said, is a change in customer behavior that arrived "literally overnight" in 2024, when inflation pushed up the cost of essentials. Shoppers began taking their cannabis discounts and diverting the savings to gas and groceries, adjusting what they buy to fit their personal budgets.

The discount culture has also dragged prices down, creating a spiral where customers come to expect markdowns. Industry data points to the same dynamic at the wholesale level: [CannIntel](https://www.cannintel.com/business/cannabis-price-compression-accelerates-mature-markets-2026) reports wholesale cannabis flower prices fell 18% to 40% across mature state markets in the first half of 2026, driven by oversupply as cultivators licensed during the 2023-2024 expansion wave reached full production capacity.

## A Retail Model Under Pressure

The economics are unforgiving for shop owners, for whom a strong holiday week is essential. The industry has seen only a modest rebound in 2026—about 5% in sales versus the same period last year—and forecasts for the end of the year and 2027 remain cautious.

The trend plays out unevenly across markets. In newer states, Green Wednesday hasn't yet taken root the way it has in places like California. "It hasn't caught on in New York the way it has in more established markets," said Vanessa Yee-Chan, who owns the Alta Dispensary in New York.

The regulatory picture remains fragmented. The federal government still classifies recreational marijuana as a Schedule I drug, though medical cannabis [was reclassified this year](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/23/nx-s1-5797245/marijuana-rules-reschedule-medical-legalization) to the less restrictive Schedule III. The Drug Enforcement Administration's broader hearing on rescheduling recreational cannabis was held in June, and anti-legalization groups have vowed to fight further changes in court.

## What's Next

For consumers, the affordability paradox offers a genuine bright spot at a time of stretched budgets—more product for less money, with stocking up before the holiday a natural habit. But for the industry, cheap weed is less a sign of abundance than a market correcting under the weight of oversupply and a discount-dependent customer base.

The question heading into 2027 is whether the modest rebound holds or whether 2025's revenue decline marks the beginning of a longer contraction. Much depends on the looming rescheduling decision and whether newer markets can replicate the holiday surges that mature states now take for granted.
