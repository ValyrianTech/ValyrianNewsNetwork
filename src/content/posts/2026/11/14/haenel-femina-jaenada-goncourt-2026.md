# Haenel Takes Femina as Jaenada Wins Goncourt

The French autumn literary prize season delivered its customary one-two punch this month, with the Prix Femina awarded to Yannick Haenel for *La solitude des professeurs est infinie* and the Prix Goncourt going to Philippe Jaenada for *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel*. The recognitions, announced on consecutive days in Paris, crown two of the year's most discussed works of French fiction — and, for Haenel, arrived barely 24 hours after he narrowly lost the Goncourt.

## A Consolation Prize, By Tradition

The Femina jury of 12 women gave Haenel the prize on the second round of voting on 4 November, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576), at a ceremony held at the Dior headquarters in Paris. He prevailed over Emma Marsantes (*N'efface pas mes cercles*, Verdier) and Lucie Rico (*Mais que fait ce sang sur le pull de Jennifer ?*, P.O.L), each of whom took two votes.

The night before, the nine members of the Académie Goncourt had given six votes to Jaenada and three to Haenel, making the latter the runner-up. That the defeated finalist should take a major prize the following day is a well-worn quirk of the French prize calendar — a consolation that keeps the tightly interconnected juries shaping the canon each autumn.

At the Femina ceremony, Haenel dedicated his book "to teachers, high-school and middle-school students," a gesture made all the more pointed by the student protest movement unfolding across France at the time.

## The Goncourt and a Historic Renaudot

The Goncourt, announced on 3 November from the steps of the Drouant restaurant by Académie president Philippe Claudel, honoured Jaenada's investigation into the murder of a young Breton woman in the late 1940s. "He makes visible a woman who had been made invisible, a victim, a young victim whom the police did not concern themselves with much," Claudel said, as [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/goncourt-2026-le-prestigieux-prix-a-ete-attribue-a-philippe-jaenada-pour-son-livre-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel-11809864).

"I feel joyful," the 62-year-old Jaenada said on arrival at Drouant. He succeeds Laurent Mauvignier, whose *La Maison Vide* won in 2025 and has since become a publishing phenomenon. Jaenada, himself a former Femina laureate for *La Serpe* in 2017, has built a devoted readership with sprawling true-crime narratives that blend novelistic drive with archival digging.

The same day, the Prix Renaudot — the historic companion prize announced alongside the Goncourt — went to François-Henri Désérable for *Le Palais* (Gallimard), winning six votes to four. It was the [100th edition of the Renaudot](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-prix-renaudot-2026-attribue-a-francois-henri-deserable-pour-le-palais-11809873).

## A Season Shadowed by Controversy

This year's prizes also carried a heavier subtext. The novel *C'était ça ou mourir* by Thélyson Orélien, once touted as a favourite, was removed from the Goncourt selection and eliminated from other prizes over suspicions of AI use — a sign of deepening anxiety about artificial intelligence in French letters. Jaenada himself later publicly claimed a "moderate and playful" use of the technology, adding a further wrinkle to the debate.

More strikingly, on 6 November more than 200 French authoresses published an op-ed in *Libération* denouncing what they called the "sexism" and "patriarchal insider system" of the major literary prizes, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-en-france-plus-de-200-autrices-denoncent-dans-une-tribune-le-sexisme-du-systeme-11811966). The intervention, which reportedly raised the question of quotas, was a direct response to a 2026 palmarès dominated by male winners — Jaenada, Désérable and Haenel among them.

## Analysis: Prizes, Sales and the Shaping of a Canon

Beyond the ceremonies, the practical stakes are considerable. In France, a Goncourt or Femina win typically triggers a substantial sales surge, carrying a title through the crucial holiday season. The dual recognition of Jaenada and Haenel is expected to lift both books — and to extend the commercial life of a slate of finalists whose names, for a few weeks at least, dominate bookshop displays and literary conversation.

The two awards also illustrate how concentrated the machinery of French literary consecration remains. The same small circle of juries, meeting within days of one another and often reading the same shortlists, effectively decides which voices reach the widest audience. That insularity is precisely what the 200-plus signatories of the *Libération* op-ed have now put in the spotlight.

For Haenel, the Femina caps a season in which his subject — the solitude and strain of teaching, written in the shadow of the assassinations of teachers Samuel Paty in 2020 and Dominique Bernard in 2023 — resonated far beyond the literary pages. His novel, a fictionalised account of his own early years teaching in the Paris suburbs, arrived at a moment when the teaching profession's difficulties were again the subject of public anxiety, a resonance that extended to French-speaking Belgium.

## What's Next

Attention now turns to whether the sexism controversy translates into pressure for reform — quotas, greater transparency, or a broader rethink of how juries are composed — in coming seasons. The Orélien disqualification, meanwhile, signals that prize committees may move to formalise rules on AI-assisted text.

For readers, the immediate consequence is simpler: two celebrated novels, now carrying the season's most coveted stamps, heading into the year-end with the wind at their backs.
