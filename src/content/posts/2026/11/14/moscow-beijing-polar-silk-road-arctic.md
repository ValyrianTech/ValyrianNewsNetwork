# Moscow and Beijing Build a Polar Silk Road in the Far North

Russia and China are quietly assembling a strategic maritime partnership across the Arctic, pairing Russian ports and sea routes with Chinese capital, shipping demand and technology. The rapprochement, which Western capitals view with mounting alarm, is reshaping the geopolitics of the Far North as melting ice opens previously inaccessible waters to commercial traffic and sharpens competition for the region's resources, according to an [analysis published by RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719).

Can Moscow and Beijing turn the Arctic into their private preserve? That is the question driving the analysis, which traces how the two powers are building what Xi Jinping first called a "Polar Silk Road" in 2017 — a vision now moving from rhetoric to infrastructure.

## Why the Arctic Matters

Climate change is the physical engine behind the geopolitical shift. The Arctic is warming up to four times faster than the global average, opening commercial sea routes while the seabed's resources sharpen appetites. According to estimates from the U.S. Geological Survey, the region holds nearly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and about 13% of its undiscovered oil.

The centrepiece is the Northern Sea Route (NSR), which dramatically shortens distances between Europe and East Asia. The journey from Shanghai to Hamburg takes roughly 18 days via the Arctic, compared with 30 to 35 days through the Suez Canal. Traffic remains constrained by ice, high insurance premiums, sparse infrastructure and a short navigation season — and Suez flows still vastly exceed Arctic volumes — yet the route's geopolitical and economic significance is increasingly hard to ignore.

## A Deepening Partnership

The most recent illustration of intent came with the visit to China by Nikolai Patrushev, Vladimir Putin's adviser and chairman of the Russian Maritime Board. According to [Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/), discussions covered linking China's Belt and Road initiative with Russia's trans-Arctic transport corridor, developing Russian ports, exchanging technology for automated container terminals and conducting joint maritime research.

Cooperation has widened considerably over the past decade, with the two countries signing a key regional cooperation agreement in 2023. Chinese actors co-finance mega-projects such as Yamal LNG and are significantly expanding their own fleet of nuclear icebreakers. China is Russia's largest trading partner, and bilateral trade exceeded $200 billion for the third consecutive year in 2025.

The motivations are complementary. Moscow wants to develop Arctic sea routes to deliver more oil and gas to China and to circumvent Western sanctions imposed over its war in Ukraine. Beijing seeks an alternative sea route to reduce its dependence on the Strait of Malacca. China's 2018 strategy already described the country as a "near-Arctic State" with "legitimate interests" in shipping, resource exploitation, scientific research and regional governance.

The commercial pull is tangible. In 2026, Russian oil exports via the NSR rose nearly 50% year-on-year, with about 20 million barrels delivered to Asia between July and mid-October, according to [Modern Diplomacy](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/10/19/russia-ramps-up-arctic-oil-exports-as-global-shipping-crisis-disrupts-trade-routes/), citing Reuters. Disruption to the Suez and Hormuz routes and the war in Ukraine have pushed tanker freight rates to record levels, making the costlier Arctic passage relatively competitive. In August 2026, Chinese shipping firm Sea Legend launched the first regular weekly container service across the Arctic, realising Beijing's long-standing ambition, the [South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/week-asia/economics/article/3364859/india-joins-china-and-south-korea-push-arctic-shipping-routes) reported.

## The Asymmetry of the Alliance

For all the momentum, analysts stress that the relationship is far from equal. Swedish military intelligence chief Thomas Nilsson warned that China will "use" Russia to gain a foothold in the Far North, and that Moscow — which once viewed growing Chinese Arctic influence with disfavour — no longer has a choice, given its dependence on Beijing to finance its war effort and supply dual-use goods such as microprocessors.

"China aspires to superpower status. One attribute of that is domination — or at least a presence — in geopolitically important zones, and the Arctic is undeniably one," said Vlastislav Bříza, a security expert at Charles University in Prague. The [East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/21/china-adapts-to-a-colder-arctic-reception/) noted that China's Arctic ambitions face growing opposition from Western Arctic states, which scrutinise its investments and scientific activity, and judged that Beijing's military capabilities in the region will remain limited in the near term.

## A Militarising North and a Western Response

Security concerns extend well beyond shipping. Russia controls about 53% of the Arctic coastline and has reopened Soviet-era bases, deploying advanced coastal-defence systems, Arctic-adapted S-400 air defences and hypersonic-missile-armed fighters, according to the Polish outlet [Defence24](https://defence24.com/geopolitics/cold-war-at-the-top-of-the-world-the-militarisation-of-the-arctic-and-the-sino-russian-alliance-on-the-northern-sea-route). Western intelligence warns that China may soon follow by deploying its own nuclear submarines.

The Western response has been slowed by a stark capability gap. The United States has only two ageing operational icebreakers, while Russia operates more than 40, including powerful Arktika-class nuclear vessels. "NATO's problem is not geography, but the lack of specialised operational platforms," Defence24 observed. NATO launched its "Arctic Sentry" framework in February 2026 to unify maritime and air patrols, intelligence-sharing and exercises, and several allies, including Sweden and Germany, have deployed fighter jets.

The European Union, meanwhile, unveiled a new Arctic strategy in October 2026 that marks a significant shift in emphasis. "The Arctic is one of the places where the tectonic plates of geopolitics collide," European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said, warning that sovereignty and territorial integrity can no longer be taken for granted. Kaja Kallas, the EU's High Representative for Foreign Affairs, pointed to drone incursions, GPS jamming and the sabotage of undersea cables as threats sapping Arctic security.

The strategy sets out four priorities — security and resilience, climate action, prosperity, and the prospects of Arctic communities — alongside plans to bolster the EU's icebreaker capacity, to monitor the Russian "shadow fleet" more closely and to extend surveillance beyond the Mediterranean and Baltic into the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean.

## What to Watch

The strategic logic is clear, but the commercial reality remains unproven. The Arctic route is geopolitically important yet far from replacing Suez, and its economic model — insurance costs, ice conditions and a short navigable season — is still being tested. The EU's flagship projects hinge on funding from the 2028–2034 budget and member-state buy-in, and transatlantic unity faces its own strains, with Washington's divergent drilling policy clashing with Brussels' insistence that oil, coal and gas "stay in the ground."

What is certain is that the Far North is no longer a distant frontier. As Russia supplies strategic geography and China supplies capital, goods and technology, the two are building a parallel maritime network — one that could fragment global shipping into competing blocs and keep the world's coolest region at the centre of its hottest rivalries.
