# US Airlines Dig Out From Longest Ground Stop in 25 Years

Airlines across the United States canceled thousands of flights on Saturday as they worked to recover from the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years, a four-hour-plus shutdown that stranded passengers and rippled through the global air travel system, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/ground-stop-flights-airlines-cancellations-faa-1fbf4992f0ddaa4c463b0e4990b6e291).

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) halted all U.S. departures at 3:54 p.m. EST on Friday after communications problems affected multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers, the facilities that guide aircraft at high altitudes. The agency [lifted the stop](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/faa-statement-nationwide-ground-stop) at 8:15 p.m. EST after 4 hours and 21 minutes—only the third nationwide ground stop in U.S. history, after the September 11, 2001 attacks and a January 2023 system outage.

## A System Overloaded by Its Own Upgrade

Trump administration officials said the failure stemmed not from a cyberattack but from new technology overloading antiquated equipment. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a contractor had been replacing a section of the system's old copper wiring with fiber when the work somehow overloaded the system, causing "intermittent" communications and radar gaps.

"Our contractor didn't meet the moment," Duffy said. Officials were still investigating whether contractor "incompetence" or a faulty router was to blame—AP later clarified Duffy had pointed to a possible faulty router, not a faulty wire.

At a White House briefing, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there were no "footprints" of a cyberattack and that the problem appeared "mechanically driven." More than 3,000 flights already airborne landed safely, and officials stressed that no one was in danger.

"There was never any lives at risk," Mullin said. "There was no flights at risk."

## Scale of the Disruption

Roughly 11,000 flights carrying more than 1.5 million passengers had been scheduled to depart U.S. airports after 4 p.m. EST on Friday, and more than 3,500 aircraft were in the sky when the order came down. At least 827 flights were diverted.

By Saturday, FlightAware reported more than 4,100 flights canceled, while aviation analytics firm Cirium put the figure above 3,200—tallies that reflect different snapshots of a still-unfolding backlog. More than 2,500 flights were canceled on Saturday alone, with hundreds more delayed.

The effects reached far beyond U.S. borders. Europe's aviation agency suspended U.S.-bound departures at the FAA's request from 5 p.m. Friday until midnight, and London's Heathrow Airport listed several U.S. flights as delayed or canceled on Saturday.

"While U.S. flights have resumed, there may be some follow-on disruption as airlines work to rebook affected passengers and get all services back to normal," said Tim Johnson, consumer director at the U.K. Civil Aviation Authority.

## Why a Four-Hour Stop Cascades

Aviation experts say the disruption compounds because aircraft and crews do not simply fly one route and stop. A single out-of-position plane or crew cascades across a carrier's entire schedule.

"The aircraft doesn't just fly from A to B and back to B and then stop," said Andrew Charlton, managing director of Aviation Advocacy. "It then moves onto C and then over to E and around and around and around."

Charlton likened the FAA's task to performing repairs on a machine that never stops. "What you're watching here is trying to change the tires of your car when the car's going at 80 miles an hour down the expressway, because nothing stops in aviation," he said.

Aviation safety expert Steve Arroyo, a former United Airlines pilot, said the nationwide scale was the key difference this time: controllers could not reliably communicate with pilots.

"If you don't have that ability... you've got to stop airplanes on the ground and not put them in the air, where you're going to risk not being able to communicate with them," Arroyo said.

## A Pattern of Aging-Equipment Failures

Friday's stoppage was the latest in a string of technology failures to snarl U.S. air travel. A September regional communications outage disrupted hundreds of flights across the Northeast after a construction crew cut a fiber optic line, and aging equipment was blamed for brief radar outages that disrupted Newark Liberty International Airport.

The FAA has already replaced thousands of copper wires with fiber optic lines and upgraded hundreds of radio systems as part of a modernization effort that began last year after Congress approved $12.5 billion in funding, according to the agency's [Modern Skies initiative](https://modernskies.faa.gov/#stats). Duffy and the White House are pushing for $30 billion more to complete the work.

Duffy blamed Congress for underfunding modernization while acknowledging the upgrade work itself had overtaxed the system. He warned that more disruptions are likely.

"We didn't think we would have this kind of outage. We did," Duffy said. "We were able to review what went wrong, and then reboot the system and go. But, you will have more of these."

Duffy said the failure was not linked to the FAA's new AI-powered air traffic tool, which the agency has been expanding. "This is all the old legacy equipment," he said. "All of the new equipment that's been deployed did not go down."

## What's Next

FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the air travel schedule should be back to normal by the end of Sunday, Nov. 15, and officials expect normal operations in time for the Thanksgiving travel rush. Bedford said airlines "will still have delays and cancellations" Saturday but promised the "worst is behind us."

Airlines are obligated to offer free rebooking or a refund to travelers whose flights were canceled. Sports leagues adjusted schedules—No. 19 Mississippi's game at Oklahoma was pushed to Sunday—and cargo carrier FedEx activated contingency plans.

The episode is likely to sharpen congressional scrutiny of FAA procurement and its multibillion-dollar modernization contracts. The central questions remain unresolved: whether contractor error, faulty hardware, or the upgrade procedure itself caused the failure, and whether Congress will approve the additional funding Duffy says is needed to keep the system from failing again.
