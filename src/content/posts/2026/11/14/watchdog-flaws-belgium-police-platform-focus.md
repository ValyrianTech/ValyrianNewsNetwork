# Watchdog Flags Flaws in Belgium's New Police Platform Focus

Belgium's police information watchdog has identified serious legal, security and privacy shortcomings in Focus, the digital platform presented as a virtuous fix for the failed I-Police project, according to reporting [published](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-plateforme-numerique-censee-repondre-au-fiasco-d-i-police-presente-de-grave-manquements-11815511) by RTBF and the Flemish newspapers Het Nieuwsblad, Gazet van Antwerpen and Het Belang van Limburg on Saturday. The findings raise fresh questions about the country's long-troubled push to modernise police IT.

The Organe de contrôle de l'information policière (COC) — the body that acts as the privacy guardian within the police — documented the problems in a report sent in June to Interior Minister Bernard Quintin and Justice Minister Annelies Verlinden. According to the reporting, the issues open the door to concrete risks of abuse.

## A platform built on a troubled legacy

Developed by the Antwerp police, Focus is now used by every local police zone as well as the federal police. It was pitched as a credible answer to the I-Police debacle — a mammoth digitisation programme that was meant to usher the police into a new era but instead collapsed into a financial sinkhole.

As RTBF [detailed](https://www.rtbf.be/article/i-police-un-fiasco-financier-de-76-millions-d-euros-un-manque-de-leadership-et-competences-du-cote-de-la-police-federale-11669282) earlier this year, I-Police swallowed at least 76 million euros of public money with little to show for it, and Interior Minister Bernard Quintin pulled the plug in late December 2025 after results fell short of expectations. A judicial inquiry was later opened in Brussels into possible misappropriation of public funds, and the affair has become a byword for procurement and leadership failures inside the federal police.

Against that backdrop, Focus arrived as the pragmatic, working alternative. On 3 November, Quintin told parliament he was pleased with the "concrete results" the application had delivered.

## What the watchdog found

The COC report tells a more sobering story. According to the findings, Focus does not always record who consults, modifies or disseminates documents — information described as crucial for tracking abuse. Access rights are too broad, and connection sessions are left open for too long.

Data can also be edited in different places without any guarantee that it is updated uniformly, meaning outdated information can keep circulating. In a police context, where decisions can hinge on the accuracy and provenance of a file, such gaps carry real operational and privacy consequences.

The watchdog stopped short of calling for the platform to be shut down. Belgium can hardly afford to suspend Focus, given that it is now woven into the daily work of local and federal forces — pulling it could risk paralysis. Instead, the COC urged the police to confront the deficiencies directly.

## Federal police pledge to act

The federal police responded in a written reaction, saying they would "set to work on the basis of these recommendations" in cooperation with the Antwerp police. That commitment, while welcome, echoes the pattern of reassurance that surrounded I-Police before its problems became impossible to ignore.

## Analysis: another test of police modernisation

The Focus episode matters beyond the technical details. It exposes a recurring tension in Belgium's police IT strategy: the pressure to deliver a working system quickly, after years of costly failure, can conflict with the slower, less visible work of building in proper auditing, access controls and data integrity.

The COC's findings suggest that speed and pragmatism may have come at the expense of the safeguards that make a police database trustworthy. Audit trails and granular access rights are not optional extras — they are what allow oversight bodies, courts and the public to hold the police accountable for how sensitive data is used. When those controls are weak, the risk is not only misuse but also the erosion of public confidence in an institution already under scrutiny.

There is also a governance question. Focus grew out of a single local police zone, Antwerp, before being adopted nationally. That bottom-up route can breed innovation, but it can also mean that national standards for privacy and security are applied after the fact rather than designed in from the start.

## What to watch

The immediate test is whether the federal and Antwerp police translate their pledges into concrete fixes — auditable logging, tighter access rules and reliable synchronisation of records — and whether they do so transparently. Lawmakers, who have spent months dissecting the I-Police fiasco, are likely to want reassurance that Focus will not become a sequel.

For now, the watchdog's report stands as a reminder that replacing a failed system is only half the battle. Making its successor trustworthy is the harder, and more important, task.
