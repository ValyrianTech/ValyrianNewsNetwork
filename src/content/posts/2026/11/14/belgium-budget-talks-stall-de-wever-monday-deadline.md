# Belgium Budget Talks Stall Before De Wever's Monday Deadline

Belgium's core cabinet has once again failed to break the deadlock in its federal budget negotiations, with Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) now set to convene the government's top ministers for a further round of talks on Sunday, a day before he must report to King Filip at the Royal Palace. The renewed inaction, following a fresh round of one-on-one talks on Friday 13 November, prolongs a political crisis that has left the country without a finalized multi-year budget and has already pushed it past a key European deadline.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/opnieuw-geen-doorbraak-bij-kernkabinet-top-van-regering-komt-zo~1793247532380/), De Wever decided not to call his vice-premiers together for overnight negotiations after a new round of bilateral meetings. Working groups were scheduled for Saturday, and the full government top — the kernkabinet — was to reconvene on Sunday 15 November, leaving Monday 16 November as the decisive moment. That same liveblog reported that Friday was the first time since 1 November that all governing parties had sat together at the negotiating table, and that the intention was to negotiate through the entire weekend — an ambition that ultimately fell short.

## Context: Months of Deadlock

The stalled talks are the latest chapter in a saga that has run for months. The coalition, known as the "Arizona" alliance, groups five parties spanning the Flemish and Francophone communities and the political left and right: De Wever's N-VA, the liberal MR, the centrist Les Engagés, the socialist Vooruit and the Christian Democrat CD&V. Its central task is to rein in a rising national debt, which per [TPO.NL](https://tpo.nl/2026/11/02/belgie-begrotingscrisis-de-wever-ontslag-koning/) now exceeds €735 billion — more than 110 per cent of GDP.

The government is seeking roughly €10 billion in savings and new revenues, with a budget covering 2027, 2028 and 2029. As [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/22/maanden-onderhandelen-zonder-resultaat-federaal-begrotingsoverl/) recounted in its analysis of the months-long impasse, Belgium has already missed the 15 October 2026 deadline to submit its budget to the European Commission — a deadline that has since passed without resolution — and officials warn the country risks falling into the sights of financial markets as debt-servicing costs climb.

The process reached a nadir on 2 November, when De Wever offered his resignation to King Filip. The monarch refused it and asked the premier to resume negotiations — a step former prime minister Yves Leterme later described as puzzling. "Offering that resignation to the King: I don't quite understand what that has changed about the situation," Leterme said, adding that "it's all a bit strange from a distance."

## The Central Conflict: Cuts Versus Taxes

At the heart of the impasse is not the size of the adjustment but its composition — specifically, how much of the €10 billion should come from spending cuts and how much from new taxes and revenues.

The liberal MR, led by party chair Georges-Louis Bouchez and vice-premier David Clarinval, insists that no more than €1.1 billion of the total may come from new revenues, arguing this was agreed in the coalition agreement. If that ceiling is breached, MR wants the entire coalition agreement renegotiated. Vooruit, represented in talks by vice-premier Frank Vandenbroucke, wants "the broadest shoulders" — higher earners and wealth — to contribute, reportedly seeking around €1.5 billion from higher taxes. Vandenbroucke has said he "cannot live in a government that stomps on everyone who is vulnerable."

Les Engagés, led by vice-premier Maxime Prévot, has its own red lines, including a demand to extend a transition scheme for unemployed informal carers until 2029. MP Anne Pirson warned the chamber: "If you want Les Engagés on board, a solution must be found." CD&V, meanwhile, is pressing for the fastest possible deal.

A further complication is structural. Several coalition party chairs — notably Bouchez and Vooruit's Conner Rousseau — are not members of the government, yet wield real power over their ministers. Political scientist Dave Sinardet of the VUB and UCLL described the resulting arrangement as a "strange hybrid situation" designed to get each party's leader around the table.

## Analysis: The Last Billion

Publicly, the parties insist a deal remains within reach. N-VA parliamentary leader Axel Ronse said the fiscal situation was so grave that failure was not an option: "It would be criminal not to reach an agreement." CD&V chair Sammy Mahdi warned that all parties would otherwise bear responsibility for "political chaos with a major economic impact."

Bouchez, for his part, argued that discretion was a sign of progress. "The most important thing is that we get a budget, not how it comes about," he said. "If you have less information, that often means it's working."

Yet the mood behind closed doors is far less optimistic. Dorian de Meeûs, a commentator for La Libre Belgique, told VRT's Terzake that "a solid €9 billion has already been found in savings and revenues, but it's usually that last billion that is the hardest... The parties must take steps toward each other." VRT NWS political journalist Benedikte Coussement put it more bluntly: "Behind the scenes there is little optimism among most parties that the big breakthrough is achievable by Monday... If not, the end of this government still looms."

The economic backdrop sharpens the pressure. Pieter Timmermans, chair of the Belgian business federation VBO, warned that "the economic situation is not getting better: inflation and interest rates keep rising, our trade balance is under pressure, the number of bankruptcies is increasing."

## What's Next

All eyes now turn to Monday 16 November, when De Wever is due at the Royal Palace to report to King Filip. De Wever himself told parliament on 12 November that he hoped to bring a positive message. "We will see," he said.

Coussement outlined three scenarios: the federal government falls; a further round of extensions is agreed; or De Wever can go to the King with a deal. A full agreement by Monday is not widely expected behind the scenes, but even a partial breakthrough that avoids collapse would likely be framed as success.

Should the talks fail completely, new elections would become more likely — a demand already voiced by opposition Vlaams Belang, whose faction leader Barbara Pas said talks were moving "slower than the Echternach procession." For now, the parties have one more weekend to find the last billion, and a government to save.
