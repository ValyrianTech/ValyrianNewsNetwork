# Microsoft Restores Services After Global Outage

Microsoft said early on Saturday that its services had been fully restored after a worldwide outage that disrupted Outlook, Teams, the Copilot AI assistant and other Microsoft 365 tools for more than two days, affecting users in more than 70 countries and roughly 430 million people who rely on the products. The company blamed a technical failure — not a cyber-attack or an AI agent — and apologised for the disruption.

## A fault that cascaded worldwide

The incident began on Thursday, 12 November, when users first reported they could not access Microsoft 365 products. By the afternoon, the problems had spiralled: Downdetector, a platform that tracks service disruptions, logged more than 130,000 reports of Outlook problems as the working day began in the United States, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/13/grote-storing-microsoft-outlook-en-teams-bedrijven-overheden/).

The disruption reached far beyond email. Microsoft Teams, the Copilot assistant, and core Microsoft 365 applications including Word, Excel, PowerPoint, SharePoint and OneDrive were all affected. So too were third-party AI assistants — Anthropic's Claude and OpenAI's ChatGPT — which lost their connections to Microsoft 365 accounts, revealing how deeply AI tools are now wired into corporate email and calendars.

Microsoft traced the root cause to a network configuration issue introduced during what it described as a "network capacity upgrade." As [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c6rrg51rkz09o) reported, routine automated checks took some servers offline, overloading the remaining ones with traffic. The company said it found no indications of malicious activity.

Cybersecurity expert Eddy Willems, speaking on VRT NWS's De Ochtend programme, described a chain reaction: a process that establishes secure connections became overwhelmed, and a network configuration change compounded the problem. "Everything is connected," he said. "If one element in the chain fails, you see it worldwide."

## A grinding, day-long recovery

The recovery proved slower and harder than Microsoft initially expected. Having first acknowledged problems in the Americas, the company reported only a "partial recovery" in some regions by early Friday. "The efforts are not going according to expectation," Microsoft said in one status update. "We are investigating what additional measures we can take to improve the overall stability of the infrastructure."

Engineers rerouted traffic around the affected servers and restored capacity gradually to keep services stable. Clearing email backlogs became a priority, and Microsoft extended its mail time-out period to 48 hours to prevent messages from being lost. By Saturday morning, the underlying problem had been resolved.

"We are deeply sorry for the disruption this incident has caused our customers and partners," a Microsoft spokesperson said. "We know the trust they place in our services and take that responsibility extremely seriously." A full post-incident report is expected once the company completes its review.

## From the US Congress to South African wineries

The outage's reach was global and cut across sectors, as [BBC News documented](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cqp9d58eldlro). In the United States, staff for the House of Representatives told Politico that a day of legislative work had been lost because everything runs through Outlook. In Japan, some firms reportedly fell back on fax machines, while the government said its shared IT platform was disrupted. Australia's spy and federal police agencies were affected, though officials said the impact was largely confined to email. Court systems in Singapore and the Supreme Court of the Philippines warned of delayed responses.

In Turkey, advisers attending the COP31 climate summit in Antalya were left unable to access Outlook. In the UK, GPs, pharmacies, police forces, schools and even MPs' parliamentary accounts were hit, though cyber minister Liz Lloyd said the impact on critical frontline services such as 999 calls was "limited." In Canada, a Toronto hospital network declared a "code grey" infrastructure failure, and the national rail operator warned passengers might miss email updates. At the smallest scale, South African winemaker Peter Pentz told the BBC his business had been brought to a "standstill."

The Anderson Economic Group, a Michigan think tank, estimated that lost productivity could approach $7 billion.

## The concentration risk beneath the outage

Beyond the immediate disruption, the episode has sharpened a long-running debate about the fragility of global dependence on a handful of cloud providers. "We have all become very dependent on the cloud," Willems said. "And the dependence on one cloud provider keeps growing. If you throw everything into the cloud, the problems can become very big."

That concern echoed across the industry, as [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cxzd9171m51qo). "The global Microsoft outage is a stark reminder of how heavily we rely on a handful of big tech providers," said Rob Demain, chief executive of the cybersecurity firm e2e-assure. Matt Saunders, field chief technology officer at the consultancy Adaptavist, noted that redundancy is easier in theory than in practice: "Classic IT disaster planning strategies suggest using a second service for resilience, but in practice, it's almost impossible for a business to function without tools such as Microsoft."

Walter Pasquarelli, an AI strategist, warned that the stakes are rising as artificial intelligence becomes more embedded. For governments, businesses and critical infrastructure, the outage "put their vulnerabilities and dependence in plain sight," he said. "Looking ahead, as AI assistants are plugged into our email, calendars and files, a failure like this may spread even further."

## What to watch

Microsoft's forthcoming post-incident report will be scrutinised for the precise technical mechanism behind the failure and whether the network configuration change was fully reversed. Software engineer Gergely Orosz suggested one structural fix: splitting services by region, as Amazon's AWS cloud does, to limit the blast radius of any single failure.

The broader question is whether governments will move from expressing concern to mandating resilience. With the UK already calling for "greater resilience" against technology failures, the outage may accelerate a reckoning over how much of the world's digital infrastructure sits in so few hands — and what happens when one of them stumbles.
