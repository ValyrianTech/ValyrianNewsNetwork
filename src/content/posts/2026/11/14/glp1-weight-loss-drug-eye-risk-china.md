# GLP-1 Weight-Loss Drug Eye Risk in Focus

A U.S. regulatory warning that popular GLP-1 weight-loss drugs may raise the risk of a rare, potentially blinding eye condition has become a trending topic on Chinese social media, prompting patients to ask whether their injections are still safe. The alert, issued by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration on November 9, 2026, requires manufacturers across the entire class of GLP-1 receptor agonists to add a warning about non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) to their labels. The Paper (Pengpai) reported the development and spoke with endocrine experts to translate the warning into practical guidance for the Chinese public.

## What the FDA Actually Said

According to [the FDA's drug safety communication](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-requires-safety-labeling-changes-glucagon-peptide-1-receptor-agonists-glp-1-ras-due-potential), the labeling change is class-wide, affecting semaglutide (sold as Ozempic and Wegovy), liraglutide, tirzepatide, dulaglutide and some newer oral GLP-1 medicines. The agency concluded that use of these drugs "may increase" the risk of NAION — a condition in which blood supply to the front of the optic nerve is suddenly lost, causing painless but abrupt vision loss in one eye.

The evidence behind the warning comes from a pooled analysis of 126 randomized controlled trials involving 134,253 participants, the FDA said. In the treated group, NAION occurred at a rate of 6.4 cases per 100,000 person-years, compared with 3.8 per 100,000 person-years among those taking placebo or other diabetes drugs. In absolute terms, that amounted to just 15 possible events — ten among patients on GLP-1 drugs and five in the comparison group.

## Not "Out of Nowhere"

As [The Paper reported](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34475342), the concern is not new. In July 2024, the journal JAMA Ophthalmology published a study of nearly 17,000 neuro-ophthalmology patients that first flagged a possible link. Among 710 patients with type 2 diabetes, semaglutide users recorded 17 NAION cases versus six among those on other diabetes drugs; among 979 overweight or obese patients, the semaglutide group recorded 20 cases against just three in controls.

"This is not an FDA warning that came out of nowhere — it is not a sudden 'weight-loss needle horror story,'" Dr. Li Bo, deputy chief physician of endocrinology and metabolism at Xinhua Hospital of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, told The Paper. He stressed that the study had limitations, including selection bias, and that the current conclusion is only that the drugs "may increase" risk — not that every GLP-1 drug directly causes NAION. The biological mechanism remains unclear.

Dr. Bu Le, director of the obesity and metabolic syndrome department at the Tenth People's Hospital of Tongji University, said most research suggests a two- to four-fold increase in risk after use, with higher risk among people who have diabetes or other baseline factors. Risk appears concentrated in the first six months of treatment — but the overall probability of occurrence remains extremely low.

## What Patients Should Do — and Not Do

Both experts were emphatic on one point: patients with no eye symptoms should not stop their medication on their own. For people who meet the indications, the drugs offer proven benefits for blood-sugar control, weight management and some heart and kidney outcomes, and the decision to continue should weigh benefits against risks.

"Stopping the drug on your own is like releasing the brake on your weight — rapid rebound follows," Bu warned.

Li advised anyone who suddenly loses vision in one eye, notices a missing patch of visual field, or sees dark shadows or distorted colors — especially without pain — to seek an eye emergency evaluation immediately rather than waiting for a routine appointment. He also noted that people with a history of NAION should proactively tell their doctor.

On other side effects, Bu said the most common complaints are gastrointestinal intolerance, with rarer reports of gallstones, pancreatitis, very rare hypoglycemia, intestinal obstruction and mild heart-rate elevation. Serious reactions severe enough to require stopping the drug are uncommon, he added.

## A Baseline Eye Exam

For those considering GLP-1 therapy, Li suggested discussing a baseline eye assessment with a doctor — especially for people with diabetes, a history of eye disease or other risk factors. The most direct test is a fundus examination, in which an ophthalmologist inspects the optic disc and retina and keeps a record for future comparison. Depending on the individual, doctors may also order visual acuity and visual field tests, orbital ultrasound or optical coherence tomography (OCT).

He cautioned, however, that the FDA did not mandate such exams for everyone, and that a normal result does not guarantee NAION will never occur.

## The Bigger Picture

GLP-1 receptor agonists have become enormously popular worldwide for weight loss, with widespread off-label and online use in China. A safety scare therefore touches a large and anxious population. The Paper's coverage reflects a broader pattern in Chinese health journalism: translating foreign regulatory signals into locally actionable, expert-sourced advice while dampening sensationalism.

The key message for the public is one of proportion. NAION is a serious condition with no reliably reversible treatment, but its absolute incidence among GLP-1 users is measured in single digits per 100,000 person-years. The warning is a reason for vigilance — particularly for sudden, painless vision changes — not for panic. For now, the science points to an association that regulators are watching closely, not a settled cause-and-effect link.

What to watch next: whether regulators in China and elsewhere issue their own labeling guidance, and whether further research clarifies the mechanism behind the apparent link.
