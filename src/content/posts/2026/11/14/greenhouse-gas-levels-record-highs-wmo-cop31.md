# Greenhouse Gas Levels Hit Unprecedented Record Highs

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases all climbed to unprecedented records in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization reported, underscoring that decades of emissions pledges have yet to bend the curve of heat-trapping pollution. The [WMO Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22](https://wmo.int/resources/publication-series/greenhouse-gas-bulletin/no-22), released 27 October, found that carbon dioxide reached 426.8 parts per million, methane 1,948 parts per billion, and nitrous oxide 339.1 parts per billion — each the highest since record-keeping began.

The findings land in the second week of the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye, where negotiators are confronting the widening gap between what the science demands and what governments have delivered.

## A Record That Builds on Every Record Before It

Carbon dioxide hit 153% of its pre-industrial level of 1750, a 53% increase, while methane reached 267% and nitrous oxide 126% of those baselines. When the Bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO₂ level stood at 377.5 ppm — meaning concentrations have risen roughly 49 ppm in about two decades.

"Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies. The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue."

Barrett stressed that CO₂ "has a very long life-time and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries." Her full statement was [published by the WMO](https://wmo.int/secretariat/ko-barrett/record-greenhouse-gases-trap-more-heat-future).

## Concentrations Versus Emissions: A Critical Distinction

The Bulletin measures the accumulated stock of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, not the annual flow of new emissions. About half of all CO₂ emitted each year remains in the atmosphere, while the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and the oceans.

This is the central scientific message that is often lost in public debate: concentrations would keep rising even if new emissions were cut sharply today, because CO₂ persists for centuries. Growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from about 0.8 ppm per year to roughly 2.4 ppm per year over the 2011–2020 decade.

Between 2024 and 2025, the global average CO₂ concentration rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024. WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed the more modest rise to recovery of land carbon sinks and reduced fire emissions after the strong 2023/2024 El Niño, which had degraded the biosphere through drought, drier vegetation and more wildfires.

"However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," Tarasova said. "We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026 because of fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño which will impact land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024."

Carbon dioxide accounts for about 66% of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases, and methane about 16%. The NOAA Annual Greenhouse Gas Index shows radiative forcing from long-lived gases rose 56% between 1990 and 2025, with CO₂ responsible for about 81% of that increase.

## Methane Offers a Faster Lever

Methane is far shorter-lived than CO₂ but a powerful near-term lever. About 65% of methane comes from human activities — cattle, rice farming, fossil fuel exploitation, landfills and biomass burning — with the remaining 35% from natural sources such as wetlands. This asymmetry matters for policy: cutting methane yields faster temperature benefits, while CO₂ cuts determine long-term peak warming.

## The Energy Transition's Own Emissions

The Bulletin flagged two trace gases tied to the clean-energy transition itself. Sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆), used as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, posted a record increase in 2025. It has an atmospheric lifetime of over 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO₂.

Molecular hydrogen (H₂) concentrations rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024. Hydrogen is not itself a greenhouse gas but has a strong indirect warming effect as it leaks from production facilities, pipelines, storage tanks and fuel cells.

"Both H₂ leakage and SF₆ persistence represent potential threats that could undermine the climate benefits of the energy transition," the WMO warned, calling for careful atmospheric monitoring to detect unintended consequences.

## The Political Stakes

The Bulletin is deliberately timed as authoritative scientific input for COP negotiators. COP31, running from 9–20 November 2026 in Antalya and co-hosted with Australia, is pushing an electrification agenda encompassing climate finance, the transition away from fossil fuels, and climate justice.

On 13 November, outgoing UN Secretary-General António Guterres delivered his final COP speech, warning that the gulf between scientific reality and political action remains the defining tension. "You cannot build peace on a burning planet," Guterres said, in [coverage by The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/live/2026/nov/13/cop31-climate-crisis-talks-turkey-latest-news-updates). "You cannot eradicate poverty when climate disasters keep throwing people back into it. You cannot promise young people a better future while destroying the natural systems on which that future depends."

Guterres also called for confronting "the disproportionate influence of the fossil fuel lobby," warning that "the future of our planet cannot be negotiated on the terms of those profiting from its destruction."

## What to Watch

The immediate question is whether 2026 sets yet another record. Tarasova expects concentrations to rise further as a developing powerful El Niño degrades land carbon sinks, compounded by continued fossil fuel burning and the increasing risk of reduced CO₂ uptake by a warming ocean.

The longer challenge is political. As record follows record year after year — this is the latest in a recurring series of Bulletin reports with no recent exception — the cumulative nature of the problem becomes the story. Each new high builds on the last, locking in more warming regardless of future pledges. Whether COP31 can convert this authoritative science into concrete action will be measured not in the atmosphere's composition this year, but in the emissions that compose it next.
