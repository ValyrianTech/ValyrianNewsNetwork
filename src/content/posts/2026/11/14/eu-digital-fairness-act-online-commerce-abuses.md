# EU Moves to End Abuses of Online Commerce

The European Commission on Wednesday unveiled its proposal for the Digital Fairness Act (DFA), an ambitious bid to outlaw the manipulative tactics that have come to define online shopping—from subscription traps and drip pricing to dark patterns and undeclared influencer marketing. Officials billed it as the most significant overhaul of EU consumer protection rules in more than 20 years, and the first to fully account for the realities of digital commerce.

"Shopping has never been more convenient, but convenience must never come at the expense of fairness," said Michael McGrath, the EU Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection. "Whether people shop online or in a physical store, they deserve the same high level of protection."

## A Rulebook Built for the Digital Age

The proposal would replace the 2005 Unfair Commercial Practices Directive with a regulation directly applicable across all 27 member states, eliminating the patchwork of national transpositions that has long frustrated enforcement. The Commission argues the change is overdue: according to its [2025 Consumer Conditions Scoreboard](https://commission.europa.eu/strategy-and-policy/policies/consumers/consumer-protection-policy/key-consumer-data_en), around 60% of EU consumers now buy online, yet online shoppers are more than 60% likelier to run into problems than those shopping in physical stores.

At the heart of the text is a blacklist of "dark patterns" deemed unfair in all circumstances—meaning authorities would no longer have to prove harm case by case. As [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/09/eu-to-blacklist-ten-manipulative-online-design-traps-leaked-draft-shows) reported from a leaked draft, the list of ten includes making it disproportionately harder to cancel than to sign up, burying prices or auto-renewal terms, repeated scarcity warnings at checkout, pre-selected add-ons, confusing double negatives and fake notifications implying someone has contacted the user. The Commission would be able to add new tricks to the list without returning to lawmakers.

## Pricing, Subscriptions and "Fairness by Design"

The DFA also takes aim at pricing practices that quietly inflate bills. It bans drip pricing—unavoidable fees revealed only at checkout—and "was/now" discounts calculated against a manufacturer's recommended price rather than a genuine prior price. On dynamic pricing, companies selling in virtual queues would have to display the current real-time price and the highest possible price. In-app virtual currencies would have to show their real-money value.

On subscriptions, the rules are blunt. A "terminate the contract here" button would be mandatory; free trials could no longer roll into paid subscriptions unless the consumer actively agrees; reminders would be required before automatic renewals; and customers would have the right to speak to a human rather than only a chatbot. The Commission cited subscription traps as costing consumers billions of euros each year.

The proposal also introduces a "fairness by design" principle, giving users the right to switch off autoplay, unrelated notifications, streak-style rewards, endless scrolling and chatbot features that simulate relationships—what officials call a "right not to be disturbed." Children receive particular attention: paid loot boxes and pay-to-win or pay-to-progress mechanics would be banned for minors in video games, and in-game currencies would be switched off by default for children unless a parent approves. The DSA's prohibitions on profiling minors for targeted advertising and on targeting ads based on sensitive data would be extended to all traders, not just the largest platforms.

In a first for EU consumer law, the draft sets rules for AI shopping agents. Users would choose which purchases must be approved, and agents would have to disclose sponsored suggestions.

## A Pro-Business Pitch—and a Fight Ahead

Commission officials have framed the DFA as both pro-consumer and pro-business. "Our consumer protection rules will help ensure that businesses compete on the merits of their products and services—not on their ability to exploit consumers or evade the rules," McGrath said, per [Reuters](https://www.yahoo.com/news/world/articles/eu-threatens-significant-fines-unfair-112205092.html). "We are not making it harder to do business. We are making it harder to do business unfairly, but consumer rights are worth little if they cannot be enforced."

That enforcement is set to sharpen. A companion revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation would empower the Commission to investigate large cross-border cases and impose fines of up to 6% of a company's global annual turnover.

Industry is far from convinced. "The Digital Fairness Act should make the existing framework more coherent, predictable and effective," said Markus J. Beyrer, director general of the business lobby BusinessEurope. "Instead, it risks introducing unnecessary obligations and overlapping requirements that undermine legal certainty and run counter to the EU's simplification agenda."

The Interactive Advertising Bureau Europe has warned of "disproportionate restrictions on personalisation," estimating potential economic losses of €10–29 billion annually and objecting to treating "dynamic vulnerability" as grounds for restricting commercial practices—a concept the industry calls too subjective to apply consistently.

## Analysis: Betting on Enforcement, Not Just Red Tape

As [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-releases-draft-proposal-for-digital-fairness-act) reported, the Commission estimates the DFA would save EU consumers €10.3 billion a year lost to hidden fees, unknown subscriptions and manipulative purchases, while its broader impact assessment puts the annual benefit—including time saved—at €13.7 billion, against recurring business costs of €678 million a year. The measure would apply 12 months after entering into force.

The central gamble is that converting a directive into a directly applicable regulation with a per-se blacklist lowers the evidentiary burden enough to make consumer protection genuinely enforceable. The central risk, critics counter, is that a new horizontal law layers complexity onto the Digital Services Act, Digital Markets Act, AI Act and GDPR even as Brussels pursues a Digital Omnibus to streamline its digital rulebook. Germany has already signalled scepticism, arguing in an October non-paper that existing directives should be amended rather than replaced, and that the case for a regulation "has not yet been sufficiently demonstrated."

Supporters see a different calculus. "Digital services and products should be fair by default," Dutch MEP Kim van Sparrentak wrote, arguing the law would ban practices "we know are manipulative" and reverse the burden of proof.

## What's Next

The proposal now faces negotiation with the European Parliament and EU member states, where the dark-pattern blacklist's scope, the reach of the Commission's delegated powers and the 6% fine regime will all be contested. If it survives—and if it holds—the DFA could do for e-commerce interface design what the GDPR did for data protection: set a de facto global standard. For now, the message from Brussels is that the era of the fine-print loophole is meant to be over.
