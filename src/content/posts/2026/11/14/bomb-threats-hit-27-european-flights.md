# Bomb Threats Hit 27 European Flights in a Single Day

At least 27 passenger flights across Europe received bomb threats on Thursday 5 November, triggering precautionary groundings, diversions and fighter-jet escorts from the Baltics to the Mediterranean — the largest single wave in a sustained campaign of hoax threats against European aviation. No explosives were found on any aircraft and every plane landed safely, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/).

Among the affected services was a LOT Polish Airlines flight from Warsaw to Brussels, which landed without incident at Brussels Airport in Zaventem. Belgium's federal police carried out a risk analysis and concluded that no additional measures were required.

## A Pattern of Escalation

The 5 November wave was the third major disruption that week. On Tuesday 3 November at least seven flights had already been hit, with aircraft making emergency landings in Warsaw, Rome, Murcia and Sofia, [according to VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/vliegtuig-noodlanding-polen-bommelding/). A further wave followed on Friday 6 November, with dozens more flights disrupted and threats directed at Copenhagen and Prague airports.

"After investigation by the federal police, no additional measures appeared necessary," Brussels Airport's press service said of the Warsaw-Brussels flight. Police are nonetheless investigating whether the incident is linked to the earlier threats.

## From Charleroi to Bucharest

The 5 November incidents spanned the continent. A Ryanair flight from Charleroi to the Italian city of Pescara was disrupted when a note bearing the word "bom" (bomb) was found in the aircraft's toilets at 13:13. The alert was quickly declared false and the flight departed for Italy.

In France, an Aer Lingus Airbus A320 en route from Dublin to Rome was intercepted by French air force fighter jets and diverted to the Évreux military airbase some 100 kilometres west of Paris, where all 157 occupants — 151 passengers and six crew — were safely disembarked. A Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome was escorted by Italian fighter jets to Fiumicino airport, while a Wizz Air service from Milan to Warsaw was diverted to Vienna. Aegean Airlines and Ryanair flights from Thessaloniki and Porto respectively were also affected, landing in Milan.

In Bulgaria, three Ryanair flights received threats. Two aircraft arriving from Copenhagen and Madrid landed at Sofia's Vasil Levski airport, while a third from London diverted to Plovdiv. Bulgarian MiG-29 fighter jets scrambled from the Graf Ignatievo airbase to intercept and escort the planes, the [Bulgarian Ministry of Defence confirmed](https://www.mod.bg/news9681).

Shortly afterwards, Romanian intelligence reported two additional threats in Bucharest, including a KLM flight to Tel Aviv with a stopover in the Romanian capital. An earlier Wizz Air flight from Milan to Bucharest — flight WMT3064 — had landed safely at Henri Coandă airport at 11:08 local time, with 182 passengers and seven crew evacuated after no explosives were found, [Bulgarije Vandaag reported](https://bulgarijevandaag.nl/artikel/eu-commissaris-bommeldingen-vluchten-bulgarije/). In Czechia, four aircraft bound for Prague received threats; police confirmed the alerts were false but had every plane checked by explosives specialists on arrival.

## 'A Pattern'

The cumulative effect, VRT journalist Gianni Paelinck observed, was hard to dismiss. "I think we can increasingly speak of a pattern," he said.

EU Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas urged caution against drawing premature conclusions, stating that it was "too early to link the incidents to one another" while calling every threat against civilians unacceptable. The Romanian intelligence service and national police forces across the continent continue to investigate the senders.

## Signs of a Hybrid Campaign

The threats came amid a wider wave of sabotage and disruption across Europe — attacks on German electricity substations, defence-related facilities in the Baltic region, and supermarket refrigerators in France. Many officials have linked the campaign to Europe's support for Ukraine following Russia's 2022 invasion, describing a "hybrid" or "grey zone" strategy that is cheap to execute, easily deniable, and calibrated below the threshold of armed response.

"It cannot be mere coincidence that so many incidents with exactly the same modus operandi are happening across Europe," Spanish Foreign Minister José Manuel Albares told the national broadcaster TVE, as [reported by Reuters](https://cyprus-mail.com/2026/11/06/wave-of-bomb-threats-afflict-european-flights-for-third-time-this-week). "The most reasonable assumption is that we are facing an attempt at hybrid aggression and a form of psychological warfare."

European technology commissioner Henna Virkkunen framed the threats within a broader pattern. "We are under attack all the time," she told Reuters. "The reason behind these operations is to destabilise our societies and create mistrust among our citizens." Pressed on attribution, she added: "It is not always Russia who is behind different hybrid operations... Quite often it is Russia."

Attribution nonetheless remains uncertain. European intelligence services have proposed that pro-Kremlin activists or hacking groups produced the coordinated threats, [The Independent reported](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/europe/russia-bomb-threats-europe-flights-b3080924.html), citing the Financial Times, though it is unclear what role, if any, Russian state intelligence played. In Italy, an airport source said threats had been sent in the name of the "fighters of the Male State", a Russian anti-feminist movement banned at home; its founder, Vladislav Pozdnyakov, denied any involvement, saying the group no longer exists.

## What's Next

Even when false, each threat triggers a full emergency procedure — diversion to the nearest suitable airport, isolation on the apron, and a search by explosives teams — causing hours of delay and significant cost to airlines and airports. By the following Friday, direct disruption was already falling as authorities grew more practiced at their response.

The central question remains unanswered: who is orchestrating the wave, and whether it reflects copycat hoaxes, individual grievances, or a coordinated state-linked campaign. With national investigations ongoing and European officials increasingly willing to name a likely culprit without formal attribution, the pressure to reach a definitive conclusion — and to decide how the continent should respond to an attack that stops just short of violence — is only growing.
