# Belgium's Fix for the i-Police Fiasco Has Its Own Fiasco

Belgium's answer to its troubled i-Police database is itself riddled with serious legal, security and privacy shortcomings, according to a confidential report from the country's police-data watchdog. The findings, revealed Saturday by Flemish newspapers, cast fresh doubt on the country's long, expensive effort to modernise how its police forces store and share information.

The platform at the centre of the new scrutiny is **Focus**, a system developed by Antwerp police and now used by every local police zone as well as the federal police. It was presented as the "virtuous response" to the i-Police debacle—the project [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-plateforme-numerique-censee-repondre-au-fiasco-d-i-police-presente-de-grave-manquements-11815511) describes as a "fiasco of 76 million euros" marred by a "lack of leadership and competences on the federal police side."

The critical report comes from the *Organe de contrôle de l'information policière* (COC), the privacy watchdog embedded within the police. Transmitted in June to Interior Minister Bernard Quintin and Justice Minister Annelies Verlinden, it identifies problems that "open the door to concrete risks of abuse," according to [Het Nieuwsblad](https://www.nieuwsblad.be/binnenland/rapport-maakt-succesvolle-opvolger-van-i-police-met-grond-gelijk-enorm-risico-op-oneigenlijk-gebruik/164350805.html), which broke the story alongside Gazet van Antwerpen and Het Belang van Limburg.

## What the Watchdog Found

The COC's concerns are specific and, taken together, describe a system with weak accountability over who sees what. Focus does not always record who consults, modifies or disseminates documents—information the watchdog calls crucial for tracking improper use. Access rights are too broad, and connection or session times are too long. And data can be modified in different places without any guarantee of uniform updating, so obsolete information keeps circulating.

That combination matters. Reliable logging is precisely the control that allows misuse of police data to be detected after the fact. Without it, and with overly generous access rights, improper consultation of sensitive records becomes difficult to spot or prove.

## An Awkwardly Timed Celebration

The report's publication lands at an awkward moment for the government. On 3 November, less than two weeks ago, Interior Minister **Bernard Quintin (MR)** told parliament he was pleased with the "concrete results" obtained thanks to Focus. Days later, the COC's findings arrived in the press—an uncomfortable juxtaposition that undercuts the narrative that the police-data problem had been solved.

There is no easy off-switch. Because Focus is already in daily use across the entire police apparatus, suspending it would risk paralysing Belgian policing. The watchdog therefore urges remediation rather than shutdown. In a written reaction, the federal police said it would "set to work on the basis of these recommendations," in collaboration with the Antwerp police.

## The Shadow of i-Police

The new episode cannot be separated from the earlier scandal it was supposed to fix. The **i-Police** project—a roughly €299 million effort to overhaul police IT awarded to French consultancy Sopra Steria—became a byword for procurement failure. A Court of Audit report in late October estimated total spending on the project at €139.4 million, including €21.6 million in licences that are now useless.

The political fallout has been intense, concentrating on Justice Minister **Annelies Verlinden (CD&V)**, who has faced parliamentary hearings and conflict-of-interest allegations and, according to colleagues quoted by RTBF, is nicknamed "Madame Teflon" in parliament. Focus emerged as the pragmatic, Antwerp-built success story that filled the gap—which is what makes the watchdog's verdict so politically sensitive. It questions the very alternative being held up as the fix.

## Analysis: The Same Pattern, Repeated

The deeper issue the COC report exposes is structural. Belgium's police-IT modernisation keeps substituting one under-governed system for another, with the same recurring weaknesses: thin oversight, shaky procurement and political accountability that clusters around the interior and justice ministers.

The absence of dependable logging is a textbook data-governance failure. In a police context, it is more serious than in most—police databases contain sensitive personal information whose improper consultation can have real consequences for citizens, from leaks to wrongful targeting. Broad access rights and stale data compound the risk: the system can both expose information to too many people and mislead those who rely on it.

## What to Watch

Several questions remain open. The COC's formal recommendations and any deadlines have not been made public, and it is unclear whether the parliamentary interior committee will schedule hearings. Whether Focus will have to be re-engineered—how much it will cost and how long it will take—is likewise unknown.

For the government, the immediate task is remedial. As RTBF reported, the federal police have pledged to tackle the shortcomings together with the Antwerp police. But the wider lesson is difficult to avoid: repairing a single platform will not repair a pattern. Until oversight is built into the process from the start rather than retrofitted after a scandal, Belgium's police-data reforms risk producing the next i-Police, one upgrade at a time.
