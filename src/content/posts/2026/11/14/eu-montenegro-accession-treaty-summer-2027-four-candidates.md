# EU Targets Montenegro Treaty by Summer 2027

The European Commission expects to sign an accession treaty with Montenegro by June 2027, setting the small Adriatic country on course to become the European Union's 28th member state — and its first new member in more than a decade. Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos made the target public in an interview with [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/europese-unie-wil-in-juni-volgend-jaar-toetredingsverdrag-ondert/), framing the milestone as the clearest sign yet that the bloc's long-stalled enlargement policy has regained momentum.

The timeline, if met, would see Montenegro conclude its accession negotiations by the end of 2026, sign the treaty in June 2027, and complete ratification by all 27 national parliaments roughly a year later — pointing to membership around 2028. That would make Montenegro the first country to join since Croatia in 2013, and the first to accede under the EU's current enlargement methodology.

## A small country, a concrete timeline

Kos has described Montenegro as effectively ready. "Montenegro is on its way to becoming the next new member of the EU. The accession treaty is already being prepared extensively," she told VRT NWS. Elsewhere she noted the country's modest size — "roughly as many inhabitants as Antwerp" — as a reason for confidence that the public and member states could be brought on board.

The scale of activity in Brussels has become a talking point in its own right. Presenting the Commission's 2026 Enlargement Package, Kos said the bloc had opened or closed 61 negotiating chapters this year alone, with more than 30 still to come before the year's end — progress she described as the strongest in 25 years, according to [European Western Balkans](https://europeanwesternbalkans.com/2026/11/04/kos-presents-three-novelties-in-this-years-enlargement-package/). The package introduced indicative roadmaps for the four most advanced candidates, a new model of "gradual integration," and a sharper focus on the link between enlargement and European security.

Earlier, during a visit to Podgorica, Kos had already signalled that a summer 2027 treaty signing was realistic — provided Montenegro completed outstanding reforms and settled disputes with Croatia. "Croatia has its own interests, and it speaks openly about them," she said at the time, as [reported](https://europeanwesternbalkans.com/2026/10/21/kos-montenegro-could-sign-eu-accession-treaty-by-summer-2027/) by the regional outlet.

## The Croatia hurdle

The single biggest near-term obstacle to Montenegro's accession is not reform but a neighbour. Zagreb has circulated an eight-point list of grievances largely rooted in the legacy of the 1990s Yugoslav wars, covering property claims, contested borders, war-crimes investigations and victim reparations.

Germany has stepped in to press Croatia toward a resolution. According to [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/16/germany-pressures-croatia-to-resolve-bilateral-disputes-with-montenegro-over-eu-accession), Berlin offered to mediate and has been manoeuvring behind the scenes to keep enlargement from losing momentum, fearing that unresolved bilateral problems could derail the wider process. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas has publicly criticised the idea that accession can be hijacked by bilateral disputes.

Montenegro's EU minister, Maida Gorčević, has pledged to resolve the disagreements, while insisting that her country will not accept a diluted form of membership. Speaking at the Euronews Enlargement Summit in Brussels, she rejected any "second-class" status. "We don't want second-grade membership inside the European Union, because that wouldn't be fair and that wouldn't be full membership as well," she said, [Euronews reported](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/03/montenegro-rejects-second-class-eu-membership-as-it-eyes-2028-accession).

## Four frontrunners, four very different paths

Kos has named four frontrunners — Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine — but their trajectories diverge sharply.

**Albania** aims to close all negotiating chapters by the end of 2027, having "really sprang into action" on reforms over the past year. The open question, Kos acknowledged, is the impact of sustained street and student protests against the government.

**Moldova**, a candidate only since 2022, opened 25 of its 33 negotiating chapters in 2026. Brussels is watching closely for Russia's reaction and for the mood of a Moldovan electorate split between pro-European and pro-Russian camps.

**Ukraine** is the largest and most strategically consequential case, and the hardest. Kos hopes all negotiating chapters will be formally opened with Kyiv by the end of 2026, and believes membership within five years is possible. "I believe that should be possible if we help Ukraine. Although that will also depend on how long the war lasts," she said. She stressed that Ukraine is held to the same high standards on rule of law, press freedom and anti-corruption as every other candidate — "and that is not easy" for a country fighting for its survival.

**Serbia** is the wildcard. Once the second-most advanced candidate, it went backwards under the government of Aleksandar Vučić, and Kos's progress report on Belgrade is notably strict. But the picture changed in late October, when a student-led movement defeated Vučić in parliamentary elections. [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/serbia-votes-students-seek-unseat-vucic-snap-election-2026-10-25/) reported that Vučić conceded defeat to the Student List, ending a grip on power that dated back to 2014. Kos said she had already received the new leadership in Brussels: "If they mean it seriously and really want to carry out big reforms, then things could go very fast for Serbia."

Analysts have urged caution, however. As [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/serbias-student-victory-raises-hopes-of-an-eu-reset) noted, the Student List's programme focuses on domestic governance, and its stance on Russia sanctions and Kosovo remains unclear — leaving Brussels hopeful but watchful.

## A geopolitical reframing

The renewed push reflects a broader shift in how Brussels frames enlargement. Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, membership has been increasingly presented as an instrument of security rather than a purely technocratic exercise. "There is strength in numbers. A larger Europe means strengthening Europe's security, growing our geopolitical weight and our ability to project influence," Kallas said when the package was unveiled.

Kos has leaned into the same argument, comparing the current moment to the 2004 "big bang" when ten countries joined at once. "I feel that more and more European leaders realise it's important to bring all those countries on board, if we want to protect ourselves in these geopolitical times," she said.

## What could still go wrong

Two chokepoints loom over the timetable. The first is Croatia: if Zagreb's grievances are not resolved before June 2027, Montenegro's treaty signature could slip. The second is the EU's own political calendar. A string of elections in key member states is due in 2027, including in France, where far-right leader Marine Le Pen is a favourite and has traditionally been sceptical of enlargement. Croatian Prime Minister Andrej Plenković has himself [warned](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/03/french-elections-could-derail-eu-enlargement-croatian-pm-tells-euronews) that political change in Paris could "affect the enlargement more substantially than anybody can imagine today."

There is also the substance of what candidates are being offered. The EU's new "gradual integration" model and Germany's floated "associate candidate" status for Ukraine have raised fears among candidates of a tiered membership that could leave them permanently in a waiting room — a sensitivity Montenegro's minister has voiced bluntly.

For now, the Commission's message is that delivery brings rewards. Whether Montenegro can deliver on time — and whether the EU can hold its own political coalition together long enough to welcome it — will define the next chapter of European enlargement. The next signposts come at the end of 2026, when negotiations with Montenegro are due to close and the remaining chapters with Ukraine are set to open.
