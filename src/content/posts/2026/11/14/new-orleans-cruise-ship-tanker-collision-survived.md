# All Aboard Survive New Orleans Cruise Ship Collision

All passengers and crew aboard a docked river cruise boat survived after a tanker apparently lost its steering and slammed into the vessel on the Mississippi River in New Orleans on Friday, sending people plunging into the water as the boat listed and toppled onto its side, the [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/new-orleans-tanker-river-cruise-rescue-passengers-757bed7a70ceb72f0d7140143fe2a764) reported.

The 520-foot asphalt tanker *Atlantic Asphalt* struck the *American Heritage*, a five-story paddlewheel cruise boat operated by American Cruise Lines, at the Port of New Orleans' Thalia Street Terminal at about 12:30 p.m. local time. The cruise boat had docked roughly two hours earlier so passengers could disembark at the end of an eight-day voyage. There were no fatalities, and 32 people were taken to hospitals, none with critical injuries.

"We have zero people unaccounted for, which truly is a miracle on the Mississippi," New Orleans Mayor Helena Moreno said at a news conference.

## How the Collision Unfolded

According to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/tanker-cruise-collision-mississippi-river-new-orleans/), the tanker was traveling upriver when it struck the stationary cruise boat. Video from the scene showed the much larger tanker hitting the *American Heritage* as metal and glass "crashed and crunched." The cruise boat began taking on water and listed within minutes before rolling over, dumping passengers and crew into the roughly 66°F (19°C) river.

Witness Jamie Holford described the scene to CBS News in stark terms. "It looked like something off the Titanic," he said. "It sounded like a war movie. Just imagine tons of steel crunching." He added that the disaster unfolded with terrifying speed: "Fifteen minutes from impact to where it rolled over."

A massive multi-agency rescue effort swung into action. Port NOLA Harbor Police, the New Orleans Fire Department, the New Orleans Police Department and the U.S. Coast Guard joined private vessels and tugboats already on the river to pull dozens of people from the water. Two Harbor Police officers who entered the water to rescue survivors were among those hospitalized and were reported to be in good condition.

"It was just chaos," Port of New Orleans worker Devon Flanagan told the AP. "You had all types of ships, like other barges and other companies down there trying to help out."

## Cause Under Investigation

U.S. Coast Guard Capt. Melanie Burnham, commander of the New Orleans sector, said the tanker appeared to have suffered a steering failure, though the precise cause remains unknown.

"The investigation is still ongoing, there seems to be some kind of a loss of steering," Burnham told reporters. "The exact reason why, we don't know that."

The National Transportation Safety Board launched its own investigation. Board members and investigators were initially delayed because a nationwide Federal Aviation Administration ground stop on Friday prevented them from flying to New Orleans; the team was to drive to the scene instead. The Coast Guard closed the Mississippi to vessel traffic between mile markers 93 and 96, and regional ferry service was temporarily suspended.

The tanker's manager, OSM Thome, issued a statement confirming that all crew aboard the *Atlantic Asphalt* were safe and accounted for and that the vessel was "stable at anchor while damage assessments take place." The company said it had activated its emergency response team and was "fully complying with the local authorities."

"We deeply regret the impact of this incident and extend our sincere concern and heartfelt sympathies to those injured," OSM Thome said. "Our thoughts remain with the passengers of the AMERICAN HERITAGE following their harrowing experience."

## Legal Fallout Begins

Late Friday, the Port of New Orleans filed a federal lawsuit against the tanker, its owner Nave Shipping SA and its management company, Thome Ship Management Pte Ltd, alleging negligence and seeking more than $1 million in damages, cleanup and repairs, [CBS News reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/port-of-new-orleans-sues-tanker-riverboat-crash/). The suit asked that the tanker be seized and sold to pay for the damage.

The lawsuit leans on a longstanding principle of U.S. maritime law: when a moving vessel strikes a stationary object, a presumption of fault arises against the moving vessel. The port alleged the tanker and its operators failed "to use all available means to avoid the risk of" a collision.

## A Narrow Escape With Wider Lessons

That no one died is remarkable given the circumstances. Moreno noted that the tanker narrowly missed a bridge carrying roughly 160,000 vehicles a day, underscoring how much worse the outcome could have been on one of the nation's busiest commercial waterways.

Martin Davies, a maritime law expert at Tulane University, said the crash's circumstances point to a serious mechanical failure. The tanker would already have navigated the difficult Algiers Point turn and, because it was moving upriver against the current, should have been traveling slowly.

"For something like this to happen for an upbound ship indicates that something must have gone really badly wrong," Davies told the AP. "This is obviously a really bad incident, but it could also have struck the bridge, which it didn't."

Louisiana Gov. Jeff Landry said he would launch a state investigation to ensure the collision is not repeated, noting that the Mississippi is "not some quiet pond." President Donald Trump praised the Coast Guard and other agencies for their rescue work in a Truth Social post.

## What to Watch

Federal and state investigators will now focus on why the *Atlantic Asphalt* lost steering and whether maintenance or equipment failures played a role. The Port of New Orleans' lawsuit could expand to additional parties as the inquiry proceeds. The Coast Guard is also working to reopen the busy river channel as quickly as safely possible, a decision with significant economic stakes for a region dependent on river commerce.

For the survivors, the immediate crisis has passed. But for a city that lives with the Mississippi at its doorstep, the collision is a reminder of how quickly a routine Friday on the water can turn into a rescue operation — and how narrowly a far greater tragedy was avoided.
