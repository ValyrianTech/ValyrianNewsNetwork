# Namur Women's Shelter Faces Closure, Leaving Many Adrift

The Espace Dignité Dames, a day shelter in Saint-Servais on the outskirts of Namur, is facing an uncertain future that could deprive vulnerable women of the only refuge of its kind in Wallonia. Run by the non-profit ASBL Educ'Actions & Dignité, the facility welcomes more than twenty women and several children each day—most of them homeless, isolated, single mothers, or survivors of violence. Without sufficient funding, the association warns that some of its activities could be suspended as early as this winter, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/a-namur-l-espace-dignite-dames-menace-un-refuge-essentiel-pour-les-femmes-en-situation-de-precarite-11813421).

## A Place to Exist, Not Just to Survive

For the women who cross its threshold, the space is far more than a service point. "Here, I feel that I exist," one beneficiary told RTBF—a sentiment that captures what the shelter offers beyond showers, hot meals, clean clothing, and laundry. It provides social and administrative support, collective activities such as cooking together or sharing a coffee, and, above all, a safe place to rest and rebuild confidence.

Mariemme, a specialised educator at the centre, described the bonds that form over time. "We become like a family," she said. The shelter operates less like an institution and more like a community, where women arrive with their own histories and are met without judgement.

Robert Bourgeois, president of the association, argues the need for a women-only space is grounded in a structural gap. Women represent only about a third of the homeless population, yet services were "naturally" designed with men in mind. "Mixed facilities are good, but there are very specific needs for women," he told [Boukè](https://www.bouke.media/info/namur-lespace-dignite-dames-besoin-daide/23010). "And here, unfortunately, this is the only service of its kind in the Walloon Region."

## A Funding Model on the Brink

The shelter opened in 2023, following the closure the previous year of a general day-shelter in nearby Jambes that Bourgeois had also founded. It is accredited by the Walloon Region and receives a regional subsidy, but that support is not enough to cover its costs—a fragility that reflects a wider dependence among small Belgian non-profits on a patchwork of public subsidies, private donations, and one-off gifts.

To keep its three educators employed and maintain activities, the association has been trying to raise €50,000. As [L'Avenir](https://www.lavenir.net/regions/namur/2026/08/20/namur-un-appel-a-la-solidarite-pour-sauver-lespace-dignite-dames-6MLJTU2S5JHKRPDGS6ODIDWKOY/) reported in August, the target was set for the end of October 2026, and donations of €40 or more per year qualify for a tax certificate. A citizen campaign dubbed "1 € magique"—"everyone gives what they can, even one euro"—was launched to mobilise broad support, [Cathobel](https://www.cathobel.be/2026/07/operation-de-solidarite-pour-soutenir-lespace-dignite-dames-a-namur/) noted.

That the shelter remains "in danger" as of mid-November suggests the goal had not been fully secured, or that its longer-term situation remains precarious. Beyond the immediate funding gap, the association must also anticipate relocating: the buildings it occupies are made available by the construction company Thomas & Piron, but the space will have to vacate them in the medium term.

## Voices of the Women Who Depend on It

The human stakes are clear in the stories of those the centre serves. Isabelle, who has experienced homelessness and addiction, found support at the shelter and has since been rehoused—yet remains deeply attached to the structure that helped her through a difficult period. Anne, a single mother, comes for what she describes as a little respite and solidarity, struggling to make ends meet while shielding her children from financial worries. At the centre, she finds a listening ear free of judgement.

Karine, quoted by L'Avenir, put it starkly: "Without them, I wouldn't be here anymore. Here I found a place where I can be myself."

Staff and volunteers fear what closure would mean. "What will become of us? Where will we go?" beneficiaries asked, as relayed by Mariemme. Claire Colette, a volunteer, did not mince words about the consequences. "If there are no more associations, it is a real catastrophe," she said. "I call that a failure to assist a person in danger. And I weigh my words. There is no structural response from the public authorities."

## A Model Worth Saving

Beyond survival, the association hopes the women-only model can inspire similar initiatives in Wallonia, Brussels, and Flanders. "With €50,000 we can already gain time," Bourgeois said. "We can continue working with the three educators who are here." He added that a foundation is being developed to secure longer-term funding and that he hopes a network will emerge, possibly with other associations already in existence.

The case also illustrates a broader tension in Belgian social policy: accreditation does not guarantee adequate or stable funding, and frontline services remain exposed to the volatility of private support. Related reporting by RTBF has documented rising precarity in the region, including a tripling over a decade in the number of patients relying on Médecins du Monde in Namur.

## What's Next

Two benefit events are scheduled to sustain the effort: "Les Silencieuses" on 20 and 21 November 2026 at the Maison de la Poésie in Namur, and a concert on 10 January 2027 at the Théâtre royal de Namur. Donations can be made to account BE79 3601 0000 0033.

For now, the question hanging over the Espace Dignité Dames is whether citizens, businesses, foundations, and public authorities will mobilise in time. If they do not, the women who rely on it will not simply lose a building—they will lose the one place in Wallonia built specifically to treat them not only as people to be welcomed, but as people to be considered.
