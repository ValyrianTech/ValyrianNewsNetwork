# Zolder's coal mine becomes The Pit: culture from industry

More than three decades after the last coal was hauled to the surface, the former Zolder mine in Heusden-Zolder has been reborn as The Pit — a sprawling site that now hosts culture, events, heritage and more than a hundred companies. The transformation, which [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/13/van-stoflong-naar-groene-long-zolder/) traces in the closing instalment of its series on Limburg's mine sites, turns a symbol of industrial decline into a contemporary gathering place and, in the words of the broadcaster, a "green lung" for the community.

The Zolder mine closed on 30 September 1992, the last of the seven Kempense mines to shut and the final coal mine in the Benelux. It had entered production later than its neighbours — first coal was raised in 1930 — and its relatively modern installations were built for a future that ultimately never arrived. When the mine closed, the engine that had driven the region's economy fell silent.

## From industrial estate to cultural hub

The most visible part of the reconversion has been economic. The mine grounds now form industrial estates housing more than 100 companies. "That approach was a good choice at the time," Mayor Mario Borremans of the GOED party told VRT. "The industrial estate is today fully built up with more than 100 companies and provides much employment." Borremans added that "the mining past remains present, but the buildings have a new function today."

That new function extends well beyond factories. The former hoisting building now houses the [ZLDR Luchtfabriek](https://www.visitheusden-zolder.be/nl/aanbod/zldr-luchtfabriek-een-blik-op-het-mijnverleden), a heritage experience that preserves the mine's original machinery — its ventilation systems, compressors and winding wheel — as a window onto the site's past. The De Muze cultural centre relocated to the grounds, and the former power plant became an event hall run by Watt17. Elsewhere on the site, an adult education centre, a social-economy recycling shop and The Max Eventcenter, which includes a VR play hall, fill out the mix.

The MijnMarkt, known locally as the "Turkenmarkt," remains a regional institution that draws visitors from across the border. And the site's most striking landmark is being restored: the surviving headframe is being fitted with a glass lift that will carry visitors to a skybox at the top, with a VR-training building planned at its base.

## A €16 million crown jewel — and a forgotten brother

The headframe restoration began with a symbolic first-stone ceremony on 6 May 2024, as [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2024/05/06/restauratie-van-schachtbok-van-voormalige-steenkoolmijn-in-heusd/). The project costs €16 million and is backed by the EU, the Flemish heritage agency Onroerend Erfgoed and the Limburgse Reconversiemaatschappij (LRM). By September 2026 the skybox had been lifted into place atop the structure, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/01/skybox-op-schachtbok-van-mijn-heusden-zolder-geplaatst/).

The Zolder mine once had two headframes; one was demolished shortly after the closure. Former miner Eddy Pluymers lamented that only a manhole cover on the parking lot marks the second shaft. "The headframe should become the crown jewel of the site," he said at the 2024 launch. "Yet I also hope its brother is not forgotten."

At that ceremony, Flemish minister Zuhal Demir (N-VA) said miners "created prosperity in Limburg" and that "it is now up to this generation to honour their legacy and give it new meaning." Her colleague Jo Brouns (CD&V) described the headframes as symbols of "typical Limburg resilience." "The closure of the mines caused a great shockwave in our province," he said, "but if you look at our mine sites now, you see engines of innovation and progress."

## The story behind the buildings

Yet the success of the bricks-and-mortar reconversion raises a harder question: what became of the miners and their stories? Paul Boutsen, a former neighbourhood worker who founded the heritage association Het Vervolg, argues that the social dimension of the reconversion received too little attention. "The miners got a good pension, but many young people found no work," he told VRT. "With the mine's closure the engine was out of the car. We had to find a new engine."

Boutsen, the son of a mine clerk who did vacation work underground, has helped create cultural projects such as the Kolenspoorfestival to keep that human history alive. "If you tear everything down, you also lose the story of the people who worked and lived here," he said.

Heritage professionals share the concern. Leen Gos of ECRU, the intermunicipal heritage and culture cooperation, noted that archives and collections are scattered among local history circles, private collectors and municipalities, with no complete overview and no structural solution for private archives. "A building's repurposing only succeeds when the story of the place, the objects and the people is also heard," she said. "Not all young people still know their family history or the economic history of this region. Yet we can draw much strength from that history for the future."

## What's next

As the skybox nears completion, Heusden-Zolder faces the same balancing act that defines post-industrial regeneration across Limburg: how to turn a site of memory into a place of the future without erasing the memory itself. The Pit has already answered the economic question. Whether it can fully answer the human one — preserving the miners' stories for generations who never saw the coal come up — remains the harder test.

For visitors, the [Visit Limburg](https://www.visitlimburg.be/nl/mijnsite-zolder) site offers a guide to the Zolder mine grounds, pointing to a place where the industrial past and the cultural present now share the same soil.
