# Brussels Loses Overflight Appeal to a Procedural Error

The Brussels-Capital Region has forfeited its own legal challenge against the contested aircraft approach route that overflies the heart of the capital, after a letter from the federal mobility minister was received by the wrong cabinet and never passed on. According to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/782254/article/2026-11-12/survol-de-bruxelles-une-erreur-prive-la-region-dun-recours), a letter dated 17 July 2026 from federal Mobility Minister Jean-Luc Crucke (Les Engagés) to the cabinet of Brussels Mobility Minister Elke Van den Brandt (Groen) was never forwarded to Ans Persoons (Vooruit), the State Secretary responsible for the overflight dossier. That letter opened a 60-day window to file an annulment appeal before the Council of State — a window that has now closed with the Region having filed nothing.

## Context: The Route at the Centre of a Decades-Old Fight

The dispute concerns the RNP 07L approach path, widely known as the "Crucke route," which allows aircraft, under certain wind and operating conditions, to land at Brussels Airport in Zaventem on a straight line over densely populated Brussels communes including Molenbeek-Saint-Jean, Koekelberg, Laeken, Jette and Schaerbeek. The route has been a fixture of Belgian politics for years. In a 14 November editorial, Le Soir's Gil Durand described the overflight dossier as the ["valet puant" — the stinking valet — of Belgian politics](https://www.lesoir.be/782687/article/2026-11-14/survol-de-bruxelles-un-cafouillage-assourdissant) "for years, even decades," and said it "certainly did not need the vaudeville" that residents have just witnessed.

The pressure on the federal government has been building. A February 2025 court judgment ordered the federal state to act on noise nuisance, and on 1 October 2026 Crucke announced a package of measures to reduce the impact of overflights. The most visible element — raising the start of descent from 3,000 to 4,000 feet and steepening the approach angle — was acknowledged by the minister himself as cosmetic, and judged to bring "no significant gain" for residents' noise wellbeing.

## Key Developments: A Letter That Never Arrived

The 17 July letter formally announced the permanent implementation of the route and, critically, started the clock on a Council of State challenge. It landed in Van den Brandt's cabinet and stopped there. Persoons reportedly only learned of its existence on 6 November, four days before Brussels' budget minister asked the Region to lodge its appeal.

The consequences unfolded rapidly. On 12 November, Le Soir and several other Belgian outlets reported that the Region had lost its recourse. The same day, the communes of Koekelberg, Schaerbeek and Molenbeek-Saint-Jean decided to file appeals of their own, their 60-day deadlines running from a separate trigger — the 16 September publication of the route in the aeronautical information publication (AIP), which expires around 16 November.

On 13 November, in the Brussels Parliament plenary, the regional Mobility Minister acknowledged her cabinet's error. Persoons confirmed that the Region would instead join the appeals filed by seven communes — Koekelberg, Schaerbeek, Molenbeek-Saint-Jean, Brussels-City, Evere, Saint-Gilles and Anderlecht — and announced that COCOF and COCOM would file requests of their own. A further legal opinion has been commissioned on whether the 17 July letter actually constituted valid notification at all.

The Region's own appeal now faces a serious risk of being ruled inadmissible. A VUB professor of administrative law, quoted by BRUZZ, called the episode a "faute de débutant" — a beginner's mistake — while noting that the Region can still graft onto other parties' procedures and that the cases, all challenging a single act, are likely to be joined.

## Analysis: Dysfunction Meets an Intractable Problem

The mishap is politically damaging on two fronts. It exposes a breakdown in communication between two Brussels cabinets from the same green-socialist majority, and it weakens the Region's hand just as the dossier reaches the Concertation Committee on 16 November, where the Brussels Minister-President is expected to demand a fairer distribution of flight routes. Flanders has meanwhile opened a conflict-of-interest procedure against federal plans to relax the wind standards that determine how often the route is used.

Yet the procedural blunder matters because the underlying problem has no clean solution. Any relief granted to residents in the capital's north-west — where the flight path currently concentrates — risks shifting noise onto eastern Brussels or Flemish Brabant along the runway 01 corridor. Each remedy produces a new set of victims, which helps explain why the conflict has outlasted successive governments. According to the Brussels Governance Monitor, 96% of RNP 07L flights exceed Brussels noise standards, while Brussels Airport handled roughly 204,147 flights and 24.4 million passengers in 2025.

Two civil proceedings remain unaffected by the Council of State setback. The Region's environmental "action en cessation," filed with nine communes, has a hearing set for 11 January 2027 in the French-speaking first-instance court. A separate action brought by 42 families from Dilbeek, Lennik and Roosdaal — open to around 200 Brussels families — is scheduled for pleadings on 8 February 2027 before the Dutch-speaking court.

## What's Next

The immediate test comes on 16 November at the Concertation Committee, where the Region will try to convert its embarrassment into leverage. The longer game runs through the courts: whether the Region's own appeal is admitted on the argument that the 17 July letter was never validly notified, whether it successfully joins the communes' filings, and whether the civil cases deliver in 2027 the relief that the administrative route has so far failed to provide. For the residents under the flight path, the editorial's verdict was blunt — whatever the legal choreography, the noise has not diminished.
