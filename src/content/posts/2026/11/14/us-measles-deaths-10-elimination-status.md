# US Measles Deaths Hit 10 as Nation Loses Elimination Status

The number of measles-associated deaths in the United States this year has climbed to 10, after Pennsylvania and Wisconsin each reported an additional fatality on Friday — a grim threshold reached as the country loses the measles "elimination" status it had held for more than a quarter of a century.

According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/us-measles-deaths-rise-10-new-reports-pennsylvania-wisconsin-rcna609245), Wisconsin's health department said a child had died but declined to release further details out of respect for the family's privacy, while Pennsylvania reported that its latest death was an unvaccinated resident of Montour County who had tested positive for the virus.

The milestone caps the country's deadliest measles year in decades. Pennsylvania has now recorded seven measles-associated deaths, Wisconsin two and New York one. Before this outbreak, none of those three states had seen a measles death in more than 30 years.

## A Record Outbreak

As of Thursday, roughly 5,400 measles cases have been recorded nationwide — more than double last year's total of 2,289 and the highest count since 1991, NBC News reported. Public health experts attribute the surge to a sharp decline in vaccination rates combined with a global resurgence of the virus.

Measles is one of the most contagious pathogens known to humans. About 90% of unvaccinated people exposed to it will become infected, and roughly one in five cases results in hospitalization. This year, about 11% of cases — some 591 people — have required hospital care, according to [CDC data](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/data-research/index.html). The virus can cause deadly complications, including pneumonia and swelling of the brain.

The outbreak has been particularly cruel to the very young. At least four of this year's deaths were in babies too young to receive their first dose of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine. In response, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin are now recommending an early dose for infants aged 6 to 11 months in areas with high case counts. The standard schedule calls for a first dose around a child's first birthday and a second at ages 4 to 6; two doses are about 97% effective.

## The Death-Toll Dispute

The figure of 10 deaths is itself contested. The CDC has reported only six measles-related deaths on its official website — a count that is nonetheless twice the number recorded in 2025, [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/12/cdc-measles-deaths) reported.

The gap between the state-reported total and the federal figure stems from a change in how the CDC tallies measles fatalities. On September 4, the agency switched to using data from the National Center for Health Statistics to track mortality trends, and it does not identify which states the deaths came from. The shift followed reporting that Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. had asked CDC Director Erica Schwartz not to count certain Pennsylvania deaths in the official tally, arguing without evidence that they were not measles-associated.

The dispute became a public clash between Kennedy and Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro, who accused the secretary of downplaying the severity of the outbreak. Pennsylvania classifies a death as "measles-associated" when a person with confirmed measles dies within 30 days of symptom onset — a long-standing approach used by the World Health Organization and the CDC. The CDC has said it is working with the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists to standardize a national definition for measles deaths.

## Losing Elimination Status

The rising toll coincides with the loss of the nation's measles elimination status, which the U.S. first earned in 2000 when the virus was declared no longer continuously spreading. The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the World Health Organization's regional arm for the Americas, met this week to review whether the U.S. still qualified. Under PAHO rules, a country loses the designation if the same measles strain spreads uninterrupted for at least 12 months — a determination made using genetic sequencing and epidemiological data. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/14/measles-associated-deaths-reach-10-in-us-after-two-more-deaths-reported), citing reporting by The Wall Street Journal, said the status had been revoked; NBC News reported that PAHO's formal announcement was expected on Tuesday.

Canada lost its status last year on the same grounds, and Spain and the United Kingdom have recently followed. The U.S. remains a PAHO member even after withdrawing from the WHO.

The designation carries no immediate legal penalty, but experts describe its loss as a significant public-health setback. "It would be a disappointment, not a shock," Matt Ferrari, director of Penn State's Center for Infectious Disease Dynamics, told Al Jazeera. "This is moving backwards from a great public health achievement, and to see that backslide is just heart-wrenching." Edwin Asturias, a professor of infectious disease and epidemiology at the University of Colorado, put it more bluntly: losing the status is "a stain on your health programme."

## Vaccination Gap at the Root

At the heart of the crisis is a widening immunization gap. Nationwide, about 75% of counties and jurisdictions with measles vaccine data fall below the 95% vaccination rate needed to prevent outbreaks, according to an NBC News investigation. The share of kindergartners vaccinated against measles fell from 95.2% in the 2019-20 school year to 92.4% in 2025-26, according to the CDC.

The outbreak has unfolded under a Trump administration that has moved to change the childhood vaccine schedule and has elevated vaccine-skeptical views, with Kennedy — who has a long history of questioning vaccine safety — leading HHS. The virus's resurgence here also reflects its spread across age groups: 40% of U.S. cases this year were among people aged 5 to 19, and 41% among adults 20 and older, up from a 29% adult share last year, per CDC data.

## What to Watch

Wisconsin Health Secretary Kirsten Johnson said the death of an infant connected to the state's outbreak had left her heartbroken. "Any death of a child is a tragedy and even more so when it is related to a vaccine-preventable disease," she said in a statement.

Attention now turns to efforts to contain the outbreak and rebuild coverage. On Tuesday, PAHO was expected to issue its formal announcement on the U.S. status. Brazil offers a cautionary precedent for recovery: it lost its elimination status in 2019 but regained it in 2024 after an aggressive vaccination campaign — though experts are skeptical the U.S. would mount a similarly robust response.

For now, health officials in the hardest-hit states are focused on the basics: early doses for infants, stepped-up vaccination drives, and persuading families to close the coverage gap that allowed a once-eliminated disease to reclaim a foothold.
