# When Fleeing Forced Marriage Isn't the End of the Story

At around six o'clock one morning, 29-year-old Lili was lying half-awake on a hostel bed in an eastern Chinese city. She had arrived less than a week earlier, hoping to find work and a life away from home. Someone tapped her leg and pulled back the curtain. It was her mother — accompanied by her brother, a cousin and two men she did not recognise. When she refused to return to her hometown, insisting "either I stay out here, or I die out here," her brother slapped her and the group dragged her into a car. As [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34445476) reports in a wide-ranging feature on the "rescue gap" (救助断点) facing women who flee arranged or forced marriages, getting away is only the first battle — and for many, one they ultimately lose.

## A rescue, then a return

Lili's story is a case study in how institutional support fails at the moment it is most needed. She had alerted police at a highway service area on the way back, but officers reportedly treated the situation as a "family dispute" and told the parties to negotiate among themselves. Later she learned that her family had tracked not only her location but the details of her daily life away from home — including what takeaway food she had ordered. Her mother's message was blunt: "Don't think about going anywhere; it can all be tracked down now."

The feature situates Lili's ordeal within a broader pattern. She had been drawn into the escape attempt through an online mutual-aid group where women share strategies, build shared documents cataloguing needs such as jobs and housing, and coordinate help. One of the volunteers, identified by the pseudonym Heping, described girls who were "too optimistic" about the risks of leaving — one was eventually found by her family and taken back after police mediation. The networks are informal, short-term and, as their organiser concedes, no substitute for a stable system.

## The legal limits of rescue

The obstacles stack up at every stage. For minors, lawyers say intervention hits a wall the moment a girl is confirmed safe. Lin Lixia, project lead at the Beijing Qianqian Law Firm, told The Paper that lawyers cannot override a guardian's decisions for a minor, and that police involvement typically ends once the child's safety is verified. Education offers little further leverage: Zou Linshan of Sichuan Yukunhe Law Firm noted that high school is non-compulsory, so education departments and schools can only "persuade" parents, not compel attendance.

Criminal law, meanwhile, sets a high bar for prosecution. The offence of "violence interfering with marriage freedom" requires actual violence; softer coercion — verbal, emotional or economic — is hard to characterise as a crime. "The idea that 'parents harming their children is also illegal' has not been fully established in society," Zou said. Wang Shuwen, also of the Qianqian firm, pointed to a possible avenue under the 2016 Anti-Domestic Violence Law: personal safety protection orders, which could in principle cover forced marriage and stalking, though she acknowledged there is no precedent of their being applied to a forced-marriage case.

The human cost of these gaps is borne disproportionately by minors. The feature recounts a Gansu girl, pseudonymised as Huihui, who was pulled out of high school at 15 and pressured into marrying a man nine years her senior. She fled, but was found after her parents filed a missing-person report; later she told the lawyers that her parents had threatened to "kill your lawyers" if she kept running. She eventually cut contact with the firm. "I'd rather die on the road to freedom than live without it," she had told them.

## A system that shelters too few

China has built an extensive anti-domestic-violence shelter network on paper — the first opened in 1995, and by 2016 there were more than 2,000 sites nationally. Yet awareness is low and usage minimal. According to All-China Women's Federation data, only 149 shelter services were provided in all of 2015. The exception proves the rule: Southern Weekly [profiled](https://www.infzm.com/contents/198828) Kunming's shelter, where director Chu Junxiu and ten female staff, working without a formal establishment or dedicated funding, received 1,196 victims and provided more than 2,700 consultations over 12 years — an outlier rather than a model.

Lawyers contrast this with overseas systems that offer short- and long-term shelters staffed with employment counsellors, psychologists and childcare support, and with the mutual-aid shelters run by social workers in Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan. The All-China Women's Federation plays an auxiliary role — coordinating temporary stays at rescue stations and communicating with police — but it is not a substitute for a full rescue chain.

## Accountability and the price of inaction

The feature's publication comes two months after a case that drew national attention and focused the debate. In September, an 18-year-old Gansu girl who had been secretly betrothed by her family, forced to drop out of school and placed by a charity in Shenzhen for work was abducted on a city street on 26 August by three men hired by her parents. A retired veteran alerted police, and Shenzhen officers mounted a cross-province operation to rescue her in Lanzhou. As [Sina](https://news.sina.com.cn/c/2026-09-11/doc-inirmruc2436644.shtml) reported, commentators argued that if the abductors faced no consequences — "caught red-handed, at most the child is sent back" — it would effectively condone the next forced marriage. Questions were also raised about how the parents obtained their daughter's Shenzhen address through a local police station.

The feature notes that, as of publication, no public report indicated that those involved in the August abduction faced administrative or civil penalties. Zou said that in mid-September, a lawyer familiar with the case understood that the individuals involved had not been subject to public-security punishment or civil liability.

None of this is new. In 2020, a 17-year-old in Maoming, Guangdong, reported her own parents to the local women's federation to stop a forced "wedding," which was called off the same day, [China Youth Daily](https://3w.huanqiu.com/a/df051c/3zQwzOTYvxr) reported. In 2023, a 16-year-old Sichuan girl who fled to Guangdong was found by the groom's family and only rescued after calling police; her parents had received 260,000 yuan in bride price. Ten ministries issued guidance in June 2020 targeting dropout caused by early marriage and childbirth — but enforcement remains weak where high school is not compulsory.

## What sustainable help would look like

The gap, in other words, is layered: policing that treats forced marriage as a private matter; an education system that cannot compel attendance; a criminal law whose violence threshold is too high; untested protection orders; an underused and underfunded shelter network; a women's federation with only auxiliary powers; and volunteer networks that are informal and unscalable. Underlying it all is a gender culture that, in some regions, prizes sons and devalues girls' education — incentivising both early marriage and the extraction of bride price.

Lin Lixia's prescription is institutional: rescue funds that fleeing women can apply for, plus shelters staffed with employment and psychological counsellors, coordinated across police, civil affairs and civil society so that an officer responding to a call can recognise a woman in need and route her to real help. As a widely shared comment under the feature put it, the rescue gap is not in the moment of a single rescue, but on the long road from getting out to standing on one's own.

For Lili, that road remains uncertain. What's clear is that until the gaps are closed, each headline rescue will remain what it has always been — a moment of luck, not a system of care.
