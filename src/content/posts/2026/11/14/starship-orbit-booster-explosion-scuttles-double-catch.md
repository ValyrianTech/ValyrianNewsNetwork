# Starship Reaches Orbit, but Booster Explosion Scuttles Catch

SpaceX's Starship rocket soared into orbit on its 15th test flight Friday, but the mission fell short of its boldest goal: an unprecedented "double catch" in which both the Super Heavy booster and the upper-stage spacecraft would have been plucked from midair by the launch tower's mechanical arms. Instead, the booster was diverted from its planned tower catch and splashed into the Gulf of Mexico, where it exploded roughly six and a half minutes after liftoff.

Even so, the spacecraft itself achieved orbital flight and deployed a full batch of satellites, marking another milestone in the company's rapid test cadence and keeping its push toward full reusability on course.

## A Historic Ambition, a Familiar Setback

The flight lifted off from SpaceX's Starbase facility in Boca Chica, South Texas, on Friday evening. Company founder Elon Musk had framed the double catch as a "critical breakthrough needed to make life multiplanetary" — the goal of recovering and recycling every piece of the world's largest rocket to enable the fast, consistent launch rates needed for future moon bases and Mars missions.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/spacex-starship-rocket-elon-musk-f9452dd86770dda36828a35bb27d65ee), the first-stage booster was diverted to an offshore point instead of returning to its Texas pad, despite previous successful booster grabs. The setback was not the news SpaceX hoped to deliver. "That certainly isn't the news that we had hoped to hear for today," said Kate Tice, SpaceX's director of quality management, before noting that the booster's return was in line with the company's "rigorous safety checks."

Speaking earlier in the week at a NASA tech summit at Kennedy Space Center, SpaceX vice president of launch Kiko Dontchev underscored what was at stake. "Falcon rockets are still not fully reusable," he said. "We still throw that second stage away every single flight. Starship, we're going to catch both the first stage and second stage."

## Mission Still Delivers Core Objectives

While the missed catch captured attention, the flight achieved the bulk of its technical goals. Ship 42, the upper stage, fired a Raptor engine for its orbital insertion burn and reached orbit — only the second time a Starship has done so, following a September test. It then deployed all 26 of its next-generation Starlink V3 satellites through a "Pez-dispenser"-style bay.

The spacecraft was also on a long, roughly 14-orbit mission expected to last up to 22 hours, and was set to attempt the world's first-ever catch of an orbital-class upper stage at Starbase on Saturday afternoon. As [Space.com](https://www.space.com/news/live/spacex-starship-flight-15-live-updates-nov-14-2026-starship-first-double-landing-attempt) reported, SpaceX officials said the heat shield appeared healthy for reentry, clearing the way for that separate, potentially headline-making milestone.

"Starship's going to stay in space. We are one step closer to an eventual return to launch site. So that's great news," SpaceX spokesperson Dan Huot said.

## Troubleshooting the Booster

The cause of the booster's abort was initially murky. Huot had said the company would "dig in" to determine what kept the booster from the tower. By Saturday, SpaceX had clarified: the booster's guidance, navigation and control system performed as expected, detecting that the booster was out of position and triggering an automated abort that sent it into the Gulf. The root cause remained under investigation.

"The team's still digging into the root cause on how we got there, and so, more to come on that," Huot said.

The rocket at the center of it all remains staggering in scale. According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/nov/13/spacex-starship-megarocket-blasts-off-earth-orbit), Starship stands 407 feet (124 meters) tall and is the world's largest and most powerful rocket. Its lower Super Heavy booster carries 33 Raptor engines, while the upper stage towers 171 feet (52 meters). The flight proceeded even as a rare nationwide FAA ground stop disrupted commercial air travel, with flights to the West Coast from Hawaii briefly paused for space debris before resuming.

## What's Next

The booster's loss deprives SpaceX of a complete-recovery demonstration on this flight, but it is a data point rather than a capability gap: the company had made three successful booster catches at Starbase before this attempt, and Flight 15 was the first try with the upgraded V3 production booster. The bigger prize — a successful capture of the upper stage — would represent the first recovery of an orbital-class spacecraft stage, advancing the full-reusability vision that underpins everything from Starlink expansion to Musk's Mars ambitions.

The stakes extend well beyond SpaceX. NASA has contracted the company, alongside Jeff Bezos' Blue Origin, to provide lunar landers for its Artemis program, which aims to return astronauts to the moon by 2028. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, reacting to the orbit milestone, wrote on X that the agency is "looking forward to some of that on-orbit Starlink capability on Artemis III."

SpaceX also plans to launch Starships from Florida within a few months and from Louisiana within a few years, a sign that the iterative, crash-and-fix approach will continue. Whether Saturday's upper-stage catch succeeds or not, Flight 15 reinforced a familiar pattern: SpaceX keeps pushing the envelope, and its failures tend to be as instructive as its triumphs.
