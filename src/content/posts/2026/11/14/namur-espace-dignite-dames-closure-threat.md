# Namur women's day centre faces closure threat

A day centre in Namur that provides the only women-only refuge of its kind in Wallonia has warned it may have to cut back services as early as this winter, as insufficient funding and an upcoming loss of its premises threaten its survival. The Espace Dignité Dames in Saint-Servais welcomes more than 20 women and several children each day, offering meals, showers, laundry, rest and social support to women facing homelessness, isolation and gender-based violence.

According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/a-namur-l-espace-dignite-dames-menace-un-refuge-essentiel-pour-les-femmes-en-situation-de-precarite-11813421), the non-profit organisation behind the centre, ASBL Educ'Actions & Dignité, is appealing for support from citizens, businesses, foundations and public authorities to keep its doors open.

## A place where women can exist

The centre, created in 2023, is described by its operators as the only service specifically dedicated to women in Wallonia. It offers a free, mixed-choice space where women can recover a sense of dignity and rebuild social ties.

"It's a step over the threshold that is open to women who are on the street, and also to isolated people and single mothers," volunteer Mireille Bouillon told [Boukè](https://www.bouke.media/info/namur-lespace-dignite-dames-besoin-daide/23010). "It's a friendly space where each woman comes into her own home, where each arrives as she is, with her baggage."

For the women who rely on it, the centre is more than a service. "Here, I feel that I exist," one beneficiary told RTBF, a sentiment echoed by another woman, Karine, in earlier coverage by [L'Avenir](https://www.lavenir.net/regions/namur/2026/08/20/namur-un-appel-a-la-solidarite-pour-sauver-lespace-dignite-dames-6MLJTU2S5JHKRPDGS6ODIDWKOY/): "Without them, I wouldn't be here anymore. Here I've found a place where I can be myself."

Specialised educator Mariemme described the bonds that form over time. "We become like a family," she told RTBF. Asked what women fear most about a possible closure, she relayed their question: "What will become of us? Where will we go?"

## Funding shortfall and an uncertain roof

The centre's future is clouded by two overlapping problems. The first is financial: without sufficient resources, some activities could be suspended as early as winter. Earlier this year, the association set a target of raising 50,000 euros to keep its three specialised educators in place, a sum reported by both Boukè in July and L'Avenir in August, though the November coverage frames the need more generally as insufficient funding.

"With 50,000 euros we can already gain time. We can continue to work with the three educators who are here," association president Robert Bourgeois told Boukè, adding that the group is also trying to build a foundation to secure longer-term funding and hopes a network of partner associations will emerge.

The second problem is the building itself. The premises currently occupied are made available by the construction company Thomas & Piron, but the organisation will have to vacate them in the medium term, RTBF reported. A change of location would add logistical pressure at the very moment the centre is fighting to stabilise its finances.

Volunteers have not hidden their frustration with the absence of structural public support. "If there are no more associations, it's a real catastrophe. I call that failure to assist a person in danger," volunteer Claire Colette told Boukè. "There is no structural response from the public authorities. Yet public authorities are morally obliged to respond to these basic needs."

## A gendered gap in homelessness services

The centre's defenders argue its value lies in addressing a specific blind spot. Bourgeois noted that women make up only about a third of the street population, which he said helps explain why general services have historically been shaped around men's needs.

"Mixed services are good, but there are needs that are entirely specific to women. And here, unfortunately, it is the only service that exists in the Walloon Region," he told Boukè. The centre's approach — combining basic help with listening, collective cooking and workshops — reflects a model of care built around trauma, safety and single motherhood.

This is not the organisation's first funding crisis. In 2022, its broader Espace Dignité in Jambes, which then welcomed up to about 70 people a day, needed 150,000 euros by the end of November to avoid closure, as [Boukè reported at the time](https://www.bouke.media/info/lespace-dignite-jambes-pourrait-se-refermer-sauf-si-150000-euros-sont-trouves/7557). The recurrence underscores the chronic fragility of day-reception services that depend on precarious, project-based subsidies topped up by community appeals.

The organisation is listed in the directory of [ARCA asbl](https://arca-asbl.org/educaction/), the Walloon federation of reception and accommodation services for people in social difficulty, which records its address on Rue de l'Industrie in Saint-Servais and its services including meals, showers, laundry and psychological support.

## What's next

The association is pinning part of its hopes on public generosity and on a pair of benefit events: Les Silencieuses on 20–21 November at the Maison de la Poésie de Namur, and a concert on 10 January 2027 at the Théâtre royal de Namur featuring Didier Laloy, Astridin Expérience and Les Cordes de la Néthen.

Beyond the immediate fundraising, the case raises a larger question about how the Walloon Region funds frontline social services. Operators and volunteers argue that basic needs — eating, washing, clean clothing and human connection — should not depend on the outcome of periodic appeals. Whether public authorities step in with structural funding, and where the centre will operate once it must leave its current premises, remain the two open questions on which its survival now hinges.
