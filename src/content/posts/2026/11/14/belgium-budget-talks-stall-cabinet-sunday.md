# Belgium Budget Talks Stall Again as Cabinet Waits to Sunday

Belgium's federal budget negotiations remain deadlocked after the restricted cabinet (kernkabinet) ended its Friday evening session without a breakthrough on the 2027 budget, with Prime Minister Bart De Wever deciding against overnight talks and the full government top not due to reconvene until Sunday. The outcome, [reported by VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/opnieuw-geen-doorbraak-bij-kernkabinet-top-van-regering-komt-zo~1793247532380/), pushes the country closer to the moment when a provisional budget may become the only option—and sets up a decisive moment on Monday, when De Wever must report to King Filip at the Palace.

## A Coalition That Cannot Close the Gap

The government, led by De Wever of the Flemish nationalist N-VA, took office in February 2025 after 234 days of negotiation. Its so-called "Arizona" coalition brings together five parties—N-VA, CD&V, and Vooruit on the Flemish side, and MR and Les Engagés on the Francophone side—that must now agree on roughly €10 billion in savings and new revenues to bring the 2027 budget into order.

That arithmetic has repeatedly defied resolution. At the heart of the impasse is a single fault line: MR insists that no more than €1.1 billion of the €10 billion may come from new taxes, arguing that ceiling was fixed in the coalition agreement. Vooruit, backed by the centre parties CD&V and Les Engagés, wants something closer to €1.5 billion drawn from "the strongest shoulders." A possible VAT increase and changes to the wage indexation system are among the sensitive items on the table.

## A Hybrid Government Structure

The difficulty is compounded by an unusual structure analysts have described as a "hybrid" construction. Of the five party leaders who formed the government, only two actually entered the cabinet: De Wever and Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés. The other three—Georges-Louis Bouchez of MR, Conner Rousseau of Vooruit, and Sammy Mahdi of CD&V—remain outside the cabinet despite wielding decisive influence over the negotiations.

To bridge that gap, De Wever has been holding one-on-one meetings with each vice-premier, allowing each to bring one additional person. That device brought Bouchez, MR's pivotal and confrontational leader, into the room. But it has not yet produced a deal. David Clarinval, the MR vice-premier, has openly floated renegotiating the entire coalition agreement if the €1.1 billion ceiling is breached—a step he said would take more time but "remove red lines that now prevent us from taking decisions."

## Resignation Refused, Talks Resumed

The past week has followed a dramatic arc. After an earlier deadline of 6 November passed without agreement, De Wever offered his resignation to King Filip. The King refused it and asked the prime minister to resume negotiations. De Wever then began bilateral talks with each vice-premier, restarted on Monday 9 November, with Bouchez joining in a two-hour session that ended in silence from all sides.

On Wednesday, De Wever publicly said he hoped to bring "positive news" to the King on Monday but declined substantive comment. "To give the ongoing talks every chance, I will refrain from further comment on the substance," he said in parliament, while stressing the economic stakes: "The economic challenges we face are not minor. Healthier finances and stable government are crucial to tackle them."

Yet insiders remain cautious. As VRT NWS political journalist Benedikte Coussement noted, "behind the scenes there is little optimism among most parties that that big breakthrough is achievable by Monday, when Prime Minister De Wever must report to King Filip... If not, the end of this government still looms."

## The Cost of Drift

The stakes are not merely political. Under Belgian law, if no approved annual budget exists at the start of the year, the state operates under "provisional twelfths" (voorlopige twaalfden)—spending one-twelfth of the previous year's budgeted expenditures each month. This keeps salaries, pensions, and benefits flowing, but freezes new policy and signals instability to financial markets. De Wever had previously said he would never accept governing under such a regime.

The business community has been blunt. Pieter Timmermans, chairman of the Federation of Belgian Enterprises (VBO), warned of a worsening economic backdrop—rising inflation and interest rates, a pressured trade balance, growing bankruptcies, and industrial capacity utilisation at just 70 percent. "Everything is taxed in this country, except breathing in and out air," he said, arguing that "the best budget measure is a better-running economy." Former prime minister Yves Leterme called the current approach "confusing," comparing the government to a bicycle: "It keeps rolling if pushed. But if no partner still invests political energy in such a project, it stalls."

## Three Scenarios for Monday

As the working groups head into the weekend and the government top prepares to meet on Sunday, three scenarios hang over Monday's report to the King. The first is a deal—De Wever arrives at the Palace with an agreement. The second is further extension, meaning more weeks of drift and mounting pressure from markets and credit agencies. The third is collapse, with the government falling and the spectre of new elections rising—a prospect Vlaams Belang's Barbara Pas has openly championed, saying "there is no other alternative than new elections."

Even within the coalition, nerves are frayed. Axel Ronse, the N-VA parliamentary group leader, said it "would be criminal not to reach an agreement" given the country's finances, but admitted, "I've learned to live from day to day." CD&V chairman Sammy Mahdi warned that all parties must recognise they would be "responsible for political chaos with a big economic impact" if talks fail.

Whether the weekend produces a compromise or merely more delay remains the open question. What is clear is that Belgium's fiscal credibility—and the durability of its year-old coalition—now rests on a handful of days of negotiation, with the King waiting at the end of them.
