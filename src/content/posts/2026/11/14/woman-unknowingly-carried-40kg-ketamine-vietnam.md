# Woman unknowingly carried 40kg of ketamine to Vietnam

A 28-year-old Vietnamese student living in Belgium was arrested at Brussels Airport last year after security officers discovered 40 kilograms of ketamine hidden in her checked luggage — a shipment she says she had no idea she was carrying. Her account, aired in a [VRT NWS Pano](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/09/pano-ketamine-smokkel-getuigenis/) investigation, lays bare how international drug networks recruit ordinary travellers as unwitting couriers and leave them to face the consequences alone.

The woman, identified only by the pseudonym "Anh" to protect her identity, was jailed at Haren prison before investigators confirmed from her phone messages that she had acted in good faith. She was released after several weeks in pre-trial detention. The compatriot who handed her the suitcase was never found.

## A friendship that became a trap

Speaking anonymously to Pano, Anh described how the scheme began innocuously. A Vietnamese compatriot she had met only weeks earlier had helped her adjust to student life in Belgium. "She helped me through the hard student life and gave me tips on how to spend my time in Belgium. So for me she was just a friend," Anh said.

Then came a seemingly innocent request: would Anh, who was about to visit her family in Vietnam, mind taking some spare suitcase kilos of sweets and chocolates for the friend's relatives? To reassure her, the friend sent photos of a suitcase packed with candy, along with purchase receipts. "That is how I was pulled into her criminal network," Anh said.

At check-in at Zaventem airport, a security agent opened the chocolate packages and bags of sweets and found white powder. "At that moment I knew it was something illegal," she recalled. Police found a total of 40 kilograms of ketamine in her baggage.

## "I would never have seen my parents again"

Anh said she is acutely aware of how much worse her ordeal could have been. "With this amount of ketamine, in my own country I would have ended up in prison for life. Then I would never have seen my parents again," she said.

Mickael Splingard, a magistrate at the Halle-Vilvoorde prosecutor's office, confirmed that Anh's case is far from isolated and that criminal networks deliberately seek out the most innocent-looking couriers. "We have seen a lot when it comes to drug couriers — from minors to the mentally disabled to very old people to cancer patients with only a few months to live," he said. "Financial gain takes precedence over any moral compass."

Splingard described how Anh's release hinged on the reading of her phone. "The lady was brought before the judge and spent several weeks in pre-trial detention. Until the moment the mobile phone was read. And those messages did indeed show that the lady was acting in good faith. Once it was clear her statement was credible, she was released immediately."

He added the blunt reality of these operations: "It is usually the couriers who get caught. Not those who send them."

## A sharply rising trend at Zaventem

Anh's case fits a broader pattern. In 2024, three drug couriers were arrested at Zaventem; so far in 2026, that figure has already reached 11. The arrests come amid a surge in the flow of ketamine into and through Europe, documented in a [companion Pano investigation](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/09/pano-ketamine/) published this week.

That reporting found that legal ketamine imports into Europe have exploded over the past decade. Germany alone imported 153 tonnes of ketamine from India last year — roughly 1,000 times the 126 kilograms recorded in 2016. About 95 percent of legal ketamine imports into Europe pass through Germany, where pharmaceutical import rules are laxer than in Belgium.

The mass legal flow creates a lucrative shadow. A kilogram of ketamine can be imported into Germany for 350 to 500 euros, while its illegal market value in Belgium runs between 7,000 and 25,000 euros. A European Union Drugs Agency report cited by Pano concludes that "large-scale import of the active pharmaceutical ingredient ketamine from India to Germany appears to be the main source of ketamine entering the EU for further distribution to legal and illegal markets."

## The couriers who pay the price

For Splingard and Belgian authorities, the asymmetry at the heart of the trade is the central problem. The organizers behind the shipments are rarely identified, while the couriers who carry the goods face the full weight of the law — even when, as in Anh's case, they are victims themselves.

Customs officer Werner Van der Cruyssen, quoted in the companion investigation, described enforcement as "mopping with the tap running," with millions of parcels passing through Belgian customs daily and only a fraction inspected. "The criminals who started this have essentially invented the egg of Columbus... they become as rich as the sea is deep," he said.

## What to watch

The full Pano documentary, part of a "Ketamine Kartel" series airing on VRT MAX, is expected to detail the route from Indian factories to German importers and onward to Europe's black market. For now, Anh's case stands as a warning about how easily ordinary travellers can be drawn into the drug trade without their knowledge — and how unevenly the consequences are distributed.

As Splingard put it, the people who send the couriers almost always remain out of reach. "It is usually the couriers who get caught. Not those who send them."
