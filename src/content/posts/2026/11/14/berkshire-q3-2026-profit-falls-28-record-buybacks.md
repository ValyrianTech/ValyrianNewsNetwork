# Berkshire Profit Falls 28% as Buybacks Hit Record

Berkshire Hathaway reported a 28% year-on-year drop in third-quarter net profit, even as the conglomerate accelerated its share buybacks to a single-quarter record, stayed a net seller of stocks, and pushed its cash reserves to nearly $377 billion. The results, released on Saturday, November 7, offer the clearest picture yet of how Greg Abel is running the company in his first year as chief executive—and of the cautious, valuation-disciplined posture Berkshire is taking toward a richly priced market.

Net profit attributable to shareholders fell to $22.112 billion from $30.796 billion a year earlier, according to [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/07/berkshire-hathaway-q3-2026-earnings-buybacks.html). Revenue, however, rose 11.24% year-on-year to $105.647 billion, and the operating businesses that Berkshire actually controls held up far better than the headline number suggests.

## A Drop Driven by Accounting, Not Operations

Berkshire's preferred measure of performance—operating earnings, which strip out swings in its vast equity portfolio—came in at $12.705 billion, down 5.8% from $13.485 billion a year earlier. The far larger decline in net profit was driven almost entirely by investment gains, which fell nearly 50% to $9.407 billion from $17.311 billion.

Under US accounting rules, Berkshire must mark its equity holdings to market each quarter, funneling unrealized gains and losses through net income. As [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34429766), the Chinese outlet that first reported the figures, noted, Berkshire itself cautions that the investment gains or losses in any given quarter are "usually meaningless" and that the resulting net income figures "can be highly misleading to investors with limited understanding of accounting rules." Warren Buffett, now chairman emeritus, made the same argument for decades: operating earnings are the truer gauge of the business.

The investment-gain slump was compounded by Berkshire's newest top holding. The company made a $10 billion strategic investment in Alphabet in June 2026 as part of the tech giant's roughly $80 billion equity raise. Alphabet shares have since fallen nearly 25%, a decline analysts cite as a key drag on the quarter's investment results.

## The Operating Businesses: Mixed, but Resilient

Beneath the accounting noise, Berkshire's operating segments told a story of modest strength offset by weakness in insurance.

The railroad, BNSF, posted after-tax profit of $1.729 billion, up about 19% year-on-year. Manufacturing, service and retail earned $4.042 billion in operating profit, up roughly 12% from $3.616 billion. Berkshire Hathaway Energy contributed $1.612 billion, up about 8%.

Insurance was the drag. Underwriting profit tumbled 43% to $1.352 billion from $2.369 billion, while insurance investment income fell 14% to $2.734 billion. That single segment accounted for most of the decline in operating earnings—a pullback that raises questions about whether the weakness is cyclical, driven by catastrophe losses or softer pricing, or something more structural.

## A Record Buyback and a Growing Cash Pile

The most striking element of the quarter was how Berkshire deployed its capital—in two directions at once.

The company repurchased about $9.6 billion of its own stock in the third quarter, more than double the $4.5 billion bought back in the second quarter and a single-quarter record, exceeding the previous high of roughly $9.3 billion set in the third quarter of 2020. Total buybacks for the first nine months of 2026 reached about $14.4 billion, a sharp reversal from 2025, when Berkshire repurchased no stock at all.

At the same time, Berkshire was a net seller of equities, shedding about $426 million of stocks in the quarter after being a net buyer of $11.625 billion in the first half. And its cash and equivalents, including Treasury bills, climbed to $377.435 billion as of September 30, up about 3% from $365.514 billion in the prior quarter. Excluding rail and utility cash and certain T-bill payables, core cash stood at about $352.3 billion, down 1.9% quarter-on-quarter—a nuance that suggests the headline increase was partly a matter of classification.

## What the Buyback Signal Means

Berkshire's buyback framework is unusually explicit. As [The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/11/09/warren-buffett-s-rule-for-stock-buybacks-is-very-specific-berkshire-spent-a-record-usd9-6-billion-on-them-last-quarter/) detailed, Buffett's 2011 rule holds that repurchases make sense only when a company has ample funds for its operational and liquidity needs and its stock is selling at a material discount to conservatively calculated intrinsic value. Under the current framework, the CEO—Abel—authorizes buybacks after consulting the board chairman, Howard Buffett, when shares are believed to trade below intrinsic value.

The record buyback, executed at an average of roughly $507 per Class B share, is therefore a statement: Abel judged Berkshire's own stock to be among the best available uses of capital. As Motley Fool analyst Daniel Sparks put it, the record repurchase is "a strong sign that about $507 was a good price for Berkshire stock, and a far weaker one than about $570 is."

That distinction matters now. By mid-November, Berkshire shares had climbed 12% to 15% above the September buyback average, trading near $570 to $593. If the pace of repurchases continues at record levels even at those prices, it would signal that Abel still sees the stock as undervalued; if it slows, it would confirm that the buyback surge was price-sensitive.

## Abel's First Year Takes Shape

The quarter is also a window into the leadership transition. Abel succeeded Buffett as CEO in January 2026, after Buffett stepped down at the end of 2025 and later became chairman emeritus. In June, Abel made his first major deal since taking over—a roughly $6.8 billion cash acquisition of homebuilder Taylor Morrison, [reported](https://www.news4jax.com/business/2026/06/01/new-berkshire-hathaway-ceo-greg-abel-makes-first-deal-since-taking-over-from-warren-buffett/) by News4JAX—a signal that he may prove more operationally engaged, and more willing to deploy capital into acquisitions, than his predecessor's hands-off posture.

The market has so far greeted the transition with equanimity. Berkshire Class A shares rose more than 12% in the month before earnings, while the S&P 500 fell 5.5%, a performance read partly as fading anxiety over the CEO change and partly as a defensive rotation during an AI-driven selloff in technology stocks. Year to date, Berkshire has gained 13%, against 7.8% for the broad index.

## What to Watch

Three questions will shape the next chapter. First, will the record buyback pace persist now that the stock trades well above its September levels? Second, how deep and how long will the insurance underwriting weakness run? And third, will Abel's early appetite for deals—Taylor Morrison, the Alphabet stake—mark a genuine shift toward more active capital deployment, or a pair of opportunistic moves within Buffett's long-established discipline?

For now, the quarter paints a coherent picture: a company whose operating businesses remain sturdy, whose reported profit is being distorted by the mark-to-market of a portfolio it does not trade, and whose new chief executive is signaling, through the twin acts of buying back record amounts of stock and hoarding record amounts of cash, that he sees few bargains in the market—except, perhaps, Berkshire itself.
