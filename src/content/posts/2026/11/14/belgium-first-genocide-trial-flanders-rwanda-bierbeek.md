# Belgium Set for First Genocide Trial in Flanders Over Rwanda

Belgian federal prosecutors want a 78-year-old man tried before the Leuven court of assizes on charges of genocide and crimes against humanity for his alleged role in the 1994 Rwandan massacre, a case that would mark the first genocide trial ever held in Flanders. The suspect, identified under Belgian court convention as Jean Baptiste R., was arrested at a care facility in Bierbeek, near Leuven, and his detention was confirmed in early November.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/14/verdachte-van-genocide-in-rwanda-opgepakt-in-bierbeek/), the man is of Rwandan origin but has held Belgian nationality since he was naturalised in 2005. He had been living for several years at an institution run by the Catholic congregation Broeders van Liefde, which renamed itself Evara at the start of 2026. The news of his arrest only became public on 14 November, weeks after he was taken into custody on 28 September.

## What the Prosecution Alleges

The federal prosecutor's office says R. served in 1994 as director of the Home de la Vierge des Pauvres in Gatagara, a residential centre for children and young people with physical disabilities. Prosecutors allege that when Hutu Interahamwe militias stormed the centre, he actively identified Tutsi who had taken refuge there, first the men, and several days later the women and children, all of whom were subsequently killed.

As [NOS](https://nos.nl/artikel/2639441-78-jarige-verdachte-van-genocide-rwanda-opgepakt-in-belgie) reported, investigators believe R. had earlier contact with the militia, organising and attending meetings that prosecutors characterise as preparations for the genocide. He is also accused of forcing staff and patients to carry identification cards stating their ethnic origin, making them easier for extremists to single out. Belgian prosecutors gathered evidence during a rogatory commission in Rwanda in June this year, interviewing witnesses and visiting the Gatagara centre itself.

The charge period covers at least 1 April to 25 May 1994. The first massacre at the centre is dated to late April, 24 April according to Dutch-language outlets, though French-language reporting has cited 21 April, with a second attack on 9 May targeting mainly women and children.

## A Legal Milestone for Belgium

Belgium has tried suspects over the Rwandan genocide before, but always in Brussels. VRT reported that such proceedings have been held in the capital six times, while other outlets have counted seven trials yielding twelve convictions. This case would be the first to reach a court of assizes in Flanders, a significant moment for a country whose legal system has become a hub for prosecuting atrocities committed far beyond its borders.

The road to prosecution has been long. According to [De Morgen](https://www.demorgen.be/nieuws/broeder-van-liefde-bleef-jaren-uit-handen-van-gerecht-na-overplaatsing-naar-vaticaanstad-nu-in-bierbeek-opgepakt-als-verdachte-van-rwandese-genocide~bb27e266/), R. fled Rwanda in 1994 and is listed as number 1,309 on the country's first-category genocide suspects list. He was reportedly found in Rome by the Italian daily La Repubblica while subject to an Interpol red notice, but Italy refused extradition. In 2007, after a Belgian complaint was reportedly filed and the case surfaced in the press, the congregation transferred him to the Vatican. He returned to Belgium around 2023, reportedly for health reasons.

Rwanda itself alerted Belgian authorities that he had returned, triggering the investigation that led to his arrest. The federal prosecutor's office currently handles 37 ongoing Rwandan genocide cases, with six people charged and four in pre-trial detention; R. is the first to be arrested on Flemish territory.

## The Order's Reckoning

The case has reopened uncomfortable questions for the Broeders van Liefde, which has in recent years confronted child-abuse scandals and publicly apologised for a culture of silence within its ranks. Luc Lemmens, the congregation's Belgian head, told VRT that the news had hit hard. "Of course we are shocked by the nature of the allegations and the human suffering connected with such facts," he said. He added that candidate brothers are screened, but noted that R. joined the order in Rwanda more than fifty years ago, before the genocide and long before he came to Belgium.

Lemmens said he did not know whether anyone within the congregation was aware of the man's past. "If that were the case, it certainly never reached me. I was only informed when he was recently arrested." De Morgen has pressed the question of whether the order shielded R., pointing to the timing of his 2007 transfer to the Vatican.

R.'s lawyer, Gilles Vanderbeck, said his client is surprised to still be arrested in 2026 and denies any responsibility for the facts, according to De Morgen.

## What's Next

The suspect remains in pre-trial detention and has appealed. The chamber of indictment was expected to rule on that appeal in the week after the news broke. Beyond that, the case must proceed toward an assizes trial in Leuven, a lengthy process that will test both the strength of the evidence gathered in Rwanda and Belgium's capacity to deliver justice for crimes committed more than three decades ago.

The charges carry heavy symbolic weight. An estimated 800,000 to more than a million people, mostly Tutsi but also moderate Hutu, were killed over roughly 100 days in 1994, and ten Belgian UN peacekeepers were among the dead. For Belgium, the trial is a reckoning with a genocide that unfolded while the world, and its former colonial power, stood by, and a test of whether universal jurisdiction can still reach those who have spent decades evading it.
