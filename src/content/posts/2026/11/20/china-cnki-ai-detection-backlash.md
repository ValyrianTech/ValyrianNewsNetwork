# China's AI-Detection Backlash: When Algorithms Judge Authors

A sudden, unexplained surge in AI-content "detection" rates at CNKI, China's dominant academic database, has drawn fire from students and scholars who say unstable algorithms are wrongly branding their original work as machine-generated — and tying their degrees and careers to a doubtful number, [The Paper reported](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34513875).

At issue is AIGC (AI-generated content) detection, a service CNKI and competitors sell on a per-character basis. When CNKI upgraded its detection algorithm on October 24, 2026, reported "suspected-AI" rates jumped. The platform attributed the shift to a "version-iteration transitional phase," but the consequences fell on the writers.

## A Ruler That Changes Its Own Scale

The Paper's "Instant Test" studio ran a controlled comparison across China's three major platforms — CNKI, Wanfang and Weipu — using identical texts. In one test, a roughly 2,000-character piece that was 100% AI-generated scored 99.9% on [CNKI](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34503761), 49.97% on Weipu and just 24.68% on Wanfang. A second, 3,500-character AI-generated text scored 67.6% on CNKI, 69.8% on Weipu and only 7.15% on Wanfang.

Then came the more troubling result. A genuinely human-written journal submission of roughly 10,000 characters, based on field interviews, was flagged as 30.1% suspected AI by CNKI, 25.22% by Wanfang and 35.94% by Weipu. On CNKI, the flagged passages included the abstract, the author's viewpoints and the conclusion — even verbatim interview transcripts were marked red. All three platforms append the same caveat: "results for reference only."

The instability is not only across platforms but across time. One student, identified by the pseudonym Xiaowu, spent nearly 300 yuan on repeat checks. The same text that showed no AI markers on November 12 was flagged as AI-generated five days later. Three submissions Xiaowu wrote personally scored 35%, 9% and 55% respectively. "There isn't even a so-called national standard, so on what basis do you say my article is AI-generated?" Xiaowu asked.

## Why Good Writing Looks Like a Machine

Experts say the error is structural, not a temporary bug. Tsinghua University professor Shen Yang, dual-appointed to the journalism and AI schools, told The Paper that AIGC detection is "a probabilistic inference technology based on statistical distribution and semantic representation" — fundamentally different from traditional plagiarism checking, which compares literal strings against existing literature.

The problem, Shen argues, is that standard academic writing and large-model generation are "statistically isomorphic." Modern academic prose evolved to be objective, terminology-precise, structurally rigorous and emotionally neutral — a "low-entropy, high-consensus, strongly paradigm-constrained" corpus. That is precisely what AI models reproduce, so rigorous human writing is easily misjudged. Frequent AI use may even be "reverse-domesticating" humans, he warned: "not only does AI strive to imitate human expression habits, humans are also being reverse-domesticated."

Xiong Bingqi, director of the 21st Century Education Research Institute, described the same paradox to The Paper: "the more standardized and fluent, the more likely to be misjudged as AI." Because AI training materials themselves draw on vast existing literature and model essays, tightly structured human writing is vulnerable. "AI detection tools must themselves withstand detection before being used to detect student theses," he said.

## The Stakes: Degrees, Careers and Reputation

The detection scores are no longer academic abstractions. Sichuan University's 2026 undergraduate thesis rules cap AI content at 20% for humanities and 15% for STEM and medical fields. Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics requires all 2026 graduates to pass AIGC detection, with anything above 40% treated as a fail, and only two attempts allowed. Shandong Jianzhu University set a principled 40% threshold for graduate theses in September 2026. Some academic forums now require submission AIGC rates of 25% or below, with an attached report, to clear preliminary review.

Young researchers describe a "broken window effect": institutions first encouraged responsible AI use, then abruptly tightened one-size-fits-all controls, with new standards applied retroactively to completed work. Researcher Zhang Lin (pseudonym) found his carefully researched paper heavily flagged; re-uploading the same text produced a lower score. In a small experiment, a purely AI-generated text scored only 7%. The unstable scores, plus being "roughly judged by an unstable value with no channel to argue," left him feeling "a touch of nihilism toward the academic circle."

## A Commercial Conflict at the Heart of Trust

Critics point to a business incentive problem. Some platforms sell both AI detection and AI "reduction" (lowering detection scores), profiting from detection counts and per-character fees. Under commercial pressure, Xiong warned, they may "arbitrarily adjust backend algorithms, change judgment thresholds, artificially raise AI-generation rates, manufacture detection fluctuations, and induce students to pay for repeated re-testing." He called for platforms to publicly disclose their judgment basis and algorithm rules — "reproducible, traceable, testable."

Liu Xingliang, director of the DCCI Internet Research Institute, put the distinction bluntly: "'Like AI-written' does not equal 'actually AI-written.'" He argued that AIGC scores "should only be a review clue, not the sole basis for deductions, rejection or determining academic misconduct. The percentage looks precise, but doesn't mean the judgment is reliable."

## Rethinking How to Judge Thinking

Some academics are already changing course. Zhou Ruiming, a professor at Huazhong University of Science and Technology, has dropped take-home papers across his courses from the fall 2026 semester in favor of in-class pop quizzes on that week's hot topics, with no prior notice. "Writing with AI, you often seem to get a result, but you're not using your brain — your cognitive level isn't being exercised," he told The Paper. "I hope students use AI to become smarter."

A July 2026 paper by Wang Tianjian and Yang Chuang of Guangzhou University identified the core dilemma: AI-generated language mimics human language, so detecting it is, in a sense, questioning humans' own style; and forced "reduction" to avoid flags restricts academic expression and lowers text quality.

## What's Next

The Paper's [companion commentary](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34510673) argued that "relying on AI ghostwriting and relying on AI detection are essentially no different — the former hands thinking and writing to machines; the latter hands judgment and evaluation to machines." Academic integrity, it said, must rest on rigorous norms, solid peer review and responsible human review, not superstition about numbers.

The unresolved questions are large. Will regulators set a national detection standard and force platforms to disclose algorithms and error rates? Will CNKI offer free re-review for anomalous post-upgrade reports? And will universities revise hard thresholds in favor of processes that keep humans in the loop? Until then, Shen's warning stands: when "an algorithm's score is directly tied to a student's degree conferral, further study, employment and academic reputation, it takes on a public character." Algorithms can err — but how institutions treat the people they judge must have a safety net.
