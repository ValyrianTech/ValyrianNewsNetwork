# Van Gogh Sells for Record €173m at Auction

A painting by Vincent van Gogh has sold for just over €173m ($190.4m) at Sotheby's in New York, the highest price ever paid for a work by the Dutch post-Impressionist, Belgian broadcaster [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/19/schilderij-van-vincent-van-gogh-bloeiende-kastanjebomen-geve/). The sale of *Blooming Chestnut Trees* (*Bloeiende kastanjebomen*), painted in 1890 shortly before the artist's death, went ahead only after a last-minute settlement resolved a dispute over the painting's former Jewish owner during the Nazi era.

## A hidden masterpiece comes to market

The painting shows three women in colourful clothing walking away from the viewer in the shade of chestnut trees, while a man in blue work clothes descends the street in the opposite direction. Van Gogh painted it in 1890 in Auvers-sur-Oise, France, in the final weeks of a life that ended in July of that year.

According to VRT, the masterpiece had not been seen in public for 60 years. Sotheby's ranked it as the fourth most expensive artwork ever sold at auction.

Bidding opened at a level well below the final figure; the painting had been estimated beforehand at around €163 million but ultimately went under the hammer for more than €173 million. The buyer was the American art dealer and gallery owner Bill Acquavella.

The previous auction record for a Van Gogh stood at roughly €107 million, paid at Christie's in New York in 2022 for *Orchard with Cypresses* (1888). The new price is therefore a jump of more than 60% over the old record.

## The restitution dispute that nearly stopped the sale

For a time, it was uncertain whether the sale could proceed at all, because of a dispute between the seller and the descendants of a previous owner, the Jewish publisher Samuel Fischer.

According to his heirs, Fischer had sold the work in 1936 under pressure from the Nazi regime, forced to do so by the economic and political circumstances the Nazis had created. On the eve of the auction, Sotheby's announced that an agreement had been reached between the current owners — who acquired the painting in 1975 — and all legal heirs of Samuel Fischer. The details of the settlement were not disclosed.

The Art Newspaper reported that auctioneer Oliver Barker opened the sale by stating that "the work is being offered for sale pursuant to an agreement between the seller and the heirs of the Hedwig and Samuel Fischer family." The settlement preserved the sale while acknowledging the family's claim.

## Why the case matters beyond the saleroom

In recent years, the number of restitution claims for artworks that Jewish collectors were forced to relinquish during the Nazi period has risen again, and many of these cases are now settled out of court through mutual agreement, VRT noted. The Van Gogh case fits that pattern — and, as [The Art Newspaper](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2026/11/20/deeper-look-van-goghs-record-breaking-blossoming-chestnut-trees-190m-sale-restitution-claim) observed, the confidential settlement may open the door for the Fischer heirs to make claims for other family paintings should they come to market in the future.

That the claim surfaced so late — the painting was sold in 1936 and had passed through several owners before reaching Argentina in 1975 — underlines how the history of artworks displaced during the Nazi era continues to shadow the market decades later, even at the very top end.

## A resilient trophy market

The result is striking given the uncertainty that hung over the sale in its final days. Fears of a contested claim might have discouraged bidders, yet the painting still drew intense competition, with two telephone bidders and a bidder in the saleroom competing in a contest that lasted less than four minutes.

The sale also stands out against a softer broader market. It was the highlight of a larger evening auction, and The Art Newspaper noted that the other pictures in the sale generally sold for around, or even below, their lower estimates — underscoring how the Van Gogh carried the entire event.

The outcome confirms the enduring appetite for major Impressionist and Post-Impressionist masterpieces as trophy assets. For an artist who, by his own lifetime's account, struggled to sell his work, the record price is a remarkable coda — and a reminder that the market's appetite for the very best Van Gogh has, if anything, strengthened.

## What to watch next

Two questions remain open. The first is where *Blooming Chestnut Trees* will ultimately reside: Acquavella is widely presumed to have been acting for an unidentified client, so the painting's final home is not yet known.

The second is what the settlement means for other families pursuing Nazi-era restitution claims. If the Fischer case encourages further claims — and further negotiated resolutions — it will have done more than set a price record; it will have added a chapter to the long, unfinished story of how the art world reckons with its past.
