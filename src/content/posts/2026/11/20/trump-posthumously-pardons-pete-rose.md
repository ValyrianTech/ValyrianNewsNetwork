# Trump Posthumously Pardons Baseball Legend Pete Rose

President Donald Trump said Thursday that he had posthumously pardoned Pete Rose, baseball's all-time career hits leader, an act that carries little legal weight but deep symbolic significance for a figure who spent more than three decades fighting to restore his reputation. The pardon came roughly a year after Major League Baseball lifted the lifetime ban that had barred the late star from the Hall of Fame.

Trump announced the move on Truth Social while returning from a meeting in China, according to NBC News. "I have just given a Complete and Total Pardon to the Late, Great Pete Rose, a true Baseball SUPERSTAR & LEGEND," Trump wrote, adding that Rose was "treated terribly by Major League Baseball in not allowing him to go into the Baseball Hall of Fame."

## A Crime That Was Already Paid For

The pardon formally addresses Rose's conviction, not his baseball scandal. Rose pleaded guilty on April 20, 1990, to two counts of filing false tax returns, admitting he failed to report $354,968 over a four-year period. As [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/pete-rose-pardon-trump-baseball-14f398d4c657ccf55cb8a77cc7e60915) reported, U.S. District Judge S. Arthur Spiegel sentenced Rose to five months in prison, a $50,000 fine, and 1,000 hours of community service. Rose reported to a federal camp in Marion, Illinois, in August 1990 and was released in January 1991; he repaid the IRS $366,042.

Because Rose completed his sentence decades ago and died in September 2024 at age 83, the pardon changes nothing in his legal record. Its significance is chiefly messaging: a presidential statement that a celebrated but flawed icon was wronged and deserves to be remembered for his achievements.

## The Gambling Ban and Its Reversal

The more consequential barrier to Rose's legacy was never the tax case but baseball's own justice. Rose agreed to a permanent ban from the sport in August 1989 after an investigation by MLB's John M. Dowd concluded he had bet numerous times on the Cincinnati Reds while playing for and managing his hometown team from 1985 to 1987.

That ban began to lift in May 2025, when Commissioner Rob Manfred ruled that permanent ineligibility would expire upon an individual's death, restoring the status of 17 people, most notably Rose and Shoeless Joe Jackson. According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/donald-trump/posthumously-pardons-pete-rose-mlb-hit-leader-betting-baseball-rcna610308), Manfred said at the time that permanent ineligibility "ends upon the passing of the disciplined individual." The decision drew sharp criticism from Marcus Giamatti, son of the late commissioner A. Bartlett Giamatti, who secured Rose's ban, calling it "a very dark day for baseball."

With the ban lifted, Rose and Jackson became eligible for Hall of Fame consideration. A 1991 rule barring anyone on the permanently ineligible list from election no longer applies to them, opening a procedural path that could lead to induction as early as 2028, when the Hall's Classic Baseball Era Committee next meets in December 2027.

## Symbolism Over Substance

The pardon does not alter Rose's Hall of Fame candidacy. That question is governed by baseball, not the presidency, and was already unblocked by Manfred's 2025 ruling. What the pardon adds is public pressure and cultural weight, reinforcing Trump's long-standing argument that Rose belongs among the game's immortals.

Trump has championed Rose for years, repeatedly contending that betting on one's own team to win is fundamentally different from betting against it. He publicly signaled his intention to pardon Rose in March 2025, and Manfred later acknowledged that Trump had been influential in the reinstatement decision.

The episode also drew attention for a factual slip: Trump's initial post described Rose's 4,256 hits as a single-season record, when the total accumulated over 24 seasons from 1963 to 1986. The post was later edited to correct the achievement.

The move reinforces a broader pattern of Trump using clemency on behalf of figures he personally champions, blending a sports grievance with a political narrative. For baseball, the debate that has shadowed Rose since 1989, over integrity, redemption, and how the sport should remember a tainted legend, remains as unresolved as ever.

## What's Next

The immediate focus now shifts back to the Hall of Fame, where Rose's case will hinge on the judgment of the Classic Baseball Era Committee rather than any act of a president. The pardon may sway public sentiment, but the voters who will decide his place among the game's greats are bound by baseball's rules, not the powers of the Oval Office.

Rose, who was a 17-time All-Star, the 1963 National League Rookie of the Year, the 1973 NL MVP, and the 1975 World Series MVP, finished his career with records for hits (4,256), games (3,562), and at-bats (14,053). Whether that resume finally earns a plaque in Cooperstown now rests, as Judge Spiegel put it in 1990, on history and the tincture of time.
