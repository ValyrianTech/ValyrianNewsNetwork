# Yunmeng Tomb Find Yields Rare Qin Bamboo Slips

Archaeologists working in Yunmeng County, Hubei Province, have unearthed a new trove of Qin-dynasty bamboo slips and more than 600 sets of lacquered wooden artifacts, the National Cultural Heritage Administration announced on 18 November. The find at the Laohudun cemetery marks the third major discovery of large-scale precious bamboo slips in Yunmeng, following the celebrated Shuihudi Qin slips of 1975 and the more recent Zhengjiahu wooden gu.

## A Cemetery Dense with History

The Laohudun cemetery sits in Chengguan Town, in the southeastern suburbs of the ancient Yunmeng Chu King City site — an area thick with Warring States, Qin and Han remains. According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/culture/20261120/9fa7363747ff42e6b709cdf2b5fa35e5/c.html), the site lies roughly 2,800 meters west of the Shuihudi cemetery and 400 meters north of Zhengjiahu, placing it within one of China's most archaeologically productive landscapes.

Excavation was approved by the National Cultural Heritage Administration in 2026 and carried out jointly by the Hubei Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology and the Yunmeng County Museum. Beginning in April, the team has cleared 113 Qin-Han tombs to date, all vertical earthen pit tombs, with coffins either single or one outer and one inner. Forty of the tombs were waterlogged — a condition critical to the exceptional preservation of organic materials.

"The site has yielded more than 1,200 sets of burial goods, including lacquered wood, pottery, metal, jade, and bamboo slips," Zhao Jun, an associate research fellow at the Hubei Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology, said, as [reported](https://www.guancha.cn/ZhengZhi/2026_11_18_908949.shtml) by Guancha.

## Lacquerware and a Scientific First

The lacquerware assemblage is unusually rich. Among the standout forms are a standing-figure phoenix-shaped spoon, a fish-pattern basin and an auspicious-beast-pattern flat flask — several of them shapes and decorative motifs never before seen. Researchers also documented wooden dove-topped staffs, oval measuring vessels, and bi and heng pendants, alongside new lacquer inscriptions including the characters "广秦者."

In five tombs, clear paintings were found on the plank doors between the coffin chamber and the head compartment. And in a bioarchaeological first, the team completely extracted human brain tissue remains from a waterlogged, oxygen-poor burial environment — rare material for studying ancient human biology beyond the skeleton.

## Texts That Rewrite the Record

The bamboo slips, recovered from three tombs — M64, M74 and M116 — carry contents of striking scholarly value. The most significant, per officials, is the first-ever seen "garrison-defense equipment register" of a ting-level administrative unit beneath a Qin county, a document that illuminates the military-logistics administration of the lowest tier of local government.

Researchers also identified the first scientifically excavated materials on ancient women's moral education — a discovery that drew wide public discussion though its actual content has not yet been published. The slips additionally include miscellaneous prayer formulae and day-book divination texts with many new entries.

The Laohudun find was one of four Yangtze-civilization-origin results announced at the same "Archaeology of China" conference, alongside the Taohuahe site in Sichuan, the Liangzhu Fanshan site in Zhejiang and the Qiaomailing site cluster in Jiangxi, [Xinhua reported](https://www.news.cn/culture/20261119/896e162df6c147fe9a2b009aa0b8840d/c.html). The National Cultural Heritage Administration described Laohudun as "the cemetery with the most concentrated unearthed Qin slips to date," with materials spanning politics, military affairs, literature, medicine, folklore and art.

## Why Yunmeng Matters

Yunmeng was a strategic fulcrum for the Qin management of the south and holds the densest concentration of Qin-culture tombs in southern China. The 1975 Shuihudi discovery — more than 1,000 slips kept by an official named Xi, running to over 40,000 characters — transformed scholarly understanding of Qin law and daily life.

The new site adds physical evidence of north-south integration: the use of green clay sealing and coffin-plank doors, together with the co-occurrence of Qin and Chu cultural vessels, points to population migration and fusion. Experts framed the work as one that, "from the dimensions of objects, images and text, activated the historical scene of a key node in China's unification process."

## What's Next

The precise number of bamboo slips has not been published, and transcription of the women's-text materials awaits scholarly analysis. Those findings — like the ting-level register before them — are likely to keep reshaping how historians understand grassroots governance and daily life at the birth of the Qin-Han empire.
