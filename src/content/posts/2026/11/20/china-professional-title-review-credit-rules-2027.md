# China Tightens Title Review With New Credit Rules

China's Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security (MOHRSS) has issued a decision amending the interim provisions governing the country's professional title review system, adding a credit-management framework and stronger supervision meant to curb fraud in the evaluation of professional and technical qualifications. The amended rules take effect on January 1, 2027, [Xinhua reported](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261119/1cf8c92bf6c74050b305f5ed11784635/c.html) on November 19.

The decision modifies 10 articles and adds five new ones to the Interim Provisions on the Management of Professional Title Review — China's first departmental rule dedicated to professional title work, which took effect in 2019. According to Xinhua, the changes focus on three areas: strengthening review supervision, reinforcing credit management, and improving review management and services.

## Why professional titles matter

Professional titles, known as *zhicheng*, are China's formal grading system for professional and technical workers — spanning levels from assistant to full senior. They are central to salary, promotion, and, in sectors such as construction, to enterprise qualification grading, bidding eligibility, and project capacity. Because a title can determine both an individual's career trajectory and a company's ability to win work, the system has long been a target for material fraud, including fabricated performance records, fake degrees, and ghostwritten papers.

The amendment's centerpiece is a credit-based integrity system. Under the new provisions, MOHRSS administrative departments will establish a credit management system for professional titles and, as needed, work with relevant departments to create rules for investigating and handling dishonest conduct. Review committee organizing units will be required to maintain integrity archives for title application and review, recording and aggregating information on dishonest conduct and reporting it upward to MOHRSS.

## Defining and punishing dishonesty

The rules spell out what counts as dishonest conduct for three groups: applicants, review experts, and review staff. For applicants, that includes false promises, falsified application or review materials, and obtaining titles through improper means. Experts and staff can be cited for taking improper benefits, violating confidentiality or recusal rules, or using their position to give improper convenience to individuals or intermediary agencies.

Applicants found to have committed dishonest conduct will be recorded in the integrity archive for a three-year period, have any improperly obtained titles revoked, and have their information included in the national credit information sharing platform. Experts and staff face three-year archive records and measures including criticism, cancellation of expert qualification, and notification to their employers, with criminal liability pursued where a crime is constituted.

The move builds on earlier groundwork. In 2024, MOHRSS issued interim measures for the supervision of professional title review, establishing an integrity archive system tied to the national credit information sharing platform and the national title review information query system, as the ministry [explained in a Q&A](https://www.12371.cn/2024/08/21/ARTI1724208208317703.shtml) published by 共产党员网. That framework already provided for records of up to three years and possible blacklisting for serious dishonest conduct.

The draft amendment was released for public comment in March 2026, with a feedback deadline of April 20, according to the [MOHRSS consultation notice](https://www.mohrss.gov.cn/SYrlzyhshbzb/zcfg/SYzhengqiuyijian/zq_fgs/202603/t20260319_568778.html). In its drafting explanation, the ministry said the change was needed because a small number of localities had seen irregularities in title review, and relevant departments and units lacked sufficient legal basis and means to handle them.

## Widening access while tightening quality

The amendment is not purely restrictive. It also clarifies that professional and technical talent in the non-public economy and freelancers enjoy equal protection of legitimate rights and equal obligations when applying for title review, and that application, review, publicity, and recommendation procedures may be handled by their employer or personnel agency. Xinhua described the measure as further opening up application channels for non-public-sector talent and freelancers while safeguarding their rights and promoting fairness.

The rules also tighten procedural standards. Review passes will require yes-votes from more than two-thirds of experts present and more than half of the committee's total membership. Organizing units must inform applicants in one go of all materials needing correction and the deadline, with failure to correct deemed abandonment. Experts and staff with conflicts of interest must apply for recusal. Retirees will be barred from applying, and public officials under disciplinary sanctions cannot apply during the sanction period.

Xinhua framed the overall intent as cementing the primary responsibility of review units, improving supervision and credit management, and fostering "a good environment of recognizing, cherishing, respecting and using talent." The ministry said it will guide localities and units in implementing the rules and continue to standardize title review work.

## What to watch

How forcefully the rules bite will depend on execution. The regulations now define dishonest conduct and its consequences clearly, but their effect hinges on the quality of the integrity archives, the technical interoperability of the national credit information sharing platform with provincial systems, and local enforcement capacity. Industry observers have described the change as ushering in a "credit veto" era for title applications, though the regulation's literal record period is three years rather than a lifetime ban.

It remains to be seen whether MOHRSS issues accompanying implementation guidance, how revoked titles and record periods will generate measurable enforcement data, and how disputes and appeals will be handled. For now, the direction is clear: China is tying professional advancement more tightly to a verifiable record of integrity.
