# Boebert, from a U-Haul, reckons with own and GOP losses

Sitting in a U-Haul van outside the Capitol with her congressional office and her "dungeon of an apartment" packed away, Rep. Lauren Boebert offered a candid post-mortem of a political collapse that took both her seat and her party's grip on Congress. In an interview with [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/lauren-boebert-republican-midterms-election-trump-de36ea30274756d7c4f90868011602ad) published Friday, the Colorado Republican said she thinks about the midterms "every day" and described the result as a verdict on more than any single figure.

"This could be a referendum on President Trump. It could be the referendum on Congress or the Republican Party," she said. Then, lowering her voice, she added, "I think it's all of it."

The interview, conducted via Zoom on Thursday and reported by national politics reporter Jesse Bedayn, marked one of Boebert's first extended reflections since losing her 4th Congressional District seat to Democrat Eileen Laubacher — a defeat confirmed about a week earlier, and one that capped a sharp reversal for a lawmaker who won the same district by double digits two years ago.

## A blue wave that reached deep

Boebert's loss was part of a sweeping Democratic takeover. Democrats won control of both chambers in the November midterms, a decisive repudiation of unified GOP rule that [The Associated Press reported](https://apnews.com/article/congress-democratic-blue-wave-midterms-house-senate-9c5c4c5283f58b1ae0786e2f410c21a0) delivered "a new era in Washington." Democrats flipped Senate seats in long-shot states including South Carolina, Texas and Kansas and were projected to hold at least 54 seats, while House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries is poised to become the first Black Speaker of the House when the new Congress convenes on Jan. 3, 2027.

A central driver, according to the AP and to Republicans themselves, was an eight-month-old war with Iran that pushed up gas prices and consumer costs. President Trump acknowledged the war's impact on the results even as he insisted he would "do it again," while Vice President JD Vance pointedly blamed "the president's war in Iran" for the wipeout.

## Sparing the president, indicting the party

Notably, Boebert declined to lay the blame squarely at Trump's feet, even while acknowledging his role in her defeat. "I'm not mad at the president," she said, "but the president did some things that hurt my voters."

Instead, she spread the blame across her party, citing high gas prices, internal GOP infighting and unfulfilled promises. "I think it's more all of us. It's all of the Republican Party," she said. "We had solutions and we didn't take action." The remarks echoed what she told colleagues in a House GOP meeting days earlier, when she said, "We collectively failed" and that "the American people let us know we have failed in this governing experience for this Congress."

Her careful framing — loyal to Trump but willing to name his costs — reflects the dilemma facing Republicans after the rout. According to [AP reporting on the GOP's response](https://apnews.com/article/republicans-trump-midterms-congress-9fc9b226f25e5c371db6021170b70d27), Republicans broadly acknowledged that voters had rejected them but showed little appetite for breaking with the president.

Boebert has at times split from Trump on issues where other supporters also broke, including the war in Iran and the administration's handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files. On her identity within the movement, she has grown less inclined toward labels. "When Jesus comes back and I'm raptured up, like all these labels are just going to fly off anyway," she said. "I respect President Trump, I still work with him, I want to continue to work with him. Not for him, with him."

## Fundraising, drama and the 'theater'

Boebert pointed to several factors in her defeat. She declined to fundraise aggressively against an opponent who vastly out-raised her, a choice she framed as a kind of fatalism. "I didn't raise millions of dollars and I kinda refused to," she said. "Colorado voters know me, and, you know, if they know me enough to get rid of me, then I guess that's my answer."

She also acknowledged the toll of persistent controversy, including a 2023 incident at a Denver theater where she was caught on camera vaping during a "Beetlejuice" performance and escorted out — a clip replayed in attack ads against her. She called the resurfacing unfair but conceded it may have fed voter fatigue. "I don't care if any of this is true or not, I'm tired of the drama," she said, describing the sentiment she believes some voters reached.

Asked whether she would do anything differently, she said no — except, she joked, "not gone to the theater."

## 'A launching pad,' not an 'L'

Boebert described the loss not as an ending but as a pivot. She said she does not view it as an "L" but as a "launching pad" for a future she did not specify. She suggested her next chapter may be financial. "Too many people come to Congress and get rich," she said. "I am not one of those, and I'm gonna go make some money now."

The image she left reporters with was the van itself, its camera vibrating as it pulled into motion. "The U-Haul is getting started," she said.

## What to watch

Boebert's departure removes one of the most visible MAGA voices from an incoming Congress that Democrats will control. Her trajectory — from 2020 insurgent to 2026 casualty — has become a case study in the movement's arc, and her refusal to fully break with Trump, even in defeat, signals that the party's post-midterm reckoning is likely to be one of tactics rather than allegiance. Whether her promised "launching pad" returns her to public life, and on what terms, remains an open question.
