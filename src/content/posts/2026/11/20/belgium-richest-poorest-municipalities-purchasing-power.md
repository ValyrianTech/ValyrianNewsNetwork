# Belgium's Richest and Poorest Towns: The Power Shift

The wealthiest municipalities in Belgium lie in the south of Luxembourg province, on the border with the Grand Duchy, while the poorest are concentrated in Brussels and Wallonia, according to new figures from the national statistics office. But the same data, [reported by RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/les-revenus-des-belges-sont-plus-bas-a-bruxelles-et-en-wallonie-mais-le-pouvoir-d-achat-y-a-plus-augmente-qu-en-flandre-11817906), reveals a counterintuitive trend: since 2020, the most modest municipalities have seen their incomes rise fastest, outpacing inflation. The result is that purchasing power has gained ground in Brussels and Wallonia while slipping slightly in Flanders.

## The geographic divide

The figures, [published by Statbel](https://statbel.fgov.be/fr/themes/datalab/revenu-disponible-administratif) on 19 November and covering income year 2024, show that Attert in Luxembourg province has Belgium's highest median income, at €45,790. It is followed by Messancy (€41,682), Saint-Léger (€38,830), Habay (€38,213) and Etalle (€38,115). Six of the ten richest municipalities sit in Luxembourg province near the Grand Duchy, with the other four in Flemish Brabant around the Brussels periphery: Kraainem, Tervuren, Oud-Heverlee and Wezembeek-Oppem.

At the other end of the scale, Saint-Josse-ten-Noode in Brussels again records Belgium's lowest median income, at €21,891. It is followed by Molenbeek-Saint-Jean (€22,220), Anderlecht (€22,931), Koekelberg (€23,815) and Farciennes (€24,248). The ten poorest municipalities are all in Brussels or Wallonia, and the Brussels Region as a whole has a lower average median income than any province.

At provincial level, Flemish Brabant is the wealthiest, ahead of Walloon Brabant and Luxembourg. RTBF notes that Luxembourg province tops neither ranking because large internal disparities pull its average down: wealthier border communes such as Arlon coexist with more modest ones like Bouillon and Florenville. Hainaut and Liège are the poorest provinces.

## Where incomes rose fastest

Across the country, median disposable income rose 21.2% between 2020 and 2024. But the distribution of that growth is striking: the poorer a municipality was in 2020, the faster its median income rose. Every one of the ten poorest municipalities beat the national average, led by Farciennes at +26.3% — the largest increase in the country. Saint-Josse and Schaerbeek both posted +25.8%, Molenbeek +24.6%, Brussels-Ville +24.3% and Charleroi +23.5%.

By contrast, the wealthiest 20% of municipalities saw incomes rise just 19.6%, and six of the ten richest municipalities grew more slowly than the national average. By region, incomes rose 23.4% in Brussels, 22.1% in Wallonia and 20.3% in Flanders.

RTBF attributes the trend to two main hypotheses: a low 2020 base caused by Covid temporary unemployment, which hit lower-paid jobs hardest, and increases to the minimum wage, social minima, small pensions and benefits under the "Vivaldi" coalition government between late 2020 and 2024, which disproportionately benefited low-income populations.

## The purchasing power twist

Because income rose 21.2% while [inflation reached 20.5%](https://statbel.fgov.be/fr/themes/prix-la-consommation/indice-des-prix-la-consommation) over the same four years, the national median real gain was barely 0.6%. Yet that average masks sharp regional differences. Only 58% of municipalities (328) saw incomes outpace inflation; the other 42% (236) lost purchasing power.

The losses were concentrated in Flanders. A majority of Flemish municipalities — 177 of 284 — saw purchasing power decline, compared with just one of 19 in Brussels and 58 of 261 in Wallonia. In real terms, median income rose 2.4% in Brussels and 1.4% in Wallonia but fell 0.2% in Flanders. The worst-hit municipality was the wealthy Flemish town of Sint-Martens-Latem, down 5.3%, followed by Horebeke (–3.9%) and Linkebeek (–3.5%).

## A caveat on the numbers

While the percentage gains look like convergence, RTBF cautions that in absolute euros the gap widened: the richest 20% of municipalities gained about €5,750 over four years, against €5,180 for the poorest 20%. Relative convergence, in other words, coexists with absolute divergence.

The poverty-risk data tells a similar geographic story. The highest risk is found in Brussels — Saint-Josse-ten-Noode at 32.8%, Molenbeek at 30.6% and Anderlecht at 28.0% — while the lowest is in Flanders, led by Zemst at just 2.8%. Fifteen municipalities exceed a 20% poverty risk, all in Brussels (eight), Liège province (four) and Hainaut (three).

Statbel stresses that these municipal figures are built from administrative data and do not replace the official national, regional and provincial poverty statistics based on the EU-wide SILC survey. The methodology is still evolving, and the historical series has been recalculated, meaning year-on-year comparisons should be treated with caution.

## What to watch

The findings land amid federal budget negotiations under the "Arizona" coalition, giving them immediate political salience. The evidence that minimum-wage and benefit increases under the previous government lifted the poorest municipalities fastest is a live argument in debates over indexation and welfare spending. Whether the Arizona government's measures preserve or erode that trend will shape the next round of figures — and with them, the real purchasing-power story beneath Belgium's regional divide.
