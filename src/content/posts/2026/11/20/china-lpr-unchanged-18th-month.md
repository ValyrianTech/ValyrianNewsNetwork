# China Holds LPR at 3.0% and 3.5% for 18th Straight Month

China left its benchmark Loan Prime Rate unchanged on Friday, holding the one-year LPR at 3.0% and the five-year-plus LPR at 3.5%, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/20261120/3be19c9469fb4992beda43b8bf6462d2/c.html). The monthly fixing, announced by the People's Bank of China via the National Interbank Funding Center, marks the 18th consecutive month without a change, [Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/china/interest-rate/news/602908) reported, leaving both rates at record lows since May 2025.

The decision matched market expectations and signals that Beijing intends to keep monetary policy steady for now, weighing support for a softening economy against constraints that limit the benefit of further rate cuts.

## What the Fixing Shows

The one-year LPR is the main reference for most corporate and household borrowing, while the five-year-plus rate anchors mortgage pricing. Neither moved this month.

Lending costs in China are already low. Xinhua reported that in October the weighted average rate on new corporate loans was about 2.9%, roughly 0.2 percentage points below a year earlier, while the rate on new personal housing loans stood at about 3.1%, broadly flat year-on-year. Those levels reflect the substantial transmission of earlier easing into the real economy and reduce the marginal case for another cut.

## Why the Central Bank Held Steady

The unchanged setting came despite signs of moderating momentum. According to Trading Economics, industrial output and retail sales growth eased in October and home prices continued to decline. Those trends would ordinarily argue for more stimulus.

Countervailing forces appear to have outweighed them. Stronger-than-expected third-quarter growth and a firmer yuan may have reduced the urgency for additional easing, Trading Economics noted. The economy expanded 4.5% in the third quarter, beating expectations, and a stronger currency has eased some of the pressure that often accompanies looser policy.

In its third-quarter monetary policy report, the PBOC reiterated its commitment to an appropriately loose stance, stressing flexible and timely adjustments to policy tools while maintaining favorable financial conditions for stable growth. That language suggests policymakers remain ready to ease if needed but are not rushing into further cuts.

## The Margins Constraint

A less visible factor also bears on the decision. China's commercial banks continue to face pressure on net interest margins, the gap between what they earn on loans and pay on deposits. With lending rates already near record lows, further aggressive cuts would squeeze that spread further, limiting the appetite for rapid easing. That structural constraint helps explain the patient pace of the current policy cycle.

## What to Watch

The unchanged fixing offers little fresh stimulus to a property sector that depends on the five-year LPR for mortgage pricing, and it confirms that no near-term rate-cut window has opened. Attention now turns to whether the PBOC acts through other tools before the end of the year. Some analysts expect a reserve requirement ratio cut could precede any LPR reduction, and the scope for a near-term move in the benchmark appears modest at just a few basis points.

The next monthly fixing in December will be watched for any break in the 18-month hold, along with external shocks, currency moves and global rate decisions that could pull forward the timing of any change.
