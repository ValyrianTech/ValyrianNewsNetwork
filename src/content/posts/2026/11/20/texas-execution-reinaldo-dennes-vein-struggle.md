# Texas Execution Took Over an Hour to Find a Vein

Texas executed Reinaldo Dennes on Thursday evening in a lethal injection that stretched to 63 minutes after prison staff repeatedly failed to find a usable vein, and once the drugs began flowing the 70-year-old moaned about pain and asked God to end his suffering, according to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/execution-florida-texas-68c0916f95f514c293fd557a47406787). The procedure at the Huntsville Unit renewed scrutiny of lethal injection practices in the nation's most active death-penalty state and came just weeks after a failed execution in Tennessee intensified a national debate over capital punishment.

Dennes was strapped to the gurney at 6:04 p.m. and pronounced dead at 7:08 p.m., a minute after the procedure was recorded as complete. The two IV needles carrying the sedative pentobarbital eventually went into his left foot and the left side of his neck after a series of unsuccessful attempts.

## A Drawn-Out Procedure

The delays were evident to witnesses even before they entered the death chamber. Michael Graczyk, an AP reporter who has covered Texas executions since 1983 and witnessed nearly 500 of them, wrote that witnesses waited in a reception office well past the scheduled time, a sign that the execution team was either encountering resistance or struggling with vein access. "Dennes was like no other," Graczyk wrote in a [first-person account](https://apnews.com/article/execution-problems-texas-dennes-97fa4efb70b3a0b0a47935cd79a5a6af).

When the curtain rose, Dennes appeared docile, showing no signs of a struggle. He delivered a brief final statement, telling the warden he was "extremely blessed" and had spent three decades knowing "the love of the father," then said, "Go, warden!" Almost immediately, his demeanor changed.

"The pain! Go! Go!" he exclaimed, according to the AP. For nearly three minutes he moaned and groaned, at times interjecting obscenities, imploring "the father" to "take this pain" and repeatedly saying, "Let me go, father." He shouted that his right leg was "killing me" and likened the sensation to a cramp, then said, "I don't think this is normal. Take me, father. Don't let me suffer like this. C'mon."

After several loud groans, Dennes began snoring sounds that grew quieter before his breathing slowed and stopped.

## A Dispute Over Suffering

Texas Department of Criminal Justice spokesperson Amanda Hernandez said all procedures were followed appropriately and that the alternate injection sites — which she said were used in accordance with state protocol — had been employed in past executions. She could not say how many attempts to find a vein had failed.

Hernandez also suggested that Dennes may have been exaggerating or faking his pain, noting that he began complaining before the drugs began flowing and described pain in areas where no IV had been placed. Asked directly whether he was faking, she replied, "I think, yeah, there were allegations. ... There was nothing abnormal on our part," according to the AP.

Rev. Jeff Hood, Dennes' spiritual advisor, offered a starkly different account. "The first thing he said was, 'My leg, my leg, my leg. It burns, it burns, it burns,'" Hood recalled, [ABC13 Houston reported](https://abc13.com/post/reinaldo-dennes-execution-man-murdered-houston-jeweler-executed-complained-pain-staff-struggled-find-vein/20059079/). "Then he proceeds to say, 'It's not working.'"

Harris County District Attorney Sean Teare, who attended the execution, told reporters he also heard Dennes complain of pain but described the span from drug flow to death as taking about 10 minutes, ABC13 reported — a discrepancy that reflects the gap between the total time on the gurney and the period after the drugs took effect.

## The 1996 Killing

Dennes, a former jewelry designer, was convicted of capital murder in the death of Janos Szucs, a Houston diamond dealer found shot multiple times in his office in January 1996. According to court records cited by the AP, a cleaning woman testified that Dennes arranged for her to open the building's back door after hours so that he and his brother could enter the night of the shooting, and that she distracted security guard David Copeland at Dennes' request. Copeland, shot twice, survived and identified Dennes as his assailant; he died about five years ago.

Szucs' safe was found empty, with no sign of some $200,000 in cash or the diamonds reportedly kept there — gems valued at roughly $3 million at the time, or about $7.2 million today, that were never recovered, [CNN reported](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/19/us/texas-execution-reinaldo-dennes). Dennes spent 29 years on death row. His brother, Jose Albert Dennes, pleaded guilty to murder a year after Reinaldo's conviction, received a 45-year sentence and was paroled in 2019.

Witness Janice Copeland, whose husband was the guard shot during the robbery, said the execution brought a measure of closure. "David always felt Reinaldo would outlive him. Unfortunately he was right," she said. "Finally after 30 years, justice has been served."

## Broader Scrutiny

Dennes' death came on a day when the U.S. carried out two executions of men in their 70s. Earlier Thursday, Florida executed Thomas Mitchell Overton, 71, its record 20th execution of the year. Florida has now carried out more than half of the nation's 39 executions in 2026 under Gov. Ron DeSantis, who leaves office in January. The pairing cast a renewed spotlight on the aging of the U.S. death row population, which can complicate vein access.

The execution also followed renewed attention to lethal injection after Tennessee's failed September execution of Christa Pike. Pike survived two doses of pentobarbital that her lawyers say likely did not enter her bloodstream correctly and instead pooled in her arms, leaving them burned and blistered. Medical experts later told [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/oct/24/christa-pike-execution-medical-experts) that the Tennessee team's handling would have been considered malpractice in any clinical setting, and Tennessee has paused executions pending a review.

For Texas, the episode raises familiar questions. The state set its single-year execution record in 2000 with 40, and vein-access problems have surfaced before, though the need for alternate injection sites remains relatively rare. Hernandez maintained that nothing out of the ordinary occurred, but as Graczyk noted, there is no way to know for certain whether Dennes' cries reflected genuine pain. What the record does show is a 63-minute procedure that, by the account of seasoned observers, looked like no other Texas execution in decades.

## What's Next

Texas has not announced changes to its lethal injection protocol in response to the Dennes case. Florida, meanwhile, plans two more executions in December. With national attention already fixed on execution methods after the Pike case, the Dennes execution is likely to add pressure on states to reexamine how they carry out death sentences — and how they respond when the process goes visibly wrong.
