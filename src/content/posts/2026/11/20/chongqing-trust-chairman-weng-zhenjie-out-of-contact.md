# Chongqing Trust Chairman Weng Zhenjie Out of Contact

Weng Zhenjie, the 64-year-old chairman of Chongqing International Trust—one of China's largest trust companies—has reportedly become unreachable, a development that raises fresh questions about governance and risk at the firm and about the widening fallout from the investigation of a former Chongqing mayor.

The situation surfaced on the evening of November 17, 2026, when the listed Chongqing Road & Bridge Co. (600106.SH) disclosed that it had received notice from its major shareholder, Chongqing Trust, that Weng "for personal reasons cannot temporarily perform normally" his duties as chairman and related board committee functions. The board named director Ma Yun to exercise his responsibilities in the interim, [Caixin](https://finance.caixin.com/2026-11-20/102506902.html) reported.

Chinese financial media have described Weng as being in a state of "失联"—out of contact—but no official disciplinary or judicial announcement has confirmed that he has been detained, formally investigated, or charged. The precise cause of his disappearance remains officially unexplained.

## What Is Known

According to the corporate filing cited by Caixin, the disclosure was terse and carefully worded, attributing Weng's absence to "personal reasons" without elaboration. Weng's most recent confirmed public appearance was on October 29, 2026, when he attended and personally chaired the fifth meeting of the ninth board of Chongqing Road & Bridge, reviewing periodic reports and external investment matters.

The timing—and the man—make the development significant. Weng has spent more than two decades at the center of Chongqing's financial establishment, and Caixin's in-depth report describes him as a "capable man" of "pivotal weight" in the city's financial circles who was "backed by former mayor Huang Qifan."

## The Huang Qifan Connection

Weng's disappearance comes just over a month after Huang Qifan, the former Chongqing mayor and a once-prominent financial technocrat, was publicly announced to be under investigation on October 13, 2026, roughly eight years after his retirement, [Caixin](https://china.caixin.com/2026-10-13/102492972.html) reported. Caixin's headline summary on the Weng story states that after Huang's October 13 announcement, "Weng Zhenjie may be under investigation or assisting investigation because of this."

Huang, now 74, was long known for his financial engineering and for restructuring Chongqing's state-owned financial institutions. Weng is widely described as an executor of several of those restructurings. This is not the first time Weng has faced scrutiny: years earlier, he was reportedly taken away for investigation in connection with the "Zhuhai Guoli case" but was released and returned to his normal work in Chongqing, [Caixin](https://wenews.caixin.com/2026-11-19/102506818.html) noted.

The Weng development fits a broader pattern. Since early 2026, a string of Chongqing financial figures associated with the city's state-financial restructuring model have been placed under investigation, culminating in Huang's own case. Whether more names follow—and whether the current probe reaches into Chongqing Trust's operations—remains unresolved.

## A Career Built on Turnarounds

Weng arrived at Chongqing Trust in 2001, when the firm was near bankruptcy, and led an 18-year turnaround that became a showcase of Chinese financial restructuring. By the end of 2019, according to a state-media profile, the firm's registered capital had reached 15 billion yuan—ranked first among China's 68 trust companies by capital strength—while trust assets totaled 212.5 billion yuan, roughly 490 times the level at the 2002 restructuring.

His reach extended across four financial sectors. He held chairmanships at Chongqing Trust; at the brokerages Southwest Securities and Guodu Securities; at Three Gorges Bank, created in 2008 from the restructuring of a near-insolvent Wanzhou Commercial Bank in just 106 days; and at Yimin Fund. He also served as a delegate to the Chongqing People's Congress and on the standing committee of the local chapter of the China Democratic National Construction Association.

In December 2025, Weng was named chairman of the listed Chongqing Road & Bridge—the very entity through which his disappearance became public.

## Governance Questions Already on Record

Even before any formal finding, there were documented governance concerns in Weng's orbit. In April 2023, the Beijing bureau of the China Securities Regulatory Commission imposed penalties on Guodu Securities and its executives, restricting several business lines for six months. Weng, as chairman, and the former board secretary were both summoned for "regulatory talks," and the regulator named Weng the "first responsible person" for the company's equity-management affairs, financial outlet Cailianshe reported at the time.

Separately, in January 2023, six Guodu shareholders—including Chongqing Trust—had been fined for concealing concerted-action relationships and illegally transferring voting rights through share-pledge agreements. Guodu's regulatory classification was cut four notches in 2021.

## Industry Under Pressure

Weng's disappearance lands during a broad regulatory tightening of China's trust sector. A new, 75-article trust-industry regulation took effect on January 1, 2026, rebuilding the supervisory framework around the "trustee" role and tightening rules on channel business and capital pools. Industry-wide capital trust assets stood at 22.25 trillion yuan as of the end of 2024, according to China Trustee Association data.

For Chongqing Trust specifically, the immediate concern is operational continuity. As recently as March 2025, Weng told the firm's annual work conference that risky projects—including those tied to developers Shimao and Longxin—had been "thoroughly resolved" and that the risk base was "clear and controllable." Whether that picture holds with its top leader incapacitated and an acting chairman in place is now an open question.

## What to Watch

Three things will determine how this story develops. First, whether Beijing issues any formal disciplinary or judicial confirmation regarding Weng, which would clarify whether he is a subject of investigation or a witness assisting it. Second, whether the probe extends into Chongqing Trust's own operations and risk exposures, given the firm's systemic size. Third, how investors in the listed Chongqing Road & Bridge weigh management continuity and any contagion from a probe into its controlling shareholder.

For now, the official record offers only a terse disclosure and a carefully worded absence. The larger story—of how far the investigation of Huang Qifan will reach into the financial empire he helped build—is still being written.
