# Senate Passes Energy Bill to Power AI Data Centers

The U.S. Senate on Thursday approved sweeping bipartisan legislation to speed up federal permitting for power plants, transmission lines and pipelines, and to force energy-hungry data centers to pay for the grid upgrades they trigger. The 62-33 vote sends the bill to the House, where a narrower version awaits reconciliation. According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/energy-permitting-data-center-power-grid-trump-b41c2017e4a9fe2e6de92ec77a4cdd0a), the measure is the first major action by Congress since the midterm elections and a top priority for the Trump administration.

Formally known as the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA), the roughly 494-page package aims to shorten environmental reviews that can stretch five years or more. It would rewrite bedrock laws — including the National Environmental Policy Act, the Clean Water Act and the Endangered Species Act — by setting statutory deadlines for permitting decisions and narrowing the grounds for legal challenges.

## Why the Grid Is Under Pressure

The bill is a direct response to surging U.S. electricity demand driven by the buildout of artificial intelligence infrastructure. Data centers require enormous volumes of power, and anger over rising electricity bills and the facilities themselves helped usher in a wave of Democrats during the midterms who pledged to clamp down on the industry.

Sponsors cast the legislation as a way to cut costs for ordinary households. "We are going to make it easier to build in America again, and make life more affordable for American families," said Sen. Mike Lee of Utah, one of the bill's four main champions, as AP reported. Sen. John Barrasso of Wyoming, the Senate's No. 2 Republican, argued that energy projects "remain frozen for years" awaiting federal approval. "Construction cannot start without first spending years in the regulatory penalty box," he said.

The bill was negotiated by the leaders of the Senate's energy and environment panels: Republicans Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and Lee, alongside Democrats Martin Heinrich of New Mexico and Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island.

## A Shift in Who Pays

Beyond streamlining reviews, the legislation targets the cost-shifting that has fueled public frustration. As the [Bipartisan Policy Center](https://bipartisanpolicy.org/explainer/explaining-the-senate-permitting-bill-the-bipartisan-american-affordability-and-jobs-act/) explains, the bill would rewrite the procedural core of NEPA — narrowing the range of alternatives agencies must study, compressing litigation into a 150-day window after a permit is issued, and making remand without vacatur the only remedy for a NEPA violation. The practical effect, supporters say, is that new generation and transmission reach the grid sooner and at lower cost.

The data-center provisions are central to that promise. The bill would require large electricity users to bear the incremental cost of the generation, transmission and distribution capacity built to serve them, rather than spreading those costs across other ratepayers. Whitehouse framed the change bluntly: "The day of data center freeloading ends when this bill passes."

## A Bitter Split Among Democrats

Fifteen Democrats joined most Republicans in supporting the bill, including Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer. Two Republicans — Josh Hawley of Missouri and Mike Rounds of South Dakota — voted no.

The measure exposed a sharp rift within the Democratic caucus. Whitehouse, a longtime climate advocate, called it "the floor, not the ceiling, of our climate and environmental aspirations," framing it as a foundation for stronger legislation. But critics argued the first beneficiaries would be fossil fuel projects, particularly gas plants built to power data centers.

"Getting more renewable electrons where they are needed doesn't tackle climate change if you simultaneously promote the burning of more fossil fuels," said Sen. Jeff Merkley of Oregon, who pushed unsuccessfully for amendments, including a ban on data centers on public lands. "Unfortunately, this bill does exactly that." Sen. Maria Cantwell of Washington warned that the bill would weaken protections for water, land, wildlife and tribal sovereignty, urging colleagues to "slow this process down."

Environmental groups echoed those concerns. Jen Duggan, executive director of the Environmental Integrity Project, called the measure "a gift to some of the biggest polluters in America" that would "pave the way for even more fossil fuel projects like gas power plants for data centers." Tribal leaders, including Oneida Nation Vice Chairman Jameson Wilson, said the bill threatens the ability to protect sacred spaces.

## What's Next

The bill now heads to the House, which passed a more limited, industry-friendly permitting version built around the SPEED Act last year. The two chambers must reconcile their differences into identical text before the measure can reach President Donald Trump, who has urged its passage and called it a "BIG WIN for U.S. ENERGY INDEPENDENCE and DOMINANCE."

Sponsors hope to enact the law before the current Congress ends in January 2027. Even if it clears that hurdle, the bill's judicial-review changes and tribal-consultation provisions are likely to face legal challenges. And with Democrats set to hold stronger majorities in the next Congress, the deeper question — whether faster permitting lowers consumer bills or mainly accelerates a fossil-fuel buildout for AI — will remain a central political test.
