# UPS Workers Block Subcontractors, 45,000 Parcels Held

UPS employees in Belgium are blocking the courier company's subcontractors around Diegem, on the outskirts of Brussels, holding back roughly 45,000 parcels in the latest escalation of a bitter labour dispute over mass layoffs. According to union secretary Hans Elsen of the Christian trade union ACV Puls, the morning shift arrived to find no work at all because UPS had rerouted its entire volume to subcontractors — the same volume it says it needs to shed permanent staff to handle.

The action, reported by [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/20/werknemers-ups-blokkeren-onderaannemers-45-000-pakjes-tegengeh/), involved between 250 and 300 workers and threatens delivery delays just as the Black Friday shopping period gets under way.

## A dispute over more than jobs

At the heart of the conflict is UPS's plan to cut up to 525 of its roughly 800 jobs in Belgium, announced at a works council in June. Most staff work at the Diegem site; the company also runs a distribution centre in Lummen, in Limburg. As VRT NWS [reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/06/23/ups-wil-tot-525-banen-schrappen/) at the time, Elsen warned the cuts would hit "from drivers to parcel sorters and the employees who handle customs clearance," and questioned the rationale, noting that processed parcel volumes had fallen only "a little" — "not on that scale" to justify such a sweeping restructuring. "Our suspicion is that they want to replace permanent contracts with cheaper freelancers," he said.

The unions now argue that UPS intends to absorb the job losses through what they call "cheap fake self-employment" (schijnzelfstandigheid), shifting work to subcontractors and freelancers rather than keeping it in-house. By blockading subcontractors rather than UPS's own sites, the workers are targeting precisely the practice they say is hollowing out permanent employment.

## From Herstal to Diegem

The current wave of action began on Wednesday, when around 200 to 300 UPS workers blockaded the European logistics centre of shoe brand Skechers in Herstal, near Liège — one of UPS's largest customers — stopping lorries from entering or leaving. Per [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/18/werknemers-ups-blokkeren-depot-skechers-in-luik-en-bedreigen-bla/), the unions warned that "anyone counting on UPS for their Black Friday orders risks not receiving their parcels," and threatened to block home deliveries by subcontractors as well.

What turned frustration into fresh blockades was the company's response. On Thursday the unions learned that Belgian management would not return to the negotiating table until the following Tuesday — after the announced actions. Elsen said the delay hardened the workers' resolve, and further blockades of subcontractors are already scheduled for Saturday, with more expected on Monday.

"We follow the volumes," Elsen said, describing a strategy that moves pickets to wherever UPS reroutes its parcels. Outside the wider Diegem region, he added, parcels were still able to leave via subcontractors.

## Pressure timed to peak season

The timing is deliberate. By striking in the run-up to Black Friday, when parcel volumes spike, the unions are maximising the economic cost of the standoff — and targeting a marquee customer in Skechers to sharpen the message. UPS, for its part, has framed the restructuring as a necessary adjustment to a changing market, saying it is "evaluating how best to organise our Belgian activities" and "examining the various options" for its operations in the country.

The Belgian cuts form part of a wider retrenchment: in April 2025 the company announced it would eliminate 20,000 jobs worldwide. That context cuts both ways. UPS can point to broader market pressures, while the unions argue that a plan to shed more than half of its Belgian workforce is out of proportion to any decline in demand.

## What to watch

Several questions remain open. It is still unclear whether the Lummen distribution centre will be affected and precisely what a social plan will contain. Much now hinges on Tuesday's talks: if negotiations resume constructively, the blockades may ease; if not, the unions have signalled they will widen the campaign to more customer sites and to home deliveries.

For customers, the immediate risk is concrete — parcels delayed, and Black Friday orders that may not arrive on time. For UPS, the dispute has become a test of how it restructures: through negotiated social dialogue and direct employment, or through the subcontracting arrangements its own workforce is now blockading.
