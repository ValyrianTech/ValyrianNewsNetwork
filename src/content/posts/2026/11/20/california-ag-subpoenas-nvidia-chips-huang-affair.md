# California AG Subpoenas Nvidia Over Chips and Huang Affair

California Attorney General Rob Bonta has served an investigative subpoena on chipmaker Nvidia, probing whether the company's advanced chips or confidential information may have reached China and whether it misled investors about the risks tied to former CEO Jensen Huang's affair with a woman U.S. intelligence suspects was a Chinese spy, [the California Department of Justice announced](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-serves-investigative-subpoena-nvidia) on Thursday, November 19, 2026.

The action adds a powerful state-level securities enforcement front to a scandal that has already cost Huang his job, wiped tens of billions of dollars off Nvidia's market value, and triggered federal, congressional, and internal corporate investigations.

## A Two-Pronged Inquiry

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/nvidia-subpoena-california-bonta-china-94c179f5aed88c49b7f77670eae40013), the subpoena focuses on two intertwined questions: whether Nvidia's advanced semiconductors or confidential information may have gone to China, and whether the company misled investors and the public about that risk. The information gathered, the attorney general's office said, will help it evaluate whether Nvidia's actions violated California law.

Bonta framed the matter squarely as an investor-protection issue under state corporate securities law rather than a purely national-security question.

"The conduct of Nvidia and its former CEO raises bright red flags, including about the ways in which the company may have misled investors," Bonta said in a statement. "My office is investigating whether Nvidia or Jensen Huang's actions resulted in China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information — and if Nvidia misled investors and the public about this risk, in violation of the law."

He added: "Nvidia is in a messy, transnational situation but the underlying principle of our corporate securities law is clear: Companies cannot mislead their investors."

The subpoena is investigative — a tool to determine whether a lawsuit is warranted — and Nvidia did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

## How the Scandal Unfolded

The subpoena caps a turbulent few weeks for the world's most valuable chipmaker. The New York Times reported in late October that U.S. intelligence had determined Alice Huang — a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed International, who is not related to Jensen Huang — was gathering information for Beijing. Jensen Huang acknowledged the affair and apologized, then resigned as CEO on November 12, effective immediately, after further reports of additional affairs with company employees. Colette Kress, the company's longtime chief financial officer, was named interim CEO, and the board appointed its first-ever chairman, Harvey C. Jones.

The affair has entangled Nvidia's chip business directly. Megaspeed bought more than $4.8 billion worth of Nvidia chips over two years and has faced allegations of diverting restricted chips into China, which the company denies. Malaysia is investigating whether chips brought in by Megaspeed or an affiliate were re-exported.

China's Foreign Ministry has rejected the espionage characterization. "The so-called Chinese spy allegation is unsubstantiated. It is nothing but smears against China," spokesperson Guo Jiakun said.

## A Growing Stack of Probes

The California subpoena layers onto an expanding set of overlapping inquiries. Nvidia's board has retained the law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz, with former acting U.S. Attorney Joon H. Kim leading an internal investigation into whether Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The FBI is examining the affair, and the SEC has reportedly launched a civil probe into whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors. A retail investor, Mark Kistenmacher, filed a putative securities class action in a California court.

Congress is moving on a parallel track. Key House lawmakers said Huang has agreed to appear for transcribed interviews on December 1, followed by Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter on December 2. As [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/20/california-ag-subpoenas-nvidia-over-worries-about-huangs-ties-with-china/) noted, Democratic lawmakers have also opened an inquiry into the Trump administration's management of Nvidia's chip sales to China.

## Market and Industry Fallout

The scandal has shaken investors. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/19/nvidia-california-bonta-subpoena-china-chips.html) reported that Nvidia shares were down 22% in the nine trading days since Huang admitted the affair, shedding 38% of their value over that stretch and on pace for a seventh straight weekly loss.

The broader worry is what the episode means for the artificial intelligence boom that Nvidia's chips underpin. Stephanie Balaouras, a Forrester group research director, framed Huang's exit as a stress test: "If Nvidia stabilizes quickly under new leadership, the AI infrastructure boom will have demonstrated resilience. If its stock, financing relationships, or ecosystem commitments remain under sustained pressure, that would add evidence to the fragile side of the AI bubble ledger."

Analysts have also warned that the high-profile espionage allegations could strengthen congressional support for tighter semiconductor export controls on China, potentially renewing broader U.S.–China trade strain.

## What to Watch

Several questions remain open. No public evidence has yet emerged that Nvidia chips or secrets were actually compromised, and Nvidia's board has said it has no reason to believe they were. Whether California's securities law reaches the alleged conduct — and how the state subpoena interacts with the federal SEC probe and the private class action — is also unclear.

The immediate test comes in early December, when Huang and Teter testify before Congress. How much they disclose under oath, and whether Nvidia can install a permanent CEO to steady the company, will shape whether the scandal remains a corporate-governance crisis or escalates into a turning point for U.S. chip policy toward China.
