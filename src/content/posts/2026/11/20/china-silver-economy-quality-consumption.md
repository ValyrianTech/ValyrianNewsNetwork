# China Pivots Silver Economy Toward Quality Demand

China is repositioning its fast-growing "silver economy" — the market serving its aging population — as a fresh engine of domestic demand, with policymakers now framing elderly consumption around quality and experience rather than basic survival. The shift was underscored at the National Aging Work Conference in Beijing on 18–19 November 2026, where President Xi Jinping issued important instructions and Premier Li Qiang delivered a speech, according to [Xinhua via gov.cn](https://www.gov.cn/yaowen/liebiao/202611/content_7086661.htm).

The demographic stakes are enormous. By the end of 2025, China had 323 million people aged 60 and above — 23.0% of the population — and 224 million aged 65 and above, or 15.9% of the total, figures cited in a [commentary in The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34517959) published on 20 November.

## A Policy Signal From the Top

Xi's instructions called for deep implementation of the national strategy to actively respond to population aging, urging officials to "vigorously develop the silver economy, foster a good environment of filial piety and respect for the elderly, and build an elderly-friendly society." Li Qiang's speech sharpened the economic emphasis, directing that the silver economy be developed "around the quality-oriented consumption needs of the elderly."

The framing matters. The silver economy is being repositioned from a purely livelihood and welfare topic into what analysts describe as a core engine of domestic demand and a key breakthrough point in China's economic structural transformation. The Paper's commentary captured the dual logic: the sector "connects on one end to the elderly's growing need for a better life, and on the other to an expanding consumer market."

## From Survival to Quality

The most consequential shift is in consumer behavior itself. China's elderly consumer base is transitioning from "survival-type" spending on basic necessities toward "quality-type" consumption — paying for comfort, health, and personal interests.

A China Aging Association survey of people aged 50 and above found that functional foods and clothing, tourism and holidays, and health and wellness each sit in the first tier of demand at around 70%. Notably, 58.3% of respondents agreed with "securing one's own health and eldercare spending first," while only 9.0% endorsed "reducing non-essential consumption" — a signal that older Chinese are prioritizing their own well-being rather than cutting back.

The spending is already visible in the travel sector. According to the 2025 National Aging Cause Development Bulletin, China operated 2,485 tourist trains in 2025, and residential and leisure eldercare trips exceeded 80 million person-times. Such experiences pull together transport, accommodation, dining, medical care, and cultural activities into a longer industrial chain.

## Supply Struggles to Keep Pace

Yet the market faces a persistent supply-demand mismatch. As The Paper's commentary put it, "on one side is the elderly's growing demand for high-quality eldercare products; on the other is 'involution' of low-end supply and insufficient high-quality supply."

Many products marketed to seniors are ordinary goods with an "elderly version" label slapped on, lacking design tailored to older bodies and daily routines. Some smart devices pack in features that overwhelm rather than help, erecting digital barriers. In wellness travel and senior training, uneven service quality and misleading advertising have eroded consumer confidence.

Policymakers have begun addressing these gaps. In January 2024, the General Office of the State Council issued China's first specialized silver economy document, the "Opinions on Developing the Silver Economy to Improve the Wellbeing of the Elderly," deploying measures across public welfare, product supply, potential industries, and factor guarantees. In 2025, the government rolled out consumption subsidies for moderately and severely disabled elderly and folded home aging-adaptation renovations into the home decoration "trade-in" program, driving sales of 7.21 million aging-adapted products.

A "silver store" in Shanghai illustrates the experiential retail model now emerging, offering hearing aids, rehabilitation aids, and smart devices in a hands-on setting — with average daily sales of about 30,000 yuan and single-day peaks near 50,000 yuan.

## The Economic Scale at Stake

Industry projections underline why Beijing is investing political capital in the sector. As of end-2024, the silver economy exceeded 7 trillion yuan, roughly 6% of GDP, and is projected to reach 30 trillion yuan by 2035 — about 10% of GDP — according to [Jiemian News](https://m.jiemian.com/article/12592979.html).

Experts point to aging-in-place services, community-based comprehensive service hubs, and AI-enabled chronic disease management as high-potential niches. China's eldercare pattern remains roughly "9073" — about 90% home-based, 7% community, and 3% institutional — though home-based service quality lags. Chen Dongsheng of Taikang has argued that healthy aging is becoming both a major challenge and a major livelihood priority, while Dang Junwu, former deputy director of the China Research Center on Aging, has stressed building comprehensive service scenarios within communities.

## The Multi-Tier Bet

The commentary's central policy argument is for a "bottom-line guarantee, universal reach, quality optional" supply structure: public policy covers basic needs, inclusive services broaden access, and the market meets quality-driven choices. State Councilor Shen Yiqin, who directs the National Aging Work Committee, tied the agenda to the upcoming 15th Five-Year Plan, pledging to tackle the pressing concerns of the elderly.

Getting the balance right is delicate. Basic services and care for the vulnerable require public backing, while culture, travel, and social participation can lean more on the market. For China, the silver economy is becoming a test of whether the country can turn demographic pressure into durable demand — and whether its supply of products and services can rise to the quality its elderly consumers increasingly expect.
