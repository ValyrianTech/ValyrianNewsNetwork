# Belgium's Justice Minister Now Under Prosecutors' Scrutiny

The criminal investigation into Belgium's failed i-Police programme has reached the top of the federal government. The Brussels general prosecutor's office is now examining the role of Justice Minister Annelies Verlinden in awarding and signing the i-Police contract with French tech multinational Sopra Steria, as [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/783052/article/2026-11-16/affaire-i-police-la-justice-enquete-sur-la-ministre-de-la-justice) and De Standaard reported on 16 November.

The move marks a turning point in a case centred on a police digitisation project that has already cost tens of millions of euros in vain and has become one of the most corrosive political controversies facing the "Arizona" coalition. At issue is whether Verlinden's earlier professional ties to Sopra Steria — she previously worked as a lawyer for the company — constituted a conflict of interest when, as Interior Minister under the previous Vivaldi government, she oversaw the procurement.

The minister denies any wrongdoing.

## How the case reached the minister

The investigation began as an ordinary criminal inquiry into the i-Police procurement. But the investigating judge concluded that certain elements of the case "could point toward the then-Interior Minister," the general prosecutor's office confirmed to Le Soir.

Under Belgian law, when a case concerns a minister acting in an official capacity, it is transferred — *dessaisissement* — from an investigating judge to the general prosecutor's office, which is empowered to investigate federal ministers. Press magistrate An Schoonjans described the shift as a routine application of statute: "In accordance with the procedure provided by law, the transfer of the investigating judge was requested and the file was then transmitted to the Brussels general prosecutor's office."

The prosecutor's office, led by Frédéric Van Leeuw, stressed that the step "does not prejudge the existence of any criminal responsibility." Verlinden's cabinet said the minister had herself asked to be heard in the investigation and to explain her conduct, arguing that "it is logical that the case continues at the level of the general prosecutor's office, since a minister is implicated."

## A project that consumed €139 million

The procedural escalation follows a damning financial reckoning. An audit by Belgium's Court of Auditors, published in late October, put total spending on i-Police at €139.4 million — including €74.4 million on the main Sopra Steria contract and several million more on preparation-phase consultancy and a related project extending an Antwerp police app nationwide.

The audit found that of €30.8 million spent on software licences, €21.6 million is now useless: €14.2 million became unusable after the programme was halted and €7.3 million was never used. It also flagged that in December 2021, purchase orders for software worth €20.7 million were issued and €17.1 million in invoices paid, contrary to the agreed phasing with Sopra Steria.

The programme, initially budgeted at €299 million, was awarded to Sopra Steria in 2021 under Verlinden. It was halted in late 2025 after an evaluation by her successor at the Interior Ministry, Bernard Quintin (MR), who concluded that more than €75 million had been spent almost entirely in vain — later likening the project to a "dead horse."

The federal police is now in legal conflict with Sopra Steria, claiming €244 million in damages and reimbursed invoices, according to commissioner-general Eric Snoeck. Sopra Steria is counter-suing.

## The conflict-of-interest question

The crux of the political storm is the two-layered conflict concern around Verlinden.

In April 2019, before entering government, Verlinden — then co-managing partner of DLA Piper's Belgian branch — represented Sopra Steria in a case before the EU General Court in Luxembourg. She became Interior Minister in 2020 and signed off on the i-Police contract the following year. A second concern: DLA Piper represents Sopra Steria in the current civil dispute, and Verlinden's partner is reported to hold a senior role at the firm.

Verlinden has rejected any suggestion of impropriety. "There is no link between the two files. I was able to judge in complete objectivity," she said earlier this year. "When I took office as minister, negotiations with the company had already been ongoing for years. All procedural rules were respected."

The underlying criminal instruction, opened by the Brussels prosecutor's office in late January, covers possible embezzlement of public funds and illegal taking of interests. A parallel sub-plot has seen Verlinden's spokesperson and a Dutch-speaking spokesperson for the Brussels prosecutor's office charged with violating professional secrecy over suspected leaks about the investigation.

## Political fallout

The affair has drawn sharp reactions across the political spectrum. Vlaams Belang's Ortwin Depoortere has demanded that Verlinden account for herself before the Chamber's Interior Affairs committee. Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) has defended her, pointing to what he cast as the "total hypocrisy" of former Vivaldi coalition partners now calling for her head.

The timing is awkward for the government. The i-Police revelations land amid a separate budget-negotiation crisis, adding to the instability of the Arizona coalition. Verlinden's dual trajectory — from the Interior Ministry that procured i-Police to the Justice Ministry whose prosecutorial apparatus now examines the case — sharpens the separation-of-powers questions at the heart of the story.

## What to watch

Several questions remain open. Chief among them: whether the general prosecutor's office will formally open a case against Verlinden or conclude that no responsibility attaches to her. The criminal instruction into embezzlement and illegal taking of interests is unresolved, as is the fate of the leak case involving her spokesperson.

Separately, the federal police's €244 million civil claim against Sopra Steria will grind on through the courts. And politically, the coming weeks will test whether the government can contain a scandal that has already migrated from the procurement office to the minister's own desk — and whether calls for accountability at the highest level of the Justice department translate into consequences.

For now, the presumption of innocence holds, and the prosecutors' careful language reflects it. But the political arithmetic is less forgiving.
