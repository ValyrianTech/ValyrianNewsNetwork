# Belgium's 'Gendarmerie 2.0' Force to Reach 1,200

Belgium's Interior Minister Bernard Quintin has laid out fresh detail on a controversial plan to build a full-scale federal intervention reserve of about 1,200 members, tasked above all with public-order policing. The proposal, developed jointly with Defence Minister Theo Francken, has been nicknamed "Rijkswacht 2.0" — Gendarmerie 2.0 — after the Belgian gendarmerie that was abolished in 2001, and it has reignited a debate about the militarisation of federal policing.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/20/quintin-wil-militair-getrainde-eenheid-in-federale-interventiere/), the new unit would be assembled from existing services and a new core of specially trained personnel, with public-order enforcement as its "priority mission."

## How the force would be built

At the heart of the plan is a merger of two existing units: roughly 400 members of the current Directorate of Public Security (DAS) and about 400 members of the Intervention Corps (CIK). To those would be added a new core of some 400 specially trained personnel — police officers with policing training plus reservist or military training — financed through the Defence budget.

At 1,200 personnel, the force would make up nearly 10% of the federal police's operational staff. The guiding principle, Quintin's office says, is that public-order enforcement would always take precedence over other support missions.

"Our ambition is to build again a real, specialised federal public-order enforcement force," Quintin said. "An intervention force of 1,200 people that we can deploy day and night, at any moment and on a large scale in the event of serious disruptions of public order. The riots on the margins of demonstrations have shown how necessary that is."

The plan also entails a sweeping territorial reorganisation. DAS and CIK are currently spread across more than 15 locations across the country; under the proposal they would be consolidated into five command posts, aligned with the five judicial areas of the courts of appeal: Brussels, Antwerp, Ghent, Liège and Mons.

The current fragmentation means it takes time to assemble units from different parts of the country, complicating a rapid, large-scale deployment. "With this new organisation we can deploy people on the ground much faster and en masse," Quintin said. "That way we can act firmly and effectively against those who want to disrupt our society."

## Roots of a contested idea

The concept traces back to mid-October, when Defence Minister Theo Francken first floated the idea of a "Rijkswacht 2.0" amid urban unrest in Brussels and Liège. "We want to build a reserve of people who are militarily trained," [Francken said at the time](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/18/defensie-en-binnenlandse-zaken-denken-aan-oprichting-rijkswacht/). "That Rijkswacht 2.0 can be deployed in bigger crises, riots or situations threatening to get out of hand."

He framed it as a proportional response to escalating street violence, and as a signal that reinforcements were coming. "If rioters attack paramedics, throw Molotov cocktails and set everything on fire, we must have a proportional response ready," he said, while conceding that the plan "will of course not be the miracle solution."

Quintin, however, has been careful to insist the project is not a return of the gendarmerie. He acknowledges that the "Rijkswacht 2.0" label carries a negative connotation in parts of the police. The gendarmerie was a national police service organised along military lines until 2001, when it was merged into the current integrated federal-local police model after repeated criticism, most notably in the wake of the Dutroux affair.

## A chorus of criticism

The plan has drawn sharp opposition from across the political spectrum — and from within policing itself. Brussels mayor Philippe Close (PS) has been among its most vocal critics. "Inventing the twentieth piece of junk, which will trigger twenty thousand debates in parliament, is nonsense," [he said when reactions poured in](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/19/gemengde-reacties-op-voorstel-theo-frankcken-voor-rijkswacht-2/). "There are already too many structures in Belgium. What I want is more police officers."

His party colleague Ridouane Chahid echoed the sentiment: "Police officers aren't asking for a new structure, they're asking for resources." Groen parliamentary group leader Matti Vandemaele argued the proposal questions the work of existing officers and pulls "the militarisation card," calling instead for effective justice, stronger youth care and prospects for young people in vulnerable neighbourhoods — "real solutions, not muscle-flexing."

Criminologist Jelle Janssens of Ghent University warned the force could have a counterproductive effect. Pointing to France's militarised CRS and mobile units, he argued that such policing breeds hostility and resistance. "You create more aggression by acting aggressively," he said, adding that images of baton-wielding officers alienate certain young people. He questioned why a new structure is needed when public-order units already exist, and called for better information-gathering and community engagement instead.

Police unions are divided. While ACV Politie, ACOD and Sypol argue the government should invest in personnel, resources and training for existing services — warning a new corps "fishes in the same dried-up pond and fragments scarce budgets further" — the military union ACMP-CGMP supports the idea, saying it proposed something similar two years ago and warning that any new force needs a clearly delineated mandate.

## What it means

If realised, a 1,200-strong dedicated intervention force operating under a unified public-order doctrine would be the most significant reorganisation of Belgian public-order policing since the 2001 reform. It reopens a sensitive question: how far the military should be involved in domestic policing, in a country that deliberately ended its gendarmerie's militarised model a quarter-century ago.

The plan also cuts across Belgium's political geography. It is jointly pushed by Quintin (MR, francophone liberal) and Francken (N-VA, Flemish nationalist), while francophone Socialists in Brussels and Flemish Greens oppose it — highlighting the bilingual sensitivity of policing and public order. There is a fiscal angle too: a new brigade could, like the historical gendarmerie, be counted toward Belgium's defence-spending targets as it works to meet NATO's 5%-of-GDP goal by 2035.

## What to watch

Much remains unresolved. The joint proposal has yet to be formally submitted to and approved by the government. It is unclear how the roughly 400 Defence-financed members would be recruited, trained and legally commanded — under Defence or Interior, with Francken suggesting they could "operate under military command" while placed at Interior's disposal. The legal framework governing deployments and the priority rule over other missions is still to be defined.

Quintin says he wants "concrete results in the short term." Whether that timeline survives contact with the objections of Brussels' mayor, the police unions and the academic critics is the central question in the weeks ahead.
