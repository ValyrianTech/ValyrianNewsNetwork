# Northeastern Florida Faces New Flood Threats

Heavy rains that have submerged large stretches of Florida's Atlantic coast and triggered dozens of rescues pushed further north on Friday, as forecasters warned that a major river could crest well above record levels and that flooding threats may stretch into next week, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/extreme-weather-flooding-florida-california-storms-46b14622b3ad77c32eb0182bfe8bc9f4).

The National Weather Service posted flood warnings and watches spanning about 200 miles, from just south of Melbourne to the Jacksonville area. Forecasters expected another 2 to 6 inches of rain through Saturday, with some areas potentially receiving an additional 8 to 10 inches.

"Severe, widespread flash flooding is expected," the weather service said in a Friday morning forecast. "In addition, areas that normally do not experience flash flooding will flood. Furthermore, lives and property (are) in great danger."

## A Record-Shattering River

The St. Johns River, Florida's longest, rose above a record level Thursday near Cocoa that had stood since 1953. According to [AccuWeather](https://www.accuweather.com/en/severe-weather/st-johns-river-smashes-73-year-record-in-florida-with-flooding-that-could-last-into-2027/1961124), the river hit 18.19 feet on Thursday with an expected crest of 18.40 feet, shattering the previous mark of 17.55 feet set on Oct. 11, 1953—73 years earlier. By Friday morning, it had climbed to 18.68 feet and was still rising.

The weather service said the river is forecast to stay at least 2 feet above flood stage through early next week. AccuWeather meteorologists warn that at least minor flooding at Cocoa could persist into January 2027, leaving roads, homes, and businesses inundated for weeks on end.

That prolonged flooding is a product of the river's unusual geography. As Florida's longest river, the St. Johns flows north for 310 miles toward Jacksonville and the Atlantic Ocean but drops less than 30 feet the entire way—only about an inch per mile. Water crawls through marshes and lakes, and farther north, tides and strong onshore winds can even reverse the flow, delaying the runoff's journey to the sea.

## Rescues and Evacuations on the Space Coast

The storms shifted north to the Space Coast on Thursday, sending rivers over their banks and prompting water rescues of both people and animals during what is supposed to be Florida's dry season, which runs from October to May.

In Brevard County, people with trailers braved knee-deep water to rescue dozens of horses and other animals at the Blue Lake Equestrian Center, an animal hospice and boarding facility in Cocoa. First responders conducted nearly 100 high-water rescues across the county, and those numbers continued to climb, Sheriff Wayne Ivey said. The city of Palm Bay ordered mandatory evacuations for some residents of a mobile home community, and shelters opened for those forced from their homes.

Nearly 200 Florida National Guard personnel with 50 high-water vehicles have been deployed to help up and down the coast, the Division of Emergency Management said. About 235 officers with the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission were also responding.

"Never seen such a thing like this in Florida," said Jazmin Foster, whose Melbourne home was inundated with water for the second time in two weeks.

## Why This Is Happening in the 'Dry Season'

Parts of Florida's east coast have been deluged with more than 17 inches of rain in some spots since Tuesday, on top of heavy rains that fell earlier this month. Several places have already seen their wettest November on record.

This week's storms have been fueled by persistent high pressure over Virginia and a low-pressure system and associated stationary front in the Gulf, which together have pushed a steady onshore flow across the Florida Peninsula. They are an offshoot of El Niño, the natural warming of the Pacific near the equator that warps weather patterns worldwide.

Forecasters said El Niño conditions are expected to persist through the spring, meaning the soggy extreme weather in Florida and California will likely continue through the winter.

## A Coast-to-Coast Extreme Weather Episode

Florida is not alone. The California coast, from the Los Angeles to San Francisco areas, was also swamped with flooding this week, leaving neighborhoods underwater and forcing some drivers to abandon stalled vehicles in floodwaters. A long stretch of coastal Highway 1 was closed south of the Monterey Peninsula due to rock and mud slides.

Also Friday, a nontropical low-pressure system was expected to form and could become a subtropical or tropical storm along the southeastern U.S. coast, the National Hurricane Center in Miami said. The southeast and mid-Atlantic coasts should expect strong winds, dangerous surf, and coastal flooding into early next week ahead of the Thanksgiving holiday.

## What to Watch

The immediate concern is the developing coastal storm and how much additional rain and surge it adds to an already-saturated region. Equally important is how the St. Johns River's slow-moving crest migrates downstream toward Jacksonville, where it could threaten new communities in the weeks ahead.

As water management officials began controlled releases through the S-96 structure into the C-54 Canal to manage historic water levels, residents are also raising questions about recovery and assistance. For now, forecasters are urging vigilance—and warning that in a season of record-warm oceans and a persistent El Niño, the extreme weather may be far from over.
