# Trump's Arch Casts Shadow Over Arlington, Families Say

ARLINGTON, Va. — Cpl. Spencer Collart's headstone faces the rising sun and the Lincoln Memorial to the east. The Marine Corps War Memorial stands nearby. Soon, President Donald Trump's gilded, towering arch could loom there too — and for the young Marine's father, that prospect is an affront to sacred ground.

"It's a billboard for an ego. It's a loud, boastful, gaudy piece of marketing dropped onto sacred ground," said Bart Collart, whose son was killed in a 2023 Osprey crash in Australia at age 21 after going back into the burning aircraft to try to save trapped pilots. Bart Collart told [the Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/trump-arch-arlington-cemetery-washington-3dad4db58e4ee94ac926daeedc1e4107) that his son would see the arch as "dirtying hallowed ground."

The proposed 250-foot-tall (76-meter) gold and granite triumphal arch — modeled on Paris's Arc de Triomphe and taller than the Lincoln Memorial — would rise on a traffic circle roughly half a mile from Arlington National Cemetery's main entrance. While Trump and his supporters frame it as a tribute to America's 250th anniversary and to military veterans, its critics say it would physically shade gravesites, disrupt the solemnity of visits and funerals, and break the historic sightline between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington House.

## A Skyline-Altering Monument

The arch is one piece of a broader campaign by Trump to remake Washington. It joins a renovated Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool, a redeveloped golf course in East Potomac Park, and efforts to affix his name to the U.S. Institute of Peace and the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts. When complete, the arch would stretch taller than the Lincoln Memorial and reshape a landscape carefully designed to symbolize a reunited North and South.

Opponents say the structure would break the alignment between the Lincoln Memorial, the Memorial Bridge and Arlington House, the former home of Confederate Gen. Robert E. Lee. Behind the fences lining Memorial Drive, however, lies an active cemetery with more than 430,000 people interred and dozens more added each week — two presidents, more than a dozen Supreme Court justices, more than 400 Medal of Honor recipients, and remains from every war dating to the Revolutionary War. An estimated 3 million to 4 million people visit annually.

"I think you could, by any definition, consider it the most hallowed ground in the United States, but I think we're not even giving it credit for how hallowed, how representative, how much sacrifice and honor is there," said Micki McElya, a historian at the University of Connecticut and author of "The Politics of Mourning: Death and Honor in Arlington National Cemetery."

Jimi Shaughnessy, veterans program manager at the nonprofit National Parks Conservation Association, visits his great-grandparents' graves regularly. He described the setting as "open... quiet... peaceful," with space to properly acknowledge the dead. Major construction, he warned, would intrude on that and on funerals, bringing noise, tourist distraction and a "looming dark shadow above me."

## Court Battles and a Temporary Halt

The project won final approval from the National Capital Planning Commission in an 8–4 vote on Nov. 5, after a hearing that stretched more than nine hours. The vote broke along federal and local lines: supporters were administration-aligned, while opponents represented the District of Columbia.

Within hours, U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan issued a 14-day temporary restraining order in the early hours of Nov. 6, blocking any "construction-related activity" and consolidating lawsuits from the District of Columbia and Virginia, a group of Vietnam veterans and an architectural historian. As [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/06/judge-temporarily-halts-work-trump-triumphal-arch-washington), Chutkan found the plan likely illegal and lacking proper congressional authorization. Plaintiffs are now seeking a longer-term injunction.

The legal objections center on whether Congress ever authorized the arch, as the Commemorative Works Act requires, and whether any funds were appropriated. Supporters counter that the project completes a plan mandated by Congress in 1925 to add a monument to the circle. "It was always supposed to be a gateway to connect D.C. to Arlington," said Jared Craig, general counsel and national vice president for Veterans for America First, which filed a brief supporting the arch. He argued it honors veterans and fallen service members and "does not impact the cemetery itself and requires no changes there."

Phil Mendelson, a D.C. council member who sits on the planning panel, argued the project lacked required congressional approval and appropriations, and that the submitted plan did not even reference the military use cited by the administration. "We cannot approve final site and building plans until we have the final site and building plans," he said.

## The Kennedy Family Weighs In

The stakes widened on Nov. 10, when nine members of the Kennedy family — including children and grandchildren of Robert F. Kennedy and Sen. Ted Kennedy — moved to intervene as plaintiffs, arguing the arch would block sightlines to the Kennedy gravesites and the Eternal Flame commemorating President John F. Kennedy. Three Kennedy brothers are buried at Arlington.

"A towering arch casting shadows across the graves of our father, mother, and uncles, as well as the graves of so many who have served our country, would also cast a shadow over our democratic principles," Rory Kennedy and Maxwell Kennedy said in a statement, [according to CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/kennedy-family-trump-triumphal-arch/), "undermining the safeguards established by our elected representatives to protect this hallowed ground for generations to come."

Rep. Jared Huffman, the California Democrat set to chair the House natural resources committee, was blunter. "This arch would dishonor America," he told the commission, calling it "a tribute to Donald Trump" rather than veterans, and comparing it to monuments built for emperors or kings.

For his part, Trump has said the arch might be dedicated to military veterans. On a broader level, planning commission chair Mark Paoletta defended the vision: "President Trump understands as president and a superb builder that it's important to our national identity to display who we are through grand monuments," he said, adding that "the great cities around the world have arches: Paris, Rome, London."

## A Question of National Memory

The dispute is as much about who controls national memory as it is about architecture. Michael Lemmon, a Vietnam War veteran and former ambassador who is the lead plaintiff in the lawsuit, put the personal cost plainly in a February court filing. "I hope to be interred in Arlington Cemetery alongside my fallen comrades when I pass away," he wrote. "The thought of being interred in the shadow of what I believe to be a personal vanity project is profoundly disturbing to me."

The White House remains defiant. "We are confident we will be fully vindicated as this litigation proceeds and look forward to seeing the completion of this magnificent Arch — a tribute to 250 years of American greatness," spokesman Davis Ingle said.

## What's Next

The government has projected that, if allowed, construction would take 33 to 39 months — meaning completion would fall after the end of Trump's second term. A Department of Justice court filing said six to eight months of excavation and foundation work could begin almost immediately, with above-ground construction starting in May 2027 at the earliest, [Reuters reported via The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/03/doj-trump-arch-end-of-term). The project's funding source remains undisclosed; some estimates reach as high as $100 million.

For now, the matter rests with the courts. Democrats, who gained control of Congress in the November midterms, have vowed to investigate and potentially block the project when they take their majority in January. And at Section 52, where Cpl. Collart rests, his father offered commissioners a simple plea at the marathon hearing earlier this month.

"Look across the road at those rows of clean, simple white headstones. Go take the time to walk amongst them," Bart Collart said. "Show some real respect for the uniform my son died in."
