# Atmospheric Rivers Drench the Pacific Northwest

The Pacific Northwest is getting soaked by back-to-back atmospheric rivers — long, narrow bands of water vapor that carry tropical moisture through the sky and unleash heavy rain or snow when they make landfall. Two of these systems were forecast to pound parts of Oregon and Washington on Friday and Saturday, following two others that already dropped multiple inches of rain across both states earlier in the week, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/13/nx-s1-6032777/rivers-in-the-sky-are-raining-down-on-the-pacific-northwest-what-are-they).

The storms are arriving alongside annual high "king tides," El Niño's warm ocean waters and rising sea levels — a combination that leaves coastal and inland waterways across the West Coast at heightened risk of flooding.

## What is a river in the sky?

An atmospheric river is a narrow band of water vapor pushed by the wind. Loaded with moisture, it dumps rain or snow when it reaches land. These rivers can stretch up to 2,000 miles long and 600 miles wide, according to F. Martin Ralph, director of the Center for Western Weather and Water Extremes at UC San Diego's Scripps Institution of Oceanography.

"It's literally a river in the sky," said Ralph, one of the leading researchers on atmospheric rivers. They are a natural climate pattern: about 50 to 60% of California's rain comes from atmospheric rivers, according to Josh Weiss, a meteorologist with the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's Weather Prediction Center.

The current atmospheric river season, which runs from October to March, is "off to a fast start," Weiss said.

## Compounding impacts

Forecasters are particularly concerned about the cumulative effects of repeated storms. "What's really problematic is when you get compounding impacts from multiple atmospheric rivers," Weiss said. Those impacts include flash flooding, urban flooding and even landslides.

According to 40 years of data from the Federal Emergency Management Agency, 90% or more of all flood damage in the Western U.S. is caused by atmospheric rivers, Ralph said. Forecasters at the National Weather Service in Seattle warned that Washington's coast could be hit by significant 20-foot swells this weekend, meteorologist Jeff Michalski said.

The threat extends beyond the coastline. [CW3E's atmospheric river outlook](https://cw3e.ucsd.edu/cw3e-ar-outlook-9-november-2026/) noted that rivers and streams in western Washington were forecast to rise later in the week as a series of systems made landfall, with several stream gauges showing elevated chances of exceeding flood stage.

[Fox Weather](https://www.foxweather.com/weather-news/rounds-atmospheric-rivers-bring-days-soaking-rain-gusty-winds-west-coast) reported that portions of western Washington could see up to a foot of rain, with wave heights of 20 to 30 feet and damaging winds of 50 to 60 mph along the coast. The National Weather Service office in Portland warned that large wave run-up could dislodge logs on area beaches, creating a danger beyond the rain itself.

## Pineapple and rum

Many people know atmospheric rivers by their nicknames, such as the Pineapple Express or Rum Runner Express, which describe where the moisture originates. A Pineapple Express that crossed the Pacific from Hawaii in late October caused devastating flooding and multiple deaths in the Southwest's Four Corners region.

Atmospheric rivers aren't limited to the West Coast. Some larger nor'easters on the East Coast can pack a "nasty" atmospheric river, Ralph said.

## Is El Niño to blame?

There is a persistent question about whether El Niño's warm ocean temperatures strengthen atmospheric rivers. Ralph's 2015 theory suggested they might — but his own research showed the opposite.

"[Atmospheric rivers] don't dance to the tune of El Niño," Ralph said.

Climate scientists say there also isn't enough data to connect the weather pattern to human-caused global warming, though a warming world does mean more water vapor in the atmosphere.

The November 2026 storms echo a comparable event a year earlier, when more than 6 million people were under flood watches in Western Washington and 18 river locations were forecast to experience major flooding, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/weather/floods/pacific-northwest-flood-alerts-atmospheric-river-brings-heavy-rain-rcna248349) at the time. That event illustrated how quickly a series of atmospheric rivers can overwhelm the region's rivers and emergency systems.

## What to watch

With more storms in the forecast and soils already saturated, the region's flood risk will depend on how much rain falls and how fast. Officials will be watching river gauges and tide charts closely through the weekend.

Ralph's advice is simple: stay alert, regardless of the year's climate pattern.

"We've got to keep our eyes out for atmospheric rivers," he said. "Whether it's an El Niño year or a La Niña year or a No Niño year."
