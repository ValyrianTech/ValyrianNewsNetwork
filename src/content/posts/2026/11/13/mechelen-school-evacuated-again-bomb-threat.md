# Mechelen School Evacuated Again After Bomb Threat

More than 2,000 pupils and staff were evacuated from the Ursulinen school in Mechelen on Friday morning after a bomb threat was phoned in — the second such evacuation at the same complex in just six weeks, and the latest in a sustained wave of false bomb threats sweeping schools across Belgium and Europe.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/13/urselinen-mechelen-opnieuw-evacuatie/), the call reached the school's reception shortly after 10 a.m. Dirk Van de Sande, spokesperson for the Rivierenland police zone, confirmed that the school began evacuating immediately. Lessons were suspended for the rest of the day while federal police explosive-detection dogs swept the premises.

## How the evacuation unfolded

The Ursulinen complex, part of the scholengroep Kitos group, houses both a primary and a secondary school whose buildings are physically interlinked — which is why a threat to any part of the site triggers a full evacuation. Primary pupils and toddlers were taken to the neighbouring Go Shil! school, while secondary pupils gathered at the nearby Onze-Lieve-Vrouw-over-de-Dijlekerk. Pupils who could not go home were received at the city's Cultuurcentrum on the Minderbroedersgang, where food and drink were provided, and parents were kept informed via Smartschool.

Figures on the number evacuated vary slightly. VRT NWS and police cited "more than 2,000" pupils and staff, while [HLN](https://www.hln.be/mechelen/2-150-leerlingen-geevacueerd-na-tweede-bommelding-in-anderhalve-maand-bij-ursulinen-in-mechelen-sommigen-duwden-en-schreeuwden-tegen-elkaar~a2f2a847/) reported that all 2,150 pupils from both the primary and secondary school were moved. Police brought in reinforcements from two federal explosive-detection dogs, and the Sint-Jacobsstraat was closed off during the operation. At the time of the latest reporting, the sweep was still ongoing and no device had been confirmed.

## Calmer than October, but a heavier mood

Ingeborg De Cooman, spokesperson for scholengroep Kitos, said the evacuation was orderly. "Our school buildings are intertwined and the safety of our pupils comes first," she said. "The evacuation went well and quickly. There was never any panic." She added that the school had learned from the October incident, so most pupils already had their belongings with them.

Not every experience matched that description. Pupils Camille Verhaeghe and Lisa Baudet, both 17, told HLN they initially mistook the alarm for a drill. "The last time it was much more chaotic. There were fewer bottlenecks and everyone knew better what to do." Two younger pupils, Ariana and Olivia, both 14, described a more unsettling scene. "We got a bit scared. What if it's real? There was also a bit of panic. Some pupils pushed each other, some shouted at each other." Asked who might be responsible, they answered simply: "AI maybe?"

De Cooman framed the repeat incident as a broader burden. "This is really an enormous loss of lesson and learning time. And what does that whole police deployment cost society?" The school plans a debriefing with pupils next week.

## A pattern that predates Friday

Friday's threat is not an isolated event. The first bomb threat at Ursulinen struck on Friday 2 October, when 1,400 pupils from the kindergarten and secondary school were evacuated — the primary school was away at a sports day at the Nekker that day. Federal police checked more than 200 rooms with three explosive-detection dogs before the buildings were cleared around 3:25 p.m. No bomb was found, and the investigation into that incident remains open. As [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/02/bommelding-mechelen-ursulinen-evacuatie/), the October call may have used an electronic or AI-generated voice; by contrast, this week's threat was a live telephone call, a difference that may matter to investigators.

The Mechelen case fits into a much larger surge. On 11 November, roughly 6,300 schools across Italy received emailed bomb threats, with more than 300 evacuated; hundreds of schools in Ireland, Northern Ireland and Scotland received similar emails, prompting an investigation into the sender. The messages reportedly came from an unknown group calling itself "Vilayat van Misoginistan" and were signed with the name "Michail Andropov" — the same Russian name used days earlier in threats against European media outlets, including Irish public broadcaster RTÉ. Earlier in November, dozens of false bomb threats also disrupted flights across several European countries.

No country has formally attributed the threats to a group or a state, and there is no evidence yet linking them to any government. Some EU states suspect Russia or Iran, but Italian Defence Minister Guido Crosetto cautioned against assuming state involvement. "It is not always the work of other countries or hostile entities," he said. "There are also attention-seekers and madmen who, in times like today, cannot wait to sow terror in a city or a region."

## The cost — and who pays

Belgium has tightened its legal response. A false bomb threat can now carry a prison sentence of up to three years under the new Criminal Code that took effect on 1 September 2026, and those convicted can be ordered to pay damages — including the cost of deploying emergency services. The financial stakes are considerable: a March 2026 false bomb threat at Brussels-South station forced the cancellation of more than 300 trains. As [VRT NWS detailed](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/03/24/valse-bommelding-brussel-zuid-straffen/), the disruption amounted to more than 18,600 minutes of delays and thousands of stranded travellers, with the rail operator NMBS signalling it would seek to recover costs from those responsible.

Whether that legal framework will be applied in Mechelen is an open question. Investigators must first establish whether the call was a deliberate act or a genuine, if mistaken, alarm — a distinction that criminal law professor Wendy De Bondt stressed matters greatly. The repeat targeting of a single school also raises the possibility of a copycat, or of a perpetrator emboldened by the unresolved October case.

## What to watch

Several questions remain unanswered. Was any device found on Friday, and when were the buildings cleared? Is the November threat connected to the October incident, and do the differences in method — a live call versus a possible recording — point to the same perpetrator? Most importantly, is Mechelen part of the coordinated European wave, or a domestic echo of it?

For the pupils and staff at Ursulinen, the practical impact is already clear: lost lessons, disrupted routines and a school community forced to weigh the chilling unfamiliarity of a bomb threat against the mundane annoyance of retrieving bicycles from a cordoned-off building. The wider lesson — that false alarms have real costs, and that the line between a hoax and a genuine threat is exactly what the law now hinges on — is one Belgian authorities, and their European counterparts, are still working out.
