# CATL Plans to Expand Its Hungary Battery Plant

Chinese battery giant CATL is preparing to add production capacity at its battery plant in Debrecen, Hungary, according to corporate filings reported by Caixin on November 13. The move would deepen the European footprint of the world's largest electric-vehicle battery maker at what is set to become its largest manufacturing base outside China—a 100 GWh, €7.34 billion facility that is only now moving from construction into production.

The precise scope of the newly reported expansion—how much additional capacity it covers, the investment involved and the target timeline—could not be independently confirmed. The Caixin report on the filing was not accessible at the time of writing, and CATL has not publicly detailed the terms. What is clear is that the Debrecen site is entering a new phase: after years of delays, it began cell trial production in late September and cleared its biggest legal challenge in October.

## A plant years in the making

CATL announced the Debrecen project in August 2022 as its second European battery factory, with a planned investment of €7.34 billion and eventual annual capacity of 100 GWh, according to [EqualOcean](https://equalocean.com/news/2026100922227-catl-starts-cell-trial-production-planned-100-gwh-hungarian-plant). Construction of the first cell building began in summer 2023, and equipment installation was completed by spring 2026. The site had already been assembling battery modules since autumn 2024, reaching cumulative module output of 537,000 units before cell manufacturing came online.

On September 22, 2026, CATL started trial production on two cell lines, [IT之家 reported](https://m.ithome.com/html/1005962.htm), describing the phase as an effort to "complete commissioning of production equipment, optimize and validate the full manufacturing process, and prepare for subsequent formal mass production." Once fully built, the plant will become CATL's largest production base outside China, with plans to employ roughly 9,000 people and cooperation ties with more than 300 local enterprises.

The ramp-up has not been smooth. As [electrive.com](https://www.electrive.com/2026/09/23/catl-begins-test-production-of-battery-cells-in-debrecen/) reported, the start of cell production had been delayed several times, at one point forcing Mercedes-Benz to pause output of its electric GLC in Bremen when CATL could not supply enough cells—prompting the automaker to temporarily source cells from China instead. The site also drew regulatory scrutiny over wastewater discharge and elevated nickel exposure among employees. Hungary's new government under Prime Minister Péter Magyar has signaled stricter environmental oversight of the battery industry, though there is no clear evidence linking that political shift to the CATL delays.

## A legal cloud lifts

Just over a month after trial production began, the Debrecen court rejected a long-running lawsuit by local residents against the plant's environmental permit. In a ruling dated October 29, the court found no illegality in the government's approval procedures and said the judgment could not be appealed, according to [OFweek / 预见能源](https://libattery.ofweek.com/2026-11/ART-36008-8460-30709911.html).

During three and a half years of litigation, the plant's permits were repeatedly revised, with standards on water use, grey-water recycling and air emissions tightened. Because the plaintiffs did not challenge the revised permits, the court found their right to sue had lapsed. "This means CATL's single largest legal risk in Hungary has been cleared," OFweek noted—a meaningful milestone for a facility of this scale.

## Why Hungary, why now

The expansion logic rests on a simple supply-chain reality: batteries account for roughly 40% of an EV's cost, and shipping cells across continents adds both logistics expense and response-time penalties compared with same-city supply. Debrecen sits a few kilometers from BMW's new vehicle plant, which began producing Neue Klasse models in late 2025, and near Mercedes-Benz, Stellantis and Volkswagen operations in Hungary and the surrounding region. Mercedes-Benz has been identified as the first and largest customer for the plant's initial capacity, with BMW also a core customer.

The first 40 GWh phase is reported to be fully booked before mass production even begins. That demand signal matters because Europe's homegrown battery industry has stumbled. Sweden's Northvolt—once dubbed "the light of Europe's battery industry"—filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the US in November 2024 and saw its Swedish parent file for bankruptcy in March 2025. The collapse traced to supply-chain instability, slow capacity ramp-up and cost control problems, and it left European automakers leaning more heavily on proven Chinese operators.

## The carbon ledger

Beyond the court ruling, the longer-term force reshaping CATL's European strategy is regulation. EU rules increasingly tie market access to local production and carbon accounting. Under the bloc's Battery Regulation, carbon-footprint class-label obligations are scheduled from August 2026, a Digital Battery Passport becomes mandatory from February 2027, and from February 2028 batteries whose carbon footprint exceeds a maximum threshold will be barred from sale in the EU.

The crucial mechanism is that carbon footprint is accounted by place of production. A battery made in Hungary is measured against Hungary's grid; one made in China and shipped is measured differently. After 2028, local production becomes a market-access requirement rather than merely a cost choice—a central reason CATL is building European capacity well above 160 GWh. Its German plant in Thuringia (14 GWh) is already operating, Debrecen (100 GWh) is ramping, and a Spanish joint venture with Stellantis (50 GWh) is targeted to come online around the end of 2026.

That does not mean the path is clear. The 2028 carbon threshold remains a genuine hurdle, and the EU-led carbon accounting methodology and China's are not yet mutually recognized. Ramp-up speed, green-power procurement and yield rates remain engineering and operational variables—exactly the kind of execution risk that sank Northvolt.

## What to watch

The reported expansion filing is the next test. Key questions remain unanswered: how much additional capacity CATL intends to add, whether the expansion requires new environmental permits that could reopen old legal battles, which automakers have signed for the added output, and how the project will be financed. CATL has not publicly addressed these points.

What is no longer in doubt is the direction of travel. A 100 GWh factory that spent years navigating litigation, permitting and delays is now producing cells, supplying European automakers and positioning itself inside the EU's carbon framework rather than outside it. The legal obstacles are largely cleared; the remaining contest is the one CATL knows best—ramping production and delivering on orders.
