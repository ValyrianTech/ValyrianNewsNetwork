# China Warns EU Countries Over Chinese 5G Equipment Bans

China's Ministry of Commerce said on Thursday it had expressed "serious concern" to several European Union member states over their moves to ban telecom equipment from Chinese suppliers, accusing them of politicizing technical issues and overstretching the concept of national security. The statement, delivered at the ministry's regular press conference in Beijing, marks the latest flashpoint in a widening technological and trade confrontation between China and Europe over the future of 5G networks.

Speaking to reporters, ministry spokesperson He Yadong said China had already conveyed its concerns directly to the governments involved, according to [Xinhua](https://english.news.cn/20261112/73ec430f4b8748ac8c560a37fc693502/c.html). "We have noted the relevant reports and have expressed serious concern to the relevant governments," He said. "China has consistently held that 5G is an important infrastructure for the development of the global digital economy, and its construction and operation should respect market rules, technological neutrality, and international rules."

## The Immediate Triggers

The ministry did not name specific countries, but the statement followed two recent national-level actions in Europe, as reported by [China Daily](https://global.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202611/12/WS6af579e1e4b03ebf8519c0d4.html). In Germany, Berlin has announced plans to classify 5G base stations as "critical network components," a reclassification that could pave the way for bans or restrictions on Chinese equipment, according to the German business daily *Handelsblatt*. In Portugal, authorities have renewed restrictions on "high-risk" 5G suppliers following a new security review, maintaining measures that effectively exclude Chinese telecom giant Huawei from its networks, according to the Portuguese outlet *ECO*.

"We firmly oppose politicizing technical issues and engaging in excessive securitization," He said. "We hope the countries concerned will promptly correct their misguided practices and provide a fair, just and non-discriminatory business environment for Chinese companies."

The wording, carried verbatim in the Chinese-language dispatch published by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34459028), is firm but stops short of announcing specific countermeasures — a warning shot that frames Beijing's position ahead of delicate negotiations in Brussels.

## A Larger Fight Over EU Law

The dispute is rooted in the European Commission's revised Cybersecurity Act, announced in January 2026, which would for the first time make the removal of "high-risk" vendor equipment mandatory. Since 2020, the EU's "5G toolbox" merely recommended that member states avoid high-risk suppliers. Implementation has been uneven: by the time the new law was announced, only 13 of the bloc's 27 member states had acted, and Germany — where Huawei supplied equipment across roughly 60% of 5G sites as recently as late 2024 — had been among the slow movers.

The new law would change the legal basis from recommendation to obligation, requiring member states to strip designated high-risk vendors from their networks within three years. It also creates a mechanism allowing the Commission to designate an entire country as a "cybersecurity threat," potentially extending exclusions into up to 18 critical sectors beyond telecoms.

The law does not name Huawei or ZTE explicitly, but its intent is widely read as targeting Chinese vendors, whose share of European 5G infrastructure is estimated at between 33% and 40%.

## Beijing's Escalating Warning

Thursday's statement is the latest step in a months-long escalation. As [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/china-threatens-eu-retaliation) reported, China's Ministry of Commerce submitted a 30-page formal document to the European Commission in April 2026, warning of retaliation if the Cybersecurity Act led to the exclusion of Chinese firms. The submission invoked China's Foreign Trade Law and the State Council's Supply Chain Security Regulations — frameworks that allow Beijing to restrict trade, investigate foreign entities, and impose reciprocal bans.

The document argued that the law's use of "non-technical risk" factors is discriminatory by national origin, violates World Trade Organization principles of non-discrimination and proportionality, and could expose European firms — German automakers, Dutch chipmakers, and French luxury and aerospace companies — to reciprocal restrictions.

The threat rests on a documented precedent. After Sweden banned Chinese vendors from its 5G networks in 2020, Ericsson's China revenues fell about 46% the following year and never recovered. Nokia's China revenues fell from roughly €2.5 billion in 2018 to about €913 million, with the combined China market share of the two Nordic vendors reportedly dropping to about 3%. China has already been restricting Nokia and Ericsson while warning the EU not to formalize its own exclusions — an asymmetry the Nordic vendors have publicly challenged.

## Resistance From Within Europe

The EU's push is itself encountering headwinds. According to a draft document seen by Reuters and detailed by [Telecom Gazette](https://telecomgazette.com/articles/poste-italiane-nears-full-tim-control-as-eu-wobbles-on-huawei-rip-out-28d0f765), member states are pressing the Commission to replace its fixed 36-month phase-out with a risk-based timeline determined by the level of risk, equipment lifespans, compatibility requirements, and the availability of alternatives.

The cost is a major factor. GSMA Intelligence has estimated that replacing mobile network equipment across the EU would cost the industry between €30 billion and €40 billion. Mason Hurlocker of the Bloomsbury Intelligence and Security Institute said the reforms carry "a range of political, operational, security and economic risks," adding that it is possible the Commission narrows its provisions "given industry cost warnings and resistance from some member states to mandatory rip-and-replace obligations."

## Analysis: A Battle Fought in Brussels

The real battlefield is not the press conference but the EU legislative process. The Cybersecurity Act must still be negotiated with EU governments and the European Parliament before becoming law, and China's submissions and statements are designed to influence that negotiation.

The countries most exposed to Chinese retaliation — Germany, the Netherlands, and France — are also those whose implementation of the existing toolbox has been the most limited. Germany in particular is caught in a bind: it carries Huawei's largest infrastructure footprint in Western Europe while its automotive sector depends heavily on the Chinese market, with roughly €90 billion in annual exports at stake.

The dispute is geopolitically loaded. The United States has been pressuring the EU to accelerate Huawei removal, even as it threatens tariffs over unrelated EU enforcement actions against American tech firms. Brussels finds itself navigating pressure from Washington to act and pressure from Beijing not to.

Even if the law passes, a full EU-wide three-year rip-and-replace is widely viewed as ambitious, and compliance is far from certain given cost, member-state reluctance, and the precedent of missed deadlines elsewhere — the UK's BT, for instance, missed its 2023 deadline for removing Huawei from its core network.

## What's Next

The immediate question is whether the final Cybersecurity Act retains the Commission's fixed 36-month deadline or moves to the risk-based timeline that member states are demanding. A second is whether Beijing will escalate beyond rhetoric — invoking its Foreign Trade Law against specific European firms — if the law passes in its current form. For now, China's language remains that of a warning, not a rupture. But with both sides dug in, the room for compromise in Brussels is narrowing.
