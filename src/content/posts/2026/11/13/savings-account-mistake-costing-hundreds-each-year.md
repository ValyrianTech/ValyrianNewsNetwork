# The Savings Mistake Costing You Hundreds a Year

Many Americans are quietly losing hundreds of dollars a year not through bad investments or risky bets, but through a simple act of inertia: keeping their cash in a low-yield savings account, according to [The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2026/11/13/savings-account-mistake-that-costing-you-hundreds-each-year/), which reported that savers "can earn much more by switching to an online bank, where the interest rates are far greater." The gap between what traditional banks pay and what high-yield accounts offer has grown so wide that the choice of where to park savings now matters as much as how much is set aside.

## The Numbers Behind the Gap

According to [Bankrate](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/savings/average-savings-interest-rates/), which surveys more than 500 banks and credit unions each week, the national average savings account yield stood at just 0.56% APY as of November 13, 2026. The best high-yield savings accounts, meanwhile, were paying around 4% APY — roughly seven times the national average.

For a household holding a modest $10,000 emergency fund, that spread is worth about $344 a year. On a $25,000 balance, the difference climbs to roughly $860; on $50,000, it approaches $1,720. Even a $5,000 reserve earns about $172 more annually at the higher rate.

The mechanics are straightforward. Bankrate notes that online banks "have less overhead than big banks with physical locations, and can offer higher APYs." With no branch network to maintain and a strong incentive to compete for deposits, digital institutions pass the savings back to customers in the form of higher interest. Traditional brick-and-mortar banks, by contrast, often pay well below market rates because their customers rarely switch.

## Why Savers Stay Put

Consumer advocates have for years urged savers to compare annual percentage yields and move idle cash to online accounts, yet the inertia persists. Two factors appear to drive it. The first is simple familiarity: an existing relationship with a big bank makes opening a new account feel like unnecessary effort. As Bankrate puts it, "if you already have an account with a big bank, you may be less inclined to open up an additional account."

The second is a lingering concern about safety. Some savers hesitate to move money to an online-only institution, worried that they are trading security for yield. Those fears are largely misplaced. Deposits at FDIC-insured banks are protected up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category — the same guarantee that covers accounts at a familiar corner branch.

## What to Watch For

The advice comes with caveats. High-yield savings rates are variable and can fall if the broader interest-rate environment declines, so the current ~4% returns are not locked in. Some banks advertise promotional or "relationship" rates that require minimum balances or a linked checking account; Bankrate advises comparing those offers against online banks that may pay more without the extra requirements. Savers should also confirm that any institution they choose is FDIC- or NCUA-insured before making a switch.

For households, the takeaway is that a low-effort administrative change can translate into real money — a rare instance of essentially free return. For banks, the wide spread underscores a persistent competitive pressure: rate-sensitive customers, once shown the gap, have little reason to stay put.

Bankrate's bottom line is blunt: an APY several times the national average is easy to find, "especially at an online bank." Whether savers act on it is another matter — but the cost of not doing so now runs into the hundreds each year.
