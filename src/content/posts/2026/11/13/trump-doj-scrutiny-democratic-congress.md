# Trump's Justice Department Braces for a New Era of Scrutiny

For nearly two years, the Justice Department has operated as an extension of the White House, unchecked by a Republican-controlled Congress as it pursued investigations into President Donald Trump's perceived enemies and defended administration positions that judges repeatedly deemed legally dubious, [according to the Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/justice-department-congress-fbi-trump-e121c4420d0010b9ba7e8003407d304a). Now, sweeping Democratic gains in the November 3 midterm elections have shifted the balance of power in Washington, teeing up an expected wave of congressional investigations into the department and the FBI.

At least publicly, the department appears unbowed.

## A Department Unaccustomed to Scrutiny

The shift marks a stark reversal for an agency that spent the first two years of Trump's second term largely insulated from legislative oversight. With Republicans controlling both chambers, Democratic lawmakers had decried what they saw as the erosion of the department's independence — through hearings and in letters — but had little power to demand accountability as the minority party.

The department is now described as depleted by the loss of thousands of career employees and by the post-election departures of senior leaders, including the Senate-confirmed heads of its National Security, Civil, and Civil Rights divisions.

Attorney General Todd Blanche has signaled that the department intends to keep pressing forward. "We will not back down. This will not slow us down. This will not stop us," Blanche said in a Fox News Channel interview, according to AP. "President Trump has two years remaining in this administration, and you better believe that we're going to use it effectively."

## 'Wartime' Footing and Warning Letters

The department's posture has alarmed critics. A top Blanche aide, Aakash Singh, told U.S. attorneys on a call to prepare for a "wartime" footing — a remark the department later said "had everything to do with public safety," but which suggested that more U.S. attorneys could be pushed out.

In the weeks before the election, the department undertook a series of politically sensitive actions: it unsuccessfully sought an indictment against former White House aide Cassidy Hutchinson; accelerated an investigation into Democratic Rep. Ilhan Omar; employed an unusual maneuver to file a fresh criminal case against former FBI Director James Comey; and charged two former FBI officials in a complaint a judge swiftly dismissed.

Meanwhile, the White House has escalated its warnings against the incoming majority. White House counsel Will Scharf sent a three-page letter to Rep. Robert Garcia, the California Democrat set to chair the House Oversight Committee, warning that aggressive oversight "may cross the line into blatant criminality," [The New York Times reported](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/11/us/politics/trump-democrats-congress-oversight-committee.html).

The warning drew sharp criticism from veterans of Washington's oversight battles. "My first-year law students would fail if they wrote a letter like that," said Stan Brand, a former general counsel to the House of Representatives.

## Democrats Promise to Fight Back

Garcia has made clear that the incoming majority intends to be aggressive. "When Republicans go low, what we're not going to do anymore is just take that, take the high road and turn the other cheek," he told CNN's "State of the Union," [Politico reported](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/11/08/garcia-days-of-us-not-fighting-back-are-over-01155486). "The days of us not fighting back are over."

Garcia indicated that even members of the president's family — including Donald Trump Jr. and Jared Kushner — could be subjects of the committee's investigations, and that the panel could examine Trump's pardons, funding for his White House ballroom, and allies including Elon Musk and newly appointed chief of staff Stephen Miller.

He characterized the administration's warnings as an attempt to intimidate the committee. "What Todd Blanche is doing right now is a threat," Garcia said. "He is threatening our committee. He's trying to stop us from doing real oversight."

For his part, Blanche accused Democratic leaders of "outright threats," saying on CNN that "that is not governing, that is not leading, that is not oversight." He told reporters on Thursday that there were no open investigations into Democrats' rhetoric and acknowledged it is not a crime to warn of looming investigations.

In a separate confrontation, the top federal prosecutor in Houston, U.S. Attorney Aaron Reitz, sent a warning letter to Sen. Richard Blumenthal over his inquiry into funding for Trump's White House ballroom, ordering him to preserve all records and accusing him of intimidating citizens.

## A Familiar Constitutional Standoff

Oversight clashes between the Justice Department and a Congress controlled by the opposing party are a long-standing feature of American governance. Republicans held Attorneys General Eric Holder and Merrick Garland in contempt; Democrats held William Barr in contempt in 2019.

What is different now, analysts suggest, is the intensity of the rhetoric and the degree to which a depleted department may struggle to function under sustained scrutiny. Because the Senate is now under Democratic control, divisions led by temporary acting officials are unlikely to win Senate confirmation of permanent leaders — a dynamic experts say could produce "a lot of inaction."

"What that means for DOJ, potentially, is just a lot of inaction, with tough decisions not being made because typically those tough decisions are made by confirmed people at the top of the components," said Greg Brower, a former U.S. attorney in Nevada.

Others are more sanguine. "I've been through so many administrations as a prosecutor. This is a lot of noise that's coming out of the elections and posturing going on," said Leah Foley, the U.S. Attorney for Massachusetts. "But things are going to settle down, and we've operated proficiently through divided governments before and we'll continue to do so."

## What's Next

As the new Congress prepares to be sworn in, the central question is how far each side is willing to push. The White House has hinted that Congress's routine oversight may amount to criminal conduct — a claim legal scholars note runs up against the Constitution's "speech and debate" protections, which shield lawmakers for legislative acts.

"It's a standoff, and I don't know what the answer to that is yet. I don't know if the Congress knows," said Brand, the veteran Washington lawyer.

With Democrats vowing to use their new majority and the administration pledging not to yield, the coming months are likely to test the boundaries of divided government — and of the Justice Department's capacity to withstand the scrutiny it has so far avoided.
