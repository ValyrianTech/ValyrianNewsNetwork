# ICE raids in Kansas became a midterm flashpoint

DODGE CITY, Kan. — When federal immigration agents descended on this meatpacking hub in late September, they set off something their superiors may not have anticipated: a political earthquake. Less than six weeks later, three historically Republican counties in southwestern Kansas swung sharply toward Democrats, helping elect the state's first Democratic U.S. senator in 94 years and signaling the limits of President Donald Trump's aggressive deportation agenda.

According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/immigration-midterms-kansas-senate-deportation-dodge-city-29579d3e5baa94587f1fe46082c6faa7), the episode has become a case study in how visible, disruptive enforcement in immigrant-dependent local economies can backfire—even in deep-red territory.

## The operation and its aftermath

Reports of ICE agents operating across southwest Kansas surfaced around Sept. 22, 2026, and sparked immediate protests. The U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Kansas later announced that 104 people were arrested during a three-day surge officially named "Operation Safe Community - Kansas," as [KWCH 12 reported](https://www.kwch.com/2026/09/29/feds-104-arrested-during-ice-enforcement-push-southwest-kansas/).

The operation focused on Dodge City, Garden City and Liberal—known as the "Golden Triangle of Meatpacking," a region responsible for roughly a quarter of the U.S. beef supply. More than 65% of Dodge City's population identifies as Hispanic or Latino, according to 2025 Census data cited in [KSN's initial reporting](https://www.ksn.com/news/state-regional/dodge-city-police-address-tension-over-federal-immigration-operations/).

Local officials in Dodge City, Finney County, Scott County and Liberal said they were not notified in advance and had "minimal to no success" communicating with federal agents during the operation. Scott City Police Chief Kevin Litten described the unannounced federal presence as "as surprising as if a tornado blew through town."

Industry groups warned of widespread absenteeism at feedlots, dairy processors and meatpacking plants. The Kansas Livestock Association and its counterparts in Texas and Oklahoma said the operations prompted delays in shipping thousands of cattle and millions of dollars in lost revenue, according to [AP's reporting on the economic fallout](https://apnews.com/article/meatpacking-immigration-kansas-texas-oklahoma-grocery-prices-6fe81b0b37cc56a0556ba64158bbc2c4).

## A disputed justification

As public anger mounted, federal officials moved to defend the operation. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin and U.S. Attorney Ryan Kriegshauser publicly named five individuals with serious criminal histories—including murder, rape and kidnapping—as among those "apprehended" during the sweep.

A [Wichita Eagle investigation](https://www.kansas.com/news/politics-government/article317530719.html) found none of the five were actually arrested in the September operation: four had been deported months earlier and the fifth was already in federal prison. The press release was later quietly edited—without notice to news outlets—to recast the five as examples of people "previously apprehended" by ICE, as [KSN reported](https://www.ksn.com/news/state-regional/feds-now-admit-five-arrests-were-not-part-of-recent-kansas-ice-sweep/).

Kriegshauser had insisted the operation was "targeted" and "intelligence-driven," pushing back on claims of discriminatory sweeps. "Operation Safe Community - Kansas was a targeted operation designed to put some of the worst of the worst behind bars," he said in the [U.S. Attorney's Office statement](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ks/pr/us-attorney-encourages-facts-not-rumors-about-kansas-ice-operations). "Contrary claims are inaccurate."

## From street to ballot box

The discrepancy and the broader disruption became fuel for a political backlash. Jose Del Real, an Army veteran who served two tours in Iraq and had not voted in a decade, ended a protest speech in a Dodge City park with a simple message: "None of this matters if you do not vote. Let's go vote."

Voters listened. Dodge City's county and two neighboring counties swung wildly to the left. That shift helped elect pastor Adam Hamilton—a Methodist megachurch pastor—over incumbent Sen. Roger Marshall, according to [AP's report on the Senate race](https://apnews.com/article/senate-kansas-election-roger-marshall-adam-hamilton-7f09e5cc69e0ac8e618376e60ef311d8). Hamilton's victory snapped the nation's longest Democratic Senate losing streak and came despite roughly $110 million in campaign spending and two Trump rallies staged in the state in October to rescue Marshall.

"This is amazing. We really did make history," Hamilton told supporters. "We're not flyover country. We're the heart of the country."

More than half of Kansas voters in the Senate race disapproved of how ICE is handling its job, according to The AP Voter Poll—consistent with 61% nationally who disapprove.

## Analysis: The limits of enforcement

The Kansas result is being read as evidence that the politics of deportation have a breaking point. Dr. Russell Arben Fox, a political science professor at Friends University, said swings as dramatic as the one seen in southwest Kansas could not have happened without some white, moderate Republicans changing their minds.

Even local Republican figures acknowledged the complexity. Scott Fischer, the local GOP chairman and a self-described strong-border supporter, called the operation somewhat performative—"There was a little showmanship"—and questioned the practicality of mass deportation. "When we say send them all home, what does that mean? Who is them?"

Organizers were blunt about what drove turnout. "If ICE hadn't come, we would still be red, 100%," said 25-year-old local organizer Amy Soberanes. "And so I thank Donald Trump for sending immigration here."

## What's next

The episode arrives as national politics recalibrates on immigration. Democrats won back control of Congress in the midterms, and Trump has sent mixed signals since—suggesting in a "60 Minutes" interview that his deportation approach was "too harsh" while simultaneously naming hard-liner Stephen Miller as White House chief of staff.

For southwest Kansas, the immediate questions remain unresolved: local officials say they have still never received a full list of the 104 detainees, and the economic ripples through the meatpacking industry continue. But politically, the message from Dodge City was unmistakable—and it is likely to shape how both parties approach immigration enforcement heading into the next presidential campaign.
