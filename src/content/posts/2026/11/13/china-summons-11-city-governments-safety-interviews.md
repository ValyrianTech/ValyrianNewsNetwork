# China Summons 11 City Governments Over Safety Failures

The Office of China's State Council Work Safety Committee has formally summoned the top leaders of 11 municipal governments across seven provinces and regions for safety-production interviews, invoking an accountability mechanism after a string of deadly accidents, [Xinhua reported](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261113/ce0d5b368a144a6e925bf30ea237cb86/c.html), republishing the Ministry of Emergency Management's official account. The move signals that local leaders' political performance records are now directly on the line for workplace-safety failures.

## Who Was Called In

The interviews fell into two categories. Three city governments were summoned because their regions suffered a "major accident" (重大事故) since the start of 2026: **Baotou** in Inner Mongolia, **Nanyang** in Henan, and **Xiangyang** in Hubei.

The remaining eight prefectures and cities were called in because each recorded **three or more "larger accidents" (较大事故) within a six-month window**. They are:

- **Shanxi province:** Lüliang, Shuozhou, Xinzhou
- **Heilongjiang province:** Mudanjiang
- **Shandong province:** Yantai
- **Henan province:** Zhengzhou
- **Yunnan province:** Honghe prefecture and Qujing

In total, the principal leaders of 11 city and prefecture governments across Inner Mongolia, Henan, Hubei, Shanxi, Heilongjiang, Shandong, and Yunnan were called in.

## A Multi-Ministry Affair

The interviews were not an emergency-management exercise alone. According to Xinhua, they were attended by relevant departments from the Ministry of Emergency Management, the Ministry of Public Security, the Ministry of Natural Resources, the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development, the Ministry of Transport, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, the Ministry of Commerce, the State Administration for Market Regulation, the National Fire and Rescue Administration, and the National Mine Safety Administration — a breadth that underscores how workplace safety now cuts across nearly every arm of central government.

## The Diagnosis: Rebounds and a "Severe" Situation

The interview body was blunt about the national picture. Since the start of the year, the official readout said, the national workplace-safety situation has been "generally stable," but in some regions accidents of larger magnitude and above have "rebounded," leaving the situation "severe."

Localities were told to reflect deeply and to learn from past accidents by drawing inferences for other risks — a reference to the Chinese principle of "举一反三" (drawing inferences from one case to apply broadly). They were further instructed to shoulder the political responsibility of "promoting development of one area, ensuring peace of one area," and to strictly follow the "three managements, three musts" (三管三必须) principle, under which those who oversee an industry, a business line, or a production operation must equally oversee its safety.

## Sector-by-Sector Directives

The interview laid out targeted demands across several high-risk sectors:

- **Fireworks and firecrackers:** step up the campaign against illegal production and violations.
- **Mining:** hard implementation of the "eight hard measures" for mine safety.
- **Hazardous chemicals:** accelerate prevention and control of major hazard sources.
- **Industry and trade:** strengthen law-enforcement inspections and standardize the management of outsourced and subcontracted teams.
- **Public safety:** rigorously investigate fire risks in crowded venues and large mixed-use complexes, tighten road-traffic enforcement, and reinforce culture and tourism precautions.

Beyond sectoral measures, the interview stressed improving the "penetration" of regulatory enforcement, pursuing unresolved major hazards relentlessly, and holding enterprise principal responsible persons, actual controllers, and ultimate beneficiaries to account. It also called for stronger links between administrative and criminal enforcement, and between administrative and disciplinary enforcement — a mechanism through which negligence can trigger Party discipline.

## Why the Timing Matters

The interviews land at a sensitive moment. 2026 is the final year of the State Council Work Safety Committee's *Three-Year Action Plan for Tackling Root Causes and Strengthening the Foundations of Work Safety (2024–2026)*, a campaign promoted at a [national video conference in February](https://www.gov.cn/yaowen/liebiao/202602/content_7059759.htm) chaired by Vice Premier Zhang Guoqing and State Councilor Wang Xiaohong. That action plan's conclusion, combined with the approach of winter — a high-risk season for mining, fireworks, and fire hazards — gives the year-end push unusual urgency.

The readout captured that pressure directly: "Around the year-end, [we] must keep the string of workplace safety taut at all times, go all out to check risks, eliminate hidden dangers, and prevent accidents, and earnestly safeguard the safety of people's lives and property and social stability."

## What to Watch

The formal interview (约谈) is not a legal sanction in itself but a political accountability tool: it triggers reporting obligations and places local leaders' evaluations at risk. The research material did not identify the specific accidents behind each city's summons, nor the casualty figures, leaving open questions about the exact triggers. What observers will now watch is follow-through — whether the 11 governments translate these commitments into inspections, prosecutions, and, ultimately, fewer accidents in the final weeks of 2026.
