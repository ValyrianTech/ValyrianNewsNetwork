# 16th-Century Mass Grave Found on Antwerp's Eilandje

Archaeologists working on a construction site in Antwerp's Eilandje district have uncovered an exceptional mass grave dating from the 16th century, containing the remains of an estimated 8 to 10 adult men stacked in a single pit roughly four metres below ground, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/13/archeologen-antwerpen-eilandje-massagraf-16e-eeuw-spaanse-omwall/). The discovery, made near the remnants of the city's Spanish ramparts (*Spaanse omwalling*), offers a rare window into Antwerp's turbulent past.

The excavation is taking place on the site of the future *Friendship Residence*, on the Rijnkaai, where the iconic 'Chiquita block' once stood. The find was first reported by [Gazet van Antwerpen](https://www.gva.be/regio/antwerpen/regio-antwerpen/antwerpen/uitzonderlijk-massagraf-ontdekt-op-eilandje-een-archeologisch-unicum-in-antwerpen/164470604.html) on 12 November.

## A Find Nobody Saw Coming

Archaeologists had been on site since earlier in 2026 searching for the remains of the 16th-century Spanish ramparts, parts of which surfaced during ground works in August. The mass grave came as a surprise even to the city's own archaeologists.

"You can see that multiple individuals have been thrown onto a heap here," said archaeologist Maarten Praet of Indar Archeologie. "These are not careful burials, because you see the feet of one skeleton lying on the head of another."

The location is what makes the discovery stand out. Three other mass graves previously found in Antwerp all lay beside an abbey or a church. "Here we are really on the edge of the city, a bit in no-man's-land," Praet said. "That's what makes this find so special."

Alongside the skeletons, the team recovered a knife fragment, pottery shards including some with 16th-century decoration, cannonballs and decorated earthenware.

## Two Competing Explanations

The cause of death is not yet known, and archaeologists are investigating two main hypotheses that may not be mutually exclusive.

The first is an epidemic. Archival research by the City of Antwerp has revealed that several plague houses (*pesthuizen*) once stood near the ramparts. "It could be that the bodies were dumped here because they were infected, but we must also investigate that in the lab," Praet said.

The second is conflict. The period of the Spanish ramparts saw major violence in Antwerp, including the [Spanish Fury of 1576](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/450-jaar-spaanse-furie-antwerpen/), when mutinous Spanish troops sacked the city and left at least 6,000 dead on the Antwerp side, and the Siege of Antwerp. The knife fragment and cannonballs found nearby could point to violence.

"Mass graves are often specifically related to past events, such as conflicts and diseases," Praet said. "Both hypotheses are possible and are not mutually exclusive."

## What Happens Next

After excavation, the skeletons will be taken to a laboratory where specialists will examine how the individuals died, their age and their sex. Physical anthropologist Anneleen Mels said the remains belong to adult men from the time of the Spanish ramparts, meaning the 16th to 17th century, though lab work has yet to begin.

The exact number of skeletons also remains uncertain. Archaeologists caution that skulls must still be matched to limbs, and skeletons were being added daily at the time of reporting.

## Context and Implications

The Spanish ramparts were built between 1542 and 1553 on the orders of Emperor Charles V to defend a rapidly growing city, and were among the most modern fortifications of their era. Remnants survive in several places across Antwerp today.

The discovery adds a human dimension to a well-documented period of Antwerp's history. If the lab work confirms disease, the grave would provide physical evidence of the plague's reach near the city's edge. If it points to battle injuries, it could offer new insight into the violence that shaped the city's decline in the late 16th century.

The dig is expected to continue until roughly the end of November, and archaeologists also hope to expose part of the old city moat. The archaeological work does not halt development: six new buildings with more than 200 housing units, an underground car park, a green courtyard and office and hospitality space are due by around 2028.

For now, the question of who these men were, and why they were buried in such haste, rests with the laboratory.
