# Euroclear Chief: No Miracle Solution on Russian Assets

The head of Euroclear, the Brussels-based clearing house that holds the bulk of the European Union's immobilized Russian central bank assets, has said there is still no workable formula for unlocking the money — warning that any transfer scheme would leave core risks unresolved. In an interview with [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/782489/article/2026-11-13/la-patronne-deuroclear-au-soir-sur-les-actifs-russes-il-ny-toujours-pas-de) published on 13 November, CEO Valérie Urbain said that "on the Russian assets, there is still no miracle solution."

Her remarks land at a delicate moment, more than a year after EU leaders failed to agree on using the frozen funds and as Kyiv's financing needs push the issue back onto the European agenda.

## The stake: €185 billion on one balance sheet

Euroclear is one of the world's largest financial market infrastructures, processing each month an amount slightly larger than total global GDP. Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, it has held roughly €185 billion in Russian securities on its books, according to the interview. Broader EU estimates of frozen Russian central bank assets put the total around €210 billion, most of it at Euroclear.

For Belgium, the exposure is extraordinary. Urbain has said the immobilized assets represent between 10% and 15% of Belgian GDP — a concentration that makes the country uniquely vulnerable to any legal or financial shock tied to the money.

## Why Urbain says transfer does not solve the problem

Urbain's central argument is that moving the assets — whether to the EU directly or into a new vehicle — would not make the underlying dangers disappear. As Le Soir summarized her position, a transfer would eliminate "neither the risk of bankruptcy for Euroclear, a key link in global finance, nor the risk of damaging the reputation of the eurozone, nor the risk of shaking Belgium and its taxpayers."

The bankruptcy concern is not rhetorical. Euroclear still holds about €17 billion in assets for customers inside Russia, which Moscow could move to confiscate in retaliation. Russian courts have already ruled against the depository: in May 2026 a Moscow arbitration court ordered Euroclear to pay roughly €217 billion over the asset freeze, and in late October Russia's courts rejected the company's cassation appeal. Euroclear's leadership was placed under bodyguard protection in 2025 after Russian threats.

Urbain has also warned of a wider reputational cost. A visible confiscation, she argues, would signal that euro-denominated assets can become a tool of political pressure rather than a neutral safe haven — a concern the European Central Bank has echoed, given the euro's role in holding roughly a fifth of global foreign exchange reserves.

## Brussels isolated by geography

Because the assets sit in Belgium, the legal and financial fallout lands on one institution and, by extension, one country. Urbain has described Belgium as "totally isolated" in the debate, noting that "by focusing only on Euroclear, we focus on Belgium."

That dynamic has shaped the politics. Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever resisted the European Commission's "reparations loan" proposal — a plan championed by Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz to use the assets, or the cash they generate, to fund a zero-interest loan to Ukraine repayable only if Russia paid war reparations. At a December 2025 summit, De Wever likened the collapsed talks to a sinking ship: "It was like the Titanic, and at the end there was nothing left." At Davos in January 2026 he called confiscation an "act of war" that "has never taken place in history."

When EU leaders failed to agree in December 2025, they fell back on a €90 billion loan guaranteed by the EU budget, with the first disbursement reaching Ukraine in June 2026. Hungary, Slovakia and Czechia secured an opt-out from contributing.

## The pressure returns

That fallback has proved insufficient. By late summer 2026, Kyiv was signalling a financing gap of roughly €23 billion for the year, and four member states — Sweden, the Netherlands, Spain and Poland — pushed to revisit the Belgian veto ahead of the October 2026 European Council, where the dossier was formally back on the table. The Institut des Sciences Stratégiques noted in September that Germany's offer to guarantee about a quarter of the financial risk, alongside a Ukrainian proposal to move custody to an EU-owned depository, were the leading compromise routes.

Urbain's latest intervention suggests those routes have not closed the gap. She has consistently argued that the assets' interest income — around €5 billion over two years, which has already been mobilized for Ukraine — should not be confused with the principal. Euroclear's board, she has said, decided the company should not "enrich itself" from the tragedy, but she draws a line at using the underlying assets as collateral.

## "Integrate them into peace negotiations"

Rather than confiscation or a loan structure, Urbain's preferred outcome is diplomatic. "The best use of the Russian assets is to integrate them into peace negotiations," she told Le Soir. She has also stressed the eventual obligation to return the principal: "We must one day return the money to the Russian central bank. When a central bank invests abroad, it's the money of the people… there is also a side of national pride."

She has said she is prepared to fight any adverse decision in court. "I really hope we don't get there," she told RTBF. "But we'll have to use every possible means to keep protecting Euroclear." Despite the deadlock, she added: "I remain convinced that we will find a solution."

## What to watch

The impasse now turns on three questions. First, whether the EU can design a structure — most likely the EU-owned depository idea — that shifts legal exposure from Belgium to the bloc as a whole. Second, whether Russia can enforce its €217 billion judgment in third countries, and how EU and Belgian courts would block it. Third, whether the euro's standing as a reserve currency can absorb the precedent of tapping a sovereign's immobilized reserves.

For now, the message from the institution at the centre of the storm is a caution against expecting a clean fix. With €185 billion still sitting on Euroclear's balance sheet and Russia's legal campaign advancing, the search for a miracle solution continues — and the clock, as Kyiv's financing gap widens, keeps ticking.
