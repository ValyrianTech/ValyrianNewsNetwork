# China Puts 18 Major Food-Safety Crimes Under Supervision

China's three highest law-enforcement and judicial bodies have placed 18 major food-safety criminal cases under centralized "supervised handling," including the formaldehyde-treated cabbage and sulfur-fumigated bamboo shoot scandals that dominated headlines in 2026, according to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34459136), citing the Central Political and Legal Affairs Commission.

The joint designation by the Ministry of Public Security, the Supreme People's Procuratorate and the Supreme People's Court, announced on November 12, represents a high-pressure nationwide crackdown on crimes that have shaken public confidence in the safety of everyday food. The list explicitly names the "formaldehyde cabbage" (甲醛白菜) case, the "fake imported health supplements" (假洋保健品) case and the "sulfur-fumigated bamboo shoots" (硫磺竹笋) case.

"Law-enforcement organs will maintain a high-pressure crackdown on food-safety crimes, resolve prominent problems through strict enforcement, and firmly build the food-safety defense line," an official with the commission said, vowing to safeguard "safety on the tip of the tongue."

## A Year of Viral Scandals

The supervised-handling designation follows a remarkable run of blogger-driven exposes that bypassed formal inspection channels and alarmed consumers across the country.

In August 2026, a video blogger revealed that some purchasers in Tunken Town, Kangbao County in Hebei province, were dipping cabbages in formaldehyde solution before transport to preserve freshness. The county government confirmed the footage was accurate, and on August 29 police criminally detained three suspects, [Jiemian News](https://m.jiemian.com/article/15326402.html) reported. The State Council Food Safety Office, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs and the market regulator intervened, launching nationwide special sampling of perishable vegetables.

The fake-imported-supplement case proved equally brazen. Products such as "Australia Youcare," marketed as imported eye-health supplements and top sellers on multiple platforms, turned out to be domestically made, with an overseas address that was actually an auto repair shop. They were "laundered" through bonded warehouses to pose as imports. A separate Ningbo case broke up a cross-border operation in which "Australian" products were manufactured by a Guangzhou factory without health-food qualifications.

In September, another expose targeted bamboo shoots fumigated with sulfur at processing sites in Qingyuan, Guangdong, yielding excessive sulfur dioxide. Qingyuan is a major bamboo-shoot producing region — its 860,000 mu (roughly 57,000 hectares) of plantations supply more than 90 percent of China's national output — and regulators sealed the sites and traced product flows, [Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20260919-9702497) reported. On October 19, People's Daily reported similar sulfur-fumigation practices in Mabian, Gulin and Xuyong counties in Sichuan, where farmers and traders did not conceal the practice.

## Signs of a Systemic Problem

The crackdown did not emerge in a vacuum. On September 24, Chen Wenqing, a Politburo member and secretary of the Central Political and Legal Affairs Commission, chaired a special meeting stressing strict punishment of food-safety crimes. According to Jiemian, Chen acknowledged that while the overall situation was "steadily improving," events that undermine the food-safety bottom line "still occur from time to time."

On October 12, the commission issued formal "Opinions on Strictly Investigating and Punishing Food-Safety Violations and Crimes According to Law." The document identified three troubling trends: food-safety crimes are increasingly networked, professionalized and chain-based; the linkage between administrative and criminal enforcement remains insufficient; and certain sectors remain high-incidence, with cases such as "formaldehyde cabbage," "dichlorvos disinfection" and "chemical-treated bayberries" cited as recent shocks to the system.

Chen called for focus on key areas — meat products, campus food, health foods, vegetables and fruits — and key links, from production and processing to online sales, terminal catering and food imports. He also urged an accelerated comprehensive revision of the Food Safety Law to clarify rules for new business forms such as fresh-food e-commerce, food delivery and livestream selling.

## The Regulator's Blind Spot

Perhaps the most pointed critique came from China's own official media. Commentary on CNR and coverage in Lianhe Zaobao questioned why bloggers and journalists consistently detected abuses that frontline regulators missed — a recurring theme that struck at the heart of the enforcement apparatus.

"From cabbage dipped in formaldehyde to sulfur-fumigated bamboo shoots, the same scale of production, the same 'industrial chain' operation, exposed by the same blogger," read a commentary cited by Zaobao. "Why can the media find it and bloggers film it, while frontline regulators are always 'half a beat slow'?"

Zaobao noted that on the afternoon before one expose, local environmental authorities had already inspected a fumigation site in Qingyuan's Yuantan Town and declared it "under control" — yet the sites were reportedly still operating the next day. The episode, the report suggested, exposed a serious failure stretching from routine supervision to remedial governance.

## What to Watch

The "supervised handling" (挂牌督办) mechanism is a distinctive Chinese governance tool: higher-level authorities publicly designate a case as a priority, set deadlines and directly supervise local handling — a structure designed partly to overcome local protectionism and enforcement inertia. Its deployment for food safety signals the political salience of the issue.

Several questions remain open. Only three of the 18 cases have been publicly named; the remaining 15 have not been disclosed, leaving the scope and specific targets of the campaign unclear. No sentencing or conviction data has yet emerged, since supervised handling is a process designation rather than a final verdict.

Nor is it clear whether the punitive campaign will address what official media have identified as a structural weakness: detection that depends on citizen journalists rather than routine regulatory inspection. The October Opinions acknowledge the administrative-criminal enforcement gap but may not fully resolve it.

For now, the message from Beijing is unmistakable. As the commission put it, for "lawbreakers driven by greed who challenge the bottom line of morality and conscience," authorities intend to "apply strong medicine and heavy fists — investigate and punish every case discovered." Whether that resolve translates into durable consumer trust — and safer food on the table — is the test that lies ahead.
