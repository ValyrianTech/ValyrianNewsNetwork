# Kratom and 7-OH: Relief and Risk as DEA Weighs a Crackdown

For Tyler, kratom was supposed to be the way out. The leaf powder helped him step away from opioid addiction in his early 20s and stay clean for a decade. Then, at a gas station in early 2025, he spotted a new product called 7-OH, advertised as a kratom product and sold as a tablet. Within minutes it delivered a euphoria he compared to opioids — what he described to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/13/nx-s1-6039203/kratom-7-oh-dea-addiction-opioid) as "a kind of a miracle."

Nearly a year and a half later, the 38-year-old was sedated in a Michigan rapid-detox program, sleeping through the withdrawals he could not otherwise escape. "I did not know at all what I was in for," he said. "It was much more potent than I had imagined."

Tyler's story captures the central tension in one of the fastest-moving drug-policy fights in the United States. Kratom — a botanical from a Southeast Asian tree in the coffee family — and its concentrated, semi-synthetic derivative 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) are promoted as life-changing remedies for pain, mood, and opioid recovery. Critics and researchers warn that the strongest of these products act like opioids, carry serious dependence and withdrawal risks, and are sold with inconsistent labeling at gas stations, smoke shops, and online. As early as Friday, Nov. 13, 2026, the federal government could move to make some of them illegal.

## A regulatory line in the sand

The path to a potential crackdown has been anything but straightforward. For the past decade, federal efforts to restrict kratom and 7-OH were repeatedly beaten back by public opposition and advocacy groups, notably the American Kratom Association (AKA), keeping the substances federally legal.

That began to shift in 2026. In August, the Drug Enforcement Administration restricted three semi-synthetic opioids derived from 7-OH and related compounds — MGM-15, MGM-16, and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MGPI) — placing them in Schedule I alongside heroin and LSD. The [Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/department-justice-announces-updates-emergency-scheduling-actions-7-oh-and-related-opioid) confirmed the Aug. 26, 2026 controls, which remain in effect.

The move triggered backlash from kratom advocates who feared the rule would sweep up natural products containing only "trace amounts" of MGPI. The AKA filed a [federal lawsuit](https://www.americankratom.org/news/federal-lawsuit-to-resolve-doj-dea-mgpi-enforcement-discretion-gap-filed) against the DEA on Aug. 26, asking the agency to ensure that natural botanical kratom with incidental traces of MGPI would remain legal.

The Department of Health and Human Services then stepped in with a new [recommendation](https://www.hhs.gov/press-room/hhs-recommends-thresholds-enhanced-kratom-products-7-oh-mgpi.html) on Oct. 9, 2026, and the DEA changed course. Rather than targeting all 7-OH products, regulators now plan to restrict a narrower set — those containing higher concentrations of 7-OH and/or MGPI.

HHS recommended two thresholds. A product would fall under Schedule I for purposes of temporary scheduling when 7-OH or MGPI exceeds 1 milligram per gram in solid products (or 1 milligram per milliliter in liquids), or when the ratio of mitragynine to 7-OH or MGPI drops below 100 to 1. The dual standard is designed to separate natural botanical kratom, which contains only trace amounts of these compounds, from products intentionally enhanced or concentrated to deliver opioid-like doses. The new 30-day Notice of Intent period began Oct. 14, meaning a new temporary scheduling order could take effect on or after Nov. 13.

"We are drawing a clear line between natural kratom and dangerous, enhanced opioid products," HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said in a statement. "We listened to the public, followed the science." Attorney General Todd Blanche put it more bluntly: "Dangerous and addictive opioids do not belong behind convenience store counters."

## The research: potent, and risky

The science behind the concern is stark. FDA's review found that 7-OH is substantially more potent than morphine in preclinical studies — roughly two to nine times more potent, depending on the study — and identified a meaningful risk of opioid-like physical dependence and withdrawal from MGPI.

Poison-control data paint a picture of rapid, recent escalation. A [CDC report](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7511a1.htm) analyzing data from 53 U.S. poison control centers found that kratom-related calls rose about 1,200% between 2015 and 2025. The analysis documented 233 kratom-associated deaths over that period, 79% of which involved multiple substances — opioids were present in 62% of fatalities. Yet 62% of call reports involved kratom without other drugs, and the report does not distinguish between traditional leaf kratom and concentrated products like 7-OH.

"I certainly have a growing list of patients in addiction clinics who are addicted to this, and in withdrawal, that we're having to manage," said Chris Holstege, a physician and professor at the University of Virginia's School of Medicine who co-authored the report. He and his colleagues were "a bit stunned" by the 1,200% increase.

David Epstein, a senior investigator at the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), described the addiction pattern that has become familiar in his ongoing research. For some people, 7-OH use "seemed to go nicely for the first couple of days, but then they found that they had to keep using to avoid withdrawal," he said. Some "couldn't make it through the night. They had to get up in the middle of the night to take more."

That trajectory is what Brian, 41, lived. He had quit opioids in 2016 but found kratom at a gas station in 2022 — sold "literally right next to the register, nothing taboo about it," he said. When a smoke-shop clerk later handed him a sample of 7-OH tablets, "it felt like I had taken an Oxycontin," Brian recalled. "It was game over." At his peak he was using 1,200 milligrams of 7-OH a day, spending roughly $150 daily, before quitting cold turkey in April 2026. The withdrawal, he said, "was a nightmare."

## For some, a lifeline

The picture is not one-sided. Kratom and 7-OH have genuine defenders who say the products kept them away from street opioids or helped them manage chronic pain. Some point to the fact that U.S. opioid deaths are falling — from more than 79,000 in 2023 to about 54,000 in 2024, according to a [KFF analysis](https://www.kff.org/mental-health/opioid-overdose-deaths-national-trends-and-variation-by-demographics-and-states/) of federal data.

Angela Wilson, 44, of Delaware, said 7-OH has helped her manage excruciating chronic pain from endometriosis and long-standing depression. She takes 32 to 40 milligrams a day, split into three doses, and said kratom powders made her anxious and shaky while 7-OH does not. "It's been truly life-changing for my family situation, my personal life," she said. Uninsured, she fears what losing access would mean. "We're not really sure, you know, what I'm gonna do when it's not available."

Even NIDA's website acknowledges the open questions, noting that "early studies suggest kratom and kratom compounds warrant further study as experimental treatments for substance use disorders, specifically opioid use disorder."

## An uncertain future

The core policy challenge is separating a botanical that regulators now say they do not intend to ban from products that deliver dangerous opioid-like doses of the same plant's most potent compounds. HHS's concentration-and-ratio approach is an attempt to draw that line scientifically, and it has narrowed — though not resolved — the fight.

The AKA's lawsuit argues that "enforcement discretion is not enough" and that a press release cannot protect a consumer from arrest or a retailer from seizure of inventory. The group says it supports scheduling of manufactured, concentrated products but wants explicit protection for natural leaf kratom.

As the deadline passes, the questions that remain are practical. Will the new thresholds hold up in court? How quickly will concentrated 7-OH products disappear from shelves — and what happens to the people who rely on them? And will regulators succeed in drawing a workable line between a traditional botanical and the potent derivative that, for users like Tyler, blurred the boundary between relief and ruin.

For his part, Tyler has not touched kratom or 7-OH since his detox in March. He doubts he could have stopped without the program. "There's no chance," he said. "It's just too addictive and too accessible."
