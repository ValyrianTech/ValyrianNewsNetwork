# Glatigny's Decree Ends the Common Core in Wallonia-Brussels

A draft decree by Valérie Glatigny, the Wallonia-Brussels minister of education, formally buries the most emblematic principle of the region's flagship education reform: a single, identical timetable for every pupil up to the age of 15. According to a [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/782490/article/2026-11-13/que-prevoit-le-decret-de-valerie-glatigny-pour-la-fin-du-tronc-commun) analysis of the text now submitted for consultation, the so-called *tronc commun* will give way to differentiated schedules built around "orientation" activities from the 2027-2028 and 2028-2029 school years.

## A reform unveiled in stages

The shift had been signalled weeks earlier. Presenting the plan on 13 October, Glatigny (MR) told education stakeholders that "at the end of the third year of secondary, technically there will no longer be a common core," as [RTL Info](https://www.rtl.be/actu/belgique/societe/activites-orientantes-fin-du-tronc-commun-et-du-ce1d-valerie-glatigny-annonce/2026-10-13/article/803210) reported. The Wallonia-Brussels government, a coalition of the liberal MR and the centrist Les Engagés, then approved the draft decree on 23 October.

The tronc commun was the centrepiece of the *Pacte pour un enseignement d'excellence*, a broad reform launched under the previous Francophone Socialist-led legislature. Its ambition was to delay early streaming into vocational streams ("relégation") by giving every pupil the same curriculum until the end of the third year of secondary school.

## What the decree provides

The text reorganises the first three years of secondary education. From 2027-2028 for the second year, and 2028-2029 for the third, pupils' timetables will no longer be identical. The core innovation is the introduction of orientation activities in the third year: six periods a week in two blocks of three, chosen by each pupil from among eight domains.

Those domains, as detailed by [RTL Info](https://www.rtl.be/actu/belgique/politique/une-importante-reforme-de-lenseignement-secondaire-approuvee-voici-les/2026-10-23/article/804363), span modern languages and communication, literature and the arts, culture and crafts, health and care techniques, mathematics and applied sciences, technology, environment and Earth sciences, and economic and social project management. Each school must offer at least four of the eight, combining theory, practice and the option to change choices during the year. Crucially, the choices carry no binding effect: a pupil will not be forced to pursue the domain explored in the third year.

Career discovery becomes a structural element. Second-year pupils will spend two days on discovery activities, while all third-year pupils will complete an observation internship in a professional setting. A second modern language will become compulsory from the second year, two periods a week, rather than the third; pupils already learning Dutch may start English, and vice versa.

The decree also overhauls assessment and progression. The CE1D certificate, taken at the end of the second year, is scrapped and replaced by a *Certificat d'enseignement secondaire inférieur* (CESI) to be earned at the end of the third year; the final CE1D session will be held in June 2027. Automatic passage from the first to the second year is abolished, with grade repetition again possible in the first three years at teachers' discretion.

## Jobs and contested consultation

The government frames the reform as protecting employment. Ministers argue that the original Pact trajectory threatened some 1,400 full-time equivalent posts and could have affected around 5,000 teachers, particularly in qualifying education. Instead, affected staff may take charge of the orientation activities, teach in upper secondary, supervise internships or organise business partnerships. Posts are to shrink gradually through a "cadre d'extinction" as staff retire naturally, rather than through outright redundancies. A further 14 million euros by 2029 will extend personalised support to the second and third years, allowing two teachers in some classes.

The reform has drawn union criticism, part of a broader conflict over education austerity. As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/2-heures-de-plus-limitation-des-dppr-tronc-commun-et-nominations-les-raisons-de-la-colere-des-enseignants-ce-lundi-10-novembre-11626291) reported during a national teachers' strike on 10 November 2025, unions said the change "calls into question the goal of the common core: offering the same education and the same possibilities to all students up to age 15."

At the October rollout, union figures voiced scepticism about timing. "We could be reassured by this, but we now have to see how it will be done in operationalisation," said Francis Penning of CSC-Enseignement, while SETCa-SEL's Adrien Rosman warned that stakeholder remarks "come relatively late" and doubted the government had time to incorporate them.

## Analysis: a finalisation or a dismantling?

The political framing is revealing. Minister-President Elisabeth Degryse (Les Engagés) insists the reform "finalises the deployment of the common core," while Glatigny conspicuously avoids the phrase. Critics see an abandonment of the equity principle; the government insists the anti-streaming philosophy is preserved through informed orientation. The tension is the central contested question: whether structured discovery reduces early tracking or, by differentiating schedules earlier, accelerates it.

Glatigny's comments suggest the intent is to help pupils "make their decision in full knowledge of the facts." Yet the practical risks are real. Small schools may struggle to offer four of eight domains. The return of grade repetition and the new CESI could reshape failure and streaming rates. And the reform lands amid a budget squeeze and disputes over teachers' working hours and job status.

## What's next

The draft is in *concertation* with coalition partner Les Engagés and social partners, and further fine-tuning of the activity domains is expected before the second-year rollout. Unions have signalled they will watch implementation closely. For pupils and families, the message is that the uniform lower-secondary school is ending—and the timetable is about to diverge.
