# Wolf Attack Kills 35 Sheep at Brecht Military Domain

A single wolf attack at the Groot Schietveld military domain and shooting range in Brecht, near Antwerp, has left 35 sheep dead, 17 injured and 3 missing, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/13/wolven-bijten-35-schapen-dood-op-militair-domein-groot-schietvel/). The incident—described as among the deadliest wolf attacks on livestock in Flanders—occurred on 4 October but only became public on 13 November, when Gazet van Antwerpen reported it and the news was confirmed to VRT NWS.

The herd of 450 sheep grazes the domain on behalf of the Ministry of Defence and the Agency for Nature and Forests (ANB), primarily to control purple moor grass after a 2021 heathland fire. The attack stands out not only for its scale but for what it reveals about the shifting geography of wolf activity in northern Belgium and the policy gaps that leave herders exposed.

## 'The bite is unmistakable'

Herder Bert Plasmans discovered the animals himself while checking the fencing. "I found the sheep myself when I went to check the net where they stand, which we do regularly," he told VRT NWS. "It was immediately clear to me that it had been a wolf attack. We have had smaller wolf attacks at that spot before, and the bite is unmistakable."

The scale of the loss left him shaken. "This is something you absolutely do not want to encounter, any farmer who works with animals will tell you that," he said. Many of the herd were pregnant ewes, and Plasmans stressed the emotional and genetic toll: "For us these are not just numbers. These are animals you work with, you experience births with them, so this does affect you." He noted the animals were rare Belgian breeds, meaning a considerable loss of genetics as well.

DNA testing by the Institute for Nature and Forest Research (INBO) confirmed wolf involvement, though the samples were of too low quality to identify the individual animals. The resident wolf pair Ben and Emma, active in the Noorderkempen region, is the prime suspect.

## How the wolves got in

A flexible, wolf-deterring electric net—known as a flexnet—surrounded the herd, but the wolves still found a way inside. Investigators discovered digging and scratch tracks under the netting in several places, indicating the animals burrowed beneath the fencing on uneven terrain.

"When wolves get inside an enclosure with that many sheep, they cause a great many casualties," said Koen Van Muylem of INBO. "When there are very many animals, the wolves think: I can take a lot of them here too." He added that wolves do not eat every sheep at once: "Wolves also lay up a kind of reserve for later," a behaviour known as surplus killing. The wolves ate from only one sheep, and several of the injured animals later died of infections.

ANB stresses that the failure does not prove wolf-proof fencing is ineffective—rather, the uneven terrain at Groot Schietveld made the enclosure easier to defeat.

## A shift from Limburg to Antwerp

The attack fits a broader pattern. Wolf attacks in Flanders appear to be shifting from Limburg toward Antwerp province. "Limburg has lived with the wolf for a number of years longer," Van Muylem said. "In the beginning there were also many damage cases, but there people have learned that those wolf-deterring fences really do work." With a properly placed and maintained fence, he said, wolves fail to get in 99 percent of the time.

In Antwerp province, far fewer pastures are fenced. "As a result the wolves have free rein and proportionally, and in absolute terms, far more victims fall in Antwerp than in Limburg," Van Muylem said. This is the sixth attack in Brecht this year, though previous ones each killed just one sheep. Elsewhere in 2026, four attacks in Kalmthout in August killed nine sheep, while cattle were attacked in Merksplas and Rijkevorsel.

## The guard dog dispute

Plasmans and Jan Loos of Welkom Wolf are calling for the deployment of livestock guarding dogs. "Those dogs stay permanently with the sheep, but the Flemish government currently forbids deploying them," Loos said. Flemish law does not permit dogs to run loose day and night in nature reserves, leaving the measure without a legal basis.

Jeroen Denaeghel of ANB acknowledged the emotional weight of the case—"for them these are more than just sheep"—and confirmed the agency is examining the issue. "The law currently says dogs may not run loose in our nature areas day and night. We are looking at that option; there is a pilot project running in Limburg... so we are working on creating a legal framework," he said. ANB has also raised an animal welfare concern, arguing dogs should not be left unprotected around the clock.

## What to watch

The Brecht attack is likely to intensify pressure on Flanders to formalize a legal framework for guarding dogs and to expand wolf-proof fencing support in Antwerp province. The disclosure coincides with ANB's publication of its updated "De wolf in Vlaanderen" damage overview, which will shape how the region balances wildlife protection against the livelihoods of herders. With several wolves circulating in the Noorderkempen—including a pair just across the Dutch border in Chaam and a wolf recently killed by a car on the E34 near Zoersel—the question is less whether attacks will recur than how quickly policy can adapt.
