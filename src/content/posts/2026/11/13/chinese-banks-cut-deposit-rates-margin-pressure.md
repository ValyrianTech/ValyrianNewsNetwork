# Chinese Banks Cut Deposit Rates Again as Margin Pressure Persists

Several Chinese banks have lowered their deposit interest rates once more, extending a multiyear easing cycle as lenders continue to grapple with pressure on their net interest margins (NIM). The move underscores a persistent tension in China's banking system: even as funding costs fall, the spread between what banks pay depositors and what they earn on loans remains pinned near historic lows.

## A margin squeeze at record lows

The numbers behind the latest round of cuts are stark. According to data from the National Financial Regulatory Administration, the commercial banking sector's net interest margin slid to a historic low of 1.4% in the first quarter of 2026, [21st Century Business Herald / China Net reported](https://www.21jingji.com/article/20260811/herald/1466880aa9861e7f8b6117f132d1b369.html). The squeeze is unevenly distributed. Large state-owned banks carried the thinnest margins at 1.29%, followed by city commercial banks at 1.38% and joint-stock banks at 1.54%. Rural commercial banks stood at 1.58%, while private banks—serving higher-risk, higher-yield borrowers—enjoyed the widest spread at 3.62%.

The big banks' exposure explains the pattern of the current adjustment. China's largest lenders have led successive rounds of deposit-rate reductions, most recently on July 25, 2026, when the big six state-owned banks kicked off the year's first coordinated cut. Smaller banks historically followed in sequence, but that synchronized model has begun to fracture.

## From lockstep to fragmentation

The defining feature of the 2026 deposit market is divergence. Rather than marching in unison, banks are now tailoring their funding strategies to their own balance sheets—and in some cases moving in opposite directions.

"The root cause lies in each institution's differing liability base, asset-deployment capacity, and risk-constraint ability, with operating pressure showing tiered characteristics," said Wu Zewei, a special researcher at Sushang Bank, in the same report. Some county-level rural banks have actually raised deposit rates to defend their local customer bases, while others have pushed long-term rates below shorter tenors.

That latter tactic—a so-called "term inversion," in which five-year deposits pay less than three-year or even one-year products—is a deliberate tool to discourage costly long-term liabilities and shorten banks' funding duration. Some institutions have paired it with a renewed appetite for long-term cash: several of the largest banks have relaunched five-year large-denomination certificates of deposit at annualized rates of up to 1.6%, signaling they still want to lock in selected long-term funding even as they trim headline rates.

"Deposit rates do not have the basis for a uniform, across-the-board cut," Wu said, predicting that tiered pricing and strategic divergence "may become the norm under interest-rate marketization."

## Where the money is going

The deposit-rate story is also a story about changing household and corporate behavior. In its third-quarter 2026 monetary policy report, released November 11, the People's Bank of China dissected a structural shift in how money is held, [Yicai reported](https://www.yicai.com/news/103437090.html).

Deposits are "term-izing": household demand deposits have fallen to 25.4% of the total, down from 35.3% at the end of 2020, while corporate demand deposits have dropped to 27.9% from 38.4% over the same period. At the same time, non-bank financial institution deposits have surged, growing 19.1% year-on-year to reach 11.2% of all deposits—up from 9.0% at the end of 2023. The central bank attributed the shift partly to households and firms channeling savings into wealth-management products, bonds, and equities, and partly to a deeper structural move toward direct financing.

In other words, some of the money that once sat in low-yielding demand deposits has left the traditional banking system entirely—or returned to it in a different form. That financial disintermediation makes it harder for banks to rely on cheap, stable funding, reinforcing the pressure to manage liabilities more carefully.

## Why the pain may not ease quickly

If cutting deposit rates were a simple fix, margins would already have recovered. The complication is on the asset side: loan rates, tethered to the Loan Prime Rate and the repricing of mortgages and past loans, have fallen sharply too. As a result, the benefit of cheaper deposits is partly offset by thinner lending income.

The margin relief that does come will arrive gradually. Wang Qing, chief macro analyst at Oriental Jincheng, noted that a large volume of medium- and long-term deposits is due to mature and reprice in 2026, which should help lower banks' interest costs and stabilize margins, [Xinhua reported](https://www.news.cn/money/20260408/dd4ef2260b7a448eb3a20541e3c008d0/c.html). Analysts at Open Source Securities have estimated that roughly 47 trillion to 54 trillion yuan (about $6.5 trillion to $7.5 trillion) of household term deposits mature this year, with a large share held at older, higher rates—repricing that could add roughly 10 basis points to listed banks' margins.

The industry has already seen tentative signs of stabilization. Commercial banking NIM held steady at 1.42% for three consecutive quarters through late 2025, ending a long one-way decline before slipping to 1.4% in early 2026. Whether that floor holds will depend on how quickly high-rate deposits reprice and whether credit demand recovers enough to lift loan yields.

## What to watch

The next signals will come from the big banks. A fresh coordinated round of deposit cuts—or an adjustment to the Loan Prime Rate—would indicate how aggressively policymakers want to defend bank profitability. The central bank's stance matters too: further cuts to reserve requirements or benchmark rates could ease funding costs, even as they underline the growth pressures that prompted the easing in the first place.

For now, the message from China's lenders is one of caution rather than recovery. Deposit rates are falling, but the margin squeeze that drove them down has not yet let up—and the industry's once-uniform approach to pricing has given way to a more fragmented, market-driven contest for funding.
