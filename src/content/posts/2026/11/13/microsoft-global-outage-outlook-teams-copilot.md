# Global Microsoft Outage Hits Outlook, Teams and Copilot

Microsoft is working to restore its cloud services after a worldwide outage knocked out Outlook, Teams, Copilot and much of the Microsoft 365 suite, disrupting businesses, hospitals and government offices across dozens of countries. The company said on Friday it was seeing "early signs of stabilisation" but that service capacity had not returned to normal, and that there was no indication the disruption was caused by a cyberattack.

The incident marks one of the most disruptive technology failures of the year and renewed questions about the world's dependence on a handful of cloud providers.

## Context: a fault that spanned the globe

The problems surfaced during the US workday on Thursday and spread outward with the sun. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/13/grote-storing-microsoft-outlook-en-teams-bedrijven-overheden/), outage-tracking site Downdetector recorded more than 130,000 reports of Outlook problems around 1pm US local time (7pm CET) alone. What began in the Americas soon reached Australia, New Zealand, Japan, India and other Asia-Pacific countries before arriving in Europe and the UK.

The [ABC's live coverage](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-13/microsoft-outage-affects-users-worldwide/107381050) reported that Outlook problem reports peaked globally at nearly 140,000 around 4am Australian eastern time. Reports of trouble spread across 74 countries at the outset, affecting not just email but Word, Excel, PowerPoint, SharePoint, OneDrive, Exchange Online and Microsoft Power Apps, as Microsoft later acknowledged in its own service-health updates.

The knock-on effects reached beyond Microsoft's own products. Microsoft connectors used by rival AI assistants ChatGPT and Claude broke, prompting OpenAI and Anthropic to report that their tools could no longer reliably reach Microsoft 365 data.

## Key developments: slow, difficult recovery

Microsoft's account of the cause pointed to infrastructure failure rather than sabotage. The company said a number of servers became "unexpectedly unavailable" after failing during automated checks, and that part of its infrastructure was "experiencing elevated load," likely contributing to connectivity errors. It later reported that it had applied an "upstream authentication/routing configuration component patch" to a portion of its systems.

"As of now, we are not aware of any malicious activity that is causing this incident," Microsoft said, adding that it would publish a post-incident report detailing "the root cause, defense-in-depth repair items, and more" once services were restored.

Recovery proved harder than the company anticipated. Microsoft initially reported only "marginal success" from its patches and warned that services restarted could become unresponsive again once they received traffic. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/13/microsoft-outage-outlook-teams-word-excel-reports) reported, the company later described "early signs of stabilisation" but acknowledged that "service capacity has not yet returned to normal levels and health remains variable as traffic is carefully reintroduced."

To prevent emails from being lost during the recovery, Microsoft said it was extending the email "timeout window" to 48 hours, prioritising the backlog and queue of delayed messages. Charles Lamanna, Microsoft's executive vice-president of Copilot, said on X: "We regret that today's outages caused disruptions for our customers, and we are working to address the issue."

## Impact: from hospitals to small businesses

The outage exposed how deeply economies and public services now run on Microsoft's cloud. The US House of Representatives said its users were affected and that its engineers were working with Microsoft. In the UK, the NHS reported disruption to its email system and remote therapy sessions, pharmacies struggled to communicate with GP surgeries about prescriptions, and several police forces urged the public to phone rather than report crimes online.

In Japan, the government's information-system infrastructure was hit, the shipping company Sagawa Express could not take online reservations, and the pharmaceutical firm Taisho handled orders by phone. New Zealand's foreign ministry said its ability to receive emails was affected, including at all its embassies, consulates and representative offices worldwide. In Australia, the federal police and ASIO, several courts, and health and disability services reported issues, while Indian airlines IndiGo and Akasa Air said their email support was disrupted.

The economic cost was immediate. [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-13/microsoft-outlook-outage-cost-to-businesses/107381570) reported that experts estimated the outage would cost Australian businesses billions of dollars, with small firms hardest hit. "For every hour that a business is out, it's actually costing them money," said Ashwin Pal, a partner at RSM Australia. "How much can a business absorb before they just basically have to throw the towel in?"

## Analysis: the risk of a single cloud

Experts framed the outage less as an isolated glitch than as a structural vulnerability. With more than 430 million people using Microsoft 365 apps and more than 90% of Fortune 500 companies using Copilot, a failure at one provider quickly becomes a failure everywhere.

"Everything is connected. If one element in the chain fails, you see it worldwide," cybersecurity expert Eddy Willems told VRT NWS. "We have all become very dependent on the cloud. And the dependence on a single cloud provider is also growing."

Mark Gregory, an associate professor at RMIT University in Melbourne, told The Guardian that the fault appeared to affect the network layer, and drew a parallel with the 2023 Optus outage in Australia. "If you lose part of that pool, then all of the users are trying to access the remaining servers, and that overloads them. And then they go down. And so you end up with a cascading problem," he said.

The incident echoes July 2024's CrowdStrike failure, which crippled Windows machines worldwide, and an Azure outage Microsoft reported only last month. Commentators note that the consolidation of corporate and government IT onto a single vendor magnifies the blast radius of any single failure — and that many organisations still lack tested contingency plans. UQ associate professor Micheal Axelsen also warned that users returning to flooded inboxes could be more susceptible to phishing, especially if spam filters underperformed during recovery.

## What's next

Microsoft's status page continued to list "service degradation" for much of Friday, and the company said it would keep restoring network traffic gradually while expanding capacity. The central questions remain open: what precisely triggered the cascade, whether any emails were permanently lost rather than delayed, and what the total global cost will be.

For regulators and corporate customers, the outage is likely to sharpen scrutiny of single-vendor reliance and of contractual guarantees for cloud resilience. For now, Microsoft's promised post-incident report — and the speed with which services genuinely return to baseline — will be the test of how well the company understood its own failure.
