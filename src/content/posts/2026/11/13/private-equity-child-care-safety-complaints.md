# Private Equity Child Care Chains Lead in Safety Complaints

The two largest for-profit child care chains in the United States — both owned by private equity firms — generated sharply higher rates of safety complaints and high-risk licensing violations than their peers across three states, according to a Democratic congressional investigation released Thursday. The findings, first [reported by The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2026/11/13/an-investigation-into-private-equity-backed-childcare-companies-found-more-problems/), turn a spotlight on how financial ownership shapes the quality and safety of a sector that serves millions of children and draws billions in public subsidy dollars.

## What the investigation found

The probe was led by Sen. Jeff Merkley (D-Ore.), the top Democrat on the Senate Budget Committee. It reviewed five years of licensing and inspection records for child care centers in California, Texas, and Virginia — states chosen because California and Texas host the most KinderCare and Learning Care Group centers, and all three publish detailed inspection data.

According to the Post, KinderCare Learning Companies and Learning Care Group together produced roughly twice as many complaints as other for-profit chains — and nearly three times the complaints of all other child care facilities — across the three states. The two chains also logged "high-risk" citations at roughly twice the rate of other for-profit centers. In Texas, high-risk categories include child neglect, unsafe sleep practices, and certain background-check failures; in Virginia, they include exposure to a life-threatening allergen, drugs, or strangulation hazards.

In Texas and Virginia, the two chains also drew more supervision-related citations than statewide averages. Private-equity-backed centers overall had a complaint rate similar to other for-profit chains, but almost twice that of all other child care centers.

## Companies push back on conclusions

Both companies disputed any suggestion that ownership structure drives the disparity. A KinderCare spokesman, Ryan James, told the Post that "nothing is more important than the safety and well-being of the children in our care," and argued that the company's commitment "often translates to higher incidents because we are more transparent in our reporting to state and local licensing agencies than other providers." James added that the report "does not establish a connection between ownership structure and higher safety citation rates."

Learning Care Group spokeswoman Marielena Santana said the company takes concerns seriously and works with regulators. "We stand behind the information we have provided and welcome a fact-based discussion about how we can continue strengthening early-childhood education," she said.

The report itself concedes it cannot establish causation — a limitation both firms emphasize. Congressional investigations do not determine criminal guilt or civil liability; their purpose is to inform potential policymaking.

The companies also declined to provide most of the information Merkley requested, despite benefiting from public subsidies, according to the report. The committee instead built its analysis from financial statements, securities filings, and state licensing records.

## The larger franchise

The scrutiny lands in a sector where private capital now runs deep. A 2024 Congressional Research Service report found that private equity is invested in eight of the nation's ten largest for-profit child care organizations. KinderCare is owned by the Swiss private equity firm Partners Group; Learning Care Group is owned by American Securities, which acquired it in 2014.

Critics have long warned that the model prioritizes investor returns over children. As [Vox reported](https://www.msn.com/en-us/news/other/what-happens-when-private-equity-owns-your-childcare/ar-AA2b2kay), child care expert Elliot Haspel wrote in 2022 that private-equity-owned chains "ultimately answer to investors or shareholders first, parents second," citing the nursing-home sector, where acquisitions have been linked to declines in quality. In 2024, the Open Markets Institute, the National Women's Law Center, and Community Change published a report arguing that private-equity-owned centers would soak up public funding while resisting reforms.

The timing sharpens the political stakes. Child care costs exceeded the cost of rent and medical services in September 2026, according to an October analysis by Bank of America, feeding directly into the affordability narrative dominating national politics.

## An academic counterpoint

Yet the evidence is more contested than the headline numbers suggest. Vox highlighted a forthcoming paper by Jessica Brown of the University of South Carolina and Chris Herbst of Arizona State University — described as the first systematic, descriptive look at private equity's spread through American child care — that found "no smoking guns." One analyst characterized the focus on private equity as something of a scapegoat or red herring.

That tension — between correlation and causation, and between transparency and accountability — sits at the heart of the debate. KinderCare's transparency argument is plausible on its face: a company that reports more diligently will log more incidents. But the report's authors note the pattern held across three states with different regulatory regimes, and that high-risk supervision citations are less easily explained by reporting diligence alone.

## What comes next

At least five states — Colorado, Connecticut, Massachusetts, New York, and Pennsylvania — have introduced or passed bills writing ownership structure into child care policy, capping what large for-profit chains can draw from state grants in some cases. A coalition of advocacy groups published model state legislation in February.

Merkley has signaled he intends to continue. When congressional Democrats take control of both chambers in January, committees will gain subpoena power — potentially forcing the release of the documents the companies withheld — and Merkley may chair the Budget Committee. That would give the investigation teeth it currently lacks.

For families and policymakers alike, the core question remains unresolved: whether the complaint disparity reflects a business model that degrades care, or simply a reporting gap that makes the largest providers easier to see. The next phase of oversight — and the academic work still to come — may finally separate the two.
