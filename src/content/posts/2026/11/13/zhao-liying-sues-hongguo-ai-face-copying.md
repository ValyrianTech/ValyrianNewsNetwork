# Zhao Liying Sues Hongguo Short Drama Over AI Face Copying

A-list Chinese actress Zhao Liying has taken the operator of the Hongguo Short Drama platform and its parent company Douyin to court, arguing that short dramas on the service used AI face-swapping and face-blending technology to graft her facial features onto characters without permission. The portrait-rights case was heard via a remote “cloud court” session at the Beijing Mentougou District People's Court on November 10, and no verdict has yet been issued, according to [IFeng](https://ent.ifeng.com/c/8xza1S7p0xf).

The lawsuit, which has climbed to the second spot on Weibo's entertainment trending chart, has become a test case for two questions Chinese courts have yet to resolve: whether an AI-synthesized face can be legally recognized as a protected portrait, and how much liability platform operators bear for AI-generated likeness infringement.

## Who Is Being Sued

Zhao filed the suit against Beijing Bimo Liuxiang Technology Co., Ltd., the entity that operates Hongguo Short Drama; Beijing Douyin Information Service Co., Ltd., its wholly-owned shareholder within the ByteDance ecosystem; and a natural person identified as Shi Mou-lun. The cause of action is a portrait-rights dispute (肖像权纠纷), [IT之家](https://www.ithome.com/1/025/939.htm) reported.

Zhao's side alleges that some short dramas on the Hongguo platform used AI face-swapping and face-blending to synthesize her facial features onto characters without authorization, and in some cases used her likeness directly as cover art to drive commercial traffic. The core dispute, in other words, is not the traditional theft of a photograph but whether a face assembled by an algorithm can be recognized in law as Zhao's own.

This is not the first time Hongguo has been drawn into such a controversy. Public reports indicate that the likenesses of actors including Jackson Yee, Deng Wei, Yang Zi, Xiao Zhan and Cheng Yi were previously used by AI short dramas on the platform through face-swapping or synthesis. Those disputes clustered around March and April 2026, when multiple celebrity studios issued dense batches of rights-protection statements. On April 6, Hongguo published a special governance announcement saying it had completed a full review of 15,000 works and handled 670 violating titles, while disclosing that 1,718 violating “comic dramas” had been taken down cumulatively in the first quarter. Customer service previously attributed the problems to the newness of the format, saying there “may be imperfections in review.”

## The Two-Layer Legal Fight

Because Zhao named both the Hongguo operator and the Douyin entity as defendants, how liability is divided inside the ByteDance ecosystem is the case's first battleground. You Yunting, a senior partner at Shanghai Dabang Law Firm, told IFeng that the two entities occupy different legal positions.

“Hongguo is a short-drama broadcast platform; they cannot invoke the safe-harbor principle, because a short-drama platform only puts content online after its own review, so its duty of care is relatively high,” You said. The safe harbor allows a platform to escape liability by promptly removing infringing content once notified, but that protection presupposes the platform bears no ex-ante review duty. As the body that reviews and green-lights content before publication, Hongguo has an active gatekeeping responsibility.

Douyin, by contrast, may still qualify. “Douyin is indeed a short-drama broadcasting platform; I think Douyin can still enter the safe harbor. Their legal obligation is to delete the relevant content after receiving an infringement notice; only if they fail to delete do they bear liability,” You said. Zhang Shuqing, a lawyer at Shanghai Jinmao Law Firm, added that micro-drama broadcast platforms may be held to higher pre-broadcast verification duties depending on their specific business type, and that platforms could also bear responsibility if algorithms amplify infringing videos.

## Does a Synthetic Face Count as a Portrait?

The more consequential question, lawyers say, is whether the AI-synthesized face infringes Zhao's portrait rights at all. Under Article 1018 of China's Civil Code, a portrait is an external image of a specific natural person reflected on a carrier that can be identified; Article 1019 prohibits forging another person's portrait using information technology and producing, using or publicizing a portrait without consent.

The governing test is identifiability, not exact replication. “If it can be recognized by the general public or a specific industry group, it should be determined that the natural person's portrait was used,” You said. “At this point the judgment is whether — from facial contours and facial features — the general public sees it as especially like the person. If the public-perception answer is ‘yes,' the court will most likely find infringement.” Where the resemblance is unmistakable, he added, a court need not even order an appraisal to make that finding.

The backdrop has shifted in the plaintiff's favor. On September 7, 2026, China's Supreme People's Court released its first dedicated adjudication guidance on AI disputes, addressing face-swapping and voice-synthesis infringements and reaffirming identifiability as the core standard. That same year, Hongguo moved to get ahead of the problem: on November 9 it announced a “virtual image creation and rights confirmation” feature letting creators upload ownership materials for AI-generated characters, IT之家 reported.

## What to Watch

Whatever the ruling, the case will give the industry a benchmark it has been groping for. It could clarify how far the safe harbor shields AI-synthetic content, and set a damages figure for a category of infringement where past awards have often been too small to deter repeat offenders — bad actors have repeatedly rebranded accounts and resumed selling counterfeit likenesses.

The unresolved variable is timing: no verdict has been issued, and a decision could take months. But the question the case poses — whether a face scraped, spliced and monetized by an algorithm is still a person's own — is one the AI short-drama boom has been forcing toward a courtroom for some time.
