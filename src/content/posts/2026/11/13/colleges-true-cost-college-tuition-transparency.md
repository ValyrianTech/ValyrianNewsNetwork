# Colleges Try to Clear Up the True Cost of Attendance

MIDDLEBURY, Vt. — The stately limestone buildings and manicured lawns of Middlebury College look expensive. Like many universities, the Vermont school is working hard to persuade prospective families that it is not — or at least not as expensive as it seems. Middlebury has announced that students from families with household incomes up to $175,000 [can enroll tuition-free](https://www.middlebury.edu/stories/archive/2026/09/middlebury-will-become-tuition-free-students-family-income-175000), joining a growing number of colleges trying to cut through widespread confusion about what school actually costs.

At first glance, such offers look like a sweeping expansion of financial aid, since $175,000 is more than double the nation's median household income. In practice, the change affects far fewer students than the headline implies — a paradox at the heart of a broader push to make college pricing more transparent.

## A small benefit, widely announced

According to [NPR and The Hechinger Report](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/13/nx-s1-6023392/real-college-costs-confusing), the number of families who will newly benefit at Middlebury is 35 to 50 in an incoming class that typically enrolls about 630 students. Most families earning $175,000 or less already attended without paying tuition, the college acknowledges.

The real purpose of the policy, experts say, is to confront a problem that has soured many Americans on higher education: how frustratingly hard it is to figure out what college will cost.

"Let's make it as non-complicated as possible," said Ian Baucom, president of Middlebury. "If your family income is $175,000 or less, at Middlebury, you don't pay tuition." The goal, he said, is to "just [be] really clear and get that out of the way, and then let the students decide."

The confusion is well documented. Nearly 80% of families considering college eliminated a school from consideration based on price, a Sallie Mae survey found. Nearly 70% of students and parents think the financial aid process is partly or completely confusing, according to a survey by the Strada Education Foundation. And more than three-quarters of adults without college degrees could not correctly estimate the price of a public four-year institution within $5,000, a Gallup and Lumina Foundation survey showed.

## The sticker-price trap

A major culprit is colleges' own practice of advertising a high "list price" that almost no one pays, then discounting it through financial aid and scholarships. So much has this spiraled that, at 445 colleges and universities, not a single student pays the advertised price, according to a Hechinger Report analysis of federal data. At private, nonprofit four-year institutions, fewer than one in five families pays the full sticker price.

Yet consumers remain fixated on that sticker price. Middlebury's full listed cost of attendance is $97,386 a year, and room, board, books and other expenses alone total $24,462 — costs that the tuition-free guarantee does not cover.

"The most important problem, I would argue, in college pricing today is the lack of transparency," said Phillip Levine, an economist at Wellesley College and author of *Hidden Tuition: An Insider's Guide to College Pricing and Financial Aid*. Levine said that even he struggled to decipher prices when he sent his sons to college, despite holding a Ph.D. in economics.

"If you're going to charge people different amounts of money, you have to provide them with some indication of what that's going to be," he said.

## Marketing or access?

Levine's research suggests that many high-profile "free tuition" guarantees are less a new benefit than a clearer restatement of aid that already existed. When families see such pronouncements, they think, "Oh, wow, the college is cutting the price," he said — but at most of the institutions making these guarantees, "the college was already free for almost all those kids."

In a [Brookings Institution analysis](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-do-free-tuition-policies-do/), Levine found that free tuition policies mostly represent a relabeling of existing aid, and that students who actually pay less under them typically have incomes just below the eligibility threshold. The primary value of the policies, he concluded, may be informational: they communicate affordability in a way families can readily understand.

The stakes go beyond any single campus. Colleges that have reached or surpassed a listed price of $100,000 a year are confronting a near-record-low public confidence in higher education. Gallup found that 38% of Americans now express a great deal or quite a lot of confidence in the institution, down from 57% in 2015 — and that the high cost of college is among the top reasons cited by those who lack confidence.

As [Gallup reported](https://news.gallup.com/poll/712322/confidence-higher-education-slips-back-slightly.aspx), cost was named by 30% of respondents as a reason for their doubts.

## A range of approaches

Middlebury is not alone. Princeton (for most families earning up to $250,000), the University of Chicago ($250,000), MIT, Harvard and Swarthmore ($200,000) and Davidson ($175,000) have all announced income-based tuition guarantees. Smaller or less-resourced schools target narrower audiences: Earlham College offers free tuition to Indiana families earning $100,000 or less, and New Hampshire's public universities do the same for in-state first-years from families earning up to $100,000.

A few institutions are simply cutting prices outright — among them Prescott College in Arizona, the University of Tulsa, and Concordia University, St. Paul in Minnesota. The University of Pennsylvania has debuted a [cost calculator](https://cost-calculator.apps.upenn.edu/) that lets families see their likely net cost before applying. "Penn is more affordable than most people realize," the calculator's webpage declares.

For many families, the confusion is personal. "It's a little bit like going to the doctor, right?" said Liz Mark, a mother from Wisconsin visiting Middlebury with her high school junior. "You don't know exactly how much a procedure is going to cost until you get all of that information and get your pre-approvals."

## What to watch

Whether income-based guarantees genuinely widen access — or chiefly serve as a communication tool — remains an open question. What is clear is that the underlying cost trend, with many private colleges now approaching $100,000 a year, is the deeper issue that clearer messaging alone cannot resolve.

Baucom frames the effort in broader terms. "The social contract of American life depends upon the idea that everyone can come into the fullness of what it means to live and think and study and work here," he said. "And that's fracturing." The task, in his view, is to make plain that college "is for every family, and families of all sorts."
