# US and Mexico Near Loss of Measles-Free Status

International health experts are meeting this week to decide whether the United States and Mexico have forfeited one of the most consequential public health victories of the past half-century: the elimination of measles. The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) convened its Regional Verification Commission for a closed-door session on November 10–13, and a formal announcement is expected next week, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/measles-paho-rfk-elimination-child-deaths-c2f73f91458992f3320d0228b7787627).

If confirmed, the decision would formally certify that measles is again spreading continuously within both countries' borders — reversing a U.S. milestone that has held since 2000 and a Mexican achievement reached in 2016. Coming after Canada was stripped of the same designation in November 2025, it would confirm that the entire Americas region has lost the measles-free status it was the first in the world to earn.

## What the designation means

Elimination is not the same as eradication. It is defined as the absence of endemic — home-grown, continuous — measles transmission in a region for at least 12 months, in the presence of a well-functioning surveillance system. Imported cases still occur; elimination simply certifies that a country has stopped the virus from circulating on its own.

PAHO's review rests on a narrow, technical test. As the agency [explained in March](https://www.paho.org/en/news/2-3-2026-update-review-measles-elimination-status), its commission assesses "whether the same virus genotype and lineage has circulated continuously for 12 months or more in a specific geographic area." The agency set the analysis period as one year from the onset of each country's outbreak: January 20, 2025 for the United States and February 1, 2025 for Mexico.

"It's solely based on genetics," Dr. Amesh Adalja, an infectious disease physician and senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, told AP. The commission presents its recommendations to PAHO Director Dr. Jarbas Barbosa, who makes the final determination.

## How the outbreaks unfolded

The numbers behind the review are stark. According to the [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/data-research/index.html), the United States has recorded 5,412 confirmed measles cases in 2026 across 47 jurisdictions — already more than double the 2,289 cases reported in all of 2025. Forty-three new outbreaks have been logged this year, and 96% of cases are outbreak-associated. Roughly 11% of patients have been hospitalized.

It is the worst U.S. measles year since 1991. Large outbreaks struck Texas, Arizona, Utah and South Carolina last year, and a 2026 outbreak in Pennsylvania's Amish country has dwarfed earlier ones.

Mexico's toll is heavier still. Its Health Ministry counts 20,552 confirmed cases and 50 deaths since February 2025, [according to The Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/mexico-measles-cases-top-20000-50-deaths-2026/), which reported the figures from Mexican media because it could not access the ministry's own bulletin. The outbreak has reached 531 municipalities across all 32 states over 21 months. Jalisco, home to Guadalajara, leads 2026 with 7,394 cases; 21 of the deaths occurred in the northern border state of Chihuahua, and most of the dead were children under 5.

The two outbreaks are not separate stories. Researchers believe an outbreak in Chihuahua stemmed from an unvaccinated Mennonite child who traveled to Gaines County, Texas — then in the grip of its own outbreak — and returned infected.

## A death toll in dispute

The human cost in the U.S. is significant but imprecisely measured. AP reports eight measles-associated deaths in the U.S. this year, the most in more than three decades; the article elsewhere refers to "about a dozen." The CDC's own data page, updated November 13, lists six deaths for 2026 and notes the figure is "subject to change" pending a standardized case definition for measles deaths being developed with the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists. The gap likely reflects death reviews still being classified.

## The vaccination backslide

The root cause is eroding immunity. CDC estimates U.S. kindergarten MMR coverage fell to 92.4% in the 2025–26 school year, down from 95.2% in 2019–20 — below the roughly 95% threshold needed for herd immunity, and leaving some 280,000 kindergartners unprotected. At the local level, pockets of unvaccinated children can seed outbreaks even in otherwise well-covered states.

Experts place part of the blame on a politically fraught federal response. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. was a leading voice in the anti-vaccine movement before his appointment, and he has moved to downsize the childhood vaccination schedule and reshape a key federal vaccine advisory board. In August 2026, President Donald Trump signed an executive order calling for childhood shots to be spaced into separate visits and for the MMR vaccine to be split into three single-disease shots — against the guidance of major medical groups, [AP reported](https://apnews.com/article/vaccine-research-autism-trump-kennedy-rfk-d10f81f221c4ae9f5b2f83dd0ee98b29).

Dr. David Margolius, director of Cleveland's health department and a leader of a coalition of big-city health agencies, was blunt about the trajectory. "It's a big deal," he told AP. "The decision represents us moving backwards."

CDC Director Erica Schwartz, by contrast, emphasized that the label itself is not what shields people. "The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does," she said in a statement this week. "Losing a designation doesn't mean we abandon an evidence-based approach. It means we remain focused on stopping outbreaks."

## Why a symbolic label still matters

Losing elimination status does not automatically trigger travel bans, border measures or trade restrictions. In practical terms, its consequences are largely reputational — a marker that a decades-long campaign has been reversed, and a signal that vaccine coverage and political will have frayed. It may nonetheless color travel and tourism messaging and is already being cited by public health experts as an indictment of the current approach.

The regional picture is deteriorating. Canada lost its status in November 2025 after the virus circulated uninterrupted for 12 months, and because regional status hinges on any single country, the Americas as a whole lost its measles-free designation then. Globally, the World Health Organization confirmed in early 2026 that Armenia, Austria, Azerbaijan, Spain, the United Kingdom and Uzbekistan had also lost elimination status — evidence of a broad, multi-region relapse.

## What to watch

Three questions will shape what comes next. First, whether PAHO formally confirms the loss of status for both countries, and on what date next week. Second, how the U.S. federal government responds — Schwartz's statement suggests no change in strategy, even as experts call for a stepped-up vaccination push. Third, how far the death toll climbs, and how many of the dead are children.

PAHO has described such setbacks as serious but reversible. Regaining elimination status typically requires suppressing transmission for a sustained period — usually a full year — after which a country can be re-verified. With shared border outbreaks linking the U.S., Mexico and Canada, any recovery is likely to require coordinated action across all three.

For now, the direction of travel is unmistakable. A victory that took the Americas 22 years to achieve is, by the commission's own genetic yardstick, being undone.
