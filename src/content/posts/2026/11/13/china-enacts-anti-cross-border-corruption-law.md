# China Enacts Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law

China has published the full text of its new Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law, a dedicated legal framework that targets transnational bribery, empowers authorities to pursue fugitives and recover assets abroad, and imposes mandatory integrity-compliance obligations on companies doing business across borders. The law was passed on 6 November 2026 and took effect the day it was promulgated.

The text appeared in the [People's Daily](http://society.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1113/c1008-40832996.html) on 13 November, more than a week after the Standing Committee of the 14th National People's Congress approved it at its 25th session. President Xi Jinping signed Presidential Order No. 89, which confirmed the law would "take effect on the date of promulgation," according to [the State Council](https://www.gov.cn/yaowen/liebiao/202611/content_7085371.htm).

## Context: A Long-Planned Legal Pillar

The legislation has been years in the making. It was listed as a key reform measure at the Third Plenum of the 20th Central Committee in July 2024 and included in the NPC Standing Committee's 2026 legislative work plan. The State Supervisory Commission drafted the law, which was reviewed twice in 2026 before passing on its third reading in early November.

The law consists of six chapters and 47 articles covering general provisions, mechanisms and responsibilities, case handling and international cooperation, integrity and compliance, legal liability, and supplementary provisions. The National Supervisory Commission leads the national anti-cross-border corruption mechanism, working alongside foreign affairs, public security, judicial administration, finance, commerce, anti-money laundering, audit, state-asset supervision, financial regulation, securities oversight, and cyberspace administration departments.

## What the Law Covers

Under Article 2, "cross-border corruption" spans five categories of conduct: bribery of foreign public officials or international organization officials by domestic entities (or vice versa within China); bribery of domestic officials by foreign entities, including the associated bribe-taking; other duty-related crimes committed abroad by domestic actors; corruption where part of the act or result crosses the border; and the flight of suspects abroad or cross-border transfer of corrupt assets.

The law also introduces a countermeasures clause. Article 6 states that where a foreign state uses anti-corruption as a pretext or improperly applies its laws extraterritorially to suppress China or discriminate against Chinese citizens and enterprises, China may take "countermeasures and blocking measures" under its Counter-Foreign Sanctions Law. Article 25 similarly bars foreign institutions from conducting anti-corruption investigations inside China without Chinese authorities' consent, and prohibits domestic entities from providing evidence or assistance to such foreign investigations.

## Compliance Burdens for Business

A substantial portion of the law targets corporate conduct. Chapter 4 requires enterprises engaged in cross-border business to build integrity-compliance management systems, conduct risk assessments, maintain accurate financial records, perform due diligence on third parties, provide staff training, and report suspected cross-border corruption to supervisory or public security organs. State-owned enterprises face stricter controls, including direct appointment of overseas financial officers and rotation of overseas staff. Article 45 allows authorities to order rectification, suspend business, or revoke licenses for failures to meet these obligations.

"Enacting the Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law ... builds the 'four beams and eight pillars' of the anti-cross-border corruption system," National Supervisory Commission Director Liu Jinguo said in the [legislative explanation](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202611/t20261105_458485.html) delivered to lawmakers in August, describing a framework that combines "punishment, prevention and countermeasures."

## Analysis: A Chinese Answer to Long-Arm Jurisdiction

The law is widely read as China's response to Western extraterritorial enforcement, particularly the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act. Rather than merely complying with foreign regimes, Beijing is asserting its own enforcement reach over transnational bribery, while simultaneously shielding Chinese entities from foreign investigations it considers improper. The blocking provisions could create friction with foreign regulators when overseas authorities seek to investigate conduct that occurs on Chinese soil.

The compliance chapter also carries practical weight for multinationals. China has signed Belt and Road cooperation documents with more than 150 countries and hosts some 52,000 overseas enterprises, making cross-border integrity a governance priority framed around the "Clean Silk Road" initiative. The due-diligence and third-party supervision duties extend the law's reach well beyond state actors.

## What's Next

Attention now turns to implementation. The National Supervisory Commission is expected to issue detailed anti-cross-border corruption guidelines that will clarify how the mechanism operates in practice. How aggressively the countermeasures and blocking clauses are invoked — and how foreign partners respond — will determine whether the law becomes a genuine instrument of international cooperation or a new source of cross-border legal tension.
