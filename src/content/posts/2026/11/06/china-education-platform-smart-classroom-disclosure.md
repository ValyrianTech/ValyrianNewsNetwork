# China Education Platform Urges Smart Classroom Disclosure

China's national education rumor-refutation platform has urged schools to proactively disclose smart classroom systems to teachers and students before introducing them, [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34418827) reported on November 6, as public debate intensifies over monitoring technology in classrooms following a controversy at Nanjing University.

The guidance, published through the "National Education Rumor-Refutation" WeChat account of the China Education Press Agency, signals an official effort to calm rumor-driven anxiety while nudging schools toward transparency rather than banning the technology outright.

## Context: A Viral Screenshot and Rising Anxiety

The intervention follows an uproar at Nanjing University over a smart classroom system that recorded attendance rates and a "head-raising rate" — a metric of how often students look up at the board or lecturer rather than down at their phones.

The dispute began when students received notices that the school would use its newly upgraded smart classroom system to record attendance rates for a one-week period. A screenshot then circulated online showing surveillance footage alongside statistics on late students, attendance and head-raising rates, [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34401814) reported. A teacher's message in a class group suggesting attendance rates could directly affect regular-course grades — and even cancel exam eligibility — further fueled the outcry.

According to Cover News, cited by The Paper, Nanjing University's smart-classroom project was awarded for more than 31.52 million yuan, covering the upgrade of 113 public classrooms on the Xianlin campus. Bidding documents reportedly included technical requirements for "face detection, facial recognition, AI headcount statistics and AI head-raising-rate calculation."

## The University Responds

On November 4, Nanjing University's Undergraduate School and IT Construction Management Service Center issued a formal explanation denying that attendance rates are linked to regular-course grades and dismissing claims that the monitoring constituted an "inspection by higher authorities."

The university stated that "without authorization," its smart classroom system does not collect biometric information such as faces and does not perform facial recognition in teaching applications. It added that "considering that classroom learning styles are more diverse, our university's smart classroom system currently no longer counts head-raising-rate data."

The university also clarified that the viral screenshot was not a current smart-classroom feed but a personal screen capture from online supervision at its Suzhou campus in May 2026.

## Three Patterns of Misinformation

The national platform used the episode to outline what it described as three common patterns in recent school-technology rumors: misattribution and reuse of old images, such as the Nanjing screenshot actually originating from the Suzhou campus in May; concept substitution that blurs functional boundaries, conflating physical security monitoring with deep biometric analysis or exaggerating tender "technical options" as fully deployed features; and over-extrapolation that ties group attendance statistics to individual classroom-state scoring or grade linkage.

## A Legal Red Line

The platform pointed to existing legal safeguards. China's Personal Information Protection Law classifies facial and biometric data as sensitive personal information, requiring specific purpose, sufficient necessity and separate consent. The Administrative Measures for the Security Management of Face Recognition Technology Applications stipulate that where non-facial-recognition methods exist, facial recognition must not be the sole verification method.

## Transparency as the Antidote

The platform's central recommendation was that schools communicate before deployment. "Before introducing systems involving behavior recording and data collection, schools should proactively disclose to teachers and students: what functions the system has, where the data is specifically used, whether biometrics are involved, and whether there are non-collection alternatives," it stated.

"Put the right to know in the hands of teachers and students, use transparency to break the 'technical black box' — only then can misreadings caused by information asymmetry be prevented at the source," the platform added. The Paper's own commentary praised the university's approach of "not avoiding questions, directly facing teachers' and students' concerns, and responding openly and transparently."

## What's Next

The episode highlights a sector-wide challenge: as China accelerates education digitalization, recording, livestreaming and behavior-analysis systems are becoming common across universities. The platform's guidance suggests that official policy will favor informed consent and disclosure over prohibition, leaving schools to reconcile the push for smart campuses with students' and teachers' privacy expectations.
