# China Passes Landmark Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law

China's top legislature voted on November 6 to pass the Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law (反跨境腐败法), establishing the country's first dedicated legal framework for combating corruption that crosses national borders—including the pursuit of illicit assets and suspects overseas. The Standing Committee of the 14th National People's Congress (NPC) approved the measure at the closing of its 25th session, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261106/bf368d13a1a64c2cb00cedf72ac95573/c.html).

The law comprises six chapters and 47 articles, covering mechanisms and responsibilities, case handling and international cooperation, integrity and compliance, and legal liability. It takes effect from the date of promulgation.

## Purpose and Scope

According to the law's stated purpose clause, the legislation is designed to "prevent and punish cross-border corruption, deepen international anti-corruption cooperation, safeguard national sovereignty, security and development interests, and promote the building of a fair and reasonable global anti-corruption governance system."

The passage caps a rapid legislative process. The draft received its first reading in August 2026 at the 24th session and was submitted for a second reading when the current session opened on November 2, [China News Service reported](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202611/t20261102_458144.html). It was approved four days later.

NPC Legislative Affairs Commission spokesperson Huang Haihua said the second draft added provisions to strengthen the protection of state secrets, work secrets, business secrets, personal information and personal privacy, the same outlet reported.

## Overseas Pursuit and Data Sovereignty

The law formalizes a legal basis for the pursuit of corrupt fugitives and illicit assets abroad (追逃追赃), expanding a framework that builds on China's existing anti-corruption regime, which includes the Supervision Law, the Criminal Law and China's commitments under the UN Convention Against Corruption. That background is noted in [People's Daily's analysis](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202611/t20261104_458364.html) of the draft.

A notable provision bars institutions, organizations and individuals within China from providing "evidence materials and other related assistance" to foreign entities without authorization from Chinese authorities. The second draft extended this prohibition to cover "data information" (数据信息)—a significant data-sovereignty and cross-border enforcement element with implications for foreign litigation and regulatory demands for evidence held in China.

The law also imposes compliance obligations on domestic enterprises engaged in cross-border business, requiring them to establish integrity and anti-corruption compliance departments or personnel commensurate with their size, business scope and risk profile. The second reading opened at the 25th session on November 2, as [People's Court Daily reported](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c2/c30834/202611/t20261104_458413.html).

## Analysis

Domestically, the legislation elevates anti-corruption cooperation and overseas asset recovery into a codified, national-security-linked framework, giving enforcement agencies a clearer statutory foundation. The second draft's addition of a "holistic approach to national security" principle reinforces that framing, alongside language committing the work to "strictly follow the Constitution and laws." Internationally, China frames the law as a contribution to global anti-corruption governance, positioning itself as a norm-shaping actor.

Yet the restrictions on providing evidence and data to foreign parties could generate friction with foreign courts and regulators seeking documents held in China—a potential flashpoint as cross-border commercial disputes and enforcement actions grow more common.

## What's Next

The law's immediate effect depends on how implementing regulations specify the enforcement mechanisms and compliance requirements outlined in its 47 articles. Observers will be watching how the new framework interacts with existing international cooperation agreements—and how foreign jurisdictions respond to the law's restrictions on outbound evidence and data.
