# US Public Schools Face Massive Enrollment Decline

Public schools across the United States are confronting a sharp and accelerating drop in student enrollment, driven by record-low birth rates and, increasingly, by Trump-era immigration crackdowns. For the first time in nearly a century — since the Great Depression era — the nation is experiencing low birth rates and low immigration at the same time, leaving empty classrooms, strained budgets, and difficult decisions about closing campuses in districts nationwide, [NPR reports](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/06/nx-s1-5971283/public-school-enrollment-ice-deportation-birthrate).

## A Demographic Cliff, Then a Crackdown

Long before the Trump administration began deporting large numbers of immigrants, many with school-age children, public schools were already losing students. The population of children under 18 began falling nationwide after 2010, [according to U.S. Census data](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2021/08/united-states-adult-population-grew-faster-than-nations-total-population-from-2010-to-2020.html), dropping by roughly 2.1 million kids through 2025.

"Birth rates here in Los Angeles County have been declining for decades, which means fewer children enrolled in our schools," said Kelly Gonez, a board member in the Los Angeles Unified School District (LAUSD), the second largest in the country.

Then came the immigration crackdown. A study by the Migration Policy Institute found as many as 1.9 million people left the U.S. during the first 18 months of Trump's second term. As part of that effort, federal officials weakened restrictions that had prevented ICE agents from operating near public schools. "Many of our families immediately went into hiding," Gonez said. "We were predicting a 2% drop in enrollment. It was double that."

## The Scale of the Decline

According to the [U.S. Department of Education](https://nces.ed.gov/fastfacts/display.asp?id=65), the number of kids in public schools nationwide peaked in 2019 and fell by roughly 1.5 million students as of the 2024 school year. Based on data gathered before the immigration crackdown, federal researchers project enrollment will decline by at least another 2 million students by 2030 — a 7% national decline in roughly a decade.

"The U.S. isn't alone in facing the challenges of demographic decline," said Thomas Dee, an economist specializing in education policy at Stanford University. "To stop or slow immigration really exacerbates those problems. Communities are now confronting very painful decisions about closing schools."

A report published in June by two education think tanks, [Bellwether and WestEd](https://bellwether.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/DecliningEnrollment_Bellwether_WestEd_June2026.pdf), warned that public schools have entered an era of "sustained" enrollment declines already reshaping districts in 40 states. The analysis found the "primary overall driver" is a sustained drop in birth rates, followed by a "national slowdown in immigration," and predicted a loss of $11 billion a year in public school funding linked to falling enrollment.

Private schools are not immune either. The U.S. Department of Education projects private schools will lose more than a million students by the end of this decade.

## Communities Hit Hardest

The losses are concentrated in immigrant-heavy communities. Texas saw classroom enrollment plunge by more than 76,000 students statewide last year, the vast majority among Hispanic kids and the children of immigrant families, according to a [Texas Education Agency study](https://tea.texas.gov/data-reports/school-performance/accountability-research/enroll-2025-26.pdf). Florida, home to a large Haitian refugee community stripped of temporary protected status in July, saw its two largest districts — Miami-Dade and Broward County — lose roughly 30,000 children combined in a single year.

In Los Angeles, teachers describe the change in stark terms. "In my school alone, we had over 200 students that didn't show up on the first day of school," said Guadalupe Cardona, a veteran teacher at Santee Education Complex. "We found that a lot of them were just gone, no longer living where they had put their address down. We lost two teachers at the beginning of the school year because of it."

Fear has spread beyond the immigrant students themselves. [Brookings Institution estimates](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-many-children-are-affected-by-parental-immigrant-detention/) that as many as 4.6 million school-age kids — including many U.S. citizens — live in homes with family members vulnerable to deportation. Vanessa Salazar, a U.S.-born former LAUSD senior, dropped out after her mother was detained and deported to Mexico. "It really threw me off balance," she said. "Coming home without my mother there to be my anchor really affected my performance in school."

## A Clash Over Framing

The White House frames the trend positively. Spokeswoman Lauren Bis said in a brief email that deportations "make American classrooms less crowded," and administration officials have celebrated a net decline in the foreign-born population of 2.9 million from January 2025 to July 2026 — the first net negative migration since the 1930s.

Advocacy groups that support sharply reduced immigration share that view. "We don't guarantee the school system X number of kids," said Ira Mehlman of the Federation for American Immigration Reform. "That is not the job of our immigration system."

Education researchers and local officials counter that the losses are disruption layered on top of an unavoidable demographic shift. Dee's research found student daily absences spiked more than 20% in California Central Valley communities hit by 2025 ICE raids, and with ICE now arresting as many as 50,000 people each month, he and others say the full impact depends on how long enforcement policies persist.

## What's Next

With LAUSD now facing a second straight year of roughly 4% enrollment declines, the district announced a citywide effort in September to identify buildings and campuses that will close. A California state study released in August projected public schools in the state alone could lose another 710,000 students over the next decade if fertility and migration trends hold.

"When your district is roughly half the size of student population it was 20 years ago, it's hard to find a sustainable path to keep campuses open," Gonez said. For districts across the country, the question now is not whether to shrink, but how — and who bears the cost.
