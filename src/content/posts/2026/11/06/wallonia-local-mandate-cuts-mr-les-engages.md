# Wallonia's local mandate cuts may boost MR and Les Engagés

Wallonia will eliminate roughly 766 local elected mandates after the 2030 municipal elections, cutting two municipal councillors and at least one alderman from every commune in the region. According to an analysis published by Le Soir on 6 November, the reform — presented as a budget-saving measure — is likely to strengthen the two parties that form the Walloon regional government, the liberal MR and the centrist Les Engagés, particularly in the rural communes where they already dominate.

## What the reform does

The measure was unveiled on 12 October as part of the Walloon 2027–2029 budget, rather than in the region's original policy declaration. As [Sudinfo](https://www.sudinfo.be/id1209115/article/2026-10-12/la-wallonie-fait-le-menage-dans-les-communes-510-conseillers-communaux-et-256) reported, every commune will lose two councillors. Every commune also loses at least one alderman, with communes above 100,000 inhabitants losing two and Charleroi — the only commune above 200,000 — losing three. In total, that amounts to 510 councillor seats and 256 alderman posts, or 766 mandates, eliminated once the assemblies elected in 2030 are installed.

A companion measure makes the administrative integration of each commune with its CPAS — the public social welfare centre — mandatory: by 2031 for communes under 25,000 inhabitants and by 2034 for larger ones. Crucially, this is not a merger of communes with one another, but of each commune's administration with its own CPAS.

The reform is one strand of a wider budget effort. The Walloon government needed to find roughly two billion euros by 2029 to balance the books, and the budget package also includes the planned abolition of the provincial tier by 2031, a reduced vehicle tax, a motorway vignette and a "job bonus" of up to 500 euros a year for lower earners.

## Why smaller councils tilt the field

Le Soir's thesis is that, behind an economic argument it describes as "strongly qualified," the reform risks favouring the largest parties — above all the MR and Les Engagés, especially in rural communes often in the hands of the two governing parties.

The mechanics are straightforward. Under Belgium's proportional system for local elections, cutting the number of seats while leaving the electoral result unchanged mechanically increases the seat share of the biggest lists and squeezes out the smallest. That effect is magnified in communes where only one or two lists compete — a pattern that is common in small, rural municipalities.

The scale of that concentration is striking. As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/des-majorites-absolues-dans-pres-de-70-des-communes-wallonnes-un-paradoxe-democratique-11451948) documented after the 2024 local elections, a single list won an absolute majority of seats in 175 of Wallonia's 261 communes — a result the broadcaster described as a "democratic paradox," driven largely by communes where only one or two lists were in the running. In those places, trimming the council can entrench the dominant list further and erase the handful of opposition seats that remain.

The effect, however, is not uniform. A [Sudinfo projection](https://www.sudinfo.be/id1209385/article/2026-10-12/six-conseillers-communaux-en-moins-charleroi-des-2030-le-ps-perdrait-sa-majorite) based on the 2024 results, applied to a 45-seat Charleroi council (down from 51), suggested the Socialist Party (PS) would lose four seats and its absolute majority, with the MR and PTB each losing one. In big cities, in other words, the reform can hurt the incumbent Socialists, whereas in rural communes it tends to consolidate the largest list — frequently an MR or Les Engagés stronghold.

At the smallest end of the scale, the arithmetic becomes stark. As [DH](https://www.dhnet.be/regions/luxembourg/2026/10/13/mesures-deconomies-en-wallonie-daverdisse-et-herbeumont-ne-compteraient-apres-2030-plus-que-7-elus-W4M3HSKNDVANZFNUKHML4IVEHM/) reported, the rural Luxembourg communes of Daverdisse and Herbeumont, both under 2,000 inhabitants, would be left with just seven councillors — a floor critics say undermines proximity, pluralism and democratic oversight.

## A reform justified by savings — and questioned

The government frames the measure primarily as a cost-saving exercise. Reducing the pool of elected officials shrinks the total envelope for local political remuneration and, with the CPAS integrations, aims to eliminate duplicated structures in small municipalities. When he first outlined the CPAS reforms in April 2025, Local Powers Minister François Desquesnes argued that in communes with fewer than 10,000 inhabitants — more than one in two Walloon communes — the duplication of communal and CPAS administrations, councils and colleges eroded efficiency and cost the community more. As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/face-au-blues-des-mandataires-la-wallonie-a-besoin-de-se-reformer-en-profondeur-estime-francois-desquesnes-11534592) reported, he put the potential saving from CPAS integration alone at up to 12 million euros.

But Le Soir stresses that the savings from cutting local mandates are modest relative to the two-billion-euro budget gap and that the economic rationale is "strongly qualified" — supporting the interpretation that the reform is as much political as budgetary. The timing has also raised eyebrows: the measure surfaced through the budget rather than the regional policy declaration, prompting accusations that it was slipped in outside the normal scrutiny. Desquesnes's response, quoted by Le Soir, was blunt: *"Encore heureux qu'on puisse sortir parfois de cette DPR"* — "Good thing one can sometimes step outside that policy declaration."

The minister has consistently cast the wider reform drive as a necessary modernisation, telling RTBF that "Wallonia needs to change, needs to reform itself in depth," and describing the ambition as to "streamline, accelerate and simplify the life of local officeholders." He has also acknowledged the pressures driving many local representatives to quit, pointing to the demands of the role and the strain of social media, "which are a way of directly challenging elected officials, sometimes incorrectly or hurtfully."

## What to watch

Several variables will shape the reform's real-world impact. The precise legal vehicle — a decree — must still be drafted and could face scrutiny from the Council of State. The final population thresholds will determine exactly which communes fall into which bracket. And the companion plan to abolish the provincial tier by 2031 requires a two-thirds majority that the MR–Les Engagés coalition does not hold, forcing it to seek support from the PS and Ecolo.

The political response from the opposition — the PS, Ecolo and the PTB — and from associations of local elected officials will be a key indicator of how contested the measure becomes. But the structural logic is already clear: in a region where single-list majorities are already the norm in two-thirds of communes, fewer seats on the council means less room for opposition — and, in the rural heartlands of the governing parties, a firmer grip on local power ahead of 2030.
