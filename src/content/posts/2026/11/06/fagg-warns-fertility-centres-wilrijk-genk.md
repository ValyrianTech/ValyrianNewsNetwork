# FAGG Warns Fertility Centres in Wilrijk and Genk

Belgium's federal medicines and health products agency, the FAGG (AFMPS), has issued formal warnings to two fertility centres — ZAS Augustinus in Wilrijk (Antwerp) and Ziekenhuis Oost-Limburg (ZOL) in Genk — following inspections carried out as part of the fallout from the country's 2025 sperm-donation scandal, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/_ernstig-voorval-fagg-geeft-twee-fertiliteitscentra-waarschuwin/) on 6 November. The regulator flagged serious irregularities in how the two centres handled sperm donation, and the warnings represent a formal escalation that could, if unresolved, lead to the suspension or revocation of operating licences.

## Context: A Scandal That Exposed Structural Gaps

The warnings are the latest instalment in a crisis that erupted publicly in June 2025, when Belgian media revealed that the sperm of a Danish donor carrying a TP53 gene mutation — associated with cancer risk — had been used across the country. The same donor's sperm led to [53 children conceived by 37 women](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/06/04/niet-52-maar-53-kinderen-belgische-kinderen-verwekt-met-het-spe/), according to figures given to parliament by health minister Frank Vandenbroucke; two pregnancies ended in miscarriage, so 51 children were ultimately born. The case far exceeded the six-family limit set under Belgium's 2007 law on medically assisted reproduction. Separately, the 6 November reporting describes the donor's sperm as having led to "more than fifty children with 38 women," wording that differs slightly from the earlier parliamentary figures.

The affair exposed weaknesses across the entire chain of fertility care: donor selection, data sharing, incident reporting and regulatory oversight. In response, the FAGG published a far-reaching [recovery plan](https://www.fagg.be/nl/news/persbericht_spermadonatie_het_fagg_zet_een_grondige_hervorming_in_om_gezinnen_en_kinderen) on 15 April 2026, overseen by crisis manager Dirk Ramaekers, which promised a strengthened inspection framework, a new legal framework abolishing donor anonymity, and a national donor register, Fertidata.

## What the Inspectors Found

The two warnings stem from inspections conducted earlier this year. At ZAS Augustinus, inspectors on 23 June identified six "major" deficiencies — the second-most-serious category — plus fifteen other deficiencies. The centre also reported a so-called "serious undesired event" that occurred on 9 September 2025 but was only relayed to the FAGG on 28 October, a delayed notification that drew scrutiny.

At ZOL in Genk, inspections on 13 and 14 July found five major deficiencies. Several promised improvements arrived months late, and the hospital could not produce a test report for the software it uses to store donor and patient data.

## Hospitals Respond

Both hospitals say they have addressed the problems while disputing their severity. "A rather technical matter in which no patients were directly involved," said ZAS spokesperson Evelien Heeren of the reported event, adding that the hospital will now report incidents "always within 14 days." In a statement, ZAS insisted that "at no point was there a risk to patient safety or to the quality of care," and said the FAGG approved its action plan in October.

ZOL spokesperson Jurgen Ritzen attributed the delays to the crisis itself: "The donor crisis came in between and that has actually delayed everything a bit," he said, adding that the deficiencies had "in the meantime all been resolved."

## Analysis: Enforcement With Teeth

A warning under the FAGG's new sanctions regime is not a symbolic rebuke. It is the formal precursor to licence suspension or revocation, and the agency has said it will escalate if action plans prove insufficient or if inspectors find the same violations at a subsequent visit. That marks a departure from the softer oversight that the scandal exposed as inadequate.

The story also illustrates the tension between systemic and individual accountability. The roots of the crisis lie in structural gaps — patchy incident reporting, fragmented data systems, and quota monitoring that the FAGG itself concedes it cannot fully track back to 2007. The reform plan targets those structures; the warnings are case-level enforcement against individual institutions.

Under the recovery plan, the FAGG is publishing inspection reports by name and plans to publish centre success rates from 2027 — a transparency drive that raises the reputational stakes for ZAS and ZOL in a particularly sensitive area of medicine.

## What's Next

The FAGG is conducting a new round of thematic inspections across all Belgian fertility centres through early 2027, meaning further warnings are plausible. Key questions remain open: how many other centres will be flagged; whether any warning escalates to a licence suspension; and whether the historical data now being assembled will reveal quota breaches still unknown to affected families. For ZAS and ZOL, the coming months will test whether their action plans hold up — and whether the regulator's newly sharpened tools will be used to their full extent.
