# Belgium's King Rejects De Wever Resignation, Sets Deadline

Belgium's King Filip has refused to accept the resignation of Prime Minister Bart De Wever and ordered him to "immediately resume the budget negotiations," according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/koning-vraagt-premier-de-wever-om-begrotingsgesprekken-te-hervat~1793247532380/), ending a week of uncertainty and handing the embattled premier a fresh — and likely final — chance to break the deadlock over the 2027 federal budget.

The Prime Minister will report back to the monarch on Monday 16 November, the palace announced after De Wever's audience at the Royal Palace on Friday morning. The decision effectively puts the ball back in De Wever's court and postpones — but does not resolve — the crisis that has gripped the five-party "Arizona" coalition.

## A resignation held in abeyance

De Wever tendered the resignation of his government on Monday 2 November, after the restricted Council of Ministers, or "kern," broke up overnight without agreement on the multi-year budget. In a firm communiqué, the Royal Palace deplored the absence of a budget deal "credible and up to the challenges of the moment," urging coalition partners to prioritise "the general interest and a sense of compromise," [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/crise-dans-le-gouvernement-arizona-le-kern-se-quitte-sans-accord-bart-de-wever-se-rend-chez-le-roi-11809122).

Rather than accept or reject the resignation outright, the King held his decision "in beraad" — in deliberation — and launched a round of consultations with the presidents of the five coalition parties: Les Engagés, CD&V, Vooruit, MR and N-VA. Those talks ran from Tuesday to Thursday before Friday's decisive audience with the Prime Minister.

The monarch's intervention is a classic exercise of the Belgian crown's crisis-management role: informing, encouraging and warning, without substituting for elected leaders. By refusing the resignation, Filip has bought the coalition time — but left the substance of the dispute untouched.

## The €10 billion gap

At the heart of the impasse is a dispute over how to find roughly €10 billion in savings by 2029 to bring Belgium's finances under control. According to [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/belgian-government-crisis-looms-budget-talks-collapse-de-tijd-reports-2026-11-02/), De Wever's Flemish nationalist N-VA and the francophone liberal MR have pushed for deeper spending cuts, including to healthcare, while their socialist and centrist partners demand higher taxes on wealth to raise revenue.

The fault line runs most sharply between MR, led by Georges-Louis Bouchez, and the Flemish socialists of Vooruit, alongside Les Engagés. Bouchez has insisted that "if the government spends too much, the solution is not more taxes, but controlling public spending, relaunching growth and protecting the purchasing power of Belgians." Vooruit's Oskar Seuntjens countered that "our only red line is justice," arguing that "every euro we get there is one we don't have to get from pensioners or people who already contribute a lot."

CD&V president Sammy Mahdi arrived at the palace visibly angry earlier in the week, warning that "there is no margin for games" and that the budget figures left no room for certain parties' posturing.

## Fiscal pressure mounts

Belgium enters the standoff with one of the weakest fiscal positions in the eurozone. The European Commission has forecast a debt-to-GDP ratio of 110.5% this year and a general government deficit of 5.2% of GDP — behind only Greece, Italy and France among euro-area members, according to Reuters. De Wever has warned that interest on the national debt, €16 billion this year, could exceed €50 billion by 2035 under current conditions.

The political uncertainty has already been felt in the markets. The yield spread between Belgian and German bonds widened by around ten basis points early in the week, a move the Federal Debt Agency's Jean Deboutte attributed "100 percent" to the failed negotiations. Belgian borrowing rates stand at their highest level in 14 years.

Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem of CD&V did not mince words, warning that Belgium is "closer to Greek conditions than we think" — an allusion to the debt crisis that pushed Greece into years of painful reform after 2010.

## A political cost

In the Chamber of Representatives on Thursday, De Wever conceded that his government would have to fall back on "provisional twelfths" — a stopgap mechanism allowing the state to spend one-twelfth of the previous year's budget each month — for at least the first months of 2027. It is the second consecutive year Belgium has resorted to the device, something De Wever had earlier vowed to avoid "at all costs."

"We must not beat around the bush. For the government this is an awful failure, and since I lead this government, I submitted my resignation to the King on Monday," he told lawmakers. He added that he "only wants to be Prime Minister of a government that prioritises this task. Otherwise it makes no sense."

The gambit — whether a genuine resignation or a shock tactic to force his coalition partners to move — has left De Wever politically weakened, publicly acknowledging failure even as he is re-mandated to try again.

## What's next

Analysts see the 16 November deadline as a pressure device calibrated to force movement without making the task impossible. "The chance seems real that it will ultimately succeed," KU Leuven political scientist Bart Maddens told VRT NWS, noting that all governing parties want to avoid elections and retain confidence in De Wever. Were snap elections to be called, he said, "that is a situation that actually all parties fear" — given Belgium's notoriously lengthy government-formation processes.

Opposition parties are watching closely. The PS's Paul Magnette has said his party will not "bail out a dying government," while Vlaams Belang argues the King should have accepted the resignation outright. On the right, the liberals of Anders have dangled the prospect of an alternative parliamentary majority.

The coming ten days will determine whether the Arizona coalition can convert its reprieve into a deal — or whether Belgium heads toward elections with its budget still unresolved and its creditors watching.
