# Chinese Universities Deploy AI to Monitor Classrooms

A growing number of Chinese universities have introduced artificial-intelligence teaching supervision systems capable of tracking attendance, "head-up rates" and front-row seating, [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34411188) reported, thrusting the use of AI monitoring in higher education into the public spotlight. The debate intensified after students at Nanjing University learned that its newly renovated "smart classrooms" would record attendance for a week, and after leaked screenshots showed dashboards tracking how often students lifted their heads.

The episode has since become a flashpoint over how far universities should go in quantifying classroom behavior—and where the line falls between improving teaching quality and surveilling students.

## The Nanjing Connection

The controversy centered on Nanjing University, whose smart-classroom project at its Xianlin campus reportedly carried a winning bid of about 31.52 million yuan and covered 113 public classrooms. According to the reporting, bidding documents had required face detection, face recognition, AI headcount statistics and AI calculation of head-up rates. Some students questioned why devices capable of recognition were installed without consulting them, citing biometric data-leak risks and arguing that "head-up rate" is a poor measure of genuine engagement across diverse learning formats.

On Nov. 4, the university's Undergraduate College and Informationization Construction Management Service Center issued a joint clarification. According to [Caixin](https://www.caixin.com/2026-11-05/102501093.html), the university said its smart-classroom system "no longer tracks head-up rate data," that without authorization it does not collect facial or biometric information and does not perform facial recognition in teaching applications, and that claims tying attendance to regular grades or describing the midterm inspection as an upper-level check were "both untrue." The circulating classroom image, the university said, was actually a personal screenshot from online supervision at its Suzhou campus in May 2026.

## A Wider Trend

Nanjing is far from alone. The Paper documented a cluster of similar deployments across the country:

- **Jiangnan University** built an AI-based "multi-dimensional teaching-state analysis and intelligent decision" platform covering 462 public classrooms, with AI analysis of 1,365 courses and 127,688 teaching hours. It tracks attendance, front-row seating, head-up and teacher-student interaction rates, and includes an AI patrol robot that generates briefs and issues risk-tiered alerts. The platform was named a Jiangsu province 2025 education digitalization innovation case.
- **Heilongjiang University** launched an online patrol function in April 2026 that lets university-level supervisors remotely query attendance, front-row seating and head-up rates. Access is restricted to the campus network, and private screenshotting or recording is prohibited.
- **Wenzhou University** started an AI-powered smart patrol platform in March 2026, completing inspections of 258 courses on its first day.
- **Northeast Forestry University** adopted an "offline guidance plus cloud patrol" model in March, capturing attendance, front-row seating and head-up rates.
- **Southern Medical University** upgraded a hundred classrooms with AI image and posture recognition for "drop-in" virtual listening, and reported measurable gains.

## Does It Work?

Southern Medical University offers the most concrete evidence cited. Per its November 2025 announcement, comparing the spring and autumn 2025 semesters, campus-wide attendance rose 16%, student attention rose 14%, and the average classroom AI score climbed 9.65 points. The share of classes scoring above 80 rose to more than 45%, with some core colleges' average scores gaining 10.42, 8.75 and 9.62 points respectively.

For administrators, the appeal is clear: shifting teaching-quality evaluation from "experience-based judgment" toward "data-driven" and "digital-evidence-based" models, a goal aligned with China's broader push for "AI + education" initiatives. Privacy safeguards cited by universities include restricting access to authorized supervisors and banning private screenshots and sharing.

## Privacy and the Meaning of Engagement

Yet critics question both the efficacy and the ethics. Biometric surveillance installed without student consent raises data-protection concerns, and skeptics argue that metrics like head-up rate flatten the rich, varied ways students actually learn—turning classrooms into what one Caixin commenter called "data workshops" where "everything can be measured, but the person may not be seen clearly."

Cost is another flashpoint. One reader commenting on The Paper argued that if the system really costs tens of millions of yuan, "raising teacher pay and teaching quality would be the real business."

## What's Next

Nanjing University said it plans to pilot attendance statistics this semester to support course construction and teaching reform with objective data, framing the effort around student-centered principles and privacy protection. How that pilot is received—and whether other institutions follow suit or pull back—will shape the next phase of China's experiment in AI-assisted higher education. For now, the balance between better teaching and intrusive monitoring remains unresolved.
