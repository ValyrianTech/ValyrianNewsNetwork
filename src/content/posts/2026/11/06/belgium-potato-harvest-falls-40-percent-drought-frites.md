# Belgian Potato Harvest Falls 40% as Drought Hits Frites

Belgian potato growers are bringing in a harvest roughly 40 percent smaller than last year, a steep decline that threatens the country's celebrated frozen-fries industry and could push up prices for consumers. As the national "Frite Week" gets under way, the sector is confronting a crop that is both too small in volume and, for many tubers, too small in size to meet the demands of processors.

According to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/781141/article/2026-11-05/patates-belges-une-recolte-inferieure-de-40-par-rapport-lan-dernier), the yield is also about 20 percent below the multi-year average. The shortfall is the product of two forces colliding: a deliberate reduction in planted area following last year's record surplus, and a scorching, rain-starved summer that stunted the crop.

## A summer the potato could not tolerate

The root cause, growers say, was exceptional heat and drought. Between mid-June and mid-August, Belgium endured three heatwaves and two months of uninterrupted dry weather.

"The summer was very dry and very hot, with three heatwave periods and two months of uninterrupted drought between 15 June and 15 August," Pierre Lebrun, coordinator and director of Fiwap, the Walloon potato sector body, told Le Soir. "As the potato is a crop suited to temperate and humid environments, it did not appreciate it."

He added that the rains which eventually arrived were a stroke of luck: no violent storms followed, sparing crops from serious damage. For some varieties, however, the harm was already done.

## The size problem: too small for the fryer

Belgium is the world's largest exporter of frozen fries, and its processors have exacting requirements. The dominant frying variety, Fontane, must reach a minimum calibre of 50 millimetres to be suitable for long frozen fries. This year, only about half of the Fontane crop clears that threshold, according to industry monitoring cited in the research.

The consequence is a shortfall in the precise raw material the processing sector needs, even before the question of overall volume is considered. Smaller tubers can be diverted to other products, but they are a poor fit for the long fries that underpin Belgium's export trade.

The North-Western European Potato Growers (NEPG) has warned that tubers are shorter and smaller than average this season, with an elevated risk of early sprouting, particularly in Belgium and France. Within Belgium itself, Flanders has fared slightly better than Wallonia.

## From glut to scarcity in a single season

The irony of the 2026 harvest is that it follows a year of plenty. In 2025, European growers produced a record crop of roughly 27.3 million tonnes across the NEPG region, collapsing prices through oversupply. Belgian growers struggled to shift the surplus, and the free-market price for factory potatoes fell as low as zero euros per tonne in spring 2026, with hundreds of thousands of tonnes left unsold in storage, according to the Belgian potato trade body Belgapom.

In response, growers across north-west Europe cut their planted area. Belgium reduced its potato area by 17 percent, compared with 15 percent in the Netherlands, 11 percent in Germany and 10 percent in France, according to analysis by the Energy & Climate Intelligence Unit (ECIU). The drought then landed on top of that deliberate contraction.

The market has swung accordingly. Belgian free-market potatoes that fetched nothing in April were quoted at more than 200 euros per tonne by September, the ECIU analysis found. Contract prices, by contrast, have remained subdued, having been agreed at 15 to 25 percent below last year's levels before the scale of the shortfall became clear.

## Counting the cost

The ECIU estimates that the drought inflicted 405 million to 622 million euros in weather-related losses across Germany, France, Belgium and the Netherlands at pre-drought contract prices, with a central estimate of about 498 million euros. Including Great Britain, the range rises to between 498 million and 818 million euros.

Tom Lancaster, a land, food and farming analyst at the ECIU, framed the shortfall as a climate signal rather than a one-off. "As one of Europe's thirstiest crops, it was inevitable that the potato harvest would be hit hard," he said. "For potato growers on the front line of climate change, the chips are well and truly down."

Cedric Porter, editor of World Potato Markets, pointed to a pattern. "Weather conditions are becoming more extreme and challenging," he said. "There have been four droughts since 2018... Investment in water management... will be essential to the continued success of the crop."

Market intelligence firm DCA has estimated output across the four main north-west European producers at about 20.8 million tonnes, down roughly a quarter on 2025. Belgium's own decline, at about 21 percent below the five-year average, is the sharpest of the four, according to the ECIU.

## A national symbol under strain

The harvest numbers carry unusual cultural weight. The fritkot, Belgium's roadside fry stand, is inscribed on UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list, and Walloon friteries serve an estimated 130 tonnes of fries a day. Roughly one in five Belgians visits a friterie each week.

That makes the timing of the shortfall pointed: the "Semaine de la Frite," running from 6 to 13 November in Wallonia, celebrates the frites value chain from producer to frituriste. This year's edition arrives with growers, in Le Soir's phrase, "le moral dans les chaussettes" — their spirits in their socks.

## What's next

The immediate question is how much of the price pressure reaches consumers. Belgium's processing sector operates heavily on contracts, and a large carry-over from the 2025 surplus cushions some of the impact, so the squeeze may not be felt at the fry counter straight away. Analysts are watching February to April 2027 as the window when residual quality problems, contract fulfilment and stock levels will become clear.

Beyond this season, the harvest raises harder questions. Will processors turn to imports of raw potatoes or finished product to meet their commitments? Could the size and sprouting issues lead to rejected or downgraded volumes and contract disputes in early 2027? And will the sector use the shock to rethink itself — through irrigation investment, more drought-tolerant varieties, or a different balance between processing and fresh markets?

The drought of 2026, coming so soon after the glut of 2025, suggests that Belgium's fry supply chain will need to adapt to a climate that no longer behaves as the potato — or its growers — once expected.
