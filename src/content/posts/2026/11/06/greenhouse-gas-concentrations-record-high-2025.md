# Greenhouse gas concentrations hit new record high in 2025

Atmospheric concentrations of the three major long-lived greenhouse gases reached unprecedented levels in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) warned on 27 October, committing the planet to further warming for decades to come. The finding, published in the agency's annual Greenhouse Gas Bulletin, sets a sobering backdrop for the COP31 UN climate summit opening in Antalya, Türkiye, on 9 November.

The [WMO](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high) reported that the globally averaged surface concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2) reached 426.8 parts per million (ppm) in 2025 — 153 percent of the 1750 pre-industrial baseline. Concentrations of methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O), the second and third most important long-lived greenhouse gases tied to human activity, also rose to new records.

"Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies. The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue."

## Concentrations, not emissions

The bulletin stresses a distinction that is often lost in climate debates: it measures concentrations — the accumulated stock of gases in the atmosphere — rather than new annual emissions. About half of the CO2 emitted each year remains in the atmosphere, while the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans. Because CO2 persists for centuries, concentrations keep climbing even as emissions growth slows.

When the bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 level stood at 377.5 ppm — nearly 50 ppm below today's figure. Growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from an annual average increase of 0.8 ppm to 2.4 ppm per year in the 2011–2020 decade. From 2024 to 2025, CO2 rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024.

According to NOAA's Annual Greenhouse Gas Index, radiative forcing — the warming effect on the climate — from long-lived greenhouse gases increased by 56 percent between 1990 and 2025, with CO2 accounting for roughly 81 percent of that rise. CO2 alone is responsible for about 66 percent of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases.

## Sink recovery, not a slowdown

WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed the comparatively smaller annual increase to the recovery of land carbon sinks after the strong 2023/2024 El Niño, which had reduced the biosphere's efficiency through drought, drier vegetation and more wildfires. "However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," she cautioned.

She warned that concentrations will likely climb further in 2026 because of continued fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful new El Niño, which will once again weigh on land carbon sinks. Risks of reduced CO2 uptake by the oceans are also rising in a changing climate.

Methane reached 1,948 parts per billion (ppb) in 2025 — 267 percent of the 1750 level — and accounts for about 16 percent of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases, though its atmospheric lifetime is far shorter than CO2's. Roughly 65 percent of methane emissions come from human activities such as cattle, rice farming, fossil fuel exploitation, landfills and biomass burning. Nitrous oxide reached 339.1 ppb, 126 percent above pre-industrial levels.

## A transition that carries its own risks

The bulletin also flags two less abundant but highly potent trace gases tied to the energy transition. Sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), used as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, posted a record increase in 2025; it has an atmospheric lifetime of over 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO2. Molecular hydrogen (H2) concentrations rose 5.3 percent between 2010 and 2024, and leakage from production, pipelines and fuel cells could undermine hydrogen's climate benefits through indirect warming effects.

"The energy transition is essential and welcome, but as with every transition, it has to be done in a smart way and informed by environmental observations," Barrett said.

## A warning for COP31

WMO releases the bulletin each year to arm negotiators at the UN climate conference. The [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779336/article/2026-10-27/une-nouvelle-annee-un-nouveau-record-la-concentration-gaz-effet-de-serre-atteint) report on the findings, carried by AFP, underscored the gap between the accumulating atmospheric stock and the global pledges meant to limit it. COP31 in Antalya, running from 9 to 20 November 2026, is seeking to turn climate commitments into concrete actions.

With the 11 warmest years on record all occurring in the past 11 years, the bulletin makes clear that emissions cuts — however sharp — will not immediately reverse the trend. The world has already banked decades of additional warming. What remains to be decided is how quickly the flow of new emissions falls, and how well societies adapt to the extremes the accumulated stock will keep producing.

For negotiators in Antalya, the message from Geneva is unambiguous: the record set in 2025 will not be the last.
