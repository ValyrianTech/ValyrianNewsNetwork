# Judge Blocks Trump's 250-Foot Arch Hours After Approval

A federal judge early Friday blocked construction of President Donald Trump's proposed 250-foot triumphal arch at Memorial Circle, issuing a 14-day restraining order less than two hours after a federal planning commission packed with presidential allies granted the project final approval.

U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan, an Obama appointee, finalized the temporary restraining order just before midnight and made it public minutes later, barring the government from beginning any construction work on the arch for 14 days. The order consolidates lawsuits filed by the governments of D.C. and Virginia, which sued days earlier, and by a group of Vietnam War veterans and an architectural historian, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/06/nx-s1-6031990/trump-arch-planning-commission-injunction).

## A Compressed, 11th-Hour Sequence

The timing was extraordinary even by the standards of a project that has been steeped in legal conflict for months. The National Capital Planning Commission voted 8-4 to approve the arch just before 10 p.m. Thursday, after nearly nine hours of public testimony from veterans, lawmakers, residents and preservation experts. The Trump administration notified the court of the approval within half an hour. By midnight, Chutkan had issued her ruling.

In a 33-page opinion, the judge scrutinized the administration's competing justifications for the arch, particularly a late emphasis on national security, and agreed that the local governments are likely to be harmed by construction. The planned 250-foot structure would stand in Memorial Circle, a traffic island on the bridge linking the Lincoln Memorial to Arlington National Cemetery.

"The threatened harms will materialize as soon as construction begins, in part because the traffic disruptions will occur immediately," Chutkan wrote, according to NPR.

## The Legal Case Against the Arch

The litigation centers on a straightforward claim: Congress never authorized the arch, a statutory prerequisite for memorials on that land. The veterans and historian, represented by Public Citizen Litigation Group, sued in February. D.C. and Virginia filed a joint suit on Oct. 31, calling the project "flagrantly unlawful," according to [The Independent](https://www.the-independent.com/news/world/americas/us-politics/trump-arch-lawsuit-dc-virginia-unlawful-b3076881.html).

"President Trump's plan to erect a monument in Memorial Circle without the approval of Congress is flatly unlawful," Wendy Liu, an attorney with Public Citizen Litigation Group and lead counsel on the case, said in a statement announcing the suit, according to [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/news/vietnam-veterans-architectural-historian-sue-over-trump-arch/). "We are calling on the Court to block this unlawful project."

The plaintiffs argue the arch would obstruct the historically significant sightline between Arlington House and the Lincoln Memorial, a view designed to symbolize national unification after the Civil War. They also contend the structure would violate D.C.'s height restrictions, federal environmental and historic preservation laws, and add risk to the region's complex airspace.

"This sacred site and this historic view should not be desecrated by the planned Arch," said Jon Gundersen, a Vietnam War veteran and former U.S. diplomat who is a plaintiff in the case, according to Public Citizen.

## A Commission Vote That Critics Called a 'Sham'

The planning commission's approval followed a marathon session that drew 131 people signed up to speak. The livestream crashed minutes into public testimony, lengthening the meeting by about an hour. Over the course of the night, dozens spoke against the arch; only a handful, including Republican Reps. Andrew Clyde of Georgia and Brian Mast of Florida, offered support.

The commission's Trump-appointed majority dismissed concerns about D.C. height restrictions and preservation laws. They maintained that Congress had authorized the project when it approved a pair of 166-foot columns on Memorial Circle in 1925 that were never built. They also pointed to recent claims by Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth that the monument would enhance national security.

That argument drew scrutiny. Interior Department official Greg Wischer said "specific national security functions have been filed under seal" in court, but that the National Park Service "has not been privy to any national defense preparations," according to NPR. Hegseth had written in a court filing that the arch "will be utilized as a dual-purpose structure: a major national monument that also incorporates military utility to support Homeland defense," The Independent reported.

D.C. Council Chair Phil Mendelson, a Democrat on the commission, sought to postpone the vote, arguing that the panel had not received a final site and building plan. His motion failed 4-8. Vice Chair Stuart Levenbach, a Trump appointee, cited the project proponent's "time constraint" and desire to "commence building very quickly."

Historic preservation groups condemned the vote. Brent Leggs, president and CEO of the National Trust for Historic Preservation, called it a "sham" that "undermines the democratic process, violates federal statute, and insults our nation's veterans who have made the ultimate sacrifice."

## Analysis: A Test of Executive Power

The case has become a high-stakes test of separation of powers and the limits of unilateral executive action. The administration had told the court it could begin excavation work the same day approval came, despite lacking congressional authorization. It planned to work "20 hours a day, year-round, for two to three years."

Critics frame the arch as part of a broader Trump effort to memorialize himself in the nation's capital, alongside projects such as a White House ballroom and changes to the Kennedy Center and the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool. Many Democratic lawmakers have cast their midterm wins on Tuesday as a referendum against such undertakings and have vowed to investigate the project after regaining a congressional majority in January.

"While this decision prevents Donald Trump from immediately implementing his plans to create a monument to his own ego, the courts and Congress must put in place permanent safeguards to stop the Trump Administration from violating the law and proceeding with this project," Rep. Don Beyer, a Virginia Democrat, said in a statement, according to NPR. Beyer has introduced a bill to prohibit and defund the arch and said he expects co-sponsors to grow from 80 to at least 200.

Public opinion appears to cut against the project. An April Ipsos survey found 52 percent of Americans opposed the arch, while 21 percent favored it, The Independent reported. Trump himself has offered conflicting framings of its purpose, telling reporters the arch was meant to mark the nation's 250th anniversary, but answering a question about whom it was for with a single word: "Me."

## What's Next

The 14-day restraining order is temporary, and the underlying legal fight is far from resolved. The administration is widely expected to appeal, and the case could move quickly through the federal courts given the compressed timeline the government has sought. Whether Congress acts — through Beyer's bill or broader oversight — could determine the project's fate as much as the judiciary.

For now, Memorial Circle's grass, already marked with white paint lines laid last weekend, will stay untouched. The question of whether a president can build a monument of this scale on federal land without Congress's blessing now rests with the courts.
