# Engie rules out forced nationalization of Belgian reactors

Engie chief executive Catherine MacGregor has said that a forced nationalization of the French energy group's Belgian nuclear fleet "is not on the agenda," pushing back against threats from the federal government as high-stakes buyout talks drag on. Speaking on the sidelines of Engie's quarterly results on Thursday, MacGregor said the company remained confident the Belgian State would negotiate in good faith, according to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/781249/article/2026-11-06/une-nationalisation-forcee-nest-pas-lordre-du-jour-engie-sur-le-parc-nucleaire), which relayed reporting by the business daily *L'Echo*.

The remarks come as the Belgian government and Engie remain far apart on the terms of a State takeover of the country's nuclear power assets — an operation codenamed "Operation Aurora" — with a key agreement now pushed back to 12 December.

## A deal that keeps slipping

The buyout has been under negotiation for more than six months. The Belgian government and Engie committed in April 2026 to negotiate "in good faith" towards a protocol of agreement, with an initial deadline of 1 October. That target was missed and postponed by mutual agreement, citing the "complexity and scale" of the operation. A second deadline of 4 November was missed as well, as [Le Soir reported](https://www.lesoir.be/780988/article/2026-11-04/rachat-du-nucleaire-letat-et-engie-loupent-une-nouvelle-echeance), with both sides now aiming for 12 December.

At the heart of the deadlock is the question of who bears the cost of decommissioning the reactors — a multi-billion-euro liability. Engie argues the government overestimates the decommissioning risk, and therefore the provisions it wants set aside to cover it. The State, for its part, is seeking to shield taxpayers from a future cleanup bill that could run into the billions.

"We are confident in the government's willingness to negotiate in good faith. A forced nationalization is not on the agenda and is not part of the discussions," MacGregor said, as reported by [RTL Info](https://www.rtl.be/actu/belgique/societe/reprise-du-nucleaire-par-engie-une-nationalisation-forcee-nest-pas-lordre-du/2026-11-06/article/805730).

## The shadow of expropriation

The reassurance follows an unusually blunt pressure campaign from Prime Minister Bart De Wever. On 27 October, *De Tijd* and *L'Echo* revealed that the prime minister had had draft legislation prepared to forcibly nationalize Engie's nuclear assets should negotiations fail. Under that scenario, the State would unilaterally set the method for calculating compensation owed to Engie, which could contest the decision in court while the government proceeded with the nuclear extension it wants.

As [Le Soir detailed](https://www.lesoir.be/779340/article/2026-10-27/centrales-nucleaires-de-wever-brandit-la-menace-dune-expropriation-dengie), a forced nationalization is legally possible in principle but could trigger lengthy appeals — and the threat was widely read as a tactic to unblock last-minute talks rather than a settled plan.

MacGregor's public dismissal of that option can be read as Engie calling the government's bluff, or at least signalling that it does not expect the expropriation route to be used. It also keeps the negotiation on a commercial footing, avoiding the legal uncertainty and valuation damage an expropriation fight would create.

## A fragile negotiating table

The talks are unfolding against a backdrop of political turmoil in Brussels. De Wever tendered his resignation after his "Arizona" coalition failed to agree on a budget requiring roughly €10 billion in savings. On 6 November, King Philippe refused the resignation and asked the prime minister to resume budget negotiations "as of now" — a decision that leaves both the government's authority and the nuclear file in limbo.

Engie, in other words, is negotiating with a government that may be on its way out. That instability cuts both ways: it weakens the Belgian side's leverage and continuity, but it also raises the risk that any agreement reached now could be revisited by a successor coalition.

## What to watch

Belgium's nuclear fleet has shrunk dramatically. Of the seven reactors the country once operated, only Doel 4 and Tihange 3 remain in service — their operational life extended to 2035 by the previous government — while the other five have been permanently shut down. The Arizona coalition's stated goal is to relaunch and extend nuclear production, making the buyout strategically important well beyond its balance-sheet value.

All eyes now turn to the 12 December deadline. If a Head of Terms still cannot be reached, the question of whether the government actually deploys its expropriation legislation returns to the fore — and with it the prospect of a protracted legal battle between the Belgian State and Engie. For now, both sides insist they want a deal. The distance between them is measured not in principle, but in provisions for a cleanup bill that neither wants to own alone.
