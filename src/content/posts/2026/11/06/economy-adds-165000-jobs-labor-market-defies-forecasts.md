# Economy Adds 165,000 Jobs as Labor Market Defies Forecasts

The U.S. economy added a surprisingly strong 165,000 jobs in October, and the unemployment rate dipped to 4.1%, the Labor Department reported Friday — a performance that far exceeded economists' expectations of roughly 59,000 and signaled a labor market still absorbing workers comfortably. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/06/nx-s1-6030867/jobs-labor-market-economy-wages-inflation), the gains came as prior months' figures were revised sharply upward, with August and September combined adding 84,000 more jobs than first estimated.

The report arrives just days after midterm elections in which inflation and the cost of living dominated the debate, and at a delicate moment for the Federal Reserve, which is weighing whether to resume raising interest rates to fight inflation that remains above its 2% target.

## Broad-Based Gains

The strength was not confined to a single industry. Professional and business services led all sectors with 70,000 new jobs, followed by health care at 42,000, transportation and warehousing at 25,000, and manufacturing at 19,000, [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/06/jobs-report-october-2026.html) reported. Restaurants and construction companies shed jobs.

"You have to look pretty hard to find something to dislike in there," Dan North, senior economist at Allianz Trade North America, told CNBC. "It's a really, really good report, and all around I think you'd say it's stronger than expected and the best one in a while."

The breadth of hiring marked a notable shift from 2025, when the economy added barely 10,000 jobs a month and nearly all gains came from health care. "The overall picture is a labor market that is on much better footing than a year ago," said Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union. "There's a much more broad based range of opportunities."

Seema Shah, chief global strategist at Principal Asset Management, called the report "a strong rebuttal to fears that the economy is heading into a meaningful slowdown."

## Wages Still Lag Prices

Beneath the headline strength, the report contained warning signs for American workers. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% for the month and 3.1% from a year earlier — a bigger annual increase than the prior month, but still likely not enough to keep pace with rising prices. Inflation climbed to [3.7% in September](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/14/nx-s1-6000593/inflation-consumer-prices-cpi-social-security-benefits), driven in large part by an energy price spike tied to the war with Iran.

"Workers are losing ground right now," Cory Stahle, an economist at the Indeed Hiring Lab, told NPR. "The longer it wears on, the more and more they're going to feel that."

Long-term unemployment also rose sharply. The average duration of unemployment jumped to 27.1 weeks, up 2.3 weeks, the highest since September 2021. Those out of work for more than 27 weeks climbed to 1.96 million, the most since December 2021. A broader measure of unemployment that includes discouraged workers and those in part-time jobs for economic reasons rose to 7.9%.

## A Political Backdrop

The report lands in the wake of a midterm election rout that cost Republicans their majorities in both chambers of Congress, with voters' frustration over high prices a decisive factor. The political fallout has already spilled into public view: Vice President JD Vance has blamed the Iran war for the party's losses, a notable break with President Trump.

The data also intensifies the Fed's dilemma. Central bankers held their benchmark rate steady the prior week, but with inflation stubbornly above target, traders now assign roughly 77% odds to a quarter-point rate hike at the December 8–9 meeting, according to the CME Group's FedWatch tool cited by CNBC. Market reaction was otherwise muted, with Treasury yields higher across the board.

Workforce participation edged up to 61.9%, and the civilian labor force expanded by 421,000 — a sign that a labor pool that had been shrinking due to demographics and reduced immigration is beginning to recover.

## What's Next

The October report offers a paradox: a labor market that is objectively stronger than a year ago, yet one in which many workers continue to lose purchasing power. Whether that tension eases will depend largely on energy prices and the Fed's next moves. All eyes now turn to the December policy meeting, where a rate hike could test whether the job market's surprising resilience can withstand tighter financial conditions — and whether policymakers can cool inflation without derailing the momentum Friday's numbers revealed.
