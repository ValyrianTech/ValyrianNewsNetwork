# Eternit Strike Ends With Five-Year Job Guarantee

The strike at fibre-cement producer Eternit in Kapelle-op-den-Bos has ended after management agreed to guarantee that no major new round of redundancies will take place for five years, union and management sources confirmed on Friday. Workers began returning to the plant in phases, though 84 jobs remain under threat from a restructuring that has gripped the site since late September.

The dispute was never only about the number of job losses. It turned on whether the remaining workforce could count on the factory having a long-term future at all — a question that now carries a firmer, if still conditional, answer.

## From 3 years to 5

The breakthrough came after a marathon negotiating session on Thursday that ran from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m., during which management raised its job guarantee from three years to five. Union secretary Danny Absillis of the socialist ABVV told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/eternit-kapelle-op-den-bos-staking-opgeheven/) that the earlier three-year offer had betrayed a lack of ambition for the plant.

"Management first promised only a three-year period with no new redundancy round. That does not give a rosy view of the future outlook management has for the factory," Absillis said. Of the upgraded commitment, he added: "Those two extra years may sound banal, but the fact that management states that it too strives for the survival of the factory means a lot. The five-year guarantee will be included in the agreement."

The guarantee is the centrepiece of a provisional settlement that ended the second bout of industrial action at the site in barely six weeks. It secures medium-term certainty for the roughly 300 staff who will remain, but it does not roll back the redundancies already set in motion.

## A second walkout

Eternit announced on 28 September that it intended to launch a collective redundancy procedure under the so-called "Renault law", threatening 95 jobs — 81 blue-collar and 14 white-collar positions, or about a quarter of the plant's 386-strong workforce. Workers downed tools the same day, with union representative Sven Robijn describing the reaction as news that "hit like a bomb." The company cited competition from non-European producers, whose costs are "more than 50 percent lower," alongside overcapacity that the war in Ukraine had sharpened, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/09/28/95-jobs-bedreigd-bij-eternit/). Some production lines at the site were running below 30 percent of capacity.

Tensions boiled over again on the evening of Tuesday 3 November, when talks escalated after management indicated that some of the workers who kept their jobs would have to give up 10 to 15 percent of their pay by moving to a different work regime. Absillis called it "the drop that made the bucket overflow." At that point the number of threatened jobs had been trimmed from 95 to 81, as [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/04/eternit-kapelle-op-den-bos-staking-vakbond-overleg/).

## Investment as a signal

Eternit's management has sought to frame the restructuring as a bid to secure the site rather than wind it down. In a statement during the dispute, the company pointed to planned investments of €19 million in 2026 and €27 million in 2027, which it said would "strengthen the site, create more stable employment and focus production more on high-quality and distinctive products."

The Kapelle-op-den-Bos plant sits within the Etex group, the multinational building-materials company. A sister Etex business, Promat, operates nearby and had open vacancies that unions had hoped might absorb some of the affected Eternit workers — an avenue that remains part of the broader discussion about avoiding forced redundancies.

## A defensive win

The value of the five-year guarantee lies as much in its symbolism as in its contractual weight. For unions, the longer horizon signals that management is committing to the plant's survival, not merely managing its decline. Yet the deal leaves the 84 threatened jobs unresolved, and the pay-regime change that triggered the second strike remains a live concern for those who stay.

The dispute also fits a wider pattern across Belgian and European heavy industry. Legacy manufacturers in energy-intensive sectors — steel, cement, chemicals and building materials — are being squeezed by cheaper non-EU imports and by overcapacity worsened since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. In that context, worker demands have shifted from one-off compensation toward longer-term security, and the Eternit settlement reflects that shift.

## What comes next

Unions and management are due to meet again on Tuesday 10 November to discuss avoiding forced layoffs, the financial package for voluntary leavers, and a supplement for unemployment on medical grounds for long-serving workers who spent years in the plant. In the meantime, production is restarting gradually; as Absillis noted, "you cannot simply restart a production line, this happens in phases."

Several questions remain open: whether the five-year guarantee is legally binding, whether it covers the whole site or only certain product lines, and whether the voluntary-departure terms will satisfy a workforce that has now struck twice in six weeks. The answer to those questions will determine whether this week's agreement marks a genuine turning point for the factory — or merely a pause.
