# First-time buyers scarce, but a record share pay all cash

First-time homebuyers made up just 22% of U.S. home purchases in the past year, near a record low, even as a record share of those who did buy — 1 in 10 — paid all cash, according to a [new report](https://www.nar.realtor/research-and-statistics/research-reports/highlights-from-the-profile-of-home-buyers-and-sellers) from the National Association of Realtors. The twin findings, detailed by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/05/nx-s1-6022491/real-estate-all-cash-offer), point to a housing market increasingly divided between buyers who can draw on inherited wealth and those who cannot.

## A share that has roughly halved

Before the Great Recession, roughly 40% of primary-residence buyers were purchasing their first home. That share has now roughly halved. This year's 22% reading ticks up only slightly from the record low of 21% reported a year earlier, underscoring how a persistent housing shortage and elevated prices continue to sideline many would-be owners.

The buyers who do manage to break in are older and wealthier than in the past. The median age of all first-time buyers remains at a record high of 40 — up sharply from around 30 in 2010. Among those who financed their purchase, the median age was 38. Among those who paid all cash, it was 54.

"Inheritances are really driving this," said Jessica Lautz, deputy chief economist at the NAR. First-time buyers who paid all cash were more likely than those who financed to use an inheritance (28%) or the proceeds from selling an inherited property (21%).

## Cash offers reshape the competition

Real estate agents say the shift is visible on the ground. "I would say in the first 15 years of my career, I saw virtually zero first-time home buyers paying all cash," said Jason Miller, a broker with Trust Real Estate Company in St. Cloud, Minn. That changed after the pandemic, when competition for homes surged. "They knew they had to absolutely have the most sterling offer possible to compete in the competitive marketplace," he said.

High mortgage rates have reinforced the trend, since financing a home for 30 years has become far more expensive. And not every family waits until death to pass on wealth. Miller said more clients are drawing help from relatives who have paid off their own home or hold equity in a second property they can sell — treats viewed as a kind of "future inheritance."

In still-competitive local markets, family money can decide a bidding war. Nate Johnson, a broker associate with RedKey Realty Leaders in St. Louis, said that of perhaps 10 offers on a table, "maybe three of those offers will be all cash." Some buyers later refinance into a mortgage and repay the relative who fronted the cash.

## A widening wealth divide

Even as personal savings remained the largest source of down payments for first-time buyers overall, more buyers tapped assets — a 401(k), stocks, or cryptocurrency — than turned to family or friends for financial help. Older buyers have had more time to save, Lautz said, but she sees another factor at work: "I think it's uncomfortable to ask mom and dad for a gift or loan when you're hitting 40 versus your young 30s."

The NAR report warns of a structural shift. "There is a growing divide among those who are saving independently and priced out of homeownership, versus those who are waiting on the sidelines but then given a generational transfer of wealth," the report noted. The share of first-time buyers relying on inheritances is likely to grow as baby boomers reach their 80s and beyond.

That divide is personal for those on the losing side. Seth Daphne Esmeson, a Seattle resident who will soon turn 33, said they and their spouse have good jobs but also debt from a car payment and student loans. They have decided that buying does not make sense at today's prices. "We have the short end of the deal," Esmeson said, and "the gap is expanding." The only realistic path, they said, might be an inheritance — a prospect they find painful: "I love my mom dearly and I don't want to look forward to that."

## A market shaped by lock-in

The forces behind these numbers extend beyond first-time buyers. Repeat buyers' median age has climbed to an all-time high of 62, up from 36 in 1981, reflecting a "lock-in effect": homeowners who secured low mortgage rates during the pandemic are reluctant to sell, constraining inventory. Sellers now have owned their homes a median of 12 years before listing — also a record — while for-sale-by-owner transactions remain near a historic low of 5%. Fully 90% of buyers and 92% of sellers used an agent or broker, the highest buyer share in four decades.

## What to watch

The data, drawn from a survey of transactions between July 2025 and June 2026, suggests that a short-lived dip in mortgage rates nudged the first-time buyer share up just one point — hardly a recovery. With affordability still strained and generational wealth increasingly the entry ticket, the central question is whether easing rates and new inventory can widen the door before the divide hardens further. For now, the market's message is clear: cash, much of it inherited, is king.
