# Haenel Wins Femina, Jaenada Takes Goncourt

France's autumn literary season reached its climax this week as the country's two most prestigious book prizes were handed out in Paris. Philippe Jaenada won the Prix Goncourt on Tuesday, November 3, for his crime-investigation novel *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel* (Flammarion), and Yannick Haenel took the Prix Femina the following day for *La solitude des professeurs est infinie* (Gallimard), the [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576) reported.

## A Divided Verdict

The distinction carried an unusual twist. Haenel, 59, had been a finalist for the Goncourt itself, but the nine members of the Académie Goncourt gave six votes to Jaenada and three to Haenel, according to the [Goncourt announcement](https://www.rtbf.be/article/goncourt-2026-le-prestigieux-prix-a-ete-attribue-a-philippe-jaenada-pour-son-livre-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel-11809864). The split verdict underscored how the season's most acclaimed titles converged in the two juries' deliberations.

Jaenada, 62, was announced by Académie Goncourt president Philippe Claudel from the steps of the restaurant Drouant, the Paris venue that has hosted the proclamation since 1914. "I feel joyful," Jaenada said on arrival, in remarks carried by RTBF.

*L'Inconnue du quai de Javel* follows Jaenada's established method of long-form true-crime investigation, in which he digs through archives and visits the scenes of the crimes he examines. The novel returns to the killing of a young Breton woman in the late 1940s. Claudel praised the book for giving voice to a forgotten victim, telling reporters it "makes visible a woman who was made invisible."

The author previously won the Femina in 2017 for *La Serpe*. He succeeds Laurent Mauvignier, whose 2025 Goncourt winner *La Maison vide* became a publishing phenomenon, selling close to 640,000 copies.

## A Novel for a Restless Autumn

Haenel's Femina, announced at the Dior headquarters in Paris, went to a book rooted in the classroom. *La solitude des professeurs est infinie*, released in August, follows Jean Deichel, the author's fictional double, as a young trainee French teacher arrives at a middle school in Mantes-la-Jolie, in the Paris suburbs. It depicts the stress, the drive to transmit knowledge and the trials of the profession.

Accepting the prize, Haenel dedicated the book "to the teachers, to the lycéens and to the collégiens." The gesture landed squarely in the middle of a national debate. An autumn movement of high-schoolers and students — which began in the Île-de-France region in late September and spread to Normandy and beyond — has demanded replacement teachers, smaller classes, repair of dilapidated buildings and reform of the Parcoursup university-admissions platform, as [The Conversation](https://www.rtbf.be/article/climat-emploi-avenir-les-raisons-derriere-la-colere-de-la-jeunesse-11806070) recounted, republished by RTBF.

Haenel said he felt the need to write about the solitude of teachers after the assassinations of Samuel Paty in 2020 and Dominique Bernard in 2023. In the novel he evokes both the joy of a lesson that works and the harder truth that, whatever pedagogues say, teaching often means defusing violence before it takes hold.

The all-female Femina jury of 12 chose Haenel over fellow finalists Emma Marsantes (*N'efface pas mes cercles*, Verdier) and Lucie Rico (*Mais que fait ce sang sur le pull de Jennifer ?*, P.O.L). The Femina étranger went to the American writer Laura Kasischke for *Baignade surveillée* (Gallimard), and the Femina essai to Jennifer Tamas for *Barbe Bleue contre-enquête* (Seuil).

## The Third Crown

The same Tuesday brought a third major award. François-Henri Désérable won the Prix Renaudot for *Le Palais* (Gallimard), a novel that plunges into the world of wine through a character blessed with an extraordinary palate, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-prix-renaudot-2026-attribue-a-francois-henri-deserable-pour-le-palais-11809873). Like the Goncourt, the Renaudot is announced moments after the senior prize each year.

## A Season Shadowed by Controversy

The 2026 prizes were also marked by an unusual controversy. The heavily promoted debut novel *C'était ça ou mourir* by Franco-Haitian Canadian author Thélyson Orélien was removed from the Goncourt selection, and from other prize lists, over suspicions of plagiarism and use of artificial intelligence. The affair prompted debate about transparency and AI in literature and even spawned a satirical counter-prize.

A structural note: all four 2026 Goncourt finalists were published by houses in the Madrigall group, the family holding of Antoine Gallimard and France's fourth-largest publisher, which also published the 2025 winner. And the Académie Goncourt deliberated with nine members rather than ten, after the death in August of Didier Decoin, the 1977 Goncourt laureate and former jury president. A replacement is expected by the end of the year.

## What to Watch

The commercial stakes are considerable. The Goncourt is a powerful sales driver, and the Femina typically gives its winner a substantial lift through the holiday season. Both winning novels were already among the best-selling titles of the season before the juries spoke.

Beyond sales, the two awards point to a French literary establishment absorbed by two questions: who gets to be seen, as in Jaenada's recovery of a forgotten murder victim, and what the country owes its teachers, as in Haenel's classroom chronicle. The commercial outturn will be measurable in early 2027; the cultural argument is already underway.
