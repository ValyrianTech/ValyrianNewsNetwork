# Belgian Readers to Crown the New Word of 2026

French-speaking Belgium's leading newspaper Le Soir and public broadcaster RTBF have opened the twelfth edition of their joint "Nouveau mot de l'annee" campaign, inviting readers and listeners to submit the terms that best capture the past twelve months. According to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779880/article/2026-10-30/quel-est-le-nouveau-mot-de-lannee-2026-vos-propositions), proposals are being collected until midnight on 15 November, after which a jury will shortlist ten words for a public vote running from 4 to 20 December.

## A Decade of Cataloguing Change

The operation, now in its twelfth year, has become a fixture of the francophone cultural calendar. Its academic anchor is Anne-Catherine Simon, professor of French linguistics at UCLouvain and president of the jury, who frames the exercise as a barometer of social transformation rather than mere wordplay.

"The launch of this operation is always an opportunity to measure the significant transformations in society that have pushed French speakers to create or borrow new words," she told Le Soir. Such words, she argued, are "witnesses" to these shifts, whether in new ways of living, new dangers or new technical developments.

The list of past winners illustrates that ambition. *Ultracrepidarianisme* took the 2021 crown, *velotafer* won in 2022, *bombe climatique* in 2023 and *aura* in 2024. Last year's title went to *technofascisme*, which captured 29 percent of the vote.

## Technofascisme Sets a High Bar

As [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-mot-de-l-annee-2025-est-technofascisme-ou-lorsque-la-technologie-numerique-est-un-instrument-de-pouvoir-autoritaire-11654315), roughly 3,300 readers and listeners voted in 2025, handing *technofascisme* a clear victory ahead of *dronisation* (15 percent) and *brainrot* (14 percent). The jury defined the winning term as a "political doctrine that seeks to dismantle liberal democracies in order to install an authoritarian regime, relying on algorithms and social networks to manipulate collective behaviour and bypass representative mechanisms."

That result has one immediate consequence for 2026: *technofascisme* is already taken and cannot be proposed again. The winning terms of previous years carry weight beyond the competition itself. As [Le Soir reported](https://www.lesoir.be/719454/article/2025-12-30/technofascisme-elu-nouveau-mot-de-lannee-2025) when it crowned the 2025 winner, the contest's output has increasingly been read as a signal of democratic unease, with Simon drawing a line from *trumpisation* in 2016 through *post-verite* and *illiberalisme* to last year's victor.

## How the Vote Works

Submissions must be words that either appeared in 2026 or acquired new popularity or a new meaning in recent months. A jury then takes over. It comprises Simon and Cedrick Fairon of UCLouvain, representatives of the Le Soir and RTBF newsrooms, and two outside voices: the stand-up comedian Siham Bouzerda and the humorist Bruno Coppens. They will reduce the submissions to ten finalists, which then go to a public vote from 4 to 20 December. The verdict is expected at the end of the year.

Simon has offered an early forecast of the themes likely to dominate. "Let's wager that there will still be talk of AI, of political and climate disruption and, of course, of words used by younger generations," she told Le Soir.

## More Than a Game

For all its playful framing, the operation carries a linguistic purpose that its organisers take seriously. Simon points out that several past winners have entered French dictionaries, including *chiller*, *justice restaurative* and *narcomicide*. She describes the contest as "an opportunity to observe the lexicon in the making and inscribing itself in the history and heritage of the French language."

That argument was echoed in Le Soir's [editorial assessment](https://www.lesoir.be/719483/article/2025-12-30/technofascisme-le-mot-de-lannee-comme-coup-de-semonce-democratique) of the 2025 result, which described *technofascisme* as pointing to "a major transformation: the growing alliance between technological power and authoritarian temptation, at the expense of democratic debate."

## What's Next

With submissions open until 15 November, the juries of readers and listeners now have two weeks to define the vocabulary of a year marked by artificial intelligence, political turbulence and climate anxiety. The ten finalists will be revealed before the December vote, and the winning word will close out 2026 — and, if history is any guide, may well find its way into a dictionary.

For now, the invitation stands: propose the word you hear everywhere, the one you think will outlast the year. In the words of the campaign, *a vos mots*.
