# Bomb Threats Hit European Flights, Media Outlets

A wave of false bomb threats disrupted European aviation for a third consecutive day on Friday, targeting flights to and from Prague, Copenhagen and France, and prompting the evacuation of several Irish media buildings in Dublin. The alerts follow one of the most disruptive weeks on record for European air travel, in which at least 18 passenger flights were affected on Thursday alone, including a LOT Polish Airlines flight from Warsaw to Brussels.

## A Pattern of Threats

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/), the Warsaw-to-Brussels aircraft landed safely at Zaventem, where Belgium's federal police carried out a risk analysis. "After investigation by the federal police, no additional measures turned out to be needed," the airport's press office said. A second LOT flight, bound for Paris, was halted during taxiing and searched after a warning about a possible dangerous object aboard, but no suspicious package was found.

The disruptions were not confined to Belgium. An Aer Lingus Airbus A320 flying from Dublin to Rome with 157 people aboard was diverted to a French military airbase at Évreux, west of Paris, escorted by French fighter jets. In Italy, a Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome was shadowed by Italian warplanes to Fiumicino, while a Wizz Air service from Milan to Warsaw diverted to Vienna. Three Ryanair flights in Bulgaria received threats, with two landing at Sofia and a third diverting to Plovdiv.

## Earlier Waves and a Growing Toll

The Thursday wave followed already-unusual incidents earlier in the week. As [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/vliegtuig-noodlanding-polen-bommelding/), at least seven flights received bomb threats on Tuesday, with aircraft in Polish airspace making emergency landings at Warsaw-Modlin, which briefly closed. A Ryanair flight from Manchester to Murcia and two aircraft bound for Sofia were also affected that day. No explosives were found in any case.

By Friday, the disruption had spread further. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/nieuwe-bommeldingen-in-europa/), Czech police reported threats against flights to and from Prague's airport, with at least eight flights involved, including a Wizz Air service from Prague to Rome that was diverted to Venice. Danish police returned to Copenhagen Airport for a second consecutive day. French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu's office said four new threats were received concerning aircraft over France, three of which landed at Orly and one at Bordeaux.

The threat also extended beyond aviation. Buildings belonging to several Irish media organisations, including public broadcaster RTÉ, the Irish Independent, Newstalk, Today FM and Virgin Media Television, were evacuated in Dublin after an emailed bomb threat. RTÉ reported that the email claimed a link to the flight threats and was attributed to a sender calling himself "Mikhail Andropov," who described himself as a leader of a group carrying out bomb threats across Europe. The email's origin could not be verified.

## Responsibility Unclear

The question of who is behind the coordinated warnings remains unanswered. EU Commissioner for Sustainable Transport and Tourism Apostolos Tzitzikostas said on Thursday it was "too early to link the incidents to each other," calling any threat against civilians "unacceptable" and stressing that national investigations are still ongoing.

Analysis by [De Morgen](https://www.demorgen.be/nieuws/live-nieuwe-golf-valse-bommeldingen-bij-vluchten-boven-europa-zeker-acht-vluchten-naar-en-van-praag-betrokken~b8e82b28/) notes that European governments have long accused Russia and Iran of hybrid attacks and sabotage, but no link to the bomb threats has been established. Some of the threats were signed by people posing as the "warriors of the Male State," a reference to a Russian ultranationalist movement, though that connection could not be confirmed. The UK-based aviation security consultancy Osprey Flight Solutions observed that seven confirmed threats in a single day is "exceptional," and that the absence of a clear target in terms of country, airline or route suggests the sender's aim is above all to disrupt air traffic.

## What to Watch

With budget carriers such as Ryanair and Wizz Air appearing disproportionately affected and new threats still arriving on Friday, European authorities face a persistent challenge: distinguishing between isolated hoaxes, copycat incidents, and a coordinated campaign. Ireland's Prime Minister Micheál Martin said it was too early to determine whether the threats came from an individual or were coordinated, but described the aviation alerts as clear "coordinated attempts" to disrupt flights. For travellers, the practical reality is one of delays, diversions and fighter-jet escorts—and the question of how long Europe's aviation security apparatus can absorb the strain.
