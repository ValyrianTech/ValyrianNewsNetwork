# Moscow and Beijing Forge a Strategic Arctic Alliance

Russia and China are deepening a highly strategic partnership in the Far North, building what analysts describe as a "Polar Silk Road" of ports, shipping lanes and technology exchanges as melting ice unlocks new routes and resources. The rapprochement is reshaping the geopolitics of the Arctic, and alarming Western capitals, prompting the European Union to adopt a new security-focused Arctic strategy in October.

According to a geopolitical analysis published by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719) as part of the European public-broadcaster exchange "Vu d'Europe," climate change is the engine behind the rivalry. The Arctic is warming up to four times faster than the global average, opening previously inaccessible commercial sea routes while the region's subsoil, estimated by the USGS to hold roughly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and about 13% of undiscovered oil, sharpens appetites.

## A Deepening Partnership

The October visit of Nikolai Patrushev, an adviser to Vladimir Putin and chairman of Russia's Maritime Board, to China illustrated the push to expand maritime cooperation. Per [Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/), discussions covered linking China's Silk Road with Russia's trans-Arctic transport corridor, developing Russian ports, exchanging technology for automated container terminals, and joint maritime research.

Cooperation has widened considerably over the past decade. In 2023 the two countries signed a key Arctic cooperation agreement. Chinese entities now co-finance large extraction projects such as Yamal LNG and are significantly expanding their own nuclear icebreaker fleet.

The motivations are complementary. Moscow wants to deliver more oil and gas to China, evade Western sanctions tied to its war in Ukraine, and secure export routes for its "black gold" beyond European waters. Beijing, meanwhile, seeks an alternative sea route to reduce its dependence on the Strait of Malacca, and access to Russian Arctic resources and infrastructure. In its 2018 strategy, China presented itself as a "near-Arctic state" with legitimate interests in shipping, resource development, scientific research and regional governance.

Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi has framed the partnership in strategic terms. "China stands ready to work with Russia to align maritime strategies and leverage their complementary strengths to protect each other's legitimate maritime interests," he said, according to the [South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/diplomacy/article/3370690/china-russia-deepen-maritime-and-arctic-logistics-ties-secure-energy-supply).

## The Northern Sea Route

At the heart of the partnership is the Northern Sea Route, the Russian-administered shipping lane along Russia's Arctic coast. The route dramatically shortens distances: Shanghai to Hamburg takes roughly 18 days via the Arctic, versus 30 to 35 days through the Suez Canal.

The numbers are climbing. In 2026, Russian oil exports via the Northern Sea Route rose by nearly 50% year-on-year, with about 20 million barrels delivered to Asia between July and mid-October, according to [Modern Diplomacy](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/10/19/russia-ramps-up-arctic-oil-exports-as-global-shipping-crisis-disrupts-trade-routes/), citing Reuters. Gazprom Neft plans to raise deliveries by around 35% this year, and Putin claims freight volume could double to 70 million tonnes per year by the end of the decade, aided by Rosneft's Vostok Oil project.

In August, the Chinese shipper Sea Legend launched the first regular weekly container line across the Arctic, realizing Beijing's long-sought ambition. South Korea and India are also eyeing the route.

Asia Times cautions, however, that Suez traffic remains far larger, and Arctic shipping is constrained by ice, high insurance premiums, sparse infrastructure and a short navigation season. "For Moscow, Chinese capital, shipping demand and technology could help turn an ambitious Arctic strategy into a functioning logistics system," the outlet noted. "The geopolitical consequences will undoubtedly be closely watched in Washington."

## Western Alarm and Military Build-Up

Western states are watching China's Arctic activity with growing suspicion. In 2025, the U.S. Coast Guard reported [five China-linked research vessels](https://www.news.uscg.mil/Press-Releases/Article/4374170/us-coast-guard-highlights-historic-operational-successes-in-2025/) operating in or near the U.S. Arctic and stepped up its own patrols. Western governments accuse Beijing of using research stations for military purposes, a charge China denies.

"China's Arctic ambitions are meeting growing opposition from Western Arctic states wary of its strategic intentions," the [East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/21/china-adapts-to-a-colder-arctic-reception/) observed, while noting that China's military capabilities in the region will remain limited in the near future.

Sweden's head of military intelligence, Thomas Nilsson, warned in an interview with [The Times](https://www.thetimes.com/world/europe/article/china-take-advantage-russia-the-arctic-fgfx6k8ww) that China "will use" Russia to gain a foothold in the Far North. Moscow, he suggested, once wary of expanding Chinese influence in the Arctic, now has little choice, depending on Beijing to sustain its war effort through oil and gas sales and for dual-use goods such as microchips.

Russia, which controls about 53% of the Arctic coastline, has meanwhile moved to remilitarize the region. Under Putin, Moscow has reactivated Soviet-era bases and, according to [Defence24](https://defence24.com/geopolitics/cold-war-at-the-top-of-the-world-the-militarisation-of-the-arctic-and-the-sino-russian-alliance-on-the-northern-sea-route), deployed Bastion coastal-defense systems, Arctic-adapted S-400 air defenses and hypersonic-missile-armed fighters, and conducts nuclear-submarine exercises.

"Russia is expanding its military presence in the North. Moscow has reopened Soviet-era bases and is testing new weapons in the region," EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said. "Drone incursions, GPS jamming and the sabotage of undersea cables are undermining Arctic security."

## Europe's Answer

The EU unveiled its own Arctic strategy in October, built around four priorities: security and resilience, combating climate change, prosperity for Arctic communities, and international cooperation.

The strategy aims to strengthen Europe's icebreaker fleet, a long-standing Finnish demand, and to intensify monitoring of sabotage and Russia's "shadow fleet." A new Centre for Maritime Security would extend shadow-fleet tracking beyond the Mediterranean and Baltic to the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean, though it is not yet operational and requires member-state buy-in. The EU also plans a "sovereign and secure" subsea cable linking Greenland to the continent and the IRIS2 satellite constellation to compete with Elon Musk's Starlink.

"The Arctic has changed dramatically, and the whole world has changed, especially since Russia's aggression against Ukraine," said Jozef Sikela, the EU commissioner who led the strategy. "The Arctic is warming up to four times faster than the global average and has become the object of genuine geopolitical rivalry."

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen put it starkly: "The Arctic is one of the places where the tectonic plates of geopolitics collide."

## What to Watch

The central question is whether the Sino-Russian Arctic axis proves structural or merely transactional. Russia needs Chinese capital, demand and technology to make its Arctic ambitions operational; China needs resources and a route that bypasses Malacca. But the asymmetry increasingly favors Beijing.

On the Western side, capacity remains the sharpest gap: the United States has only two aging operational icebreakers, versus more than 40 Russian vessels. Whether Washington funds an expansion, and whether the EU's strategy can deliver on a ten-icebreaker fleet and a not-yet-operational security centre, will shape the balance of power in the thawing Far North.
