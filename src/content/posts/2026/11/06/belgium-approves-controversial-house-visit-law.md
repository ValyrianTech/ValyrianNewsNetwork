# Belgium Approves Controversial House-Visit Law

Belgium's federal Chamber of Representatives approved a controversial law early on 5 November that allows police to enter private homes to arrest people without valid residence permits, capping a marathon overnight debate that ran until just after 5 a.m. The so-called *woonstbetredingen* (house-visit) bill, championed by Asylum and Migration Minister Anneleen Van Bossuyt (N-VA), passed the full plenary session after a committee had already endorsed it in October, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/woonstbetredingen-bossuyt-parlement-ice/).

## What the law does

Under the measure, police may enter a private residence between 5 a.m. and 9 p.m. to arrest people without valid papers, with the aim of enforcing deportation — for example, transfer to a closed detention centre. The law is designed to make it easier to expel people in irregular stay who persistently refuse to cooperate with their return.

Several safeguards are written into the text. It applies only to people who pose "a danger to public order or national security"; an arrest is possible only if a decision already exists that the person cannot stay in Belgium and it is clear they are not complying. An investigating judge (*onderzoeksrechter*) must always authorize the entry, one or more officials from the Immigration Office (Dienst Vreemdelingenzaken) must be present, and a locksmith may be called to break open a lock. People may be arrested even if children are present, though children themselves cannot be arrested; the law states that separating children from parents must be avoided and trauma minimized.

## Sharp criticism from legal and civil society

The law drew opposition from across the legal establishment. The Association of Investigating Judges, representing all 116 investigating judges, advised Parliament not to adopt it, arguing existing tools for house searches suffice. They were backed by the two bar associations, OVB and Avocats.be, which noted there is no avenue for appeal when an investigating judge authorizes an entry.

Henri Heimans, a retired investigating judge, called the law a "juridisch gedrocht" ("a legal monstrosity") on VRT NWS *De Ochtend*, arguing the phrase "a danger to public order or national security" is too vague. In the same article, VRT noted that Heimans was a 2024 candidate for the opposition party Groen. The Federal Migration Centre, Myria, warned about the impact on children: "De scheiding van gezinsleden wordt vermeden, staat in het ontwerp, maar niet verboden" ("Avoiding the separation of family members is in the bill, but it is not prohibited"). Police themselves reportedly raised practical safety concerns.

Civil society compared the house visits to the violent arrest operations of ICE, the U.S. immigration enforcement agency, with protest banners reading "No ICE in Belgium."

## Protests on the eve of the vote

On 4 November, thousands of people formed a human chain around the Brussels Palace of Justice at Poelaertplein, with simultaneous actions in Ghent, Leuven, Bruges, Namur and Charleroi, as [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/04/duizenden-demonstranten-vormen-menselijke-ketting-rond-justitiep/). Organizers described a national mobilization with more than 50 gatherings at town halls across the country. Opponents argue the bill violates the inviolability of the home and the right to private and family life.

## A vote amid a government crisis

Opposition parties PS, PVDA, PTB and Ecolo-Groen made a last-ditch request to postpone the vote, citing the federal government crisis, but it was rejected. Vlaams Belang abstained, arguing the bill had been diluted. Within the majority, no votes against were recorded; Les Engagés MP Xavier Dubois, long critical of the law, was absent.

The vote landed in the middle of a severe political crisis. Around 2 November, Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) offered his resignation to King Philippe after the government failed to reach an agreement on the 2027 budget, as [Politico](https://www.politico.eu/article/belgian-government-in-crisis-over-failed-budget-talks/) reported. The crisis was the explicit reason the opposition sought to delay the house-visit vote.

Van Bossuyt hailed the outcome. "We kunnen dit toch wel historisch noemen" ("We can certainly call this historic"), she said, adding that after "years of political opposition and a lot of disinformation, it can now finally enter into force." She said the aim is to deport "rapists, drug offenders and hate preachers," who "will no longer be able to hide behind a front door." She stressed that it is the independent investigating judge — not the minister — who decides whether someone poses a danger to public order or national security.

At the committee stage in October, the minister had already sought to reassure critics, describing the measure as "geen heksenjacht" ("not a witch hunt") and defending the investigating judge's role. A [VRT NWS report](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/07/woonstbetredingen-kamercommissie/) from that stage noted that Leuven had already announced it would not permit house visits.

## Local resistance and what comes next

Multiple municipalities have adopted motions opposing the law, including the City of Brussels and Leuven. Leuven's mayor, Mohamed Ridouani (Vooruit), has said he will have to implement the law "dik tegen zijn goesting" ("very much against my will") — a delicate position for parties that govern both locally and federally.

The central legal question remains how narrowly the investigating judges will interpret "danger to public order or national security" in practice, given the absence of a statutory definition. The law is widely expected to face a challenge before the Constitutional Court, on grounds that could include privacy, non-discrimination, children's rights and the lack of an appeal route. How quickly the measure is operationalized will also depend on the unresolved budget crisis and on how municipalities and mayors handle non-cooperation. Belgium has now placed one of its most contentious migration tools on the statute book; the fight over how it is used is only beginning.
