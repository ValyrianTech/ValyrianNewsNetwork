# Kremlin Denies Role as Hackers Target Europe's Freezers

The Kremlin on Thursday flatly rejected accusations that Russia was behind a wave of coordinated cyberattacks that remotely overrode supermarket refrigeration systems across Europe, calling the claims "absurd" — even as two pro-Russian hacktivist groups claimed responsibility and cybersecurity experts put the likelihood of Kremlin-linked involvement as high as 95 percent.

The attacks, which struck overnight on 2–3 November, pushed freezer temperatures in supermarkets across at least seven countries to as high as 50°C (122°F), spoiling food and forcing stores to empty their shelves. Belgian chains including Delhaize, Colruyt, Carrefour and Intermarché were among those affected, according to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/02/supermarket-freezers-across-europe-hit-by-second-suspected-cyber-attack).

## Moscow Pushes Back

"These are absurd claims," Kremlin spokesman Dmitri Peskov told journalists, as [HLN](https://www.hln.be/buitenland/kremlin-ontkent-russische-betrokkenheid-bij-cyberaanvallen-op-diepvriezers-in-europese-supermarkten-absurde-beschuldigingen~a90633ba/) reported. "As always, any substantiation, any evidence is missing. And yet such unfounded accusations are made against our country, as if Russia is involved in everything."

No European authority has formally attributed the attacks to a state actor, and hard public evidence of Russian state direction remains absent. The episode has nonetheless revived a familiar pattern in European security debates: a disruptive cyber-operation, a claim of responsibility by pro-Russian groups, and a denial from Moscow that leaves the central question unresolved.

On 4 November, two collectives — NoName057(16) and Z-Pentest — claimed responsibility via a Telegram video and posts, publishing what they said were 2,500 lines of login credentials from Danish refrigeration manufacturer Danfoss. The messages were deleted shortly afterwards, and the claims could not be independently verified. Even their authenticity would not prove how attackers gained entry.

## How the Attack Worked

The November wave followed a much larger operation on 11–13 October that hit as many as 700 French supermarkets, pushing freezers from as low as –27°C to well over 50°C and causing tens of millions of euros in losses. Individual stores reported losses ranging from tens of thousands of euros to more than €130,000.

Rather than deploying malware, attackers appear to have exploited internet-exposed refrigeration controllers and remote-management interfaces — largely Danfoss and Dixell XWEB units — often protected by weak or default passwords. "A few commands to a temperature controller can destroy stock overnight and close stores," Patrick Lamplmair of the cybersecurity firm Tributech told Reuters, noting that thousands of controllers remained reachable from the internet even after the French attack.

"Retail is an easy target because retail chains run enormous estates of refrigeration controllers, building management systems, door access panels and point-of-sale gear," said Tom Hegel of SentinelOne. The absence of any financial demand, he added, points to sabotage rather than extortion.

## A Belgian Nightmare

In Belgium, the disruption was felt from distribution level down to individual franchisees. Stephan Moelans, chief executive of Sabcobel, which operates Delhaize's refrigeration systems, told Le Soir that his teams "started mobilising technicians at 1am" and spent "all night and morning disconnecting the affected installations."

Ahold Delhaize spokesperson Sarah De Meester said distribution centres were unaffected and deliveries continued, though fresh and frozen ranges were "temporarily somewhat limited" in affected shops. Colruyt reported problems in five stores, Intermarché in 15 and Carrefour Belgium in around 15 of its 700 outlets. A Carrefour spokesperson was blunt: "It's a cyber-attack, there is no other reason."

For smaller operators, the financial hit is direct. Franchisees of a Limburg Delhaize told HLN they expected to absorb tens of thousands of euros in losses themselves — a reminder that the cost of such attacks often lands on the businesses least able to withstand it.

## The Attribution Question

European officials have not been reticent about the broader threat. EU Food and Agriculture Commissioner Christophe Hansen called the repeated attacks "a deliberate attempt to disrupt our food supply chain and undermine consumers' confidence in it."

Others go further. Xavier Bellekens, chief executive of the Scottish cybersecurity firm Lupovis, estimated a 95 percent probability that a Kremlin-linked group was responsible, based on similarities to digital infrastructure previously used in sabotage operations tied to the Russian government. Belgian ethical hacker Alvin Coessens cautioned that far less is known about the two collectives: NoName057(16) is estimated to have around 4,000 active members and has previously targeted Belgian government and media sites, but who directs them remains unclear.

The attribution gap matters. Both groups emerged from a milieu of pro-Russian hacktivism that Western governments have linked to state interests — Denmark's military intelligence has publicly described NoName057 and Z-Pentest as tools of Russian hybrid warfare, and the US Justice Department charged actors in December 2025 over Russian support for related collectives. Yet analysts note that the November claim surfaced only after media had already named the groups as suspects, raising the possibility of claim-jumping for propaganda value.

## A New Front in Hybrid War?

The deeper question, as [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/781217/article/2026-11-05/cybermenace-lepisode-des-frigos-marque-t-il-le-debut-dattaques-sur-les) asked, is whether the freezer episode marks the beginning of a sustained campaign against physical and industrial infrastructure. The same week, attackers targeted Coolworld, a supplier to the French blood establishment, whose freezers were switched to defrost mode — a sign that the vector is not confined to retail.

The technique's appeal to attackers is its economy. Exposed operational-technology devices are abundant, remediation is slow, and the damage is immediate. Forescout has counted almost 13,000 vulnerable services across more than 6,000 public IP addresses in 99 countries, and roughly 80 percent of scanned Danfoss units carried known vulnerabilities.

That combination — cheap, effective and hard to attribute — makes recurrence likely regardless of who ordered the attacks. France's Paris prosecutor has opened a formal investigation, and its cybersecurity agency ANSSI has issued guidance urging operators to remove internet exposure, use VPNs, update firmware and change default passwords.

## What to Watch

Three questions will shape how this story develops. First, whether any EU or Belgian authority — the Centre for Cybersecurity Belgium or the federal prosecutor — formally attributes the attacks to a named group or state. Second, whether the credential dump attributed to Danfoss is authenticated and confirmed as the entry vector. And third, whether the vector migrates from supermarket refrigeration to more critical systems such as water, energy and food logistics.

For now, the Kremlin's denial stands unchallenged by formal attribution, and the groups' claims remain unverified. What is not in doubt is the vulnerability the episode exposed: Europe's cold chain, it turns out, was reachable from the internet — and someone reached it.
