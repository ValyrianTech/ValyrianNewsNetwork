# China Exposes 10 Cases of AI-Fabricated Online Rumors

China's Ministry of Public Security has publicly named 10 cases in which individuals used artificial intelligence tools to fabricate and spread online rumors, a disclosure intended to warn the public against AI-fueled disinformation, [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261124/da721fd5f4394e0c97616f98ecdcabe8/c.html) reported. The cases, published by the ministry's Cybersecurity Bureau and released under a year-end roundup framing, span fabricated disasters, fake police imagery, bogus food-safety scares and impersonation of public figures.

The release is part of the bureau's "Clean Net 2026" (净网—2026) campaign, an annual cybersecurity enforcement drive that authorities have run in successive thematic waves throughout the year, each targeting a specific category of online rumor.

## A Year-End Deterrent, Not a Single Event

The November 24 disclosure is a consolidated roundup rather than breaking news. Earlier waves this year focused on a different rumor category each time: a batch in late May and early June included a viral AI-generated video of a supposed collapse at the Zhangjiajie glass bridge; 20 cases in late July centered on flood and disaster-related rumors; and 14 cases in mid-August targeted corporate and business rumors. Each batch has followed the same "typical cases" format that the ministry has used to signal enforcement priorities.

## The Ten Cases

Three of the cases drew the most serious response. In the first, a man surnamed Ma used AI to generate a fake video showing the Zhangjiajie Grand Canyon glass bridge collapsing with crowds fleeing, a clip that spread widely and caused public panic before [local police in Cili County reported](https://hn.rednet.cn/content/646049/57/15913770.html) his arrest and the imposition of criminal coercive measures. A second suspect, surnamed Wu, generated a fake video of a Guangzhou night market submerged in floodwater despite no such disaster occurring. A third, surname Han, produced fake images and videos featuring police insignia and vehicles, and depicted himself in a People's Police uniform to mislead netizens. All three were placed under criminal coercive measures.

The remaining seven cases resulted in administrative penalties. They include a fabricated claim of a 4.6 billion yuan rare-earth smuggling bust in Lianyungang; a forged celebrity social media post used to stoke a fan dispute; a fictional gaokao exam score-swap story; a fake video of an "artificial poison rice" production line in Hubei; false reports of a norovirus outbreak that supposedly closed schools across several provinces; an AI-generated video falsely claiming a school's uniforms contained plastic; and the unauthorized AI impersonation of a public figure to sell goods via livestream commerce.

## Why the Cases Split Between Criminal and Administrative

The severity gradient reflects China's legal distinctions. Fabricating false information about dangers, epidemics, disasters or police incidents — and spreading it widely enough to seriously disrupt social order — falls under Article 291-1 of the Criminal Law, which carries up to three years' imprisonment and up to seven years when serious consequences result. Lesser disturbances are handled under Article 29 of the Public Security Administration Punishments Law, which provides for detention of five to ten days and fines of up to 1,000 yuan.

The pattern is deliberate: disaster and accident rumors, along with impersonating police, trigger criminal coercive measures, while consumer, food, exam and entertainment fabrications draw administrative sanctions.

## A Tightening Regulatory Net

The enforcement push sits atop a broader labeling regime. China's Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content, issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China and three other departments, took effect on September 1, 2025, and require all AI-generated text, images and video to be clearly labeled. Crucially, the labeling rule is a transparency requirement rather than a safe harbor — content that fabricates facts can still be punished even when marked as AI-generated, and stripping labels before republishing is separately penalized.

For content creators, the message is a warning. The ministry has repeatedly used these roundups as a deterrent, and the decision to give AI-generated disinformation its own consolidated year-end batch signals that the category has become a recognized enforcement priority distinct from ordinary rumor-mongering.

## What's Next

The release does not disclose nationwide enforcement statistics for the year, nor does it detail how authorities technically detect AI-generated content or whether platforms bear liability for failing to flag it. Those gaps leave open questions about how the labeling regime will be enforced at scale. What is clear is the trajectory: as AI tools become cheaper and easier to use, Chinese authorities are treating the ability to manufacture convincing falsehoods as a public-order problem — and calibrating the punishment to the panic it produces.
