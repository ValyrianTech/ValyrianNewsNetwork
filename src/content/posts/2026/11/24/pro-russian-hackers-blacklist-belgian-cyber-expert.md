# Pro-Russian Hackers Blacklist Belgian Cyber Expert

The pro-Russian hacktivist collective NoName057(16) has placed Belgian cybersecurity expert Pascal Geenens on a public blacklist of individuals it deems hostile to Russia—and is offering cryptocurrency bounties for additional sensitive information about them. The listing, confirmed to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/23/noname-hackers-hacktivist-pro-russisch-eastwood-zwarte-lijst-pas/) by Geenens himself, marks a shift from the group's usual website-disruption attacks toward the targeted intimidation of named researchers and officials.

## A researcher on the front line

Geenens is vice-president of cyber threat intelligence at Radware, an Israeli firm that specialises in defending against distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks. He has tracked NoName057(16) closely for years, publishing half-yearly trend reports in which he has repeatedly concluded that the group is the most active hacktivist collective.

That visibility put him on the group's radar. According to VRT NWS, NoName maintains a project it calls "Tribunaal NoName057(16)"—a list of foreign citizens it accuses of working against the group or of anti-Russian activity. An English-language version of the appeal has circulated in a NoName Telegram channel since late September 2026. "Dear friends, we would like to remind you of our project," the post reads, adding that for useful information about the listed individuals—information not found in public sources—the group is willing to pay.

Geenens noticed his own name at the start of October. "NoName knows my name because I have been quoted in the press about the group many times and have written numerous reports," he said. "I knew I was on their radar. But this of course makes it very concrete."

## From DDoS attacks to doxxing

The listing is part of a broader escalation. A more detailed version with profiles of the targets appeared on a Tor-accessible dark-web page in late October. According to [Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/security/noname057-eu-officials-doxxing/), 18 high-profile European figures were doxxed, among them Italian Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani, Eurojust president Michael Schmid, Europol's European Cybercrime Centre head Edvardas Šileris, and German federal police president Holger Münch. Each profile reportedly contains email addresses, phone numbers, Telegram handles and social-media accounts—and, per the Italian news agency ANSA, résumés and home addresses.

Geenens is measured about the threat. "In principle I am not worried as long as the activities stay in the cyber world," he said. "My employer keeps an extra close eye on my account, and I have always shielded my family and private information as best I could." He remains cautious about one detail: the bounty. "The only thing that slightly concerns me is that they promise money for information, and from experience I know they pay."

## Belgium a persistent target

The case sits within a documented campaign against Belgium. The federal prosecutor's office told VRT NWS that between June 2023 and June 2026 the country was hit by roughly 1,500 DDoS attacks attributed to the pro-Russian hackers, striking about 220 organisations on 96 different days. The last recorded attack was on 19 June 2026. "The attacks came in waves," spokesperson Yasmina Vanoverschelde said. "They often coincided with political events or with Belgian support for Ukraine, for example around the local elections of October 2024." The federal prosecutor's office is currently running about ten investigations into the group.

The blacklist campaign appears to be a response to Operation Eastwood, the coordinated international strike of July 2025. [Europol](https://www.europol.europa.eu/media-press/newsroom/news/global-operation-targets-noname05716-pro-russian-cybercrime-network), which coordinated the operation with Eurojust, reported that law-enforcement authorities from twelve countries dismantled more than 100 servers, issued seven arrest warrants and notified over 1,000 supporters of their potential criminal liability. Belgium was among the supporting countries.

Geenens says the hackers claim his information was used in that operation. He stresses he did not actively cooperate but acknowledges that his published research into the group's attacks and tactics could have been drawn upon. The retaliation framing, analysts caution, remains an interpretation rather than a confirmed group statement.

## A signal worth taking seriously

The operational reach of NoName057(16) is contested. As Cybernews noted, researchers believe much of the posted material was already public and that the group tends to exaggerate its impact. But contact details can still be weaponised—for harassment, or for credential-stuffing attacks aimed at extracting more sensitive data such as home addresses. The Geenens case illustrates the personal toll borne by researchers who publicly document Russian-linked cyber operations, and the growing pressure on employers and national agencies to protect them.

For now, Geenens is keeping the threat in perspective—with a researcher's eye on the incentive that worries him most. As Western agencies weigh further enforcement, the question is whether targeted doxxing becomes a routine feature of Russia's hybrid playbook, and how Belgium's open investigations translate into protection for the people on the list.
