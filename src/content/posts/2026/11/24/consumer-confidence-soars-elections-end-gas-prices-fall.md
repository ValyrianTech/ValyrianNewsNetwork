# Consumer Confidence Soars as Elections End, Gas Prices Fall

Americans' confidence in the economy surged in November to its highest level in nearly two years, as two major sources of recent anxiety eased at once: the midterm elections concluded and gasoline prices fell sharply. The Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index jumped to 103.3 this month from 91.9 in October, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/consumer-economy-confidence-inflation-9d13d96cc3e4bac8f334f9145c718e42) — a reading that beat every economist forecast and marked the second straight monthly increase.

The improvement was broad, spanning all age groups and generations, with higher-income households posting the largest gains. It arrived after the index had sunk in September to its lowest level since April 2014, even below the depths of the pandemic.

## A Rebound After the Political Storm

The November 3 midterm elections were dominated by voter frustration over the rising cost of living, and Democrats won full control of both chambers of Congress, as [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/03/g-s1-149526/2026-midterms-congress-balance-of-power). With that contest resolved, a key source of economic uncertainty lifted.

"The headwinds and uncertainty generated by the midterm elections are no longer posing a risk to the economic outlook," Christopher Rupkey, chief economist at FWDBONDS, told [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/us-consumer-confidence-surges-highest-level-nearly-two-years-2026-11-24/).

The results carried a pointed political split. Confidence rose sharply among Democrats and independents but declined modestly among Republicans — a familiar pattern in sentiment surveys that track partisan control of government.

## Relief at the Pump

Gas prices have been in sustained decline. The national average for regular gasoline fell to $3.61 a gallon on Tuesday, down 17 cents from a week earlier and sharply below the $4.30 average a month before, according to AAA figures cited by AP.

The drop has been historic in scope. The national average plunged 50.5 cents between October 29 and November 19 — the biggest three-week decline on record outside the 2008 financial crisis, according to a [CNN review](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/19/economy/gas-prices-fall) of AAA data going back to 2000.

"Prices have been in a free-fall," Patrick De Haan, GasBuddy's head of petroleum analysis, told CNN. Multiple forces are at work: seasonal declines in driving as temperatures drop, oil futures retreating from triple-digit territory as the feared re-escalation of the US-Israeli war with Iran did not materialize, and the easing of refinery outages in the Great Lakes region.

Even so, gas is not cheap by historical standards. Prices remain well above the $3.07 motorists paid a year ago, and analysts caution that a sudden geopolitical flare-up could halt or reverse the decline. "Geopolitics are still the biggest wildcard," De Haan warned.

## A Stronger Job Market

The survey also revealed growing optimism about employment. The share of Americans describing jobs as "plentiful" soared to 30.8% — the highest since May 2025 — from 26.2% in October, according to Reuters. The share viewing jobs as "hard to get" fell to 18.4%, the lowest in more than a year.

The survey's labor market differential, a measure closely correlated with the official unemployment rate, widened to 12.4 from 4.8. That shift aligns with an acceleration in hiring: the unemployment rate ticked down to 4.1% in October from 4.2%.

"Write-in responses in November were slightly less negative than previous months," said Fergus McCormick, a senior economist at the Conference Board. "While consumers continued to express frustration with high prices and rising costs, some comments cited hope for the general economy."

## Inflation Still Casts a Shadow

The brighter mood does not mean inflation has been tamed. The consumer price index rose to 4.0% in October, up from 3.7% in September and still well above the Federal Reserve's 2% target. Consumers' one-year median inflation expectations eased only slightly, to a still-high 4.5% from 4.7%.

The strengthening labor market, however, is expected to keep the Fed on its tightening path. Economists expect the central bank to raise interest rates again in December and likely to keep tightening beyond that, as Reuters reported, as it works to bring inflation back toward its 2% target. Signs of a firmer job market, the report noted, are likely to clear the way for further increases beyond the December hike.

## An Encouraging Signal — With Caveats

Economists have grown wary of reading too much into confidence surveys. Americans have spent at a solid pace through 2026 even as sentiment readings languished, with consumer spending recording two back-to-back quarters of robust growth. The National Retail Federation nonetheless forecasts healthy holiday sales growth, even as prices rise faster than average weekly paychecks.

Rupkey flagged another risk on the horizon: whether turbulence in AI-related stock valuations could dent spending through the so-called wealth effect. "Time will tell whether the stock market's troubles with AI valuations will lead to a slowdown in real consumption spending," he said.

For now, the November reading offers a rare bright spot. A polarizing election is settled, the pump is kinder to household budgets, and hiring is holding up. What's next comes into sharper focus Wednesday, when the Commerce Department releases the inflation measure the Fed watches most closely — a data point that will help determine how much further the central bank raises rates as it presses its fight against inflation into the new year.
