# COP31 Ends Without Deal on Fossil Fuels and Finance

The COP31 climate summit closed in Antalya, Turkey, on Sunday after two days of overtime, ending without agreement on a set of overarching political decisions — the longest UN climate conference on record and the first time a summit's central political deal has collapsed since Copenhagen in 2009.

According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20261123/dd34253be20e45e793580b9e6ab3a67b/c.html), the conference did adopt a series of decisions on issues including a just transition, mitigation, adaptation and finance, but delegates could not reach consensus on the "political decisions" that were meant to crown the two-week gathering. The failure leaves the most contentious questions — how explicitly to address fossil fuels and how concretely to fund poorer countries — unresolved ahead of COP32 in Ethiopia next November.

## A Summit Defined by Two Fault Lines

The collapse centred on two linked disputes. The first was whether the final text would explicitly name **fossil fuels**, the primary driver of global warming. A first draft circulated earlier in the week mentioned "fossil fuel" or "fossil fuels" six times. That language was unacceptable to oil-producing nations, and the revised draft presented in the early hours of Sunday dropped the phrase entirely, retaining only an indirect nod to the COP28 agreement in Dubai to "transition away" from fossil fuels in energy systems.

That was too much for the EU, Pacific island nations, Panama and others. The EU's representative, Irish Minister Darragh O'Brien, called it "unconscionable that we would have a climate pact that makes no mention of fossil fuels," adding that the decisions "go nowhere near the level of ambition needed," as [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cqwy06zrd26lo) reported.

On the other side, Saudi Arabia — speaking for the 22 Arab League countries — and Russia resisted any explicit phase-out language. Saudi delegate Mohammad Ayoub argued that Arab nations account for a small share of historical greenhouse gas emissions, while Russia's delegate warned that moving away from oil and gas too quickly would cause shortages and "fuel poverty." About 130 governments, roughly two-thirds of those represented, backed explicit language on moving away from fossil fuels, but the consensus rule gave the blocking minority effective veto power.

## Finance Gap Leaves Poorer Nations Empty-Handed

The second fault line was money. At COP30 in Brazil last year, nations agreed to triple finance for adaptation — helping poorer countries cope with more intense heatwaves, droughts and floods. But this year's text contained no concrete figures and no baseline against which the pledge would be measured, raising fears that richer nations would have too much room to manoeuvre.

"Rich nations and polluting governments cannot keep arriving empty-handed," said Mohamed Adow of the African climate think tank Power Shift Africa.

## Guterres: 'Deeply Disappointed'

UN Secretary-General António Guterres, attending his final COP before stepping down, said he was "deeply disappointed" and took aim at what he called "powerful fossil fuel interests" taking "a page from Big Tobacco's playbook: distorting the science, delaying policy, blocking action, and piling up huge profits while people everywhere pay the price."

COP31 President Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister, said parties had failed to agree on the text and so the decision was not adopted, criticising some countries for being "hung up on one word or phrase" and for bringing unrelated issues into the climate talks.

China's delegation head, Vice Minister of Ecology and Environment Li Gao, said Beijing was "deeply disappointed" but urged countries not to lose faith in the multilateral process. "Mutual recrimination does not build trust," he said, adding that upholding multilateralism and solidarity is the only way forward. A day later, Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun repeated that China was "disappointed" but stressed the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and called the global green transition "irreversible."

## A Process Under Strain

The failure has revived debate over whether the UN's consensus-based model can function in a divided world. Because any single party can block a decision, a determined minority can stall the entire conference — a dynamic critics say gives outsized influence to petro-states. Former US Vice President Al Gore said it was "outrageous that a handful of petro-states have veto power over any mention of fossil fuels," and argued the COP process is "in need of significant reform."

The geopolitical backdrop sharpened the deadlock. The United States had no official delegation in Antalya, and COP31 was the first annual UN conference to formally discuss how the 1.5°C target from the 2015 Paris Agreement is likely to be missed. The world is currently on track for roughly 2.8°C of warming by 2100 under current policies, according to the UN, and 2026 could end as the hottest year on record.

UN climate chief Simon Stiell said he would seek advice on possible reform of the consensus process ahead of COP32. The process has "suffered setbacks before and come back stronger," he told delegates. "It must do so again."

## What Survived — and What Comes Next

The conference was not a total loss. Delegates made progress on a "just transition mechanism" to support workers and communities shifting to clean energy, and more than 80 countries backed Turkey's target to raise electricity's share of global energy use to 35% by 2035. COP31 was also notable for its unusual dual-presidency model, co-hosted by Turkey and Australia, with Turkey's Kurum serving as COP President and Australia's Climate Minister Chris Bowen as President of Negotiations. Bowen attributed the outcome to "the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory" and "unbridgeable red lines."

The road now runs to Addis Ababa. COP32, scheduled for November 2027, will inherit both the unresolved text and a harder question: whether a consensus-bound process can still deliver binding ambition, or whether the EU and a "coalition of the willing" will increasingly pursue parallel agreements outside the formal UN framework. Former UN climate chief Christiana Figueres, an architect of the Paris Agreement, argued that countries were right to walk away rather than endorse a weaker political commitment that would undermine progress. Whether that refusal preserves ambition or simply postpones the reckoning is the question COP32 must answer.
