# The One Route 66 Gas Station That Welcomed Black Drivers

During the Jim Crow era, Black families traveling Route 66 — the highway that stretched more than 2,000 miles from Chicago to Los Angeles — had to carry their own food and gasoline, because most restaurants, gas stations, and motels refused to serve them. But a small, family-owned business just east of Oklahoma City offered fuel, food, and a place to rest for the night. According to the [National Trust for Historic Preservation](https://savingplaces.org/stories/preserving-the-legacy-of-the-threatt-filling-station), the Threatt Filling Station was the only known Black-owned and operated gas station along the famous "Mother Road."

As [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/24/nx-s1-5928337/threatt-filling-station-oklahoma-route-66) recounts in a new feature marking Route 66's centennial, the station embodied a form of welcome that was rare and dangerous to find in an age of segregation. Its story is at once a portrait of Black travel during Jim Crow and a modern restoration saga, as the descendants of its founder work to bring the long-closed property back to life.

## A refuge built by a family

According to Linda Fisher, the granddaughter of founder Allen Threatt Sr., the station did whatever travelers needed. "Grandpa took care of whatever they needed to have," she told NPR. "If they didn't have any food, they fed them. If they needed some kind of shelter or blankets or those types of things, Grandpa and Grandma made sure they got those kinds of things."

At the Threatt Filling Station, she said simply, "everyone was welcome."

That openness stood in stark contrast to the surrounding landscape. Threatt had moved from Alabama to the farming community of Luther, Oklahoma, in the early 1900s as part of the Great Migration, seeking stability and a farm of his own; his father had been born into slavery. Oklahoma was one of the few places where Black migrants could own land and form their own towns. Between 1865 and 1920, Black Americans settled more than 50 all-Black towns in the state — the most of any state, according to the [Oklahoma Historical Society](https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=AL009).

Yet many of those communities had disappeared by the Great Depression, and the area around the station was flanked by "sundown towns" — all-white communities that warned non-white people to leave before dark or face violence or police harassment. Fisher said there was a sundown town on either side of the filling station during the Jim Crow era.

## A century of welcome

Allen Threatt Sr. built the original filling station in 1915 after noticing drivers passing a corner of his property on what was then the Ozark Trail. When Route 66 was founded in 1926, the trickle of cars became a steady stream. After the original wooden building burned down, he replaced it with a sandstone structure built to resemble a small, welcoming house.

According to the [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/places/threatt-filling-station.htm), which listed the property on the National Register of Historic Places in 1995, the "house type" station was built using local sandstone with Bungalow/Craftsman features typical of the period. Over time, the station and its surrounding property became a community hub — with dances in the adjacent restaurant, social club meetings, barbecues, and occasional games by Negro Leagues players on a baseball diamond.

The [National Trust for Historic Preservation](https://savingplaces.org/11-most-endangered-historic-places-2021) notes the threat the station helped travelers escape: the threat of violence, including racism and lynchings, in sundown towns after nightfall. While the station was not listed in the Negro Motorist Green Book, it still served as a place of refuge. Allen and Alberta Threatt had 11 living children, and a couple of them took over operation in the 1950s.

The building closed to the public in the mid-1970s, as the Interstate Highway System launched under the Eisenhower administration siphoned traffic off Route 66. By 2021 the structure was derelict, and that year the National Trust listed it among America's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places.

## A restoration in the family's hands

A few years before that listing, a trio of Threatt's grandchildren decided to restore the property. Through nonprofit grants and family labor, the filling station now has a restored exterior, 1940s ceramic Conoco gas pumps, new interpretive signage, and a gift shop. The family plans to build an RV park, a restaurant, and perhaps even a new baseball diamond.

"We're all standing on Grandpa's shoulders — Grandpa and Grandma's shoulders," Edward Threatt, a grandson and one of the restoration's leaders, told NPR. "We have this property today because of Grandpa, and so we represent him, we represent our mothers, our fathers. And we're just trying to do them proud, that's all."

## Why it matters now

Route 66 has long symbolized American mobility and freedom — but for Black travelers, that freedom was sharply curtailed. The highway's centennial offers an occasion to revisit that myth through a racial-equity lens, and to recognize the safe havens that made travel possible. The restoration also reflects a broader movement to preserve African American heritage sites tied to the segregation era, from Green Book locations to civil rights landmarks.

What distinguishes the Threatt project is that it remains in the family's hands — descendant-led preservation rather than institutional stewardship. As the family works toward opening the site more fully for the Route 66 centennial, questions remain about how the planned RV park, restaurant, and ball diamond will be funded and completed. But for a station that once offered shelter to travelers with nowhere else to turn, the welcome, its custodians say, is still very much open.
