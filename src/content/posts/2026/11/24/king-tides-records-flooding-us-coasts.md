# King Tides Threaten Records and Flooding on US Coasts

Exceptionally high king tides along the Pacific Coast could break four-decade-old records and flood low-lying communities this week, with California and South Florida facing the greatest risk as the surge collides with one of the busiest travel periods of the year. In the San Francisco Bay Area, tides are forecast to peak at 2.8 feet (0.9 meters) above normal on Wednesday, approaching the region's all-time record set more than 40 years ago, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/king-tides-flooding-california-florida-a770ac04481a2d7fae4838add07ef61a).

## What Is Happening

Tides in the Bay Area were expected to reach 2.6 feet (0.8 meters) above normal on Tuesday before cresting at 2.8 feet on Wednesday, said Dial Hoang, a National Weather Service forecaster. The region's highest recorded tide reached 2.9 feet (0.9 meters) above normal in San Francisco on January 27, 1983 — during the strongest El Niño on record.

San Francisco's famed Embarcadero waterfront, which flooded during heavy rain just last week, is again at risk. The weather service issued a high surf warning for Wednesday, when breaking waves of 20 to 30 feet (6 to 9 meters) could overpower swimmers and sweep people into the ocean.

"Remain out of the water and stay off of jetties, piers, and rocks. Never turn your back to the ocean!" the agency warned in a social media post. Heavy rain forecast from Tuesday evening into Wednesday could worsen street flooding.

King tides are the highest tides of the year, occurring when the gravitational pull of the moon and sun aligns to raise water levels. Their effects this year are being amplified by an "ongoing super El Niño," which has warmed the ocean and increased the volume of seawater through thermal expansion, AP reported.

## A Stacked Set of Hazards

The 2026 event is not a single threat but the coincidence of several. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/23/california-high-tides-floods-weather-thanksgiving) detailed, the astronomical king tides are stacked atop an elevated baseline caused by the super El Niño — potentially the most intense in 1,000 years, with Pacific surface temperatures expected to peak roughly 4°C warmer than usual.

A planetary-scale band of warm water known as a Kelvin wave has added 6 to 12 inches or more to water levels along the California coast, according to NOAA estimates cited by the Guardian. On top of that, potential storm surge from winds and heavy rain from atmospheric rivers could push water even higher if they arrive at the peak of the tides.

"King tides" are "the very highest high tide cycles of the year," Daniel Swain, a climate scientist at the California Institute for Water Resources, explained in a recent briefing. This year, he said, they have raised concerns about "widespread and potentially significant – maybe even record-breaking – coastal flooding."

The stakes extend well beyond the beaches. "Even places that are not along the Pacific coast, but that are inland – San Francisco Bay, Monterey Bay, San Diego Bay, even places much farther inland along the San Joaquin-Sacramento estuary that are tidally influenced – could see significant to record-breaking inundation," Swain said.

## Vulnerable Communities and Infrastructure

Farther north, in Humboldt County near the Oregon border, the sheriff's office ordered evacuations in a small coastal community where some buildings flooded on Monday, saying people needed to leave by Tuesday morning.

"The tides are expected to be higher by Tuesday, and the southerly winds will have been blowing for 24 hours by then, and so that will push the storm surge even higher," said Matthew Kidwell, a National Weather Service meteorologist in Humboldt County.

In the Pacific Northwest, weather service offices in Portland and Seattle warned of coastal flooding through Friday, with the most significant flooding possible from Tuesday through Thanksgiving Day. Washington State Ferries canceled several sailings, and about 300,000 people are expected to use the ferry system over the holiday weekend.

Much of the Bay Area's waterfront infrastructure — including San Francisco International Airport and bayshore neighborhoods — sits on low-lying reclaimed land. The airport has been protected with vinyl sheet-pile walls, concrete seawalls and concrete-capped earthen berms, and workers staged sandbags and pumps, the San Francisco Chronicle reported. But the highest-risk parts of its barrier wall are only about 8 feet tall, while a recently completed upgrade at nearby Oakland airport raised its perimeter seawall to 13 to 17 feet.

Some of the most exposed residents live in San Rafael's Canal District, where more than 10,000 people — mostly Latino — live in apartment buildings at or below sea level. If water overtops the neighborhood's canal bank, it could flood central roads and block evacuation routes.

## South Florida's Separate Struggle

South Florida faces a parallel but distinct threat. King tides are expected to cause coastal flooding from Palm Beach to Miami, with water levels remaining elevated for hours before and after high tide on Wednesday and Thursday mornings.

Many communities there were just beginning to dry out after slow-moving storms dumped more than 17 inches (43 centimeters) of rain less than a week earlier. The St. Johns River near Cocoa has been above flood stage since November 2 and last week broke a record set in 1953, and it is forecast to remain at least 2 feet (0.6 meters) above flood stage.

## Analysis: A Recurring Test, Not a One-Off

The record being challenged is more than four decades old, and forecasters are framing the current event as approaching or exceeding it — consistent with the broader pattern in which rising baseline sea levels make routine astronomical tides more destructive over time. Earlier in 2026, storm-driven waves eroded Southern California beaches from Long Beach to San Diego, leaving some "sand-starved" and without a natural protective buffer.

"This is a water level we've never experienced before, so I think there are going to be some areas that are going to see flooding that have not seen it in the past," said Mark Merrifield, director of the Center for Climate Change Impacts and Adaptation at UC San Diego.

Kelsey Ducklow, a statewide coastal resilience coordinator at the California Coastal Commission, captured the compounding nature of the risk. "You're getting water from every direction, there's no place for it to go," she told the San Francisco Chronicle.

Critically, the same constellation of factors is expected to recur. Additional king-tide windows are forecast for December 23–25 and January 21–22, 2027 — meaning California faces the same stacked flood-risk scenario three times this season. As the Guardian put it, the state needs to be lucky not just once, but three times.

## What to Watch

Whether the Bay Area sees routine nuisance flooding or a severe compound event will hinge on timing — specifically, whether forecast rain arrives at the peak of the tides. Officials have had weeks of advance warning and have staged barriers, pumps and sandbags across the region.

San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie urged residents to plan carefully for the holiday. "As you make plans for Thanksgiving, keep the weather in mind. Check conditions before traveling, and avoid unnecessary trips when roads are flooded or winds are strong," he wrote on social media.

The key questions in the days ahead are whether Wednesday's tide breaks the 1983 record, whether airports and ferry service are disrupted at the height of holiday travel, and how the region's defenses hold up when the tides return around Christmas and in late January.
