# Seniors Embrace Tesla Self-Driving, Safety Questions Linger

Joan Spalding has navigated the steep, winding roads near her home in Evergreen, Colorado, for more than 50 years. These days, the 89-year-old barely touches the wheel or the pedals. Instead, she types her destination into her Tesla Model Y's touchscreen and presses a button. "I just get in and go," she told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/23/nx-s1-6020559/senior-drivers-tesla-automated-driving-safety).

Spalding is part of a growing cohort of older Americans using Tesla's Full Self-Driving (Supervised) system — a paid driver-assistance feature — to extend their driving years and preserve their independence. The trend reflects a genuine mobility benefit for an aging population, but it also raises urgent questions about whether the technology is safe for drivers whose reflexes and judgment may be declining, and whether the company's safety claims can be trusted.

## A Practical Lifeline

For Spalding, the decision was born of necessity. After a series of fender benders and a serious crash two years ago while making a left turn, she realized her driving abilities were slipping. "I made a wrong decision. I wasn't focusing on what I should be doing … I was lucky," she said. "But I realized then that my abilities were not where I wanted them to be."

Her son Mike Spalding, already a Tesla owner, suggested she get one too. "It would be safer for mom if she was in a wreck because it's got all kinds of safety features. And then safer for people around her if we didn't have any more crashes," he said. "It's better than me."

The appeal extends beyond safety. Spalding says the technology allows her to remain in the home she and her late husband built more than 50 years ago. "I'd have to move," she said of life without it. "This way, I think I can drive it for as long as I want to."

She is not alone. Larry Dimmock, 98, became a minor internet phenomenon after praising his Tesla's driving in a YouTube video. "I feel safer riding with this auto system than I do driving or riding with anybody. It's just been flawless," Dimmock said. "I'll get in the car and go somewhere, otherwise, I probably wouldn't." Tesla CEO Elon Musk amplified the video, which has been viewed more than 3.5 million times, and has publicly promoted the trend, [posting on X](https://x.com/elonmusk/status/2118739371939803498) that "Tesla self-driving gives mobility to seniors."

## A Growing — but Unquantified — Market

Tesla says its FSD system has more than [1.7 million active subscriptions](https://assets-ir.tesla.com/tesla-contents/IR/TSLA-Q3-2026-Update.pdf), a 67% increase from a year ago. But the company does not disclose how many of those subscribers are seniors, or whether it even tracks the figure. That gap in public data leaves regulators and safety researchers guessing at the scale of the phenomenon — even as anecdotal evidence and viral videos suggest it is spreading.

Families, meanwhile, face an increasingly common dilemma: when is it time to take the car keys away? For some, driver-assistance technology has become a way to postpone that painful decision.

## Experts Sound the Alarm

Safety researchers are deeply skeptical. Tesla officially calls the feature FSD (Supervised) and insists drivers must remain attentive at all times, ready to take control when the system encounters a situation it cannot handle. The trouble, experts say, is that humans — particularly those with age-related impairments — may be unable to do so.

"It's not a technology that I would drive with. It's not a technology that I would suggest that my loved ones use," said Bryan Reimer, a research scientist at MIT who studies how drivers interact with technology. "I think it has a few safety gaps that just make me uncomfortable."

Alexandra Mueller, a senior research scientist at the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, warns that automated systems can lull drivers into complacency. "It's good enough, often enough, where people go, 'The next time I'm in this similar situation, I know how it's going to behave,'" she said. "This can be a huge problem. Ultimately it is very unsafe if you have drivers who are not fully competent due to age-related impairments or decline."

## The Credibility Gap

At the heart of the debate is a dispute over Tesla's safety statistics. The company [claims](https://www.tesla.com/fsd/safety) that FSD is up to nine times safer than human driving, but it does not release enough data for independent verification. Reimer is blunt about the marketing. "Tesla has continually over-promised and under-delivered," he said. "Full self-driving is not full self-driving, but yet the name feels so real."

Those concerns have been reinforced by investigative reporting. A [Reuters examination](https://www.reuters.com/world/tesla-presented-misleading-full-self-driving-safety-data-european-regulators-2026-06-15/) published in June 2026 found that Tesla presented safety statistics to regulators in Sweden and the Netherlands that researchers described as misleading marketing — relying on invalid data comparisons that exaggerated the system's safety. The reporting also found Tesla had claimed FSD could have saved 32,000 lives based on unrealistic assumptions.

The scrutiny has reached Washington. Sens. Ed Markey and Richard Blumenthal formally [urged the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration](https://www.markey.senate.gov/news/press-releases/sens-markey-blumenthal-demand-nhtsa-require-greater-transparency-from-tesla-autonomous-vehicle-companies) to review Tesla's FSD crash statistics, calling the data weak and misleading and questioning whether the agency has independently evaluated the company's safety claims. Tesla did not respond to NPR's requests for comment.

## The Road Ahead

The senior-driver story is part of a larger reckoning over how automated systems are marketed and regulated. A Tesla Model 3 crashed into an overturned box truck on Interstate 75 near Naples, Florida, on May 31, 2026, killing three people — a collision tied to the driver-assist system in federal filings that surfaced in November and [reported by Electrek](https://electrek.co/2026/11/16/tesla-autopilot-fsd-overturned-truck-naples-i75/). According to Tesla's own report to federal regulators, Autopilot or "Full Self-Driving" was engaged, at 71 mph — though with the software version redacted, it is not possible to confirm whether Autopilot or FSD was active. The three dead were two Tesla passengers, aged 18 and 19, and a 25-year-old former Marine who had stopped to help the truck driver. It is a reminder that the technology's real-world performance remains far from flawless.

For regulators, the challenge is twofold. They must decide how to police safety claims that are difficult to verify, and how to weigh the genuine mobility benefits against the risks for vulnerable drivers. For families, the question is more personal: whether a system that is "better than me" on a good day is good enough on the worst one.

For her part, Joan Spalding has no plans to give up the wheel — or, more precisely, to give up the button. With her hands free, she sometimes prays the rosary as the car carries her home through the pines. It is, she says, a kind of freedom she thought she might have to surrender. Whether that freedom comes at a hidden cost is a question that will follow the technology for years to come.
