# Princeton Scientist Held by ICE Spotlights Airport Arrests

A Chinese-born neuroscience researcher at Princeton University is being held in federal immigration custody after she was detained at a Washington-area airport while trying to fly home from a conference — a case that has thrust the Trump administration's expanding airport enforcement practices into sharp relief.

Eva Yi Xie, a 24-year-old third-year doctoral student at the Princeton Neuroscience Institute, was detained by Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers at Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport on Thursday, November 19, as she attempted to board a flight back to New Jersey, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/princeton-neuroscientist-ice-detention-china-3e7ff7ea1ce8a492a8d80018e9fe1d38). She had presented a research poster at the Society for Neuroscience conference in the nation's capital the day before.

She remains in ICE custody at the Caroline Detention Facility in Bowling Green, Virginia, pending removal proceedings, and now sits at the center of a legal clash over whether a pending asylum application grants the right to live, work, and study in the United States.

## A Divide Over Legal Status

At the heart of the dispute are two irreconcilable accounts of Xie's standing in the country.

The Department of Homeland Security, which oversees ICE, says Xie entered the United States on a temporary visa on March 16, 2016, with a required departure date of September 15 of that year — an entry that coincided with her arrival from Shanghai at age 14. The agency asserts she overstayed and never held valid authorization to study.

"Xie has NEVER held valid authorization to study in the United States," DHS said in a statement. "To be clear, a pending application does not confer legal status in the United States." The agency said she "will receive due process and remain in ICE custody pending her removal proceedings."

Xie's lawyer, Kerry Doyle, tells a markedly different story. She says Xie came to the U.S. at age 14 with her family and has had an asylum application pending for roughly a decade. According to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/princeton-neuroscientist-eva-yi-xie-ice-detained-rcna610603), Doyle maintains that Xie "had permission to live, work and study in the country while her case was pending."

"Her arrest and detention is without legal basis as ICE and DHS are fully aware," Doyle told NBC News. She called the arrest "an incredible waste of time and resources — to what end?"

Doyle has filed a habeas corpus petition in federal court in Virginia challenging the legality of the arrest, arguing that ICE had no grounds to detain Xie without a warrant because she was neither a flight risk nor a danger to the community.

## A Familiar Research Profile, an Unusual Detention

Xie's academic record underscores what is at stake. She graduated from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 2024 with degrees in computer science and mathematics, and by her early twenties had already assembled a research portfolio that colleagues describe as unusually broad.

Her primary adviser, Princeton neuroscience professor Carlos Brody, described the work she presented in Washington as discovering "an unexpected link between how brains make decisions and how they navigate through space." Colleague Nathaniel Daw noted that it is "not typical for a student to have three advisors," attributing the arrangement to the breadth of her skills across theoretical computer science and rodent physiology.

Even from detention, Brody said, Xie sent messages to colleagues "to make sure that her experiments could move forward and the grant applications she is part of could be submitted."

"Eva is a gentle, kind, caring person who is contributing enormously to the United States," Brody said. "She belongs back home in Princeton, doing the science that she loves, and helping our country excel and grow."

Princeton's graduate school dean, Rodney D. Priestley, said the news was "distressing for me and many members of the Princeton University community," adding that the university has arranged legal counsel for Xie and remains in contact with her family and legal team.

## The Broader Enforcement Push

The case has drawn attention because it fits a pattern. Since the summer of 2026, ICE has sharply increased detentions at airports, targeting people it says have overstayed their visas. A [New York Times investigation](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/28/us/ice-arrests-airports-visa-overstay.html) in July documented the administration arresting foreign citizens with expired visas as they moved through airports — including the spouses of Americans.

DHS frames the practice as closing a loophole, saying it reversed a "Biden-era policy" that allowed people in the country illegally to "jet around our country." The agency says those in custody are offered a free flight plus $3,000 to self-deport.

Just two days after Xie's detention, masked immigration officers detained a father and daughter on an airport jet bridge in Seattle as the family traveled to a baptism — a separate episode that, like Xie's, has fueled criticism of the administration's tactics.

The numbers behind the policy are stark. According to DHS data cited in the research, ICE deported 483,200 people and made more than 491,000 immigration arrests in fiscal year 2026 — the highest annual totals in the agency's history.

## Political and Scientific Fallout

The detention quickly became a political flashpoint in New Jersey. Democratic Sen. Cory Booker said his office contacted ICE and did not receive a "satisfactory response" about why Xie was detained. Gov. Mikie Sherrill, also a Democrat, directed her administration to coordinate with federal lawmakers and Virginia officials to learn more.

"While ICE claims to go after the worst of the worst, time and again the record shows they do not — and their tactics are making New Jerseyans less safe," Sherrill's spokesperson, Steve Sigmund, said in an email.

The case has also alarmed advocates for international scholars, who warn that high-profile detentions of researchers erode the trust that draws global scientific talent to American institutions.

"Due process and the rule of law build the trust that lets scientists, researchers, and students feel safe pursuing their studies and work here," said Gisela Perez Kusakawa, executive director of the Asian American Scholar Forum. "That trust is what attracts the world's best scientific talents to the United States, and it is the foundation for our country's next generation of scientific leaders."

## What to Watch

The immediate question is legal: whether the habeas petition succeeds in forcing a court to examine, and potentially halt, Xie's removal proceedings. The case tests a contested premise — that DHS treats a pending asylum application as conferring no legal status, while immigration attorneys argue applicants are lawfully permitted to remain and may obtain work authorization while their cases are decided.

Beyond the courtroom, the episode poses a broader question for the United States: whether a sweeping enforcement campaign at airports will deter the international students and researchers on whom its scientific enterprise depends. For now, Xie remains in custody in Virginia, her research paused and her future in the country unresolved — a single case that has come to symbolize a much larger national argument.
