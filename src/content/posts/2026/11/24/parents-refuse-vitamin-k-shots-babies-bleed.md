# Parents Refuse Vitamin K Shots, and Babies Bleed

A routine injection given to newborns within hours of birth has become the latest casualty of medical mistrust, and pediatricians say the consequences are arriving in emergency rooms — babies bleeding from the eyes, nose and rectum, some of them dying from a condition that a single low-risk shot almost always prevents.

The rate of U.S. newborns who do not receive intramuscular vitamin K has more than doubled, from 3.1% in 2017 to 6.8% in 2025, according to [research published in JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2856300) analyzing more than 5 million births. A companion study in JAMA Pediatrics found the rate of vitamin K deficiency bleeding, or VKDB, nearly quadrupled over the same period. The findings, reported by [NPR and KFF Health News](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/24/nx-s1-6001723/vitamin-k-deficiency-refusal-vaccine-misinformation-newborn-bleeding), offer the first hard national data on a trend physicians had been watching with growing alarm.

## A shot that became a victim of its own success

Babies are born with very low levels of vitamin K, which does not pass easily through the placenta and is present only in small amounts in breast milk. The vitamin is essential for blood clotting. The injection has been standard newborn care since the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended it in 1961, and it is safe, with virtually no severe side effects.

The shot was so effective that generations of doctors encountered a case of deficiency bleeding perhaps once in a career. Dr. Ivan Hand, director of neonatology at NYC Health + Hospitals/Kings County and a co-author of the AAP's vitamin K policy, put it bluntly. "We're a victim of our own success," he said. "Since we've been treating babies with vitamin K, we haven't seen much deficiency bleeding, so people think it doesn't exist."

The protection it offers is stark. Among babies who receive the shot, [fewer than 1 in 100,000](https://www.cdc.gov/vitamin-k-deficiency/about/index.html) develop the most serious form of bleeding. Babies who don't receive it are 81 times more likely to develop VKDB — and of those who do, about 1 in 5 die. Roughly half of cases involve bleeding into the brain, which can occur without visible warning signs.

## What refusal looks like in the delivery room

At Corewell Health's Butterworth Hospital in Grand Rapids, Michigan, at least one family on average now declines the injection each day. Dr. Torrie LeBlanc, a pediatrician there, still remembers a newborn girl whose parents had said no. About 36 hours after birth, she began bleeding "from everywhere" — her eyes, nose and rectum.

Nurses and doctors rushed her to the neonatal intensive care unit and pumped in fresh frozen plasma and other blood products. With their daughter on the verge of bleeding to death, the parents agreed to vitamin K. She survived, largely because treatment came within minutes. Had the baby been home when the bleeding started, her doctors believe, she would have died.

"It just made me sad more than anything else," said Dr. Solomon Behar, a pediatric emergency medicine physician in Los Angeles who recently treated an infant who died of VKDB. "This was something that literally was a historical footnote up until the recent modern time, when people have decided that Mother Nature is someone that wants to work with them, rather than is trying to kill us."

## The misinformation ecosystem

Health officials link the trend to the same anti-vaccine currents that have eroded uptake of childhood immunizations. Many parents mistake the shot for a vaccine, though it is a vitamin. A since-debunked 1990 study linking vitamin K to childhood cancer seeded decades of doubt, and prominent conservative influencers, wellness podcasts and parenting forums have amplified fears that the injection is unnecessary or harmful. In 2023, one widely followed podcaster aired an episode mocking the shot, research noted, fueling further skepticism.

The political climate has compounded the problem. When Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. was pressed at a congressional hearing to assure parents the shot is safe, he declined. "I've never said anything about it," he said. Rep. Kim Schrier, a Washington Democrat and physician, told him: "The doubt you've created about all of medicine and science is causing parents to make dangerous decisions." An HHS spokesperson later said vitamin K "remains the standard of care" and blamed the prior administration for the refusal trend.

## Doctors learn to listen, not lecture

Confronted with entrenched mistrust, hospitals are changing how they talk to families. LeBlanc launched a two-year initiative that created plain-language fact sheets and trained staff in "motivational interviewing," a technique adapted from substance-use treatment that treats parental autonomy as paramount. The minute parents feel judged, she said, they stop listening.

"We can't walk into rooms where families don't know us from the doorknob and say, 'You have to do this,'" said Dr. Jaspreet Loyal, a pediatrician at Yale New Haven Hospital who has long studied vitamin K refusal. "We have to practice humility, to sit there and listen, and then come back the next day and sort of use that nudge approach."

The results are partial. LeBlanc converts about half of initially refusing parents; colleagues report success rates of 20% to 40%. Some clinicians now accept oral vitamin K as a harm-reduction compromise, even though it is less effective, requires repeated doses and has no FDA-approved formulation. "The injection is the gold standard," LeBlanc said. "However, I would rather have them have some protection with the oral than no protection at all."

## A data vacuum that hides the toll

One reason the misinformation persists is that the harm is largely invisible in official statistics. Neither the CDC nor the states systematically track vitamin K refusal or the bleeding that follows. VKDB has never been made a reportable condition, and deaths are often attributed to more immediate causes, such as a brain hemorrhage.

"If you don't track it, you don't document it," said Dr. Robert Sidonio Jr., a pediatric hematologist-oncologist at Children's Healthcare of Atlanta. "They have to make it a reportable health condition, just like a new measles case. That's the only way it's going to change."

Dr. Loyal argues the absence of data cuts the other way, too. "The lack of data is almost acting like a reassurance for families that this risk is worth taking," she said.

## What to watch

The two November studies established correlation rather than definitive causation, their authors caution. But the biology is not in dispute — vitamin K is required for clotting — and the effect sizes are large enough that clinicians consider causation highly plausible. Doctors and some lawmakers have urged the CDC to begin tracking refusals and bleeding events, a step that could sharpen both the public-health response and the conversations happening in delivery rooms.

For now, the burden falls on individual physicians like LeBlanc, who carries each refusal home with her. What if the next time she sees that baby is in the ICU? She would blame herself, she said, for not getting through. "I couldn't convince them."

The babies who survive tell one story. The ones who do not — a 7-week-old boy in Maryland, an 11-pound girl in Alabama, a newborn in Texas — tell another, one that public-health officials say is entirely preventable.
