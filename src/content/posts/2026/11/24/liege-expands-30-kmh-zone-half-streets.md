# Liège Expands 30 km/h Zone to Half of Its Streets

The city of Liège will soon limit traffic to 30 km/h on roughly half of its streets, after the municipal council approved a major extension of the city's low-speed zone on the evening of Monday 23 November. The measure lifts the share of roads capped at 30 km/h from about 25% to approximately 50% of the municipality's territory, an expansion the city presents as a road-safety and traffic-calming initiative, particularly for pedestrians, cyclists and children.

"It is a 30 km/h zone on a very large scale, extending from the city centre into several contiguous neighbourhoods," mayor Willy Demeyer said, as reported by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-ville-de-liege-elargit-sa-zone-30-la-moitie-des-rues-du-territoire-bientot-concernees-11820117).

## Context: A Years-Long Calming Strategy

The decision caps a gradual approach. Liège had already introduced 30 km/h zones neighbourhood by neighbourhood — first in Bressoux and Longdoz, then Saint-Léonard in 2024 — but this vote extends the limit to the whole "greater" city centre. The move follows other efforts to reduce car pressure in the centre, including expanded cycling and e-scooter infrastructure and the tram that entered service in spring 2025, which has already calmed many central arteries.

According to [L'Avenir](https://www.lavenir.net/regions/liege/liege/2026/11/23/une-decision-majeure-a-liege-la-cite-ardente-passe-en-zone-30-kmh-dans-pres-de-50-des-rues-de-son-territoire-3JDNM6VPOJCAFMDV7ATWA6GB5U/), the new perimeter runs from the Guillemins station area to the Thier-à-Liège, taking in the centre as well as Outremeuse, Amercœur, part of Saint-Laurent, Sainte-Marguerite, the Coteaux de la Citadelle and Grivegnée-bas.

## Regional Limits on the Perimeter

The zone was defined in consultation with the Service public de Wallonie (SPW), the regional public service responsible for major roads. That coordination came with a caveat: the quays and several regional axes were explicitly excluded from the perimeter, because speed limits on those roads fall under regional rather than municipal authority.

"The zone was the subject of consultation with the SPW, which however wished to exclude the quays and several regional axes from the perimeter," Demeyer said. The exclusion highlights a persistent tension between the city's traffic-calming ambitions and regional control over the busiest thoroughfares — and means the measure's real-world effect will depend partly on how much through-traffic is pushed onto those carved-out axes.

## Safety at the Fore

City officials frame the extension primarily around safety. Municipal councillor François Schreuer, a close observer of mobility issues, called the decision "a turning point in the history of the city."

"Safer for everyone, but particularly for active users — pedestrians and cyclists — and for children too," he said. "We can hope this 30 km/h zone translates concretely into a reduction in the number of accidents and their severity."

Schreuer also argued the lower limit could modestly ease congestion in the most clogged areas, citing studies on traffic flow. That claim is more debatable than the safety rationale and will require empirical verification as the zone takes effect.

The Brussels precedent is a frequent reference point. When the Brussels region made 30 km/h the norm in January 2021, officials later reported that the number of road deaths and serious injuries fell sharply. According to [RTBF's 2021 assessment](https://www.rtbf.be/article/bilan-zone-30-a-bruxelles-le-nombre-de-victimes-sur-les-routes-divise-par-deux-10904242), Brussels recorded 5 deaths and about 100 serious injuries in 2021, down from 11 deaths and 121 serious injuries in 2020 — a decline the region's mobility agency described as a dramatic reduction in serious casualties.

## Analysis: A Landmark for a Walloon City

For Liège, Belgium's third-largest agglomeration, moving from 25% to 50% of streets is one of the largest expansions of a low-speed zone undertaken by a Walloon city, putting it closer to the ambition of the Brussels region-wide model. It effectively turns most of the urban core into a low-speed area, even as key regional arteries remain outside the city's reach.

The political dimension is clear: the measure consolidates the PS-led majority's long-running "apaisement" agenda and its post-tram vision for the city. It has been broadly welcomed within the majority, but the reaction of motorists, logistics operators and businesses on the affected and excluded axes remains untested.

## What to Watch

Several questions will determine how the expansion plays out. The city has yet to detail the exact implementation and signage timeline for the new perimeter. It will also need to track whether accidents and average speeds fall in the zone — and whether traffic simply shifts onto the excluded quays and regional roads. How neighbouring municipalities and motorist associations respond, and whether Liège eventually targets an even larger share of its streets, will show whether this "turning point" becomes a new baseline for mobility in the Cité ardente.
