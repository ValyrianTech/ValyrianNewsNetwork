# Ice Halts Belgian Coastal Tram; Service Back by 11am

The entire Belgian coastal tram network ground to a halt on the morning of Tuesday, 24 November, after a night of freezing temperatures coated the overhead power lines in ice and knocked out the traction stations that feed them electricity. No tram ran anywhere between De Panne and Knokke-Heist for several hours, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/24/geen-kusttram-door-vrieskou-kust/). Normal service resumed around 11:00 once temperatures climbed back above freezing, though follow-on delays remained possible.

## A cascade, not a single fault

The disruption had two intertwined causes. Overnight freezing and a hard frost of about −2°C along the coast, combined with very humid, windless air, caused mist to freeze directly onto the catenary — the overhead wire that powers the trams — along the full length of the route. "On old trams the ice is driven off by the tram itself, but at the coast that is not the case," said Jens Van Herp, a spokesperson for the Flemish public transport operator De Lijn.

That ice then triggered a second, compounding failure. Ice acts as an insulator, so the trams had to draw extra current from the traction stations to keep moving. Because that surge came all at once, the stations tripped out. "If too much current is demanded at once, they think something is on fire," Van Herp explained. "So it is a safety mechanism that was triggered." He confirmed that all traction stations went down because of the cold.

The result, [Lokaal Nieuws reported](https://www.lokaalnieuws.online/2026/11/24/kusttram-vertraging-vrieskou-ijs-bovenleidingen/), was that the specialist tram used to clear ice from the lines could not even leave its depot, since the stations powering it were offline. With no trams running, De Lijn advised passengers not to board at all. "They are either not running or moving at walking pace," the operator said during the morning. "The traction stations keep failing, so we cannot guarantee that passengers will reach their destination."

## Too few buses for too long a route

De Lijn deployed replacement buses, but the sheer length of the route — roughly 67 kilometres of coastline — meant the substitute fleet was far too thin to absorb the demand. "Because it covers such a long route, there are also too few replacement buses, which makes the disruption great," Van Herp said. Passengers who did take the bus were also slower, since road traffic moves more slowly than a tram on its own dedicated track.

The Kusttram is one of the world's longest tram lines, running almost the entire Belgian coast from De Panne near the French border, through the seaside resort of Oostende, to Knokke-Heist near the Netherlands. Its long, exposed seaside route makes it unusually vulnerable to coastal weather — humidity, mist, salt air and wind — and the disruption came on top of a raft of scheduled engineering works already thinning service.

## Recovery depended on the weather, not a repair

Rather than being fixed, the network recovered on its own once conditions changed. "Temperatures had to rise above freezing again. Then the network could recover by itself," Van Herp said, adding that trams were running smoothly again around 11:00. De Lijn had sprayed anti-freeze products that morning, but conceded it cannot treat the whole network. "We will now look at what extra measures we need to take for the coming days," Van Herp said.

According to [De Lijn's official travel information](https://www.delijn.be/nl/lijnen/55000/belangrijke-berichten/), an "unexpected disruption — weather situation — delays" notice for the KT line remained active after the recovery, running until 08:00 on Wednesday, 25 November. That points to residual and follow-on delays rather than a renewed full stoppage. De Lijn gave no fixed hour for a complete return to normal, advising passengers to check its app or website for live updates.

## A recurring winter weak point

The episode laid bare a structural vulnerability in one of Belgium's flagship public transport services. Because the coastal fleet lacks the onboard ice-clearing capability found on older tram systems, a single freezing night with the wrong mix of humidity and stillness can paralyse an entire regional network — and the operator's only real remedy is to wait for the thaw. With more cold weather forecast, the coming days will test whether De Lijn's promised extra measures can keep the coast moving, and whether the long-term question — how to make the Kusttram resilient to the very seaside climate that defines it — finally gets an answer.
