# China's Two-Launch Lunar Plan as the Moon Race Splinters

China has formally disclosed how it intends to put astronauts on the Moon: a "two-launch, lunar-orbit rendezvous" architecture that sends a lander and a crewed capsule to lunar orbit on separate rockets, where they dock before astronauts descend to the surface. The plan, unveiled at the 7th Manned Space Academic Conference in Beijing, places China among a growing set of powers each pursuing a different route to the same destination — what Chinese state media calls a "multi-route parallel era" in the global push to return humans to the Moon, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261111/569ca4f81a5f481eb1434c9f8c114b8c/c.html).

## How China's Architecture Works

Under the disclosed plan, two rockets lift off separately. One carries the *Lanyue* (揽月) lunar lander; the other carries the *Mengzhou* (梦舟) crewed spacecraft. Both are dispatched into an Earth-Moon transfer orbit, then rendezvous and dock in lunar orbit. Astronauts transfer from *Mengzhou* into *Lanyue* for the descent to the surface and the return trip home.

The approach avoids building a single ultra-heavy rocket capable of carrying crew and lander together — the so-called "all-payload" concept behind NASA's Space Launch System. Compared with assembling a mission in low Earth orbit across many launches, China moves the docking to lunar orbit, sidestepping the risks of storing and assembling hardware in near-Earth space for long periods.

Wang Xiang (王翔), deputy chief designer of China's manned space program and chief designer of its lunar exploration program, framed the choice as one of sustainability. The two vehicles "shaking hands" in lunar orbit, he said, matches existing technical capabilities and launch facilities; developing the spacecraft and lander separately gives a clear division of labor and enables standardized, reusable product lines.

The trade-off is real. China's task chain is shorter than NASA's multi-launch model, but its margin for error at each link is narrower — placing higher demands on insertion and rendezvous precision, autonomous spacecraft control, and astronauts' ability to judge and respond without an external backup.

## The Hardware Already Flying

China is not starting from scratch. The program has completed several major test milestones: a zero-altitude escape test for *Mengzhou*; a landing-and-ascent test for *Lanyue*; a low-altitude demonstration flight of the Long March 10 (长征十号) rocket; and a maximum-dynamic-pressure escape flight for *Mengzhou*.

Wang said the Long March 10 low-altitude verification flight was a key pre-flight check and that the same effort achieved China's first rocket re-entry and recovery — a step toward reusable launch technology. China's stated goal is to land astronauts on the Moon before 2030.

## Three Other Routes, Three Different Logics

The significance of China's disclosure is less about any single engineering detail than about how starkly it clarifies the divergence among national programs. As Xinhua's comparative analysis put it, the different routes are not ranked; they are choices shaped by each system's industrial, budgetary, and institutional constraints.

**The United States** has moved away from an Apollo-style compact cadence. In 2026 NASA adjusted its Artemis sequence: Artemis 3, originally the crewed landing, was reassigned to a near-Earth orbit rendezvous and docking technology demonstration, with the first crewed landing pushed to Artemis 4 in 2028, [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/GuoJi%C2%B7ZhanLue/2026_10_24_905619.shtml) reported. The new approach spreads risk across three independent launches and three suppliers in a serial-release model. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman acknowledged the hardware reality bluntly: "What we have in hand today is not enough to replicate Apollo 9." The essential move is to trade speed for control — more launches in exchange for the ability to release or abort each one independently, so astronauts need not wait in orbit for an unready lander. NASA is choosing redundancy over complexity.

**Russia** is retrenching under budget pressure. According to Vladimir Solovyov, chief designer at the Energia rocket and space corporation, the cost of a crewed lunar flight far exceeds robotic missions. Russia's choice is to shift from full self-development toward integrating existing technology, scaling back its goal from an independent crewed landing to maintaining a circumlunar presence. That is a budget judgment, not a technical one.

**Europe** buys its seat at the table through a subsystem. ESA Director General Josef Aschbacher described the European Service Module, which provides propulsion for the Orion spacecraft, as the critical system that enables astronauts to fly to the Moon and return. Europe trades a subsystem for entry qualification — avoiding the astronomical cost of an independent super-heavy rocket and lander, at the price of ceding control over the schedule.

## Where the Sharpest Disputes Lie

The most contentious engineering question concerns SpaceX's Starship lander, which relies on orbital cryogenic propellant transfer and therefore multiple refueling launches. Former astronaut and NASA safety advisor Charlie Precourt put the figure at 22 launches; SpaceX founder Elon Musk rebutted that "the real number is more likely 6 to 8." Both positions carry their own engineering rationality: the safety advisor guards the risk boundary, while the contractor answers to schedule and cost pressure.

A deeper fault line runs through lunar governance. The Artemis Accords allow "safety zones" around landing sites — proposed exclusion radii of roughly 1 to 5 kilometers — under the rationale of avoiding harmful interference. Isaacman said in September 2026 that first arrivals drawing such zones would significantly raise the difficulty for later arrivals to access surrounding sites. Because prime lunar south-pole sites are scarce, overlapping safety zones could sharply compress the space available to those who come later. Legal scholars criticize the mechanism as procedural language masking substantive exclusivity, potentially creating de facto appropriation that conflicts with the Outer Space Treaty's non-appropriation principle. The core tension: when the power to draw zones rests with first arrivals and prime sites are highly scarce, a collision-avoidance tool can degenerate into an access barrier — something engineering cooperation alone cannot bridge.

## Cooperation in the Gaps

Competition has not eliminated collaboration. Under the International Lunar Research Station framework, China and Russia have a memorandum of understanding on lunar power-station cooperation; Lev Zelyony, chief scientist at the Russian Academy of Sciences' Space Research Institute, said Russian and Chinese researchers are collaborating on comparative lunar soil studies. Europe's service module remains an irreplaceable propulsion and life-support subsystem for Orion even after NASA adjusted its Gateway plans. These ties are more technical than strategic, and they tend to advance in the gaps left by governance disagreement.

What remains contested are the core technologies — heavy-lift launch, lunar in-situ resource utilization, and deep-space navigation — that each power treats as a competitive high ground.

## What to Watch

Crewed lunar exploration is now a long technical endurance race rather than a single sprint. Each program is pursuing its own architecture while testing the ground for shared rules of lunar governance. In the coming years, the pace of test flights and verification will determine who first steps onto the lunar surface reliably — and whether the Moon becomes a venue for durable cooperation or a new frontier of strategic rivalry. For China, the next milestones to watch are the first flights of the Long March 10, *Mengzhou*, and *Lanyue* — the hardware that must prove the two-launch plan works.
