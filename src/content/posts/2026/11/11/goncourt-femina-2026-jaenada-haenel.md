# Goncourt and Femina Crown Two French Masters

France's autumn literary season delivered its verdict in a matter of days: Philippe Jaenada took the 2026 Prix Goncourt for his true-crime investigation L'Inconnue du quai de Javel, and Yannick Haenel won the Prix Femina for La solitude des professeurs est infinie, a novel drawn from his years teaching in the Paris suburbs. According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/goncourt-2026-le-prestigieux-prix-a-ete-attribue-a-philippe-jaenada-pour-son-livre-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel-11809864), the two awards — announced a day apart — remain among the most prestigious honours in French letters, capable of transforming a book into a national bestseller overnight.

Yet the celebrations unfolded against an unusually charged backdrop. Just days after both top prizes went to men, more than 200 women authors published an open letter denouncing what they called the "sexism" of the literary-prize system — a reminder that France's most venerable cultural institutions remain contested ground.

## A Goncourt for the Archives

The Goncourt was awarded on 3 November to Jaenada, 62, for a 528-page book published by Flammarion that reconstructs the murder of a young Breton woman in the late 1940s. As tradition dictates since 1914, the announcement came from the steps of the Drouant restaurant in Paris, proclaimed by Académie Goncourt president Philippe Claudel.

The vote was decisive: Jaenada received six of the academy's votes, while Haenel took three, with Clémentine Mélois and Sylvain Prudhomme also on the final shortlist. Claudel framed the winning book as an act of restitution. He praised Jaenada for rendering "visible a woman who has been made invisible, a victim, a young victim whom the police did not concern themselves much with."

Jaenada, arriving at Drouant, was characteristically understated. "I feel joyful," he said. He succeeds Laurent Mauvignier, who won the 2025 Goncourt for La Maison Vide.

The win caps a distinctive career. Jaenada has built a loyal readership with sprawling, meticulously researched narratives that sit halfway between fiction and journalistic inquiry — Sulak (2013), La Petite Femelle (2015) and La Serpe (2017) among them. That last book, about a 1941 triple murder in a Dordogne château, earned him the Femina in 2017. As AFP put it at the time: "Entrust an investigation to Jaenada and you will finally know the truth."

On the value of literary prizes, Jaenada has been candid. "When you win a literary prize, it's wonderful, it's joy," he said. "Because it is one of the important ways to make your book known, to sell books and to have readers."

## The Femina Goes to the Classroom

The following day, 4 November, the Femina went to Haenel, 59, for La solitude des professeurs est infinie (Gallimard), a novel that follows Jean Deichel — a fictional alter ego — as a young trainee French teacher arrives at a middle school in Mantes-la-Jolie, in the Paris suburbs. A 12-member all-female jury chose it at the Dior mansion in Paris, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576), preferring it to works by Emma Marsantes and Lucie Rico, who also drew votes.

Haenel, present at the ceremony, dedicated the book "to the teachers, to the high-school and middle-school students" — a pointed gesture as France was in the grip of a student protest movement. He has said the novel grew out of a need to write about teachers' solitude after the murders of the schoolteachers Samuel Paty in 2020 and Dominique Bernard in 2023.

The book renders both the rewards and the violence of the classroom. Teachers, Haenel writes, spend their time "calming things down, defusing, neutralizing: whatever the pedagogues say, it is violence that comes first in middle schools; and being a teacher consists of halting its unleashing." He also captures the quiet joy of the profession — "when a face lights up fleetingly, when a pupil shows understanding, when a lesson works, or a paper is excellent."

It is a return to form for Haenel, whose Jan Karski won the Prix Interallié in 2009 and whose Tiens ferme ta couronne took the Prix Médicis in 2017. He is also a columnist for the satirical weekly Charlie Hebdo.

## Beyond the Two Headlines

The wider prize season offered further laurels. The Prix Renaudot, announced the same day as the Goncourt, went to François-Henri Désérable for Le Palais, published by Gallimard, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-prix-renaudot-2026-attribue-a-francois-henri-deserable-pour-le-palais-11809873). The Femina's companion prizes went to American writer Laura Kasischke for Baignade surveillée (Femina étranger) and to Jennifer Tamas for Barbe Bleue contre-enquête (Femina essai).

## A Season of Controversy

The 2026 season was shadowed by two disputes that speak to broader anxieties in French publishing. The novel C'était ça ou mourir by Thélyson Orélien, once presented as a favourite, was removed from the Goncourt selection and eliminated from other prizes over suspicions that artificial intelligence had been used in its writing.

And on 6 November, more than 200 women authors signed an open letter in Libération denouncing the "sexism" of the prize system and the "masculine prize list" of 2026. The protest landed with particular force because both of France's flagship prizes had, once again, gone to established male novelists. Defenders of the system point to the Femina's all-female jury and to a shortlist that included several women; critics counter that the highest honours still concentrate overwhelmingly among men.

## What to Watch

For Jaenada and Haenel, the immediate prize is commercial: a Goncourt or Femina typically sends a novel into six-figure sales and reshapes an author's public standing. For the French literary establishment, the questions are larger. Whether the industry reckons with the sexism its critics describe — and how it draws boundaries around AI-assisted writing — will shape not only the next prize season but the credibility of institutions that France regards as part of its cultural identity.

For now, two seasoned novelists stand at the summit of a season that rewarded archival rigor and classroom testimony alike — and that left the prizes themselves under a scrutiny they rarely face.
