# EU Moves to End Abuses in Online Commerce

The European Commission unveiled a landmark package on Wednesday to overhaul consumer protection for the digital age, proposing a Digital Fairness Act (DFA) that would ban manipulative "dark patterns," trap subscriptions and misleading influencer marketing. Announced alongside a revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation (CPC) Regulation, the proposals mark the most significant reform of EU consumer rules in more than 20 years and would hand Brussels direct powers to fine companies up to 6% of their global annual turnover.

Presenting the package in Brussels were Henna Virkkunen, Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy, and Michael McGrath, Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection.

## A rulebook built for a different era

The reform targets a body of law that predates the explosion of e-commerce. As [Eunews](https://www.eunews.it/en/2026/11/11/eu-unveils-digital-fairness-act-tighter-rules-on-influencers-targeted-advertising-and-trap-subscriptions/) reported, McGrath said the main reason the package is necessary is that the EU's consumer protection framework "was designed before the exponential growth of e-commerce and digital markets."

The DFA would replace the 2005 Unfair Commercial Practices Directive with a regulation directly applicable in all 27 member states, reducing the patchwork of national interpretations. Central to the proposal is a "blacklist" of banned practices that authorities would deem unfair in all circumstances—removing the need to prove harm case by case.

## What the Digital Fairness Act would ban

The proposal takes aim at the design tricks that critics say distort consumer choice. According to the [European Commission](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_2407), the package includes a ban on manipulative marketing tactics—so-called "dark patterns"—such as fake claims that a product is almost sold out to pressure consumers into purchases they might not otherwise make.

Among the practices set for a blacklist are making it disproportionately harder to cancel than to sign up, burying prices or auto-renewal terms, and pre-selected options that quietly add extra products or quantities. "Drip pricing"—unavoidable fees that only appear at checkout—would be outlawed, and "was/now" discounts would have to be calculated against a genuine reference price.

The rules also extend to pricing transparency. In virtual ticket queues for cultural and sports events, companies would have to display the current price in real time alongside the highest possible price. Purchases made with in-game currencies would have to show their real-money value.

Subscriptions receive particular attention. Free trials could not roll into paid subscriptions without active consent, consumers would get reminders before auto-renewals, and a dedicated "terminate the contract here" button would be required. Consumers would also gain the right to speak to a human being rather than being left with a chatbot.

## Protections for children and against addictive design

The package introduces sweeping safeguards for minors. As [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/11/eu-targets-subscription-traps-addictive-design-and-hidden-fees-with-new-digital-rules) reported, paid loot boxes and pay-to-win or pay-to-progress mechanics would be banned for minors, and advertising based on profiling children would be prohibited for all traders—extending Digital Services Act restrictions beyond large platforms.

All users would gain the right to switch off addictive design features, including autoplay, non-related notifications, streak-style rewards and relationship-simulating chatbots. "Consumers would be given the option to disable addictive design features such as endless scrolling and autoplay videos," Euronews noted.

## First EU rules for influencers and AI shopping agents

For the first time, the EU would legislate on influencer marketing. Creators would be required to disclose commercial content clearly and would be barred from exploiting the trust of minors in their audience. Brands and agencies would share responsibility for ensuring compliance.

The proposal also breaks new ground on artificial intelligence. Under the DFA, consumers would choose which purchases an AI shopping agent may approve, and agents would have to disclose sponsored suggestions.

## Brussels gains direct enforcement powers

The accompanying revision of the CPC Regulation would transform how EU consumer law is enforced. At present, the Commission facilitates but cannot directly sanction cross-border breaches. Under the new rules, Brussels could investigate cases likely to affect consumers in at least two-thirds of member states and two-thirds of the EU population, targeting non-SME companies, with fines of up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover.

Non-EU companies would be required to appoint a legal representative in the EU. Euronews noted that the enforcement approach has delivered mixed results: Apple's €500 million fine in April 2025 prompted changes to its App Store policies, while Google antitrust cases produced more limited behavioural change. The Digital Markets Act already allows fines of up to 10%, and the Digital Services Act up to 6%.

## Economic stakes and a crowded rulebook

The Commission's impact assessment estimates the rules would spare consumers €88.4 billion in financial losses between 2025 and 2035. Including time lost to unfair practices, the annual benefit is put at €13.7 billion, against recurring costs to business of €678 million a year once overlaps are accounted for.

Yet the package lands amid tension over regulatory overload. EU leaders have called for simpler rules since the 2024 European elections, even as 2026 has produced a glut of digital proposals. The DFA would sit alongside the Digital Services Act, Digital Markets Act, AI Act and the recently proposed EU Kids Act.

"The rationale to update rules, some created 20 years ago, to digital economy makes sense, but there are rules already in place across consumer protection, privacy, platform regulation, AI, etc.," Isabelle Roccia, Managing Director for IAPP in Europe, told Euronews. "Does it embrace the Commission's simplification objective or unduly add to the complexity of online governance?"

Monika Zdzieborska, counsel at Clifford Chance, flagged three fault lines for the negotiations ahead: coherence with existing laws, whether protections should be defaults rather than opt-outs, and proportionality across very different kinds of services. Itxaso Domínguez de Olazábal of European Digital Rights warned that rules on addictive design still rely too heavily on individuals finding the off switch rather than requiring companies to prevent harm.

## What's next

The proposals must now be negotiated by the European Parliament and member states. Germany has signalled reservations, arguing existing directives should be amended rather than replaced by a new horizontal regulation. Once agreed, the regulation would apply 12 months after entering into force.

"The Single Market lives through its 450 million consumers," Virkkunen said. "Our laws must keep up with the way people live, shop and do business today." Whether that ambition survives the legislative process—and delivers the teeth its supporters demand—will determine how fair Europe's digital marketplace becomes.
