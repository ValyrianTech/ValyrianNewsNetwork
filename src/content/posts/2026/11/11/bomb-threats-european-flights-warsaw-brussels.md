# Bomb Threats Hit Flights Across Europe, Including Warsaw-Brussels

A LOT Polish Airlines flight from Warsaw to Brussels was among at least 27 European passenger flights targeted by a wave of bomb threats on Thursday, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/). The aircraft landed safely at Brussels Airport in Zaventem, and Belgian federal police concluded after a risk analysis that no additional measures were needed. No explosives were found on any aircraft across the entire campaign.

The scare was the most disruptive day yet in a multi-day series of false threats that began earlier in the week and spread across the continent, forcing diversions, fighter-jet escorts, and airport closures from Poland to Portugal.

## What Happened to the Warsaw-Brussels Flight

The threat against the LOT aircraft was received while it was en route to the Belgian capital. After landing, the airport's press office said "after investigation by the federal police, no additional measures turned out to be necessary," VRT NWS reported. Police are investigating whether the incident is linked to the earlier, similar threats. A second LOT flight, bound for Paris, was halted during taxiing and searched, but no suspicious package was found.

Belgium was struck twice that day. A Ryanair flight from Charleroi to Pescara in Italy was disrupted after a note bearing the word "bom" (bomb) was found in the aircraft's toilets at 13:13. Once the alert was confirmed false, the flight departed for Italy with a delay.

## A Continent-Wide Disruption

The scale of the 5 November wave was extraordinary. [NOS](https://nos.nl/artikel/2638119-opnieuw-bommeldingen-europese-vluchten-vliegtuig-breekt-vlucht-naar-rome-af) reported that an Aer Lingus Airbus A320 traveling from Dublin to Rome was diverted to the French military airbase at Évreux, roughly 100 kilometers northwest of Paris, intercepted and escorted by French fighter jets. French authorities said the passengers and crew were safe. A Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome was escorted by Italian fighter jets to Fiumicino, while Aegean and Ryanair flights landed in Milan and a Wizz Air flight from Milan to Warsaw diverted to Vienna.

In Bulgaria, three Ryanair flights received threats: two aircraft from Copenhagen and Madrid landed precautionarily in Sofia, while a third from London diverted to Plovdiv. Four aircraft bound for Prague were also affected, and further incidents were reported in Denmark and Austria. Late in the day, Romania's intelligence service reported two more threats, including a KLM flight to Tel Aviv with a stopover in Bucharest.

The pattern had begun earlier. On Tuesday 3 November, at least seven flights were hit, including two aircraft in Polish airspace that diverted to Warsaw-Modlin and briefly closed the airport, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/vliegtuig-noodlanding-polen-bommelding/). In each case, no explosives were found.

## Escalation Beyond Aviation

By Friday 6 November, the campaign had widened beyond airports. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/nieuwe-bommeldingen-in-europa/), threats reached Prague and Copenhagen airports and extended to media organizations in Ireland and the United Kingdom, including the Irish public broadcaster RTÉ and Sky News, as well as TF1 in France. Hungary scrambled fighter jets to escort three civilian flights, including one from Brussels to Sofia. France's prime minister reported threats on four flights over French territory, three of which landed at Orly and one at Bordeaux. These threats arrived by email.

Irish Taoiseach Micheál Martin said it was too early to say whether the media threats came from an individual or were coordinated, but described the aviation threats as clearly "coordinated attempts" to disrupt flights.

## Who Is Behind the Threats?

No formal attribution has been made, and EU Commissioner for Sustainable Transport and Tourism Apostolos Tzitzikostas said it is "too early to link the incidents to one another." But security experts increasingly view the campaign through the lens of hybrid warfare.

"Looking at these scenarios, there are indeed state actors who could be behind this to destabilize Europe," Eric Schouten, CEO of aviation security firm Dyami, told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/hoe-kan-je-luchtvaart-beschermen-tegen-valse-bommeldingen-europa/). "This fits entirely within the picture of how Russia operates at the moment. Just look at the supermarkets, blood banks, the railways and logistics centers." He cautioned against panic, however: "We should not panic, but for now this will be part of Europe."

Schouten noted that such threats often arrive by phone or email from "disposable agents," making attribution difficult. Copycats, disgruntled individuals, activist groups, and even airline employees have been cited as alternative culprits. Under international rules, aircraft must land or be held as soon as possible and evacuated for inspection, though the approach varies by country.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is whether the threats will continue or fade. European officials have not named a perpetrator, and the gap between expert suspicion and official attribution remains wide. What is clear is the operational and economic cost: diversions, closed airports, scrambled fighter jets, and evacuated buildings, all without a single device found.

For travelers, the practical lesson is that a bomb threat, even a false one, now carries real consequences for European air travel. As investigators work to trace the source, the continent is confronting a form of disruption that is cheap to mount, hard to attribute, and increasingly part of the security landscape.
