# EU Eyes Montenegro Treaty by Summer 2027

The European Commission expects it can sign an accession treaty with Montenegro in June 2027, a step that could make the Balkan country the bloc's 28th member state in 2028 — its first new member since Croatia joined in 2013. Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos made the timeline clear in an interview with [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/europese-unie-wil-in-juni-volgend-jaar-toetredingsverdrag-ondert/), while assessing how the other leading candidates — Ukraine, Serbia and Albania — are faring.

"Montenegro is on its way to becoming the next new member of the EU," Kos said. "The accession treaty is already being prepared."

## A Decade in the Making

Montenegro has been a candidate since 2010 and in accession talks since 2012. According to the Commission, it has closed 21 negotiation chapters and met all the conditions to close the remaining ones, with decisive progress in rule of law, economic governance, statistics and public administration. If negotiations conclude by the end of 2026 as hoped, roughly a year would then be needed for all member-state parliaments to ratify the treaty — pointing to membership in 2028.

Kos brushed aside concerns about scale, noting Montenegro has roughly as many inhabitants as the Belgian city of Antwerp. "I am certain we will bring the population on board," she said.

The most visible obstacle is a diplomatic dispute with Croatia, which has pressed a series of bilateral grievances linked to the legacy of the 1990s Yugoslav wars — including property claims, contested borders, war-crimes investigations and victim reparations. In October, Zagreb circulated an eight-point list of demands. As [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/16/germany-pressures-croatia-to-resolve-bilateral-disputes-with-montenegro-over-eu-accession) reported, Germany has stepped in to pressure Croatia to resolve the disputes and offered to mediate, while EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas publicly criticised any attempt to hijack the accession process.

## The Four Frontrunners

Kos named Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine as the four leading candidates. Albania has opened all negotiation clusters in just over a year and aims to finish all chapters by the end of 2027. Moldova, a candidate since 2022, has opened 25 of its 33 chapters and was described by Kos as the "best student in the class" — achieving the best results despite "unprecedented external pressures" from Russia and a divided domestic public.

Ukraine presents the most complicated case. It must carry out the same deep reforms on rule of law, press freedom and anti-corruption as other candidates, all while fighting Russia — a war that disrupts the functioning of its parliament. Kos hopes all negotiation chapters will be officially opened with Ukraine by the end of 2026, and she believes membership within five years is achievable.

"It is possible that Ukraine will be a member of the EU within 5 years," she said. "I believe that should be possible if we help Ukraine. Although that will also depend on how long the war lasts."

## Serbia's Opening

Serbia was once the second-most advanced candidate but regressed under the government of Aleksandar Vučić, with setbacks in fundamental rights, freedom of expression and public administration. That political landscape shifted in late October, when the student-led "Student List" defeated Vučić in snap parliamentary elections, ending more than a decade of his rule, as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/25/serbian-leader-aleksandar-vucic-concedes-defeat-in-fiercely-contested-parliamentary-election) reported.

Kos said she has already received the new leadership in Brussels. "If they mean it seriously and really want to carry out major reforms, then things could suddenly move very quickly for Serbia," she said.

## A Geopolitical Push

The 2026 Enlargement Package for the first time includes a dedicated security and defence chapter, reflecting Brussels' framing of enlargement as a security instrument amid tensions with Russia. Kos compared the moment to the EU's 2004 "big bang," when ten countries joined at once.

"This is something we haven't seen since the 'big bang' of 2004," she said. "I feel that more and more European leaders realise it is important to bring all those countries on board, if we want to protect ourselves in these geopolitical times."

## What to Watch

Several variables could still alter the timeline. Croatia's disputes must be settled before Montenegro can finalise its bid. Ukraine's path remains tethered to the war and to eventual peace negotiations. And a string of elections in key member states in 2027 — including France, where far-right leader Marine Le Pen, a traditional sceptic of EU expansion, is favoured to win the presidency — could sap political momentum.

For now, Brussels is betting that speed itself is a safeguard. Concluding Montenegro's negotiations by the end of 2026 and signing in June 2027 would send a signal that enlargement is real — not merely rhetorical — and could pull the other frontrunners along in its wake.
