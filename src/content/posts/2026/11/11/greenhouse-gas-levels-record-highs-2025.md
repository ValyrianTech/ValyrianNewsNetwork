# Greenhouse Gas Levels Hit Record Highs Again in 2025

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases reached new record highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) warned, locking the planet into further warming for decades to centuries. The [WMO's Greenhouse Gas Bulletin](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high), released on 27 October 2026, reported that the global average carbon dioxide concentration climbed to 426.8 parts per million (ppm) — 153% of the pre-industrial level of 1750.

"Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies."

## A Record Built Over Decades

The finding is the latest milestone in a relentless upward march. When the Bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 level stood at 377.5 ppm. Growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from roughly 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year over the 2011–2020 decade.

From 2024 to 2025, CO2 rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024. WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed the more moderate rise to the recovery of land carbon sinks and reduced fire emissions after the strong 2023/2024 El Nino.

"However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," she cautioned. "We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026."

Methane and nitrous oxide — the second and third most important long-lived greenhouse gases linked to human activity — also set records. Methane reached 1,948 parts per billion (267% of the 1750 level), while nitrous oxide hit 339.1 ppb (126% of pre-industrial levels). CO2 alone accounts for about 66% of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases.

The cumulative effect is measurable. According to the NOAA Annual Greenhouse Gas Index, radiative forcing from long-lived greenhouse gases rose 56% between 1990 and 2025, with CO2 responsible for roughly 81% of that increase. The past 11 years have been the warmest on record.

## The Gap Between Stock and Flow

The Bulletin measures concentrations — the accumulated stock of gas in the atmosphere — rather than annual emissions. Roughly half of the CO2 emitted each year remains in the atmosphere; the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans. Because CO2 lingers for centuries, even steep future cuts to emissions cannot quickly reverse the concentration already built up. This is why the WMO stresses "committed" future warming.

As [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779336/article/2026-10-27/une-nouvelle-annee-un-nouveau-record-la-concentration-gaz-effet-de-serre-atteint) reported, the UN agency framed the finding as a warning that the planet is "vouee a un avenir plus chaud" — condemned to a warmer future.

## A Warning for COP31

The Bulletin is timed to inform negotiators at the COP31 UN climate conference in Antalya, Turkiye, which — according to the WMO release — runs from 9 to 20 November 2026 and is seeking to turn climate commitments into concrete actions. But the chasm between the bulletin's findings and current policy ambition remains wide.

## The Energy Transition's Hidden Risks

The Bulletin also flags two potent but less abundant gases tied to electricity production. Sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), used as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, has a lifetime of over 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO2. Its 2025 increase was a record high.

Molecular hydrogen (H2), meanwhile, rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024. It is not a greenhouse gas itself but has a strong indirect warming effect via atmospheric chemistry. Leakage from production facilities, pipelines, storage tanks and fuel cells poses a growing concern as hydrogen scales up as an energy carrier.

"Both H2 leakage and SF6 persistence represent potential threats that could undermine the climate benefits of the energy transition," the WMO warned.

## What's Next

Barrett's warning is stark: "CO2 has a very long life-time and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries." The Bulletin's findings will loom over COP31, where negotiators must reconcile the pace of emissions cuts with the physical reality of a planet already committed to additional warming.
