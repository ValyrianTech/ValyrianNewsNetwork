# Asustek, Acer Shift Some Capacity Back to Mainland China

Taiwan's two leading PC brands, Asustek and Acer, are moving part of their production capacity from Thailand and Vietnam back to mainland China, a reversal that has drawn attention on the island to the deep entanglement of cross-strait supply chains. Beijing's State Council Taiwan Affairs Office responded on Wednesday by reaffirming its support for Taiwanese businesses investing on the mainland, [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34450991) reported.

Speaking at the office's regular press conference on the morning of November 11, spokesperson Zhang Han said the mainland's complete industrial system and mature supporting network make it "the best choice" for Taiwan-invested manufacturing.

## Why the PC Makers Are Coming Back

The capacity shift is primarily a response to a severe cost shock in components rather than a political decision. According to Nikkei Asia reporting cited by [IT之家](https://www.ithome.com/1/018/779.htm), AI infrastructure demand has triggered acute shortages of memory and storage chips, sending bill-of-materials costs soaring for PC makers.

A gaming-PC executive told Nikkei that DRAM prices alone had risen "at least eightfold in a year," with CPU, circuit board, and passive component costs also climbing. "This adjustment is fundamentally driven by cost pressure," the executive said. "PC makers can no longer absorb the cost increases themselves."

Omdia data illustrates the squeeze: the bill-of-materials cost for mainstream PCs retailing between $500 and $699 rose from $435 in the first quarter of 2025 to $699 in the third quarter of 2026. Entry-level machines under $500 saw their costs climb from $275 to $471 over the same period.

China's advantages — a mature industrial workforce, lower costs, a complete supply-chain system, and full access to components — have made it the optimal location for computer production again. The suspension of US high tariffs on laptops and desktops has also reduced the incentive to keep manufacturing outside China.

## A Shift in Foundry Priorities

The brands are turning to lesser-known Chinese contract manufacturers, including Luxshare Precision, Huaqin, and Longcheer, because major Taiwanese foundries such as Quanta, Compal, Inventec, and Foxconn have pivoted toward more profitable AI data-center server orders.

A joint venture between Luxshare and Shanghai Liulian Intelligent Technology, known as LuxPC Union, has reportedly won orders from HP, Dell, and Asustek over the past year. Dell, the world's third-largest PC maker, is reportedly weighing a similar move.

Acer said it would continue to adjust its supply chain dynamically. "We dynamically adjust supply-chain operations to enhance resilience and maintain competitiveness," an Acer spokesperson told Nikkei, adding that the company has long worked with multiple manufacturers and suppliers to maintain flexibility.

## Beijing's Message to Taiwanese Investors

Zhang's remarks at the press conference framed the development within Beijing's broader economic strategy, referencing the newly unveiled 15th Five-Year Plan as offering stronger momentum and support for Taiwanese firms.

"Tight and resilient cross-strait industrial and supply chains not only benefit the growth of Taiwanese firms and the wellbeing of compatriots on both sides, but also global supply-chain security, stability, and flow," Zhang said.

"At present, the blueprint of the 15th Five-Year Plan has been laid out; high-level opening-up and deep reform are efficiently linked, releasing stronger momentum... We will, as always, support the broad community of Taiwanese businesses and firms to seize opportunities and invest and develop on the mainland."

The response aligns with Beijing's consistent messaging that the mainland remains the most attractive destination for Taiwanese capital, a narrative aimed at reassuring investors and countering warnings from Taipei about the risks of mainland investment.

## Deep Interdependence Despite Diversification

The reversal is notable given the earlier trend of offshoring. Nikkei Asia reported in 2023 that Dell was among the first PC makers to require suppliers to avoid China-made components and shift capacity to Vietnam; HP followed with a goal of moving up to 70% of its capacity out of China.

The current shift underscores how deeply Taiwan's PC supply chains remain embedded in the mainland despite years of diversification efforts, and how AI-server demand has reshuffled foundry priorities, opening space for Chinese contract manufacturers.

Global PC shipments fell 21% year-on-year in the third quarter of 2026, according to Omdia, with a projected 24% decline in the fourth quarter. Counterpoint forecasts a 27% drop for the same period.

## What to Watch

The scale of the capacity being shifted remains unclear, as does whether these are temporary, cost-driven adjustments or a durable reversal of the diversification trend. The interaction with US export controls and tariff policy, particularly whether the tariff suspension holds, will be a key variable. Whether Dell and HP expand the trend will also determine how significantly the balance of PC manufacturing shifts back toward the mainland.

For now, the episode offers a reminder that economic gravity, driven by cost and supply-chain realities, can override geopolitical currents in the global electronics industry.
