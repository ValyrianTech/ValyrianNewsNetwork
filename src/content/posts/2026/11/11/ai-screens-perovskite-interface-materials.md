# AI Screens Perovskite Interface Materials at Record Pace

Researchers in China have built an artificial-intelligence framework that screens self-assembled monolayer (SAM) molecules for perovskite solar cells, dramatically narrowing a vast chemical search space to a handful of promising candidates. The team reports that its top-ranked molecule produced a device with 27.2% power conversion efficiency that retained about 95% of its initial performance after 2,000 hours of continuous operation, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261111/1758a7bf96c044e2bd69ba0aaf224515/c.html), which carried a report originally published by *Science and Technology Daily*.

The work, published in the journal *Nature Synthesis*, was led by Cai Fengchun's team at the University of Science and Technology of China, in collaboration with the Southern University of Science and Technology and City University of Hong Kong.

## Why the interface is the battleground

Perovskite solar cells are among the most closely watched next-generation photovoltaic technologies, promising high efficiency at lower cost than conventional silicon. A crucial component of today's best inverted (p-i-n) perovskite devices is the self-assembled monolayer — a nanometer-thin film of molecules that sits at the interface between the perovskite absorber and the substrate, controlling how charge carriers are extracted and how stable the device remains.

As the Chinese Academy of Sciences' [Hefei Institutes of Physical Science](https://hf.cas.cn/zhxw/jrtt/202604/t20260421_8189421.html) has noted, SAM-based inverted perovskite devices have surpassed 27% certified efficiency and become a core direction for low-cost, high-efficiency photovoltaics. Yet these devices face a persistent efficiency–stability tradeoff: under ultraviolet light, heat, and humidity, the hydroxyl anchoring bonds between the SAM and the substrate can break, causing molecules to detach and device performance to decay rapidly — a key barrier to commercialization.

Designing a better SAM is hard because the requirements pull in different directions. A molecule must match energy levels, form the right interfacial interactions, be synthetically feasible, and remain compatible with solution processing. "An excellent computed metric does not mean the molecule will make a usable device material," the research team noted, adding that traditional trial-and-error development is slow and struggles to balance multiple conflicting requirements.

## From 90,000 structures to three

To close that gap, the team embedded synthetic feasibility into the screening process from the outset, connecting computational design with experimental reality. Starting from carbazole derivatives in the PubChem database, the researchers extracted 308 functional fragments corresponding to more than 90,000 potential combination structures.

They first selected 24 fragments, then used modular assembly to build 300 candidate molecules. An initial energy-level screen narrowed the field to 124 candidates. These were then scored across eight indicators — including synthetic feasibility, molecular energy levels, hole reorganization energy, aromaticity, and heteroatom content — to decide which molecules merited experimental synthesis.

The team synthesized the three highest-ranked candidates. Crucially, the ranking of their measured device performance matched the eight-descriptor composite score, validating the scoring system's predictive power — a rare closed-loop confirmation in AI-guided materials discovery.

## What the numbers mean

The top-ranked molecule delivered 27.2% efficiency. Under 65°C, continuous illumination, and maximum-power-point tracking, the device held roughly 95% of its initial efficiency after 2,000 hours. Those figures land in territory that matters for commercialization, where both high efficiency and durable operation are prerequisites.

The result arrives amid an intensely competitive global race in perovskite and perovskite-silicon tandem photovoltaics, a field where Chinese institutions and companies are prominent. Earlier this month, module maker Longi reported a perovskite-silicon tandem cell reaching 35.31% efficiency — 35.25% certified — using engineered SAM molecules to control molecular stacking, with the device retaining 94% of its initial efficiency after 1,500 hours, as [pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/11/06/longi-achieves-35-31-efficiency-in-perovskite-silicon-tandem-solar-cell-based-on-self-assembled-molecules/) reported.

## An approach built to travel

The framework's designers emphasize that it is modular and can be extended to other interface-material systems, offering a new design path for high-efficiency, stable optoelectronic materials. By treating synthetic feasibility as a first-class screening criterion, the method addresses a common weakness of purely computational materials discovery: candidates that look brilliant on paper but cannot be made in the lab.

## What to watch

Several questions remain open. The 27.2% efficiency appears to be a research-reported value; it is not clear from the source whether it was independently certified by a recognized testing center. The work also does not yet show how the top molecule performs in larger-area modules rather than small research cells, nor how it compares head-to-head against incumbent SAM materials in the same study. Cost and manufacturing scalability of the newly synthesized molecules were not detailed.

Still, the reported agreement between predicted ranking and measured device performance suggests a maturing role for AI in the lab — not as a replacement for experiment, but as a way to decide which experiments are worth running. For a field where each synthesis-and-test cycle is slow and costly, that efficiency may prove as valuable as any single record.
