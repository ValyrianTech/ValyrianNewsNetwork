# Berkshire Q3 Profit Falls 28% as Buybacks Accelerate

Berkshire Hathaway reported a 28% decline in third-quarter net profit on Saturday, even as it sharply accelerated share repurchases, stayed a net seller of stocks and pushed its cash reserves to a record. The results, the latest under CEO Greg Abel, paint a picture of a conglomerate turning inward and defensive as it navigates a turbulent market.

Net profit attributable to shareholders fell to $22.112 billion from $30.796 billion a year earlier, [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/07/berkshire-hathaway-q3-2026-earnings-buybacks.html). Revenue, however, rose 11.24% year on year to $105.647 billion.

## Operating Earnings Slip on Insurance Weakness

The decline in headline profit was driven largely by investment gains, which fell nearly 50% to $9.407 billion. Berkshire has long cautioned that mark-to-market swings make quarterly net income figures potentially misleading, a caveat reiterated in its filing and echoed by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34429766): "Any investment gains or losses in a particular quarter are usually meaningless, and the resulting net-income figures could greatly mislead investors who have limited understanding of accounting rules."

The more telling gauge — operating earnings, which exclude investment swings — fell 5.8% to $12.705 billion from $13.485 billion. Insurance underwriting was the biggest drag, tumbling 43% to $1.352 billion, while insurance investment income slipped 14% to $2.734 billion.

The weakness was partly offset by strength elsewhere: the BNSF railroad earned $1.729 billion, up about 19%, manufacturing, service and retail rose roughly 12% to $4.042 billion, and Berkshire Hathaway Energy climbed about 8% to $1.612 billion.

## The Most Aggressive Buyback Quarter on Record

Berkshire bought back about $9.6 billion of its own stock in the quarter — a single-quarter record and more than double the $4.5 billion repurchased in the second quarter, according to [Berkshire's earnings release](https://berkshirehathaway.com/news/nov0726.pdf). Buybacks for the first nine months of 2026 totaled roughly $14.4 billion.

The acceleration, as The Paper noted, "sends a clear signal: high-quality acquisition targets meeting the company's standards remain scarce, and Berkshire itself may be the asset most worth investing in right now." It was also a reversal in market posture: after being a net buyer of roughly $11.6 billion of equities in the first half, Berkshire was a net seller of about $426 million in the third quarter.

## A Record Cash Pile and a Concentrated Portfolio

Cash reserves climbed about 3% to $377.435 billion as of Sept. 30 from $365.514 billion in the second quarter — a figure that underscores management's difficulty finding attractively valued deals. CNBC noted a nuance, however: excluding railroad and utility cash and T-bill payables, "core" cash actually fell 1.9% to $352.3 billion.

Berkshire's five largest equity holdings — Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America and Coca-Cola — together accounted for roughly 65% of its equity portfolio's fair value. The Alphabet stake, a large AI-adjacent bet built through a $10 billion strategic investment in June, has been a notable drag, with the shares down nearly 25% since then.

## Why the Stock Is Rising Anyway

Despite the weaker earnings, Berkshire shares outperformed. Class A stock rose more than 12% over the past month while the S&P 500 fell 5.5%, and Berkshire is up 13% year to date versus 7.8% for the index.

Analysts attribute the divergence to two forces: a violent rotation out of AI and technology stocks that has pushed defensive money back toward Berkshire's low-tech profile, and easing investor anxiety over the leadership transition from Warren Buffett, who stepped down at the end of 2025 after roughly 60 years, to Abel.

## What to Watch

The quarter offers an early read on the Abel era's capital-allocation philosophy. Three questions loom: whether the Alphabet bet recovers or curbs Berkshire's appetite for large tech wagers; where the record cash pile will be deployed, whether in a major acquisition, further buybacks or continued accumulation; and whether the 43% slump in insurance underwriting is a cyclical soft-market issue or a structural margin problem heading into 2027.

For now, Berkshire appears content to bet on itself — and to wait.
