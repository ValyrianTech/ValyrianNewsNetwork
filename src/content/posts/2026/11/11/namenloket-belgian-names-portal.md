# New Portal Makes 30,000 Belgian Names Searchable

Where does your surname come from, and what did it originally mean? Belgians can now find out through the Namenloket, a new website on proper names launched by Ghent University (UGent), which makes the origins of more than 30,000 Belgian family names searchable, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/09/nieuwe-website-maakt-meer-dan-30-000-belgische-familienamen-en-p/).

The platform does more than answer questions about surnames: visitors can also explore the history of place names, animal names, water names and even storm names.

## From Library Shelves to a Search Box

For decades, much of the research into names existed only on paper, locked away in libraries or printed in limited runs. The Namenloket changes that by fully digitising Frans Debrabandere's 1993 *Woordenboek van de Familienamen in België en Noord-Frankrijk* (Dictionary of Family Names in Belgium and Northern France).

"Much research into names was done in the past, but that knowledge sits mainly in paper dictionaries," explained Linde Lapauw, project coordinator of the Namenloket at UGent.

According to [UGent's Namenloket portal](https://namenloket.be/over-namenloket/), the project is an initiative of Variaties vzw carried out with the university's Department of Linguistics, the Instituut voor de Nederlandse Taal and the Koninklijke Commissie voor Toponymie en Dialectologie, among other partners.

"The beauty of the website is that we make one man's life's work accessible," said Anne-Sophie Ghyselen, professor of Language and Literature at UGent. Debrabandere spent years poring over historical documents to establish where family names come from and what they originally meant, including when a name first appeared in written sources.

## What Each Search Reveals

Each entry offers information about a name's meaning or origin and often the oldest known occurrences in historical sources. The database also supports targeted searches: users can, for instance, pull up every family name recorded in historical sources before the year 1500.

"You can, for example, look up all surnames derived from a mother's first name," Ghyselen said.

The project aimed to make the material both rigorous and readable. "We have linguistic editors who wrote texts for a broad public. These texts are always reviewed by experts," Ghyselen explained. "So the information is scientifically reliable, but also accessible to a broad public."

## Acknowledging the Gaps

Not every Belgian will find their surname. Because the original dictionary was based largely on names that appeared more than once in Belgium and neighbouring countries around the 1990s, many surnames belonging to Belgians with a more recent migration background are missing.

"We realise this is a gap," Ghyselen said. Visitors who cannot find their name can submit it through a "Donate your name" button. "On that basis we draw up a list for further research, so we can expand the database and have it also reflect the current population."

The researchers plan to grow the database in the coming years. Digitisation of a dictionary of Flemish municipality names is already under way, with water names and first names to follow. According to Variaties vzw, the project runs through February 2027.

## Names as Heritage

Beyond the searchable dictionary, the portal answers broader questions about naming: why storms receive names, and why pets are increasingly given human names such as Odette or Patrick.

"The animal-name section notes that people used to give their dogs or cats no name at all," Lapauw said, adding that the shift reflects a changing bond with pets.

At its heart, the project is about preserving knowledge. "That knowledge is part of our heritage and tells us much about our history and identity," Lapauw said.

For a country whose surnames echo occupations, nicknames and ancestral first names, the Namenloket offers an accessible way to read those stories — and an invitation to help write the next chapter for names still missing from the record.
