# 6,300 Italian Schools Hit by False Bomb Threats

A single coordinated wave of email bomb threats reached roughly 6,300 schools across Italy on the morning of 11 November 2026, triggering precautionary evacuations at several hundred institutions, while hundreds of schools in Northern Ireland, Scotland and the Republic of Ireland received the same message. No explosives were found anywhere, and authorities in all three countries said the threats were not credible.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/11/bommeldingen-scholen-europa/), an anonymous email signed by an unknown group calling itself the "Vilayat of Misoginistan" and attributed to a figure named "Mikhail Andropov" landed in school inboxes across Italy early in the day. Initial reports spoke of more than 300 schools, all of which were evacuated; the education ministry later confirmed the message had reached around 6,300 of the country's roughly 7,600 state schools, many of them via spam folders.

## A Morning of Evacuations

The alarm began in Rome before spreading nationwide within minutes. According to [Rainews/Rai](https://www.rainews.it/maratona/2026/11/allarmi-bomba-in-oltre-cento-scuole-in-tutta-italia-mail-agli-istituti-studenti-evacuati-6fdcb91e-5ac8-48bf-85e9-8ab4fecf3e5c.html), evacuations and checks were reported in Turin, Milan, Bologna, Trieste, Naples, Cagliari and dozens of smaller cities from the Piedmont to Calabria, involving students of every age group — including small children who waited outdoors under thermal blankets in Trieste as police inspected their schools.

Not every institution evacuated. At the historic Parini high school in Milan, headmaster Massimo Nunzio Barrella chose to keep lessons running after consulting the postal police. "It seemed like an email from a Martian — 'We have come to annihilate you,'" he told AdnKronos, as quoted by Rainews. "The fact that it was sent en masse made me think differently. If it had been addressed only to the Parini, I would have evacuated immediately."

The message itself was lurid. Rainews published excerpts in which the sender claimed to have left "a powerful explosive device with a remote detonator" in the building, threatened to "carry out a genocide against Italians," claimed to have planted a bomb in the prime minister's car, and mocked European authorities as "incompetent fools, incapable of doing anything to stop us."

## Across the Channel and the Irish Sea

The same email reached more than 300 schools in Northern Ireland, with similar messages sent to schools in Scotland and "several" institutions in the Republic of Ireland. Unlike Italy, no schools there were evacuated on police advice.

"Based on investigations and information gathered so far, the police consider this email, which came from outside the United Kingdom, a false alarm," the Police Service of Northern Ireland said, as [reported by Rainews](https://www.rainews.it/articoli/2026/11/allarmi-bomba-via-mail-anche-nel-regno-unito-irlanda-e-scozia-minacce-false-671459bc-7354-45af-aa1d-901dc44075d1.html). Deputy Chief Constable Anthony McNally added: "We take incidents of this kind very seriously... however, at this stage, we believe the risk to the public is low." Irish police said the threat was "not deemed credible" and recommended no evacuations.

## Who Is 'Mikhail Andropov'?

The recurring signature is the thread linking this wave to a broader campaign. The same "Mikhail Andropov" pseudonym appeared the previous week in threat emails to European airports and to media outlets including Ireland's RTÉ, Bulgaria's Nova TV and Britain's Sky News, [according to a Rainews analysis](https://www.rainews.it/articoli/2026/11/allarmi-bomba-nelle-scuole-chi-si-nasconde-dietro-lo-pseudonimo-mikhail-andropov-39e31a11-93d7-4008-adaa-ee62f4d8ecbe.html).

The name deliberately evokes Yuri Andropov, the Soviet leader from 1982 to 1984, and earlier messages carried the title "Message to the West." Yet despite the Soviet imagery, there is no evidence attributing the threats to Russia or Russian organisations. The emails were sent from constantly rotating addresses, some traced to educational institutions abroad — a school in Lithuania, a university in Thailand, and, in the Italian case, an address reportedly linked to Taiwan — suggesting compromised or spoofed accounts.

"Vilayat" derives from the Arabic wilaya, meaning a province or territory, juxtaposed with "Misoginistan," an internet meme mocking misogyny — a construction that appears designed to attract attention and blur real intent behind provocation.

## Rome's Response

Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni convened an emergency meeting at Palazzo Chigi at 16:00 with her deputy prime ministers, the interior and defence ministers, and the heads of the intelligence services and the National Cybersecurity Agency. "At the moment, the checks carried out do not reveal any element that would make us believe a concrete danger exists," Meloni said. "The reports seem rather attributable to a strategy aimed at generating alarm and concern."

The government's tone was cautious about attribution. Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi said it was "premature to speak of a common command/coordination," describing "a trail of events that, at least in the form of emulation, has the common trait of seeking to engage the public system through alarms that often fortunately prove unfounded." Defence Minister Guido Crosetto went further: "It is not always the work of foreign countries or hostile entities. There are also attention-seekers and madmen." Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani framed it as "a strategy that aims to create chaos and undermine citizens' trust, if not to interfere in democratic processes."

## A Low-Cost, High-Impact Tactic

Criminologists see the school hoaxes as a textbook hybrid attack. "The school bomb hoaxes are an immaterial threat that has social, cognitive and institutional consequences," Arije Antinori, a professor of criminology at Sapienza University of Rome, told ANSA, as quoted by Rainews. "It attacks the system of relationships... it overloads institutions with tasks and creates an environment in which it becomes hard to distinguish an authentic threat from a false one." Schools are pivotal, he argued, because they are "the gateway to an interdependent system of families, institutions, media and public opinion."

The episode also exposed gaps in preparedness. Italy's National Coordination of Teachers called for a national digital-alarm assessment system linked to police and cybersecurity agencies, arguing that emergency management cannot depend on individual schools' capacity to interpret threats. Rome's social policies assessor, Barbara Funari, noted that evacuations fall hardest on students with disabilities and their families.

## The Latest Phase of an Escalating Campaign

The 11 November school threats are the latest stage of a multi-week wave of mass hoax bomb threats across Europe. On 5 November, around 30 flights were held on the ground or made emergency landings, with fighter jets scrambled in some countries. A day later, a new round of false threats hit airports in Prague and Copenhagen and targeted media organisations in Ireland, the UK and France. On 19 October, hundreds of schools in Northern Ireland, Scotland, Hungary and Serbia had already received threatening emails.

Attribution remains unresolved. Some European countries suspect Russia or Iran, but no government has formally attributed the threats to a specific group or state. Tajani said those who hit Italy were "certainly pro-Russian, but we don't know if the Moscow government is behind it."

## What to Watch

Meloni was due to brief opposition leaders on the morning of 12 November on "the complex picture of alarms and reports in recent days, also in relation to hacker activities." The open questions are whether investigators can establish a common hand behind the waves, whether the recurring "Andropov" signature reflects a single actor or opportunistic emulation, and whether Europe will move toward the cross-border coordination that officials — including an EU aviation security committee that met in Brussels the same day — have identified as a priority. For now, the concrete damage is measured not in explosions but in lost school hours, anxious parents and the slow erosion of confidence that a threat, once reported, can be trusted.
