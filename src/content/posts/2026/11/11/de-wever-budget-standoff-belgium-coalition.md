# De Wever's Budget Standoff Tests Belgium's Fragile Coalition

Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever resumed his stalled federal budget negotiations on Wednesday, completing a round of one-on-one talks with his deputy prime ministers before a deadline-driven report to King Philippe on 16 November. But as the bilateral consultations wrapped up with budget minister Vincent Van Peteghem, the central question in Brussels was no longer whether the talks had restarted — it was whether De Wever's negotiating method still works.

Speaking at the Armistice Day ceremony at the Congress Column in Brussels, the premier offered a wry answer when asked whether there was a truce inside his government. "A truce in the government? We are still waiting for that," De Wever said, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/premier-de-wever-hervat-gesprekken-wapenstilstand-in-de-regeri~1793247532380/).

## A deadline set by the palace

The current round follows a dramatic sequence of events. De Wever offered his resignation to King Philippe in late October after budget talks collapsed. After consulting the five coalition party presidents, the king refused the resignation on 6 November and asked the premier to restart negotiations "as soon as possible," setting a reporting deadline of Monday 16 November at 10:30.

According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/negociations-budgetaires-du-federal-en-panne-la-methode-de-wever-pose-de-plus-en-plus-question-11813881), the palace communiqué stated that all five party presidents had given a formal commitment to make concessions and to put "the sense of compromise and responsibility first." De Wever, for his part, has cleared his agenda — skipping the EU summit in Dublin on 13 and 14 November and freeing his weekend — to focus on the budget.

The task is substantial. The "Arizona" coalition, which took office in early 2025, is trying to find roughly 10 billion euros in savings by 2029 to rein in Belgium's federal deficit. An earlier effort that included a VAT reform raising taxes on luxury goods was shelved after opposition from the francophone liberal MR.

## The fault line: taxes versus cuts

The core dispute is about how to close the gap. Vooruit and Les Engagés argue that the "strongest shoulders" — higher incomes, wealth and capital — should contribute through new taxes. The MR insists the February 2025 coalition agreement caps new revenue at one-ninth of the total effort, roughly 1.1 billion euros, and warns that breaching that limit would require reopening the entire agreement.

"To do the exercise fairly, everyone must make an effort. And then we look at the people with the strongest shoulders," Les Engagés president Yvan Verougstraete told De Tijd, while conceding that in the failed round "everyone stayed clinging to their big principles."

This week's bilateral format — in which each deputy premier was allowed to bring one additional person — brought MR president Georges-Louis Bouchez to the table as the "plus one" of vice-premier David Clarinval. For VRT NWS analyst Ivan De Vadder, the arrangement is unprecedented: "Never before have a premier and a shadow premier discussed policy together at the government table," he [wrote](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/08/de-schaduwpremier-mee-aan-de-regeringstafel/), describing it as "the De Wever-Bouchez government."

## The method under fire

The resumption of talks has not quieted criticism. RTBF reported that the bilateral meetings produced no breakthroughs or major concessions, with each party holding to its positions and taboos. De Wever reportedly presented spreadsheets to each delegation that were "largely similar, barely modified" from earlier rounds.

One anonymous negotiator told RTBF: "He lacks creativity, we don't see where he's going." Another described "a real policy of small steps, but very small steps," adding: "In short, we're going in circles."

The premier's office has rejected suggestions that he is losing time, arguing it is pointless to convene the full cabinet only to watch talks collapse within minutes. But De Vadder noted that serious criticism of De Wever's approach has surfaced for the first time — that he "started too late," that by focusing only on a multi-year budget "he boxed himself in," and that "he only wants to negotiate on the basis of his own note, which essentially reflects his own party's position."

Former prime minister Yves Leterme added his voice on VRT NWS's De Ochtend, calling the current methods "confusing." Noting that De Wever had once vowed never to accept "provisional twelfths" yet now governs under them, Leterme said the premier's resignation offer had changed little. "The drive has somewhat gone out of it," he said, comparing the government to a bicycle that keeps rolling only while it is being pushed.

## Why it matters

For the second consecutive year, Belgium will enter 2027 operating under provisional twelfths — a month-by-month extension of the last approved budget — for at least its opening months, as De Wever himself told the Chamber of Representatives. Under that regime, salaries, pensions and health spending continue, but there is no room for new expenses or new revenues. Because Belgium runs a structural deficit, the mechanism mechanically deepens it, adding urgency to a deal.

The political stakes are equally high. Opposition parties on both flanks are demanding elections. Vlaams Belang leader Tom Van Grieken argued that "if politicians can no longer work it out, we must go back to the voter," while PS leader Paul Magnette said that if the De Wever government "is incapable of governing, then it has only one option: organise elections."

## What to watch

Three scenarios are in play ahead of Monday's royal audience, as VRT NWS political journalist Benedikte Coussement outlined: no deal and the government falls; further extensions with a new deadline; or De Wever arriving at the palace with an agreement in hand.

Most observers consulted by RTBF view a breakthrough as a bold bet, with the 16 November report potentially serving as a transitional visit to request more time. Real negotiations, they note, are unlikely to begin before Friday or the weekend. Whether Belgium's five-party coalition can translate formal promises of compromise into a budget — or whether the standoff finally breaks it — will be decided in the days ahead.

The deeper issue may be structural. Only two of the five party presidents actually serve in cabinet, leaving the others — Bouchez, Vooruit's Conner Rousseau and CD&V's Sammy Mahdi — with influence but no ministerial responsibility. As that "hybrid construction" sits at the heart of the deadlock, the question of who truly holds power in Belgium is proving as difficult to resolve as the deficit itself.
