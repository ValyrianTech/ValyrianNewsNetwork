# ICE Records Show Deportations, Arrests at New Highs

U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement recorded its highest-ever annual deportation and arrest totals in fiscal year 2026, according to figures released by the Department of Homeland Security and first reported by [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/ice-deportations-arrests-hit-record-high/). The numbers mark a new peak in immigration enforcement—but they arrive amid a fierce dispute over how such figures are counted, and as the political ground beneath the crackdown appears to be shifting.

According to DHS, ICE carried out 483,200 deportations in FY2026, which ended September 30, surpassing the prior record of 442,640 set the year before. The agency also logged more than 491,000 immigration arrests, including nearly 475,000 administrative—or civil—arrests. The deportation total is roughly 78 percent higher than the 271,484 recorded in FY2024, President Biden's last full year in office.

## A Record by One Measure, a Decline by Another

The celebration at DHS was immediate. "Fiscal Year 2026 saw the highest number of ICE deportations and arrests for any year in ICE's history," DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin said in a statement. "The total number of ICE arrests in Fiscal Year 2026 was more than all three full fiscal years under Biden combined, as the patriots of ICE work around the clock to make America safe again."

But the record is highly sensitive to definition. DHS's "deportations" figure combines formal removals with voluntary departures and voluntary returns—a broad measure that critics say inflates the headline. A [report published a day earlier by the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse (TRAC)](https://tracreports.org/reports/783/) at Syracuse University, drawing on case-by-case records obtained through a FOIA lawsuit, found that actual formal removals have *declined* under Trump.

TRAC documented 39,893 removals in February 2025, the first full month of Trump's second term, falling to 26,908 by July 2026—a 13 percent drop. It projects roughly 316,312 removals for all of FY2026, about 29 percent lower than the record 446,180 recorded in FY2024 under Biden.

"Contrary to news reporting and public perception, the number of individuals removed from the United States actually declined after President Trump assumed office in January 2025," TRAC wrote. It noted that even adding voluntary departures and returns, projected deportations for FY2026 of about 481,008 are roughly comparable to the 489,251 peak in FY2009 under President Obama.

CBS itself acknowledged the distinction, noting that "a record number of reported deportations does not necessarily mean a record number of formal removal orders carried out."

## The Enforcement Surge Behind the Numbers

The figures are the product of an extraordinary expansion of resources and targets. Roughly $170 billion was allocated for immigration enforcement and border security through the July 2025 tax-and-spending law, including $75 billion in additional funding for ICE and $45 billion for detention capacity. ICE has been instructed to target approximately 19,000 arrests per week—above its historical pace.

Monthly arrest figures reflect the intensity: 50,925 arrests in August alone eclipsed July's monthly high, with more than 48,000 arrests in September. Much of the enforcement has targeted people without serious criminal records. A Migration Policy Institute analysis cited by CBS found that only about 20 percent of roughly 980,000 deportations carried out jointly by ICE and Customs and Border Protection between January 2025 and July 2026 involved someone with a criminal conviction.

## Political Reckoning

The record figures land at an awkward political moment. Republicans suffered sweeping losses in the November midterms, and President Trump himself conceded in an interview with "60 Minutes" that parts of the crackdown "may have gone too far." He expressed openness to working with congressional Democrats on immigration legislation—a notable shift for a president whose political identity has rested on mass deportation.

CBS national exit polling found that 58 percent of midterm voters said Trump's immigration enforcement had gone too far, while 22 percent said it was about right and 20 percent said it had not gone far enough.

Yet the administration shows little sign of operational retreat. After the midterms, Trump elevated hardliner Stephen Miller to White House chief of staff—a signal that the enforcement agenda will continue even as its architects face new scrutiny. Multiple administration officials told CBS they do not expect the crackdown to slow.

Democrats, who take control of both chambers in January, have vowed to scrutinize ICE's operations, spending and tactics, with some calling to abolish the agency. But their leverage is constrained: billions have already been appropriated to ICE, and the president retains veto power. The White House's top lawyer has already warned the incoming House investigative chair that aggressive oversight "may cross the line into blatant criminality," according to [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/11/us/politics/trump-democrats-congress-oversight-committee.html).

The agency has also faced internal turmoil. Reporting by [The Intercept](https://theintercept.com/2026/10/15/ice-omar-raid-midterms-political/) described what officials called "midterm panic moves," including voter-fraud probes across nine states and a raid on the home of Rep. Ilhan Omar. In September, ICE leadership quietly ordered officers to stop arresting undocumented immigrants without criminal histories before the directive was reportedly reversed days later—an episode DHS publicly denied.

## A Counting Fight With Real Stakes

The dispute over how to count deportations is more than semantics. Immigration hawks have grown skeptical that DHS is inflating its numbers, and the Oversight Project has sued the agency to release fuller data, as [The Free Press](https://www.thefp.com/p/immigration-hawks-are-suing-trumps) reported. The administration claims more than 3 million people have been deported; advocates argue the figures don't add up.

TRAC's analysis points to a structural shift in how people are leaving the country. Voluntary departures ordered by immigration judges soared in FY2026—more than 100,000 orders, up from about 9,000 in FY2024. Roughly 134,000 people left under such orders in FY2026, versus 5,400 in FY2024, an increase of more than 2,000 percent. Such departures, which carry no bar on future legal reentry, now make up a far larger share of the total than they did early in Trump's term.

## What to Watch

The coming months will test whether the enforcement surge can be sustained—and whether its accounting survives scrutiny. Democrats' oversight, ongoing litigation over DHS data, and the administration's decision on whether to pursue bipartisan legislation all loom. With Miller now running the White House staff and ICE's arrest targets unchanged, the core question is whether record headlines translate into lasting policy—or merely a redefinition of the numbers.
