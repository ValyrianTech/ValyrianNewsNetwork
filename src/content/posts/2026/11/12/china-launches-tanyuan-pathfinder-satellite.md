# China Launches Pathfinder Satellite for Space Origins Plan

China successfully launched the Pathfinder satellite of its Tanyuan ("Origins Exploration") space science program from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in Gansu Province at 7:41 p.m. Beijing time on 11 November, sending the spacecraft into its planned orbit aboard a Long March 2D rocket. According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261112/4ca508fbf9764761aa4386907d7b1187/c.html), the mission was declared a complete success and marked the 677th flight of the Long March launch vehicle family.

The launch represents the first concrete step in an ambitious national program that the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) is positioning as a flagship effort to investigate the origins of the universe, of space weather, and of life itself.

## A Four-Satellite Testbed

While Xinhua's dispatch describes a single "Pathfinder satellite," the CAS National Space Science Center (NSSC) provided a more detailed technical account, [republished by Ifeng](https://gs.ifeng.com/c/8xy3LbTvMsS), revealing that the mission is in fact a "one rocket, four satellites" combined configuration comprising four spacecraft — designated A, B, C, and D.

Pathfinder A carries five distinct payloads: a Magnetic Field and Velocity Field Imager (MHI), a Very Large Area Gamma-Ray Space Telescope Pathfinder (VLAST-P), a High-Resolution Atmospheric Boundary Layer Lidar (HiRAL), a Langmuir Probe (LMP), and a Space Weather Effects Monitor (SWEM). Satellites B, C, and D will fly in formation to constitute a Space Distributed Low-Frequency Interferometric Imaging Spectrometer technical test system.

After reaching orbit, the combined body will separate A from the BCD cluster, then deploy the remaining three satellites and conduct formation control and inter-satellite measurement verification, alongside on-orbit testing of the payload technologies.

## A Technology Pathfinder

The Pathfinder satellite is explicitly a technology test satellite. Its purpose is to conduct on-orbit verification of key enabling payload technologies for two upcoming flagship missions — the Hongmeng Plan, targeting the so-called "Cosmic Dark Ages," and Kuafu-2, which will study solar activity and magnetic cycles — as well as for payloads identified in China's National Medium- and Long-Term Plan for Space Science. After completing its verification mission, the satellite will carry out demonstration detection and research of the space environment.

The spacecraft has a design life of two years and operates in a 600-kilometer sun-synchronous dawn-dusk orbit. The CAS is responsible for overall program implementation, with the NSSC handling program management and ground support; the Innovation Academy for Microsatellites built the satellite system, and the 8th Academy of the China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation developed the launch vehicle. The MHI payload was developed by the Xi'an Institute of Optics and Precision Mechanics, HiRAL by the University of Science and Technology of China, and VLAST-P by the Purple Mountain Observatory, while the NSSC developed the LMP, SWEM, and BCD interferometric payloads.

## The Broader Tanyuan Program

The Pathfinder is the opening move in the Space Tanyuan program, a major national science and technology initiative led by CAS. As [ScienceNet](https://news.sciencenet.cn/htmlnews/2025/11/555787.shtm) reported when the program was announced on 24 November 2025, CAS plans to develop and launch four major space science satellite missions over the next three to five years, during the 15th Five-Year Plan period (2026–2030): the Hongmeng Plan, Kuafu-2, an Exo-Earth Survey to detect habitable Earth-like exoplanets, and an Enhanced X-ray Timing and Polarization Space Observatory for extreme-physics and black hole studies.

Stated goals include new breakthroughs in understanding the cosmic dark ages, the solar magnetic activity cycle, exoplanet detection, and the verification of physical laws under extreme cosmic conditions.

The program builds on CAS's Space Science Pilot Program, launched in 2011, which has already produced and launched eight science satellites — including Wukong (DAMPE) for dark matter, Micius (QUESS) for quantum communications, HXMT (Huiyan) for X-ray astronomy, Kuafu-1 for solar observation, and the Einstein Probe (Tianguan). Those missions, according to CAS, have delivered a series of original results and marked China's transition from "following" to "running alongside" — and in some fields "leading" — global space science.

## Analysis: De-Risking the Big Missions

The Pathfinder's significance lies less in its own science return than in what it enables. By flying and validating payload technologies now, China is attempting to de-risk the far more ambitious and expensive missions that will follow this decade. The distributed low-frequency interferometry demonstration by satellites B, C, and D, for example, is a prerequisite for future radio astronomy concepts that could probe the cosmic dark ages — one of the most sought-after observational targets in modern cosmology.

The four-satellite launch also showcases China's growing capability in multi-payload and formation-flying missions, and its use of the workhorse Long March 2D — now with 677 flights across the Long March family — underscores the high cadence and reliability of the country's launch program.

## What's Next

Attention now turns to the on-orbit verification phase, during which engineers will confirm whether the payloads perform as designed in the harsh space environment. Success would clear the way for the Hongmeng Plan and Kuafu-2 missions to proceed on schedule, and would strengthen China's position as it seeks to move from a participant in global space science to a leader in several frontier fields.
