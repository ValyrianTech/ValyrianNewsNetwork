# Femina Goes to Haenel as All-Male Prize Sweep Stirs Fury

France's literary season crowned its winners this month: the Prix Femina went to Yannick Haenel for *La Solitude des professeurs est infinie*, a day after the Prix Goncourt was awarded to Philippe Jaenada for *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel*. But the celebrations were quickly overshadowed, as more than 200 women writers denounced the "sexism" of a prize system in which all five major French literary awards of 2026 went to men.

## The Femina honours

According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576), Haenel received the Femina on 4 November at a ceremony at Dior's headquarters in Paris for his Gallimard novel recounting his years teaching in suburban schools. The 12-member female jury chose him over finalists Emma Marsantes (*N'efface pas mes cercles*, Verdier) and Lucie Rico (*Mais que fait ce sang sur le pull de Jennifer ?*, P.O.L).

The author, present at the ceremony, dedicated his book "to teachers, to high-school and middle-school pupils," at a time of student protests. The novel follows Jean Deichel, the author's double, a young trainee French teacher arriving at a middle school in Mantes-la-Jolie, west of Paris, where he discovers the stress, the urge to pass on knowledge, and the trials of the profession.

In the same round of prizes, the Femina étranger (foreign novel) went to American writer Laura Kasischke for *Baignade surveillée* (Gallimard), and the Femina essai to Jennifer Tamas for *Barbe Bleue, contre-enquête* (Seuil).

## A celebrated, contested career

After studying in Rennes, Haenel, 59, taught in the 1990s before turning to writing. He is the author of *Jan Karski* (Prix Interallié 2009) and *Tiens ferme ta couronne* (Prix Médicis 2017), a genre-defying novel mixing literature and cinema. He is also a columnist at the satirical weekly *Charlie Hebdo*.

Haenel said he felt the need to write about "the solitude of teachers" after the murders of the teachers Samuel Paty and Dominique Bernard. In the novel, the narrator dwells on the teacher's "joy" — "when it works, when a face lights up fleetingly, when a pupil shows understanding" — but also on the need to "calm things down, to defuse, to neutralise: whatever the pedagogues say, it is violence that comes first in middle schools."

## The Goncourt, a day earlier

The Femina came the day after the Goncourt, which rewarded Philippe Jaenada for *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel* (Flammarion), and the Renaudot, given to François-Henri Désérable for *Le Palais* (Gallimard), as the [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/goncourt-2026-le-prestigieux-prix-a-ete-attribue-a-philippe-jaenada-pour-son-livre-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel-11809864) reported. Jaenada's book investigates the 1949 murder of Louise Cansot. In 2025, the Femina had gone to Nathacha Appanah for *La Nuit au cœur* (Gallimard).

## The backlash

The story did not end with the awards. On 6 November, more than 200 women writers published an open letter denouncing the "sexism" of the major prizes, all of which went to men in 2026 — a sweep the [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-en-france-plus-de-200-autrices-denoncent-dans-une-tribune-le-sexisme-du-systeme-11811966) reported as drawing a sharp response across French literary circles.

"We express our anger at this system of literary rewards whose functioning, specific to France, does not at all seem to us able to reflect the richness and diversity of the literature being written today," the signatories wrote. "The juries have accomplished the heroic feat of rewarding only men. All the more heroic given that this was a literary season that had been described as open."

The letter, initiated notably by philosopher Camille Froidevaux-Metterie and writers Louise Chennevière and Anne Godard, was signed by figures including Annie Ernaux, Virginie Despentes, Anne Berest and Adèle Yon. It aimed its fire at the closed nature of the prize juries: "The old system of patriarchal mutual self-interest is working at full capacity, and all the more efficiently because the members of the juries of these 'great prizes' are appointed by co-optation and, for the most part, sit for life."

## Why it matters

France's *rentrée littéraire* concentrates enormous cultural and commercial power in a handful of autumn prizes. Winning the Goncourt can transform a book's fortunes, with past winners surpassing a million copies. That concentration makes the composition of the juries — and the question of who they choose to honour — a matter of genuine public weight.

The 2026 season was already freighted with controversy before the winners were named: the Goncourt Academy had removed Thélyson Orélien's debut novel from its selection over alleged plagiarism and suspected AI use, and reportedly screened remaining contenders with AI-detection software.

## What's next

The prizes have been handed out, but the argument they triggered is likely to outlast the season. The signatories' demand — a jury system that better reflects contemporary writing — points to a structural debate over co-optation and lifetime appointments that the awards themselves cannot settle. For Haenel and Jaenada, the honours bring readers and sales; for French letters, the question of who gets to decide what counts as the year's best book remains open.
