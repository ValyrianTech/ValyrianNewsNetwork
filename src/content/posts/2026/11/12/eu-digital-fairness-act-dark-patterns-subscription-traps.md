# EU Moves to Curb Dark Patterns and Subscription Traps

The European Commission on Wednesday unveiled its proposed Digital Fairness Act (DFA), a sweeping overhaul of EU consumer protection rules aimed at banning manipulative online sales tactics, subscription traps and hidden fees, and introducing rules for influencer marketing for the first time. A companion revision to the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation would let the Commission investigate and fine companies up to 6% of global annual turnover in large cross-border cases.

Commissioner Michael McGrath, who steers the file, called the package the most significant reform of EU consumer protection rules in more than 20 years, according to the [European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/making-your-online-experience-fairer-2026-11-11_en).

## Why it matters

The push reflects a widening gap between offline and online protections. Around 60% of EU consumers shop online, yet online shoppers are over 60% more likely to experience problems than those buying in physical stores, according to the 2025 Consumer Conditions Scoreboard cited by the Commission. The DFA aims to guarantee the same high level of protection whether people shop online or in a store.

\"Shopping has never been more convenient, but convenience must never come at the expense of fairness,\" McGrath said. \"The DFA will make sure the digital economy works for consumers, not against them.\"

## What the rules would do

The proposal would replace the 2005 Unfair Commercial Practices Directive with a regulation that applies directly in all 27 member states. At its core is a blacklist of practices deemed unfair in all circumstances, so authorities would no longer have to prove harm case by case.

According to [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/09/eu-to-blacklist-ten-manipulative-online-design-traps-leaked-draft-shows), which reviewed a leaked draft, the list of ten banned \"dark patterns\" includes making it disproportionately harder to cancel than to sign up, burying prices or auto-renewal terms, pre-selected add-ons, repeated scarcity warnings at checkout, false claims about availability or popularity, and fake notifications implying someone has contacted the user. The Commission could add new tricks to the list without returning to lawmakers.

The text also outlaws drip pricing, where unavoidable fees appear only at checkout, and bans \"was/now\" discounts calculated against a manufacturer's recommended price rather than a competitor's real price. It requires real-time price display for dynamic pricing on tickets to sporting or cultural events, and clear conversion rates for in-game and virtual currencies.

On subscriptions, free trials could no longer roll into paid plans unless the consumer actively agrees. Consumers would get reminders before automatic renewals, a \"terminate the contract here\" button, and the right to speak to a human in customer service rather than a chatbot.

## Addictive design and children

All users would gain the right to switch off features designed to keep them engaged, including autoplay, notifications unrelated to their own activity, streak-style rewards and relationship-simulating chatbots. For minors, paid loot boxes and pay-to-win mechanics would be banned in video games, in-game currencies would have to display their real-money value and be off by default for children unless a parent approves, and profiling-based advertising targeting minors would be prohibited for all traders — not only the large platforms already covered by the Digital Services Act.

In a first for EU consumer law, the draft also sets rules for AI shopping agents: users would choose which purchases they must approve, and agents would have to disclose sponsored suggestions.

## Enforcement and cost

The companion revision to the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation would give the Commission direct investigative powers and the ability to fine companies up to 6% of global annual turnover in large-scale cross-border cases — matching the ceiling already available under the Digital Services Act, though below the Digital Markets Act's 10%. Non-EU companies would have to appoint a legal representative in the EU.

The Commission's impact assessment estimates the rules would spare consumers €88.4 billion in financial losses between 2025 and 2035, with an annual benefit of €13.7 billion once lost time is counted, against recurring business costs of €678 million a year.

## Pushback and open questions

The proposal already faces resistance. Germany, in a non-paper reported by Agence Europe, argued that existing consumer directives should be amended rather than replaced by a new horizontal law, saying the case for a regulation \"has not yet been sufficiently demonstrated.\"

Stakeholders also question whether the DFA overlaps with existing rules. \"There are rules already in place across consumer protection, privacy, platform regulation, AI, etc.,\" Isabelle Roccia, managing director for IAPP in Europe, told [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/11/eu-targets-subscription-traps-addictive-design-and-hidden-fees-with-new-digital-rules). Monika Zdzieborska of Clifford Chance said the debate would centre on \"three fault lines\": coherence with the DSA, AI Act, GDPR and Kids Act; whether harmful features should be off by default rather than switched off by users; and proportionality across very different sectors.

The proposal must now be negotiated by the European Parliament and member states. The regulation would apply 12 months after entering into force.

## What to watch

Two questions will shape the final text. The first is defaults: civil-society groups such as European Digital Rights argue consumers should not bear the burden of switching off addictive features, and that companies should be required to prevent harmful practices by design. The second is timing and scope: with the parallel EU Kids Act on a separate track, decisions on minors, addictive design and age checks in one file are likely to influence the other. How quickly the co-legislators move — and whether Berlin's preference for amending existing directives prevails — will determine whether the DFA arrives as a bold new regime or a more modest patch on the existing rulebook.
