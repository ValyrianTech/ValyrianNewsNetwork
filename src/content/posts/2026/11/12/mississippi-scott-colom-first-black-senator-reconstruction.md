# Mississippi Elects First Black Senator Since Reconstruction

Mississippi has elected Democrat Scott Colom to the U.S. Senate, making him the state's first Black senator since the post-Civil War Reconstruction era. Colom, a 43-year-old local district attorney, defeated Republican incumbent Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith in a race the Associated Press [called](https://apnews.com/article/scott-colom-mississippi-senate-midterms-56c18f292a734443992f9466c3b0f346) on Wednesday, November 11, 2026, after officials spent more than a week processing absentee and provisional ballots.

The narrow victory — decided by a few thousand votes — is a seismic political shift in a Deep South state that has the largest share of Black residents of any U.S. state but has been represented at the top by white conservatives for generations. It also marks the first time Mississippi has sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1982.

## A Historic First in a State Defined by Race

The scale of the milestone is difficult to overstate. The last Black senator from Mississippi was Blanche K. Bruce, who took office in 1875 during Reconstruction — a period when senators were chosen by state lawmakers rather than by popular vote. Colom is therefore the first Black man in Mississippi's history to be popularly elected to the Senate.

"All I did was have the courage to imagine a night like this," Colom told a crowd of hundreds gathered at Tougaloo College, a historically Black institution in Jackson, for his victory party. "I had the courage to believe in Mississippi. I had the courage to understand that we can show the country that Mississippi matters."

The win resonates deeply in a state whose history is bound up with slavery, voter suppression and the civil rights movement. Nearly 40% of Mississippi's population is Black, yet its top political representation had long remained white.

Vera Finley, a supporter at the celebration, framed the moment in generational terms. "I am here to celebrate my parents and my grandparents. I am not one generation out of the cotton fields," she said. "Both of my parents picked cotton. And my great-grandmother was sold here as a slave from Virginia to here. So I am so excited to be on this side of history."

## How Colom Pulled Off the Upset

Colom, the son of a onetime Republican who worked for President Ronald Reagan, built his campaign on economic discontent and a sharp contrast with Hyde-Smith, whom he framed as a nonentity in Washington — a critique that landed in a state accustomed to long-serving, powerful senators.

His most widely circulated ad came after Hyde-Smith seemed to brush off high beef prices by suggesting consumers could "choose other proteins." Colom cut a 30-second spot featuring incredulous voters saying they would "pick another senator instead," and drove a "pick your protein" taco truck around the state.

He also worked the crowd at a late-October Trump rally in Corinth, Mississippi, walking the line of Trump supporters to ask about their economic concerns — gas prices driven up by the Iran war, grocery bills and health insurance. When Trump called Hyde-Smith to the stage, she offered just nine words in six seconds: "Is this not the best president in our lifetime?"

Colom concluded the campaign with an ad filmed at the Jackson home of Medgar Evers, the civil rights leader assassinated in 1963. "He was killed for helping Mississippians vote," Colom said. "On Tuesday, use your vote and honor everyone who died for that right." At his victory speech, he noted it was fitting that the race was called on Veterans Day, paying tribute to Evers' military service at Normandy and his later fight for voting rights.

Strong turnout in the Mississippi Delta's majority-Black counties powered the result, and nearly all of the state moved leftward from 2024, when Trump carried Mississippi by nearly 23 points.

The irony was not lost on observers: In 2022, President Joe Biden nominated Colom to a federal district judgeship, but Hyde-Smith blocked his confirmation hearing. On Wednesday, Hyde-Smith called Colom to concede.

"While this is not the outcome we worked for or hoped for, I respect the decision of Mississippi voters," she said. "I want to congratulate Scott Colom and wish him well as he takes on the tremendous responsibility of representing our great state."

## Part of a Broader Democratic Surge

Colom's win is one piece of a wider Democratic wave across the South, with victories also noted in North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia and Texas, as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/11/scott-colom-mississippi-senate-race) reported. Senate Democrats will hold at least 55 seats — their largest midterm gain since 1986 — in what many are framing as a decisive rebuke of President Donald Trump.

"Scott ran the campaign of a lifetime and re-imagined what's possible for Democrats in the South," Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said. Former President Bill Clinton, who campaigned with Colom in the final days, said he won "because you believe in the promise of your state's people. And they believed in you right back."

NAACP President Derrick Johnson said Mississippi voters, most notably Black voters, "made history because they refused to be intimidated, discouraged, or counted out" in a state that has seen "some of the nation's most difficult battles for voting rights."

## What's Next

Colom will now take on the responsibility of representing a state where racial divisions have long defined its politics. He has pledged to serve all Mississippians, vowing to "treat your children like I want my children to be treated" and to "work to make every Mississippian's life better."

He has also framed his tenure as a test for those who follow. "My job is to do well enough as the next United States senator that the next time some young Black boy or girl has the work ethic, the vision and the values," he said, "that nobody says because of anything I've done that they shouldn't get a chance."

With no automatic recount law in Mississippi, any challenge would have to come through a court-ordered, county-by-county review — an unlikely prospect given Hyde-Smith's quick concession. For now, the result stands as one of the most striking outcomes of the 2026 midterms, and a vivid illustration of a changing political map in the American South.
