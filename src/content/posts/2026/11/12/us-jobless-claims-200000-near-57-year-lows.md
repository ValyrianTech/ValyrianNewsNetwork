# US jobless claims tick up to 200,000 near 57-year lows

More Americans applied for unemployment benefits last week, but jobless claims remain at historically low levels, with layoffs rare and most workers enjoying unusual job security, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/jobs-unemployment-economy-layoffs-7a08c78cfc6af1aff2ac8652bd532336).

The Labor Department reported Thursday that initial jobless claims rose to 200,000 for the week ending November 7, up from a revised 191,000 the prior week and above the 195,000 economists had forecast. The four-week moving average, which smooths out week-to-week volatility, held unchanged at 195,250. Applications for benefits are a proxy for layoffs, and economists track them closely for signs of where the job market is headed.

## Context: A remarkably tight labor market

Claims have stayed at historically low levels throughout 2026, never topping 230,000 in any single week and coming in at or below 200,000 for nine straight weeks. The year's low point was 189,000 in July, the lowest single weekly reading since 1969.

Those numbers look strong even against the recent past. The best single week for claims in the pre-pandemic year of 2019 was 196,000, and that year averaged roughly 218,000. The current four-week average sits below the best week of that earlier era.

## Hiring has cooled even as layoffs stay scarce

The resilience in claims tells only part of the story. So far this year, employers across companies, government agencies and nonprofits have added an average of 86,000 jobs a month, including a surprisingly strong 165,000 in October, according to the AP. That is modest by historic standards but an improvement on a lackluster 2025, when monthly job creation averaged just 9,700 as high interest rates and President Donald Trump's unpredictable trade policies discouraged hiring.

Economists describe the resulting dynamic as a low hire, low fire market. Firms that struggled to recruit during the tight 2022-2023 period are now hoarding the workers they have, reluctant to cut staff even as demand cools. Workers, in turn, are not quitting to chase better jobs at the rate they once did, which keeps both separations and new applications low.

That combination presents something of a puzzle for policymakers. Layoffs sit near 57-year lows, yet hiring has slowed and consumer sentiment remains deeply subdued even after a modest rebound in early November. Consumer spending, meanwhile, has stayed resilient, and a surge in investment in artificial intelligence has lifted economic growth, offsetting the softer hiring picture.

## A cost-of-living squeeze beneath the headline

For households, the reassuring layoff numbers can obscure a more painful reality. Real wages have been declining, with average hourly earnings rising more slowly than consumer prices. Many households report that high prices have eroded their living standards, a sentiment that helps explain why confidence remains weak even as the unemployment rolls stay thin.

The tension extends to monetary policy. A run of hotter-than-expected inflation data has pushed economists to expect the Federal Reserve to keep borrowing costs elevated, with some forecasters projecting further rate increases rather than the cuts markets once anticipated. Higher rates, aimed at cooling inflation, also raise the cost of credit for households and businesses alike.

## What's next

The jobless claims report is a coincident indicator, not a leading one: it describes the present better than it predicts the future. Analysts caution that claims data often look best right before conditions shift, so the extraordinarily low readings should be read alongside the sluggish hiring rate rather than in isolation.

For now, the picture is one of unusual stability. Most workers who want a job have one and face little risk of losing it, while those seeking a new position face a slower, more cautious market. Whether that balance holds will depend on whether hiring picks up before the broader economic uncertainty begins to show up in a claims figure that is currently almost impossibly low.
