# Belgium Off Track for 2050 Climate Neutrality

Belgium is still not on course to reach climate neutrality by 2050, and its steadily growing car fleet is the single biggest obstacle, according to the third edition of the national Climate Transition Barometer published on 12 November. The report, compiled by the Climate Change Service of Belgium's Federal Public Health Service, concludes that while greenhouse gas emissions continue to fall, the pace of progress is far too slow — and that domestic transport is moving decisively in the wrong direction.

The findings were [reported by VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/12/belgie-niet-op-schema-voor-klimaatneutraliteit-in-2050/), which foregrounded the car fleet as the central problem, and detailed in the [official press release](https://news.belgium.be/nl/transitiebarometer-vooruitgang-blijft-te-traag-om-klimaatneutraliteit-belgie-te-bereiken-tegen-2050) accompanying the report. Climate neutrality — emitting no more greenhouse gases than can be captured or offset — is a binding European Union objective to which Belgium is committed.

## What the barometer measures

Since 2024, the federal climate officials have published an annual "transition barometer" assessing whether Belgium is on track. Rather than tracking annual emissions alone, the barometer examines more than 100 indicators — including electric vehicle and heat pump sales and the rollout of solar and wind capacity — to gauge whether today's investment decisions will deliver tomorrow's reductions. The 2026 edition, based on 2024 data, covers five sectors representing 98.5% of Belgian emissions, plus land use and international transport.

## Progress, but not enough

There is genuine good news. Belgium's emissions in 2024 were 32% below 1990 levels, the report found. The production of electricity and heat is the strongest performer: at its current pace, that sector could approach climate neutrality even before 2050, helped by expanding wind and solar capacity. Yet fossil fuels still accounted for 69% of final energy consumption, against roughly 16% for renewables, underscoring the need for much faster electrification.

Industry emissions have fallen 42% since 1990, accelerating in recent years, particularly in chemicals and mineral products. But researchers caution that this decline may partly reflect reduced output or relocated factories rather than genuine decarbonisation alone. Buildings show real improvement — the ten-year trend now aligns with some climate-neutrality scenarios — though the picture is mixed: in 2025, five times fewer heat pumps were sold than fossil-fuel boilers, and renovation rates remain too low, with no national indicator currently tracking them accurately.

Agriculture is a weaker story. Although livestock and soil-fertiliser emissions are falling, the sector's total emissions have barely declined over the past decade, partly because of rising fossil energy use within farming.

## Transport moving the wrong way

It is transport, however, that the researchers describe as "worrying." Despite cleaner engines and fuels and the rise of electric cars, domestic transport emissions in 2024 were 19% higher than in 1990. The car fleet keeps growing, petrol and diesel vehicles continue to be added, and — as the report notes — there is no visible structural shift toward public transport, rail or inland waterways.

International transport is worse still: aviation and shipping emissions were 67% higher than in 1990, with renewable energy and electrification not yet deployed for lack of mature solutions.

A second concern is the shrinking natural carbon sink. Forests, grasslands and other land uses absorbed only 0.6% of Belgium's territorial emissions in 2024 — about 80% less net absorption than in 1990. Reversing that trend is essential both for meeting 2030 targets and for offsetting residual emissions by 2050.

The report also highlights Belgium's consumption-based footprint. Accounting for emissions generated abroad to produce imported goods and services consumed in Belgium, the country is a net importer of greenhouse gas emissions — meaning the transition will also require changes in production and consumption patterns.

## A warning from the minister

The overall verdict is blunt: "The transition is underway, but must accelerate." Gains already made must be sustained, while a genuine trend break is needed, particularly on transport and land use.

Federal Climate Minister Jean-Luc Crucke (Les Engagés) acknowledged the mixed picture. "This barometer shows that Belgium is making progress, but not fast enough yet to achieve climate neutrality by 2050," he said. "There are positive signals — in industry, agriculture and the energy sector — but these must now spread to the whole of society."

Drawing a parallel with public debt, Crucke warned that inaction merely builds up an unpayable "climate debt" for future generations. "We no longer have the luxury of continuing at the same pace," he said. "Companies, citizens and regional and federal authorities must work together to turn our next climate plan into a real accelerator of the transition."

## What to watch

The barometer points to the next National Energy and Climate Plan (NECP) as the key test of whether Belgium can deliver the transport "trend break" researchers demand. Electric vehicle incentives, company-car rules and public transport investment are the main levers — but climate policy is split between federal and regional authorities, making coordination a persistent challenge. How that fragmented system turns ambition into emissions cuts will determine whether Belgium's 2050 goal remains a realistic prospect or slips further out of reach.
