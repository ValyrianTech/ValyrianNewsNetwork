# Belgian Youth Face a 'Blocked Horizon,' Says Pilet

Young people in Belgium are growing up beneath what one of the country's best-known political scientists calls a "blocked horizon" — a sense that a cluster of economic, social, climate and international problems have no solution in sight. In an interview with [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/pouvoir-d-achat-inegalites-climat-les-jeunes-ont-une-perspective-d-horizon-bouche-analyse-le-politologue-jean-benoit-pilet-11814304) on Thursday, Jean-Benoit Pilet of the Universite libre de Bruxelles (ULB) argued that weak purchasing power, widening inequality and climate anxiety are reshaping how a generation sees its future — and how it engages with politics.

The appearance came during RTBF's "Parlons solutions" civic operation, whose fifth edition was devoted entirely to young people, following weeks of student and pupil protests over education austerity in the autumn of 2026.

## A generation that is not one bloc

Pilet's central caveat is methodological. "It is very difficult to talk about youth," he told La Premiere's Matinale programme. "We have to divide into groups, into profiles, and see whether those profiles are more present among 18-25 year-olds than in the rest of the population."

That distinction matters because the most common political attitude among young people — indifference — is often misread as a generational failing. The "indifferent" group makes up 20 to 25 percent of 18-25 year-olds studied, but Pilet stresses it is no larger than among other age brackets. "This indifferent, somewhat distant relationship to politics, you find it everywhere," he said.

What is genuinely more prevalent among the young is a category Pilet calls "unconventional activists" — people interested in politics who act through movements, demonstrations, occupations and events rather than through parties or elections. By contrast, young people who favour classical forms of political participation are proportionally fewer than in the wider population. Apparent disengagement, in other words, is often simply a different channel.

## A sharp gender divide

The most striking finding in Pilet's analysis is the divergence between young men and young women. In 2024, the traditional pattern of youth leaning further left than their elders held in Brussels and Flanders but no longer in Wallonia. Yet that regional picture masks a far deeper split along gender lines.

"Young women vote much more to the left. They vote much more for the PTB, much more for the PS," Pilet said. "But young men vote clearly more centre-right." In Wallonia and Brussels, the centrist Les Engages drew notable support from young men in 2024. In Flanders, the gap is most pronounced on the far right: "There are four times as many young men aged 18 to 25 voting Vlaams Belang as young women in the same age group."

The divide mirrors a broader European trend in which young women tilt left and young men tilt right — a pattern with real consequences for how parties court a generation that is anything but uniform.

## Purchasing power overtakes climate — but not concern

The surveys Pilet cites point to another shift: economy and inequality have overtaken climate as the top concerns among young people, as they have for the general population. That is a change from the 2018-2019 elections, when climate featured far more prominently. "Now that is no longer really the case," he noted.

But Pilet is careful to distinguish between the priority given to an issue and the policies people are willing to back. Climate sensitivity, he argues, now shows up in the solutions young people embrace rather than in headline anxieties. "They are much more open to far more ambitious policies on mobility, on energy, on consumption" when asked what should be done.

What binds these strands together, for Pilet, is the accumulation of worries. Economic hardship, social inequality, climate change, artificial intelligence and the international situation are not seen in isolation but as interlocking pressures with no short-term remedy.

"What we see among many young people is a blocked horizon: the idea that there is a series of issues for which no solution is found and for which they do not necessarily see short-term improvement," he said. That framing helps explain the spontaneous, cross-issue character of the [student mobilisations](https://www.rtbf.be/article/manifestations-des-etudiants-en-belgique-pourquoi-les-eleves-manifestent-ils-11793821) that swept Wallonia and Brussels this autumn.

Those protests began in early October in the Liege region before spreading to Mons, Charleroi, Namur and Brussels, driven by grievances including a 10 percent increase in teaching hours without salary compensation, tighter school budgets, a rise in the minerval from €835 to €1,194 for 58 percent of students, and the end of the €12 annual TEC transport pass for 18-24 year-olds.

## Trust is low — but not uniquely so

The sense among many young people that they are not heard is real, but Pilet cautions against reading it as a generational rupture. "Young people are dissatisfied with politics, but in fact the Belgian population, very broadly, across all generations, is dissatisfied with politics and expresses the same thing," he said.

The gap lies elsewhere: in what Pilet calls "descriptive" or "mirror" representation — whether young people can find decision-makers who resemble them and share their experience. Youth remain under-represented in parliaments and governments, which reinforces their difficulty in recognising themselves in those who govern.

Pilet also distinguishes between age effects and generation effects. Opinions tend to moderate over a lifetime, "but that does not erase the differences." Today's young people are expected to remain more open on questions such as immigration and international openness than previous generations were at the same age.

## What's next

Pilet's most concrete argument concerns the mechanics of participation. Compulsory voting, as practised in Belgium, partly protects against the under-representation of youth seen in countries such as France, Canada and the United States, where lower youth turnout lets older generations dominate results. "Even if there is a small gap in aspirations, once the vote is expressed, in political representation that gap can become enormous," he warned.

The wider picture is sobering. A separate RTBF "Parlons solutions" edition devoted to the challenges facing [one generation](https://www.rtbf.be/article/sante-mentale-logement-precarite-quel-avenir-pour-les-jeunes-revivez-parlons-solutions-consacre-aux-defis-d-une-generation-11814001) noted that one in seven children is at risk of poverty, 14 percent live in deprivation, and nearly one in four young people in Wallonia and Brussels suffers from anxiety or depressive disorders — with child-psychiatry consultations up 226 percent over a decade.

For Belgium's political class, the message is that young people are neither apathetic nor a single constituency to be won with one message. They are a generation facing accumulated pressures, split sharply by gender, and — if Pilet is right — mobilised less through the ballot box than through the street. Whether that energy can be translated into representation will shape the country's politics well beyond the next election.
