# Hoax Bomb Threats Hit 6,300 Italian Schools

Around 6,300 schools across Italy received the same hoax bomb threat by email on Wednesday, prompting evacuations and closures at hundreds of institutions, while similar threatening messages were sent to schools in Scotland, Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland. No explosives were found, and no concrete danger was identified, but the wave of alerts forced authorities across at least four countries into emergency coordination.

The Italian Education Ministry confirmed that the identical email had reached roughly 6,300 schools, according to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/11/at-least-300-italian-schools-evacuated-after-email-bomb-alerts). Initially, Italian authorities had reported more than 300 schools, all of which were evacuated; the far larger figure reflects the full distribution list the message reached, though not every school that received it chose to evacuate.

## A Coordinated Hoax Wave

The threatening email contained violent language directed at Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni. According to copies obtained by Italian media, it was signed by a purported "Mikhail Andropov, leader of the Vilayat of Misoginistan" - the same pseudonym Reuters reported was used in bomb threats sent days earlier to newsrooms in Ireland and Bulgaria.

Meloni moved quickly to calm the country. In a video message after meeting her cabinet, intelligence services and cybersecurity experts, she said law enforcement had found no concrete threat during checks of the evacuated buildings. "Whoever is sending them wants our fear, lost days, suspicion and chaos," Meloni said, as [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/italy-bomb-threats-schools-meloni-evacuation-0f312fda4ee2e9cdcebd0d6e1a40d9e8) reported. Italian law enforcement was working with European counterparts to identify the source, she added, noting that no evidence had yet linked the hoaxes to any government.

Education Minister Giuseppe Valditara told the La7 television channel that intelligence services were investigating. "It is clear that there is a strategy at play," he said. "It is difficult to say right now who might be behind this." He urged calm, adding that "in all likelihood, this is a cyberattack rather than a genuine threat."

The disruption inside schools varied widely. At Rome's Mamiani high school, students poured into the streets mistaking the alarm for the recess bell, learning of the bomb threat only once outside. "They are saying it was a bomb alert because an email arrived from Russian hackers in which they said they would have killed everyone," said 18-year-old student Anita Bartocci. "It was pretty violent." In some schools, pupils initially did not realise it was a bomb alert at all, treating it as a routine fire drill.

## Schools Beyond Italy Targeted

The threatening emails were not confined to Italy. According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2026/nov/11/bomb-threats-hoax-schools-scotland-northern-ireland), about 1,000 schools in Scotland - from Dumfries in the south to Aberdeenshire and Orkney in the north - received warnings, and at least 300 were affected in Northern Ireland. A similar "generic communication" reached schools in the Republic of Ireland.

In Scotland, police were quick to reassure parents and staff. Police Scotland said the messages did not represent a credible threat and were being treated as hoaxes, with its cyber and fraud unit liaising with law enforcement across the UK and internationally to trace the sender. "We understand how distressing messages of this nature can be for pupils, parents, carers and school staff," a spokesperson said, adding that there was "no requirement to evacuate schools or keep children away from education based solely on the content of the emails."

Northern Ireland's Education Authority advised schools to remain open, noting that the messages resembled a bogus threat sent the previous month. Irish authorities likewise assessed the threat as "not credible" and did not call for evacuations. Most lessons in the UK and Ireland continued, though some sites were evacuated as a precaution.

## A Wider Wave of Disruption

The school alerts are the latest phase of a broader wave of bomb threats and disruptive incidents across Europe in November. Earlier in the month, dozens of passenger flights were diverted, grounded or escorted by fighter jets after receiving threats, and newsrooms - including Ireland's RTE and Bulgaria's Nova TV - were evacuated after emailed warnings. The same period has seen cyberattacks on supermarket refrigeration systems in France, Germany, Romania and the UK.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/11/bommeldingen-scholen-europa/), the Belgian public broadcaster, the pattern is precisely what has fuelled European anxiety about hybrid warfare - activities designed to divide, distract and destabilise without crossing the threshold of open conflict. No country has yet directly attributed the threats to a group or another state, and there is no proof linking the hoaxes to any government.

That ambiguity has kept officials cautious. Some European officials suspect Russia of orchestrating disruptions in countries supporting Ukraine, a charge Moscow denies. But Italian Defence Minister Guido Crosetto warned against jumping to conclusions. "It is not always the work of other countries or hostile entities," he said. "There are also attention-seekers and madmen who, in times like today, cannot wait to sow terror in a city or a region."

Crosetto put the logistical ease of the threat in stark terms. "It's too easy. Any one of us can use the available means and instruments to send an email to a school, a daycare, an airline, saying that there's a bomb."

## Analysis: Why Hoaxes Matter

Even when no device exists, hoaxes exact real costs. Antonello Giannelli, head of Italy's national school principals' association, said the episode "shows how much irresponsibility, when combined with the extraordinary communicative power of the tools we have today, can cause damage and disruption on a large scale." Beyond the immediate disruption - lost lesson time, stressed teachers, frightened pupils and parents - mass threats test emergency protocols, strain police resources and amplify public anxiety.

The timing is significant. The threats arrived as the European Commission stepped up coordination on aviation security after a week of flight disruptions, convening experts from member states plus Norway, Switzerland and Iceland. The school alerts elevated the response from transport security to the wider protection of civilian institutions, including children.

The signature attached to the threats - a fictitious-sounding "Vilayat of Misoginistan" - has also drawn attention to misogynistic framing that security specialists say can point to a mix of coordinated disruption, personal grievance and copycat behaviour. Competing theories remain unresolved precisely because the threat is cheap, anonymous and borderless.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is whether the wave escalates. On Thursday, some Italian schools received fresh threats signed with the same name but sent from a different address, in a more aggressive tone that included a demand for Meloni's resignation - signalling that the campaign may be adapting rather than fading.

Investigators across Italy, the UK and Ireland now face a familiar challenge: distinguishing a state-backed operation from solitary actors exploiting open distribution lists. Until attribution is established, governments are likely to keep two messages running in parallel - reassuring the public that the threats are not credible, while treating every hoax as a live security incident. For Europe's schools, the lesson is that the front line of hybrid disruption may not be a power grid or an airport, but a crowded distribution list and an inbox.
