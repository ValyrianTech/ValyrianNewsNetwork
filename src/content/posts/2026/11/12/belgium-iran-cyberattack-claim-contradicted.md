# Belgium's Iran Cyberattack Claim Reversed Within Hours

Belgium's interior minister said on Thursday there were "indications" that Iran-linked hacker groups were behind the cyberattacks that sabotaged supermarket refrigeration systems across the country this month — but within hours the foreign minister and the federal prosecutor's office publicly declined to confirm the claim, exposing a split inside the federal government over who is responsible.

Interior Minister Bernard Quintin (MR) told the Belga news agency that Belgium's security services had "indications of possible links with known Iranian actors," adding that "the federal prosecutor's office is investigating those indications further," in an interview reported by [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/12/quintin-aanwijzingen-voor-betrokkenheid-iran-bij-cyberaanvalle/). "We take these cyberattacks extremely seriously and are deploying every necessary means to map the threat," he said. "There is no reason to panic, but we remain extremely vigilant."

## A Contradiction in Real Time

The claim did not last the day. In a written response, Foreign Minister Maxime Prévot (Les Engagés) said the available data "are not sufficient to hold Iran officially responsible," and pointedly noted that any formal accusation of foreign involvement must be communicated by the Foreign Ministry after an investigation and federal consultation, according to [VRT NWS's live coverage](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/iraanse-betrokkenheid-kan-momenteel-niet-bevestigd-worden-min~1793610043244/). "The investigation into the incidents in Belgium is still ongoing," Prévot said. The federal prosecutor's office issued the same message, saying it could not confirm Quintin's statements at this stage of its inquiry.

The episode illustrates a distinction that matters in Belgian protocol: attributing a cyberattack to a foreign state is a Foreign Affairs responsibility, requiring completed investigation and interdepartmental coordination. By speaking first, the interior minister appears to have run ahead of that formal process.

## What Happened to the Supermarkets

The underlying incidents remain serious. From early November, hackers targeted internet-connected refrigeration and freezer controllers at supermarkets and shops across several European countries, remotely driving temperatures as high as 50°C and forcing retailers to throw away food worth tens of thousands of euros.

In Belgium, affected chains included Delhaize (around 40 stores), Carrefour (about 15 of its 700 Belgian outlets), Intermarché (10 to 15 stores in Wallonia and Brussels), Spar (around five stores) and an IKEA outlet in Mons, alongside independent butchers. The attacks exploited a known vulnerability in controllers made by Danish manufacturer Danfoss, and the same playbook had already hit hundreds of French supermarkets in October. The federal prosecutor's office has opened a criminal investigation.

Quintin said Belgium's military intelligence service, ADIV, may — under certain conditions — take countermeasures against serious incidents to neutralize threats, and he asked the Defence Ministry to consider the option. He also urged the Foreign Ministry to back a French request for European sanctions against Iran. "Iran has attacked France and possibly Belgium too. We cannot stand by passively," he said.

## The Motive Puzzle

French authorities had already moved toward attribution. On Monday, the Paris prosecutor announced that Iran-linked hacker groups were behind the October attacks on French refrigeration systems, and the French foreign minister pointed to a cyber unit of Iran's Revolutionary Guard. That context helps explain Quintin's remark — but it does not resolve the core mystery: why Iran would target France and Belgium at all.

Both countries have kept a relatively neutral posture in the confrontation between Iran and the United States, which makes them unusual targets. "What does Iran have to gain strategically from an attack on European countries like France and Belgium?" VRT NWS defence and Iran expert Jens Franssen asked in a [motive analysis](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/10/iran-cyberaanval-koelinstallaties-frankrijk-waarom/). If Tehran intended to signal to countries actively supporting U.S. operations, the U.K. — home to bases such as RAF Fairford used by American forces — would be a more logical target, yet it has apparently not been hit by these attacks.

Several hypotheses compete. One points to old wounds: France hosts the headquarters of the Iranian opposition MEK, and in 2018 Belgian police foiled a plot against the group, for which Iranian diplomat Assadollah Assadi was later convicted and then exchanged in 2023 for Belgian aid worker Olivier Vandecasteele. But the MEK has largely relocated to Albania and Assadi was already exchanged, so the theory does not fully account for the current targeting. Another possible Belgian-specific trigger: Reza Pahlavi, son of the last Shah, visited Brussels in early November and met several government ministers — a visit Tehran could view as a provocation.

Some analysts also point to Russia. A security-service source notes that Moscow and Tehran are close allies, raising the possibility that Iran is "doing a service" for Russia. Pro-Russian hacker groups NoName057(16) and Z-Pentest claimed responsibility, but those claims could not be independently verified, and experts are skeptical that they reflect genuine authorship rather than publicity-seeking. The Kremlin denied involvement, calling the accusations "absolutely absurd."

Franssen's framework is blunt: "To carry out this kind of operation a country needs three things: the technical knowledge, the political will and a motive. Iran has all three." Which motive prevailed, however, remains unclear.

## A Harder Security Question

Behind the attribution dispute lies a simpler and more uncomfortable failure. The controllers were reachable over the public internet, despite a prior French incident and warnings to lock them down. Ethical hacker Keanu Nys of the firm Spotit noted that there is no legitimate reason for such equipment to be publicly accessible, and that operational technology used for refrigeration is often less well secured than ordinary IT and managed by third parties.

Belgium's Centre for Cybersecurity (CCB) said it was concerned by a rising number of incidents, partly because artificial intelligence is making it easier to find and exploit vulnerabilities. "All IT systems are potentially vulnerable, and with AI that can now be exploited successfully, especially by advanced actors," the centre said, describing a fast-changing situation over the past six months.

## What to Watch

The federal prosecutor's investigation now carries the burden of either substantiating or discarding Quintin's claim. How the government reconciles the interior and foreign ministers' positions will shape whether Belgium's push for EU sanctions against Iran carries credibility in Brussels. And the broader question — whether the culprit was Iran, Russia acting through Iran, or publicity-seeking hacktivists — is unresolved.

For now, the clearest lesson is domestic: attributing a state-sponsored cyberattack is as much a political act as a technical one, and in Belgium this week the politics ran ahead of the evidence.
