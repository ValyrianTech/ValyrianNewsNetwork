# Greenhouse Gases Hit Record High, Locking In Warming

Atmospheric concentrations of the principal heat-trapping gases reached unprecedented levels in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization has reported, warning that the build-up is committing the planet to decades of additional warming regardless of how quickly emissions fall. The finding, published in the WMO's annual Greenhouse Gas Bulletin, arrives just two weeks before negotiators gather for the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye.

## A New Record Year

According to the [WMO](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high), the globally averaged surface concentration of carbon dioxide reached 426.8 parts per million in 2025 — 153% of the pre-industrial baseline of 1750. Concentrations of methane and nitrous oxide, the second and third most important long-lived greenhouse gases tied to human activity, also set new records. Methane reached 1,948 parts per billion, or 267% of the 1750 level, while nitrous oxide climbed to 339.1 ppb, 126% above pre-industrial times.

"Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies." She noted that the past 11 years have been the warmest on record and that the trend is set to continue. Because CO2 persists for decades to centuries, she added, it will affect the global climate "not just for years but for decades and even centuries."

The bulletin reports on concentrations — the amount of gas accumulating in the atmosphere — rather than new annual emissions. Roughly half of the CO2 emitted each year stays in the atmosphere, with the remainder absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans.

## Growth Slows, But Barely

From 2024 to 2025, the global average CO2 concentration rose by 2.8 ppm. That is above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge recorded in 2023/2024. WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed the marginally slower growth to the recovery of land carbon sinks and reduced fire emissions after a strong El Niño, which had reduced the efficiency of the biosphere through drought, drier vegetation and more wildfires.

Yet Tarasova cautioned against reading the figure as progress. "A 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," she said. She warned that concentrations are likely to rise further in 2026 because of continued fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño, which would again stress land carbon sinks. The risks of reduced CO2 uptake by the ocean are also growing.

The longer view is starker still. CO2 growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year over the 2011–2020 decade. When the bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual CO2 average stood at 377.5 ppm — nearly 50 ppm below today's level. According to the NOAA Annual Greenhouse Gas Index, the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases rose 56% between 1990 and 2025, with CO2 responsible for about 81% of that increase.

## The Energy Transition's Own Emissions

The report also flags two potent but less abundant gases tied to the shift toward clean energy. Sulphur hexafluoride, used as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, has the highest 100-year global warming potential of any gas monitored — roughly 24,300 times that of CO2 — and an atmospheric lifetime exceeding 1,000 years. Its increase in 2025 was a record high. Molecular hydrogen, increasingly used as an energy storage medium for steelmaking, shipping and long-duration storage, rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024; it is not itself a greenhouse gas but influences atmospheric chemistry and has a strong indirect warming effect.

Both illustrate what the WMO calls the need for a "smart transition" to clean energy: careful monitoring is required so that leakage and persistence do not undermine the climate benefits of decarbonization.

## What to Watch at COP31

The bulletin's timing is deliberate — it is the WMO's flagship scientific input for the UN climate conference, which runs from 9 to 20 November in Antalya. COP31 is framed around turning existing commitments into concrete action rather than setting new targets, with methane reduction among the priorities on the agenda.

The core message from Geneva is that the arithmetic of the atmosphere does not respond quickly to policy. Because concentrations accumulate, even a sharp fall in emissions would not immediately reverse the build-up. That makes the coming decade's decisions consequential in a way that will outlast any single summit — and makes the next bulletin, due in late 2027, a test of whether the world's carbon sinks can keep pace with a warming planet.
