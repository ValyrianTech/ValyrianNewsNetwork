# White House Warns Trump Probes Could Be Criminal; Garcia Won't Back Down

The White House has warned that congressional investigations into President Donald Trump and his administration could amount to "blatant criminality," setting up a direct constitutional confrontation with the Democratic-controlled Congress that will be sworn in in January. Representative Robert Garcia, the California Democrat expected to chair the House Oversight Committee, said Thursday that Democrats will not be "threatened or intimidated."

In a letter [dated Monday](https://assets1.cbsnewsstatic.com/hub/cms/prod_cms_alt/file/2026/11/11/6464b90e-a9e6-401f-ae03-a8216e411085/garcia_letter_signed_11.10.2026.pdf) and made public Wednesday, White House counsel Will Scharf accused Democratic lawmakers of making "abusive and capricious threats" to investigate the administration. He wrote that threats against executive branch officials, or efforts to intimidate them through congressional proceedings, "may cross the line into blatant criminality."

## A Standoff Months in the Making

The letter, first reported by Punchbowl News, follows the November 2026 midterm elections in which [Democrats won full control of Congress](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/04/g-s1-150276/2026-midterms-democrats-investigations), ending Republican majorities and ushering in a new era of divided government. Garcia had warned Trump officials to "lawyer up" as his party's victories became clear.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/12/nx-s1-6037540/trump-white-house-democrats-investigations-robert-garcia), Garcia told NPR's *Morning Edition* on Thursday: "We're not going to be deterred, and we're certainly not going to be intimidated by Donald Trump or anyone in the administration."

Speaking to reporters outside the Capitol on Wednesday, Garcia said that "Donald Trump is clearly hiding something" and that "the truth is that he needs to understand that Congress is a coequal branch of government." He defended his "lawyer up" post, arguing the White House was "thin-skinned" and could not "take any sort of heat."

"If you have abused the American public, you should lawyer up," he said. "If you're a corporate CEO and you're ripping off the American people, you should lawyer up."

## The Investigative Agenda

Democrats are preparing a broad oversight agenda, according to [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/world/white-house-confronts-democrats-over-planned-probes-claiming-possible-criminality-3011329), which quoted the letter and reported on the standoff. The planned probes include how Trump and his family have grown wealthier since he returned to office, the administration's handling of the Jeffrey Epstein investigation, and Immigration and Customs Enforcement operations.

Garcia said investigating alleged corruption involving Trump's family would be a top priority, citing a $2 billion Saudi investment in a firm led by Trump's son-in-law, Jared Kushner. He also called for investigations into former Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem and former DHS adviser Corey Lewandowski over reported possible pay-to-play schemes. Other targets include Trump's White House ballroom and other construction projects, as well as the family's role in mineral deals and Trump's crypto dealings involving special envoy Steve Witkoff.

"Our job is to investigate, to take issues to the courts, to make criminal referrals, and to write good legislation so that this kind of corruption doesn't happen again," Garcia said. He added that Democrats would continue pursuing the impeachment of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over accusations of overseeing civilian killings at sea, misappropriating funds and launching illegal wars. "I expect that the issue around impeachment hasn't ended," he said.

## A Broader Pattern of Warnings

The letter is the latest in a series of administration statements framing aggressive oversight as potentially unlawful. Attorney General Todd Blanche said in a CNN interview that aired Sunday that "there could be" a crime to investigate if rhetoric from Democrats turned threatening, according to [NPR's companion report](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/12/nx-s1-6037548/white-house-warns-democrats-over-future-probes-citing-possible-criminality). Trump has separately described Democratic investigative threats as "extortion at the highest level."

Blanche later appeared to strike a more conciliatory tone during an appearance in Missouri, saying: "Congress has oversight authority. It always has, it always will... we're not going to stop working with Congress."

The Justice Department also moved against individual lawmakers. Aaron Reitz, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, wrote to Connecticut Senator Richard Blumenthal on Wednesday demanding he preserve all records relevant to his investigations. Blumenthal, who is poised to chair a powerful Senate investigative committee in January, said he "will not be intimidated" and accused the administration of "weaponizing the Department of Justice to baselessly target Congress in direct violation of the Constitution."

## Checking the Balance of Power

The confrontation raises core questions about the separation of powers — Congress's long-established oversight and subpoena authority versus the executive branch's framing of such scrutiny as potential criminal intimidation. The White House letter and the Justice Department's record-preservation demand signal a strategy of treating aggressive oversight as legally actionable.

Some Republicans offered measured reactions. Current Oversight chair James Comer of Kentucky told The Associated Press the panel is "very independent" and that "regardless of who is in the White House, there's always going to be a strong desire for the Oversight Committee to be a check and balance on the executive branch." He called Garcia's "lawyer up" comments "bad taste" but acknowledged Democrats "won the election and it's going to be his committee."

Outgoing Republican Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky, a frequent Trump critic, said he hoped Democrats would hold "real hearings about real things, not another Russia-gate," while adding that "there is some real corruption there around the fringes." Representative Mark DeSaulnier, a California Democrat expected to chair the House Ethics Committee, was blunter: "If they're trying to intimidate, at least for me, it's not going to get me. I don't work for a mafia, a mob organization."

## What's Next

The new Congress convenes in January, when Garcia is expected to take the Oversight gavel and Democrats will control both chambers. Whether the White House follows through on its warning — and how courts weigh Congress's investigative authority against the administration's claims — will define the next two years of divided government. For now, Garcia's message is that the investigations will proceed. "We're not going to be deterred," he said.
