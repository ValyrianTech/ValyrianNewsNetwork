# Flemish Energy Bills Hit Highest Level in Years

Energy bills for Flemish households have climbed to their highest level in years, with a typical family signing a new electricity contract this month facing an estimated annual bill of €1,546 — €100 more than in October and the highest figure since January 2023, according to the Flemish Utility Regulator (*Vlaamse Nutsregulator*, VNR).

The picture is even sharper for gas. A household with average consumption signing a new natural gas contract in November can expect to pay roughly €2,356.30 a year — more than €300 more than last month, [Het Laatste Nieuws reported](https://www.hln.be/economie/energiefactuur-vlaamse-gezinnen-was-in-jaren-niet-zo-duur~a634e96a/), citing the regulator's monthly price update published on 12 November.

## What the Figures Show

The VNR bases its estimates on annual consumption of 3,500 kWh of electricity and 17,000 kWh of natural gas. The figures apply to households signing a *new* contract this month. Families on a fixed contract are shielded for now, but those on variable tariffs will feel the impact as prices reset.

Combined, the two bills could approach €3,900 a year for a household taking out new electricity and gas contracts in November — a figure that drew shock from readers of the HLN live blog covering the energy crisis.

The regulator [confirmed](https://www.vlaamsenutsregulator.be/nieuws-en-persoverzicht/maandelijkse-cijfers-over-energieprijzen-vlaanderen) that the expected annual price for both a new electricity contract and a new gas contract rose this month. "It is uncertain how prices on the wholesale markets will evolve further, partly due to the conflict in the Middle East," the VNR noted. "The expected prices may therefore still change."

Solar panel owners are seeing a small silver lining: households feeding surplus electricity back into the grid receive about 6 cents per kWh under a November return-delivery contract — the highest feed-in compensation since November 2023, though still far below the roughly 44 cents per kWh paid for consumption.

## Why Bills Are Rising

The increase traces back to wholesale energy markets. The VNR and HLN attribute the pressure to higher wholesale prices driven by ongoing geopolitical tensions in the Middle East, where the US–Iran conflict has kept gas and oil supply under strain for months.

The wholesale numbers are stark. Belgian day-ahead electricity traded around €300 per megawatt-hour for days in November, and the average price between 9 and 11 November reached €320 per MWh — "the most expensive three-day run since the gas crisis at the end of December 2022," said Matthias Detremmerie of energy supplier Elindus. Electricity is currently four times more expensive than a year earlier.

A grey, windless spell of weather — a so-called *dunkelflaute* — has compounded the problem by cutting wind and solar output to near zero, even as cold weather across much of Europe drives demand higher. Europe also entered winter with the lowest gas stocks in more than a decade, according to the International Energy Agency, leaving the continent more reliant on global liquefied natural gas supplies.

## Inflation Pressure Builds

The energy surge is feeding directly into the cost of living. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/29/energieprijzen-doen-inflatie-naar-5-26-procent-stijgen/), Belgian inflation reached 5.26% in October — the highest level since April 2023 — with energy inflation at 33.22% year-on-year. Natural gas was 52.5% more expensive than a year earlier, heating oil 30.8% more, and electricity 23.7% more.

Consumers are responding by shopping around. The number of people comparing energy contracts through the VNR's *V-test* tool is climbing again, with an estimated 1.6 million comparisons expected in 2026, up from 1.3 million in 2025, according to figures requested by Flemish MP Kris Verduyckt (Vooruit). "The number of people comparing their energy contracts is a barometer of the severity of the energy crisis," Verduyckt said.

## A Political Vacuum

The price surge is arriving without a short-term regional relief package in place. As [P-magazine reported](https://p-magazine.com/nl/articles/vlaamse-regering-raakt-het-niet-eens-over-maatregelen-tegen-stijgende-energieprijzen), the Flemish core cabinet failed in March 2026 to agree on measures against rising energy prices, with the government preferring what it called monitoring and structural solutions over stopgap measures.

Minister-President Matthias Diependaele and Energy Minister Hans Bonte have emphasised energy independence and electrification over direct intervention, pointing to geopolitical pressures beyond the region's control. That leaves households largely exposed to market forces as winter sets in.

## What to Watch

The near-term trajectory hinges on two variables: the weather and the Middle East. A colder-than-expected winter would deepen demand and strain already low gas inventories, while any diplomatic breakthrough in the US–Iran conflict could ease wholesale prices. Conversely, a prolonged *dunkelflaute* or further escalation abroad would keep the pressure on.

For Flemish families, the practical advice remains unchanged: compare contracts, consume self-generated solar power where possible, and watch for indexation effects. The Planning Bureau had projected the *spilindex* — the threshold that triggers wage and benefit indexation — would be exceeded in November, which could offer partial relief to some households even as bills climb. Whether the region's political leaders will move from monitoring to action remains the open question hanging over the winter.
