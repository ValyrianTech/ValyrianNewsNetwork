# Procedural Blunder Costs Brussels Its Airport Appeal

A paperwork failure inside the Brussels regional government has stripped the Belgian capital of its most direct legal weapon against a controversial aircraft approach route over the city. As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/une-erreur-prive-la-region-bruxelloise-d-un-recours-contre-le-survol-de-bruxelles-11814315) reported, the Brussels-Capital Region can no longer challenge the RNP 07L approach path at Brussels Airport before Belgium's highest administrative court, the Council of State, because the 60-day window to file an annulment action quietly expired while the letter that started the clock never reached the minister responsible for the file.

The mistake was confined to the cabinet of Brussels Mobility Minister Elke Van den Brandt (Groen). Federal Mobility Minister Jean-Luc Crucke (Les Engagés) notified that cabinet on 17 July 2026 of the "upcoming permanent implementation" of the route, which concentrates landings over the north of Brussels and is known to opponents as the "route Crucke." That notification set a 60-day deadline for the region to contest the decision in court. But the letter was reportedly never passed to either Ans Persoons (Vooruit), the secretary of state for environment and climate who is legally competent for the overflight dossier, or to the rest of the regional government.

Persoons only learned of the July letter on 6 November 2026 — long after the deadline had lapsed. "We are deeply disappointed. For months we have been working flat out on the overflight file. This error makes us lose an important lever in our fight against this new route, which affects so many Brussels residents," she said, according to [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/mobilite/2026/11/12/une-erreur-prive-la-region-bruxelloise-dun-recours-contre-le-survol-de-bruxelles-nous-sommes-profondement-decus-22IEOCOZEBFITGW6JULA5JTX74/).

## Why the Route Matters

The RNP 07L procedure is one of the most contested pieces of aviation infrastructure in Belgium. Its landings are funneled across the north-west of the capital, and critics blame it for chronic noise, sleep disruption and a decline in quality of life for several hundred thousand residents. Because the route was introduced under Crucke's federal authority, it has become a lightning rod in a dispute that has pitted the federal government, the Flemish region and Brussels against one another for years.

The timing of the blunder is especially painful for the region. According to the same reporting, the route's formal test phase ended on 31 October 2026, but the approach was retained and written into Belgium's aeronautical publication without an end date. Crucke has said the current path was kept because the air-traffic control service skeyes presented no alternative, and he has asked for a study of a curved variant. For the thousands of households beneath the flight path, that effectively formalized a route they had hoped to overturn in court.

## A Cabinet That Disputes the Clock

The episode is politically awkward because it stems not from an opponent but from within the Brussels coalition. The Van den Brandt cabinet maintains that it "does not consider this letter to be an official notification" — a position that, if accepted, would imply the 60-day clock never validly started and could yet allow the region to refile. That argument is legally untested, and it remains unclear exactly where or how within the cabinet the letter was set aside.

The pressure had been building publicly before the revelation. On 10 November 2026, Brussels Finance and Budget Minister Dirk De Smedt (Anders) publicly urged the regional government to lodge a Council of State appeal to annul the retention of the route, as [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/belgique/politique-belge/2026/11/10/nuisances-des-avions-dirk-de-smedt-demande-un-recours-en-annulation-du-maintien-de-la-route-crucke-UK3P3EYJWNBN5FWSRA2G2A7DTA/) reported. Two days later, the government was due to examine the overflight dossier at its Council of Ministers meeting. No official government communiqué had been issued as the story broke; the account rests on reporting by De Standaard and Le Soir, relayed by RTBF, La Libre, La DH and BRUZZ.

## Two Lawsuits Remain — But No Ruling Before Winter 2027

The loss of the Council of State avenue removes one lever, but not every lever. Two judicial proceedings are still in motion. The first is an environmental cessation action brought by the Brussels Region against the Belgian federal State, joined by nine Brussels communes — Koekelberg, Ganshoren, Jette, Berchem-Sainte-Agathe, Schaerbeek, Evere, Woluwe-Saint-Pierre, Anderlecht and the City of Brussels — with a hearing scheduled for 11 January 2027. The second is an action by dozens of families from Dilbeek, Lennik and Roosdaal before the Dutch-speaking first-instance court of Brussels, later opened to hundreds of Brussels households, with pleadings set for 8 February 2027.

Those dates matter. No court decision is expected before winter 2027, leaving a long gap in which political deadlines will dominate. The federal government, the Flemish government — which invoked a conflict of interest on 23 October 2026 — and the Brussels government remain locked in a standoff over how flight paths are distributed. A Comité de concertation was scheduled for 16 November 2026, and Brussels has demanded a clear federal justification for the route by 1 December 2026.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is whether the region will try to argue that the 60-day clock never validly began because the July letter was not a formal notification. That reading could, in theory, let Brussels renew its Council of State challenge — but it is a legal argument without clear precedent.

Beyond the courtroom, the political fallout is acute. Brussels residents who have spent years campaigning against the flight path now confront a route entrenched in the aeronautical record with no expiry date, and a regional government whose own internal breakdown cost it a court date. For a dispute that has already produced multiple condemnations of the Belgian State and millions of euros in penalties, the episode adds a fresh chapter — and a pointed lesson in how a single misplaced letter can outweigh years of legal preparation.
