# USDA SNAP Rule Would Cut 6.3 Million From Food Aid

The U.S. Department of Agriculture on Tuesday proposed a rule that would tighten eligibility for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, the food assistance program widely known as food stamps, in a move anti-hunger advocates say would sharply increase food insecurity across the country.

The proposed rule, published in the Federal Register on Nov. 10, targets "broad-based categorical eligibility" (BBCE), a mechanism used by more than 40 states that ties SNAP eligibility to receipt of benefits through the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families program. Under the change, households above SNAP's income thresholds would no longer qualify merely because they receive non-cash TANF benefits such as childcare assistance or job training.

According to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/usda-proposes-snap-eligibility-rule-change-food-stamps/), the USDA projects a roughly 17% reduction in SNAP enrollment, estimating that 6.3 million people would lose access in 2029, when the rule would take effect if finalized. The department also estimates that nearly 1.8 million children would lose access to free school lunches and about 5,500 low-income women would lose supplemental food aid.

## How the Rule Would Work

BBCE was authorized by Congress to streamline administration and lower state costs, allowing states to align SNAP income and resource limits with TANF-funded programs — often raising gross income eligibility to 185% or 200% of the federal poverty level. Households using BBCE still pass through traditional income and net-income screening.

The new rule would cut off benefits for people with gross household income just above 130% of the poverty line — about $35,500 a year for a family of three, according to the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities.

The USDA frames the change as closing a loophole. A department spokesperson said "broad-based categorical eligibility has bloated the welfare rolls for decades," adding that "correcting this loophole is long overdue." The rule text argues that current standards mean even households with $1 million in wealth but no income could technically receive food stamps.

White House spokeswoman Allison Schuster said the administration wants SNAP to serve only "eligible Americans who need it most," and that "rather than throwing money at a broken system, the Trump Administration will continue to stop fraudsters stealing from these important programs."

## Critics Warn of Worsening Hunger

Anti-hunger advocates and Democratic lawmakers sharply criticized the proposal. Ty Jones Cox, vice president for food assistance at the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, said: "Instead of listening to the public sentiment on affordability following the election, the administration is continuing its attacks on vital food assistance and the families who need help to put food on the table."

Rep. Angie Craig of Minnesota, the ranking Democrat on the House Agriculture Committee, called the proposal "heartless and cruel."

Gina Plata-Nino, SNAP director at the Food Research and Action Center, said the administration is "continuing on this path of making sure that SNAP is dismantled," warning that states "have so much on their plate that they cannot continue running the program." She added that reduced access to food assistance "is going to raise our negative health outcomes" and that FRAC believes the USDA's loss figures underestimate the true impact.

## Part of a Broader Push

The proposal is the latest in a series of moves to narrow SNAP. The sweeping "One Big Beautiful Bill," enacted in July 2025, expanded SNAP work requirements, and as of Oct. 1, 2026, states must shoulder a larger share of the program's administrative costs. According to USDA figures cited by CBS News, more than 5 million Americans have lost food stamp access since that law was enacted.

The Trump administration issued a similar BBCE restriction proposal in 2019, which the Biden administration withdrew about two years later. The current proposal is open for public comment before any final rule could take effect.

## What to Watch

The proposal now enters a public comment period, during which states, advocates and lawmakers are expected to weigh in. The central dispute is over the scale of the impact: the USDA's estimate of 6.3 million people losing access versus advocates' contention that the true figure is higher. With more than 40 states relying on BBCE and states already absorbing higher administrative costs, the rule — if finalized — would reshape food assistance administration nationwide, with effects projected to land in 2029.
