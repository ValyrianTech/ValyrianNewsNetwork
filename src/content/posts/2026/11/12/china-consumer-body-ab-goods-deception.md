# China Consumer Body Targets 'AB Goods' Deception

The China Consumers Association (CCA) on Thursday condemned a fast-spreading e-commerce practice known as "AB goods," in which sellers offer the same listing at the same price but ship products of markedly different quality to different buyers, and warned that such conduct crosses both the line of good faith and the red line of the law. In a statement [published](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20261112/ef72ed6f3a884dbc9ea6b192050b5fbf/c.html) on its official website and carried within hours by state media including Xinhua and CCTV, the national consumer body framed the issue as a matter of fair trade and consumer dignity — and called on platforms to act as "gatekeepers" of consumer rights.

The statement lands amid a surge of complaints online over the past year, with shoppers documenting down jackets, leather shoes and rugs that arrived looking nothing like what was advertised or shown on livestreams.

## What 'AB Goods' Means

According to the CCA, the term describes e-commerce operators selling "the same product through the same link at the same price" while "sending goods of clearly different quality to different consumers." The association identifies two main patterns.

The first is a bait-and-switch: a merchant shows shoppers high-quality "A goods" via livestreaming or the product detail page, then ships inferior "B goods." What the consumer sees and what they receive are entirely different. The second, as the CCA describes it, is the targeted downgrading of rural and remote consumers — judged to have inconvenient returns and high costs of defending their rights — who receive "B goods," while urban or more rights-aware buyers receive normal-quality "A goods."

The association characterized the practice as "看人下菜碟" — literally "serving different dishes to different guests" — and as "bullying the weak," describing it as "seemingly clever but in reality crossing the bottom line of good faith and touching the red line of the law."

## Three Legal Arguments

The CCA structured its case around three statutes. First, it argued that passing off inferior goods violates consumers' right to know and right of independent choice under Articles 20 and 23 of the Consumer Rights Protection Law, which require operators to provide truthful, comprehensive product information and to ensure actual quality matches what is shown in advertising, descriptions or samples. For buyers who receive low-quality goods, the group said, the conduct "is suspected of goods not matching the description, passing off inferior goods, and may even constitute fraud."

Second, the association said that using algorithmic profiling to ship different goods violates personal information rights and the right to fair trade. It cited Article 24 of the Personal Information Protection Law — which requires automated decision-making to be transparent and fair and bars unreasonable differential treatment in transaction terms — along with Article 21 of the rules governing algorithmic recommendations online.

The CCA argued that platforms tag consumers based on consumption habits, delivery region and return records, precisely filtering out groups deemed "inconvenient to return" or "weak in rights awareness." Using algorithms in this way to "price-gouge existing customers" (杀熟), "cheat newcomers" (欺生) and "bully the weak" (凌弱), it said, "is essentially abuse of personal information — infringing both consumers' personal information rights and their right to fair trade."

Third, the group invoked Article 2 of the Anti-Unfair Competition Law, which requires operators to follow the principles of voluntariness, equality, fairness and good faith. "AB goods," it said, disrupts market order and damages trust in online consumption, producing the inevitable result that "the dishonest profit while the law-abiding lose out" and "everyone feels insecure, every household suffers."

## An Investigative Counterpoint

The CCA's warning carries weight as a signal of regulatory posture, but independent reporting paints a more complicated picture of the phenomenon's causes. In a March 2026 investigative report [published](https://m.jiemian.com/article/14100477.html) by Jiemian News, reporters traced "AB goods" to intensifying price wars and traffic competition in livestream commerce — and found that deliberate, region-based differential shipping is technically and operationally difficult.

An e-commerce back-end order-management staffer told the outlet that such systems manage inventory and order data and do not have a function to tag shipments by region. A women's-clothing seller said regional separation "is basically unrealistic," because it would require separate warehouses and two shipping lines — costs most merchants would not bear unless operating at very large scale. Jiemian concluded that differences in quality received across regions are more likely the combined result of sample-versus-mass-batch discrepancies and supply-chain quality-control failures than of deliberate geographic discrimination.

The report also documented more clearly established problems. Jiemian found 2,660 complaints related to "AB goods" on the Black Cat complaint platform, concentrated in e-commerce and livestreaming. Cited examples included shoes advertised as "full-grain calf leather" that turned out to be inferior synthetic leather, and a rug advertised as 1.6 cm thick that arrived at just 1 cm.

The root of the problem, the report suggested, lies in how the industry has evolved. Livestream sellers often show a high-quality sample, take orders based on audience response, then commission mass production — a model meant to reduce inventory risk that has "deformed" so that the displayed "A goods" and the mass-produced "B goods" use different fabrics and workmanship. Under factory-direct shipping and third-party cloud-warehouse models, sellers may never even physically see the products they sell, eliminating the final quality-control check.

Jiemian also found that unverified "quality inspection reports" can be purchased on second-hand trading platforms for 200 to 350 yuan, complete with a forged inspection-agency seal. Lawyer Zhu Han of Shanghai Rongli Tianwen Law Firm told the outlet that if goods received clearly differ from the sample or description, the conduct is unlawful regardless of subjective fraud; if a merchant deliberately displays high-quality samples and ships low-quality goods, it constitutes fraud.

## What the CCA Wants

The association directed recommendations at three groups. Platforms, it said, must "earnestly fulfill their principal responsibility" and strengthen proactive screening and governance of big-data price discrimination and differential supply. Merchants should resist being "hijacked by traffic or held hostage by involution" and honor the traditional business ethic of "童叟无欺" — cheating neither the old nor the young. Consumers were urged to strengthen their awareness of evidence preservation, keeping product page screenshots, livestream recordings, order records and photos of the goods received so they can seek redress through consumer associations if disputes arise.

"Good faith is the cornerstone of the market economy and the biggest 'golden signboard' for operators," the CCA wrote.

## What's Next

The CCA is a social organization rather than an enforcement agency, so its statement is a signal of intent rather than a ruling. The key questions now are whether major platforms respond with new policies for detecting sample-versus-mass-batch discrepancies, and whether the State Administration for Market Regulation or local market regulators open investigations.

Under the Consumer Rights Protection Law, consumers may claim "退一赔三" — a refund plus three times compensation — in cases of fraud. But reliance on that remedy puts the evidentiary burden on consumers, and how effective it is for rural shoppers — the very group the CCA says is most victimized — remains unclear. With no comprehensive national data on how widespread "AB goods" is, the 2,660 Black Cat complaints stand as only a partial indicator. What is clear is that the dispute has moved from social-media grumbling to a formal test of whether China's e-commerce platforms can police the supply chains they have grown dependent on.
