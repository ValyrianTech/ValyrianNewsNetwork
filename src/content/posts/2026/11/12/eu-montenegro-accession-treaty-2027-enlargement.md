# EU Targets Montenegro Treaty as Enlargement Regains Momentum

The European Union expects to sign an accession treaty with Montenegro by summer 2027, setting the Balkan country on course to become the bloc's 28th member state in 2028 — the first new member since Croatia joined in 2013. The target, confirmed in the European Commission's 2026 Enlargement Package adopted on 4 November, signals a renewed push to revive a process that had stalled for more than a decade, while casting fresh attention on the uneven pace of the remaining candidates: Ukraine, Serbia, Albania and Moldova.

"Montenegro is on its way to becoming the next new member of the EU. The accession treaty is already being prepared," Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/europese-unie-wil-in-juni-volgend-jaar-toetredingsverdrag-ondert/).

## A Decade of Stagnation Nears Its End

Montenegro has been a candidate country since 2010 and opened accession negotiations in 2012. It has taken a largely technical, consensus-driven approach, and has now provisionally closed 21 of its 33 negotiating chapters. According to the Commission's [2026 Enlargement Package](https://enlargement.ec.europa.eu/news/2026-enlargement-package-shows-reform-commitment-delivers-progress-towards-eu-membership-2026-11-04_en), subject to member states' agreement, Podgorica could provisionally close the remaining chapters by the end of this year.

If negotiations conclude as planned, the accession treaty could be signed in June 2027. Ratification by all 27 national parliaments would follow — roughly a one-year process — with full membership potentially finalised in 2028. For a country of roughly 630,000 people, which Kos compared to the size of Antwerp, the finish line is finally in sight.

"This is testament to Montenegro's sustained efforts to advance on the European path and implement the reforms needed to turn EU membership into reality," the Commission said in its report, as [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/eu-says-montenegro-on-track-to-become-blocs-next-member-state-by-2028/a-79855011) reported.

## Croatia's Veto Leverage

The path is not frictionless. Because accession requires unanimity among all 27 member states, Croatia — an EU member since 2013 — holds effective veto leverage. Zagreb has raised eight demands linked largely to unresolved issues from the 1990s Yugoslav wars, including compensation for former detainees at the Morinj camp, the search for 14 missing Croatian citizens, war crimes prosecution, property rights, maritime border demarcation and ownership of the naval training ship Jadran.

These demands are not formally part of Montenegro's accession negotiations, but they must be settled for the process to advance. Recent steps suggest de-escalation: Montenegro renamed a Kotor municipal swimming pool that had honoured a former water polo player Croatia alleges served as a camp guard, and granted retroactive approval for a memorial plaque at the former Morinj facility.

"Croatia has its own interests, and it speaks openly about them," Kos said during a two-day visit to Podgorica in October, according to [European Western Balkans](https://europeanwesternbalkans.com/2026/10/21/kos-montenegro-could-sign-eu-accession-treaty-by-summer-2027/). She said she was encouraged by the dialogue and that the two countries were moving towards resolving their disputes.

## The Four Frontrunners

The Commission identified four "frontrunners" — Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine — and, for the first time, introduced "indicative roadmaps" setting out concrete milestones for the year ahead.

"The significant progress achieved by some candidates over the last year allows us to turn the promise of enlargement into reality," said Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. "Step by step, we are building a more complete, stronger Union."

**Albania** is roughly a year behind Montenegro. It has opened its last remaining cluster and met interim rule-of-law benchmarks, and aims to conclude accession negotiations by the end of 2027. The Commission flagged continued challenges in justice, organised crime and corruption, and noted the country's "largest civic mobilisation since its democratic transition" — street protests against the government — warning that accession requires a whole-of-society approach and constructive dialogue.

**Moldova**, a candidate since 2022, has opened 25 of 33 negotiating chapters and met the conditions to open all remaining clusters. Its target of closing negotiations by early 2028 is described as "ambitious but achievable," though it remains exposed to Russian interference and a deeply divided population — part strongly pro-Russian, part committed to a pro-European course.

## Ukraine: High Bar, Hard Conditions

Ukraine, granted candidate status alongside Moldova in 2022, has opened four clusters over the past year and met the conditions to open all remaining clusters. The Commission praised its anti-corruption framework as effective but urged acceleration on rule of law and the functioning of democratic institutions. Kyiv's formal target is to close negotiations by the end of 2028.

Kos suggested a faster horizon is conceivable. "I believe it is possible that Ukraine joins the EU within 5 years... though that will also depend on how long the war lasts," she told VRT NWS. "Ukraine is fighting to survive at the moment, but we set the bar just as high for them as for the other candidate countries. And that is not easy."

## Serbia's Fresh Start

The Commission was pointedly critical of Serbia, stating it "did not make any substantive progress in accession negotiations" during the reporting period, citing increasing societal polarisation, low alignment with the EU's common foreign and security policy and negative trends on fundamentals. Serbia has long refused to align with EU sanctions on Russia.

Yet the political landscape has shifted. On Sunday, 25 October, Serbia's student-led movement defeated long-time leader Aleksandar Vučić in snap parliamentary elections, ending more than a decade of rule by his Serbian Progressive Party, as [DW reported](https://www.dw.com/en/serbia-election-vucic-concedes-defeat-to-student-led-movement/a-79753482).

"After Montenegro, they were actually the furthest along, until under the Vučić government they took steps backwards in recent years," Kos said. "If they are serious and really want to carry out major reforms, then things could suddenly move very quickly for Serbia."

## The Wider Picture

The package also delivered stern assessments elsewhere: North Macedonia still needs to adopt constitutional amendments; Bosnia and Herzegovina has increasingly disengaged from its EU path; Kosovo's reform process stalled amid a prolonged institutional crisis and it has not been granted candidate status; Türkiye's negotiations remain at a standstill; and Georgia's accession process is "de facto halted," with the Commission describing it as "a candidate country in name only."

Enlargement is now framed explicitly as a security imperative. "Enlargement is not just about making the European Union bigger but about making us safer and stronger," said High Representative Kaja Kallas. "Enlargement is a direct investment in Europe's security." The Commission also presented a "common framework for gradual integration" in strategic sectors — energy, transport, digital and defence — designed to give candidates tangible benefits before accession.

Kos repeatedly invoked the 2004 "big bang" enlargement, when ten countries joined at once. "This is something we haven't seen since... 2004," she said. "I feel that more and more European leaders realise it is important to bring all those countries on board, if we want to protect ourselves in these geopolitical times."

## What to Watch

Attention now turns to the Council, which must weigh the Commission's recommendations. The EU Enlargement Forum on 15–16 November will be a key venue for next-stage discussions, and Brussels will launch dialogues on gradual integration with partners over the next six months.

The June 2027 treaty target for Montenegro matters less for its precise date than for what it represents: the first credible, near-term enlargement in over a decade. Whether that momentum holds will depend on Croatia's remaining demands, on the pace of ratification across 27 capitals, on whether Serbia's new government translates its mandate into reform, and on a war in Ukraine that continues to shape everything around it.
