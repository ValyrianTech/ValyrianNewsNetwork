# Bomb Threat Wave Hits European Flights, Including Brussels

A broad wave of false bomb threats disrupted civil aviation across Europe in early November, forcing precautionary landings, diversions and fighter-jet interceptions in more than a dozen countries. Among the affected flights was a LOT Polish Airlines service from Warsaw to Brussels, which landed safely at Brussels Airport on Thursday, November 5, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/). No explosives were found in any of the incidents, and authorities have not formally linked them or assigned blame.

## What happened

The LOT flight was one of at least 27 passenger flights reported to have received threats on November 5 alone, a figure cited by VRT NWS; other outlets put the number between 18 and 30 amid rolling updates. On the same day, Belgian police also responded to a note bearing the word "bom" ("bomb") found in a toilet aboard a Ryanair flight from Charleroi to Pescara, Italy. The alert was lifted and the aircraft departed after checks.

At Brussels Airport, the press service said that "after investigation by the federal police, no additional measures were needed," and that police were examining whether the LOT incident was linked to earlier, similar events.

Across the continent, the responses were far-reaching. An Aer Lingus Airbus A320 bound from Dublin to Rome was diverted to the French military airbase at Évreux, west of Paris, after French fighter jets were scrambled to intercept and escort it; all 157 people on board landed safely. A Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome was escorted by Italian jets into Fiumicino. In Bulgaria, three Ryanair flights were affected, two making precautionary landings at Sofia and a third diverting to Plovdiv. Czech police confirmed that four planes bound for Prague had received false threats. Additional alerts hit Bucharest, including a KLM flight to Tel Aviv with a stopover there, according to Romania's intelligence service.

## A second wave in the same week

The November 5 disruptions were the second such episode in days. As VRT NWS [reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/vliegtuig-noodlanding-polen-bommelding/), at least seven flights were targeted on Tuesday, November 3, including planes from London and Copenhagen that made emergency landings at Warsaw-Modlin and a Manchester-Murcia flight carrying 121 passengers that landed in Spain. Two flights from Bergamo and Dublin were diverted to Sofia, and two flights landing in Rome—one from Dublin, one from Lisbon—were affected, one escorted by an Italian fighter jet. No explosives were found.

The pattern continued the following day. On November 6, [further threats](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/nieuwe-bommeldingen-in-europa/) struck flights and airports from Prague and Copenhagen to France and Spain, with Hungarian fighter jets escorting three civilian aircraft. French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu reported that four flights over France received threats. In a marked escalation, the alerts extended beyond aviation to media organisations in Ireland, the United Kingdom and France, with emailed threats forcing evacuations at outlets including Irish broadcaster RTÉ. Irish Taoiseach Micheál Martin described the aviation threats as "coordinated attempts" to disrupt flights.

## Who is behind the threats?

No group has claimed responsibility, and the Kremlin denies involvement. EU Commissioner for Sustainable Transport and Tourism Apostolos Tzitzikostas said it is "too early to link the incidents to each other." But security experts increasingly describe the campaign as part of a broader pattern of hybrid or "shadow" warfare against Europe.

"This fits perfectly within the picture of how Russia operates at the moment," Eric Schouten, chief executive of the aviation security firm Dyami, told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/hoe-kan-je-luchtvaart-beschermen-tegen-valse-bommeldingen-europa/). "We must not panic, but for now this will be part of Europe—the new normal." He pointed to attacks on supermarkets, blood banks, railways and logistics centres, and warned that the use of "disposable agents" makes attribution difficult.

Jan Balliauw, a Russia expert at the Egmont Institute, went further. "Speaking of a hybrid war is too cautious. This is a shadow war," he said, arguing that Europe should respond more assertively. "Russia sows chaos with us, but we can do the same in Russia."

Not everyone is convinced of Moscow's hand. Prof. Tim Haesebrouck of Ghent University cautioned that "for now there is no evidence that it is Moscow," noting the threats could just as well come from environmental activists, an Iran-linked group, or copycats. He recalled that Russia was linked to a series of school bomb threats in the Baltic states, Slovakia and the Czech Republic in 2024.

## Why it matters

The threats expose a structural vulnerability in European aviation: even when hoaxes are quickly debunked, each alert triggers costly groundings, diversions and security deployments. Aviation security analysts note that the sheer volume—dozens of flights in a single day—delivers a psychological and economic effect that a single credible plot could not.

The timing is also sensitive. With European elections looming and support for Ukraine a persistent fault line, some analysts argue that sowing disruption is itself the objective, eroding public confidence and testing the continent's emergency-response coordination. The European Commission has activated a mechanism for member states to exchange information on the incidents.

## What to watch

Investigators are still tracing the origin of the emails and notes behind the alerts, and the question of attribution remains open. For travellers, the practical impact has been primarily delays: aircraft held on remote stands while passengers and baggage are screened. Authorities are urging passengers to build extra time into their plans and to check flight status before travelling.

Whether the campaign intensifies—or shifts toward new targets as it did with media organisations—will be a key test of Europe's resilience in the weeks ahead. For now, the continent is treating the disruptions less as isolated hoaxes than as a sustained, deniable campaign that may be part of the cost of doing business in an era of hybrid pressure.
