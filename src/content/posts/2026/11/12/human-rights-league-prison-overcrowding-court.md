# Human Rights League Takes Prison Overcrowding to Court

Belgium's Human Rights League (Liga voor Mensenrechten) has summoned the Belgian State before a court over the persistent overcrowding of the country's prisons, in what the organisation says is the first lawsuit to target the systemic problem across the entire prison system rather than a single institution or individual case. The move, announced on 12 November, follows a formal notice of default (ingebrekestelling) served on the federal government in June 2026.

The action coincides with the launch of the League's "national prison days", a nationwide campaign to draw attention to conditions inside Belgium's detention facilities. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/12/overbevolking-in-onze-gevangenissen-liga-voor-mensenrechten-naa/), it is the first legal case to cover the full Belgian prison system.

## A Structural Problem, Not a Single Cell

Belgian and European courts have repeatedly condemned the country over its detention conditions, but those rulings have addressed individual detainees or specific prisons. Recently, for instance, the Flemish Bar Association (Orde van Vlaamse Balies) obtained a conviction over conditions at Ghent prison.

The League wants to go further. The organisation argues that the issue is not a temporary capacity shortfall but a structural failure of detention policy. "Whoever deprives people of their freedom also has the responsibility to detain them in dignified conditions," said Hendrik Vandekerckhove, an advocate and spokesperson for the League, as [Het Nieuwsblad](https://www.nieuwsblad.be/regio/antwerpen/regio-antwerpen/antwerpen/liga-voor-mensenrechten-dagvaardt-belgische-staat-tegen-overbevolking-in-gevangenissen/164438874.html) reported. "The government is failing to do so today."

Vandekerckhove added that the lawsuit aims to force the federal government to take both immediate and structural measures to reduce overcrowding and end "undignified detention conditions." The League is asking a judge to compel the state to bring the prison population back within available capacity, and argues that such conditions weigh not only on detainees' rights but also on the work of prison staff.

## The Scale of the Problem

Official figures illustrate the pressure on the system. As of mid-March 2026, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/03/17/overbevolking-gevangenissen-grondslapers/), 644 detainees were sleeping on mattresses on the floor of their cells — approaching the record of 672 groundsleepers registered in December 2025. Some facilities are far beyond capacity: Bruges held 868 detainees for 612 beds, while Antwerp held 714 for 439 places.

The problem has come into sharp focus in Mechelen, which VRT NWS Pano described as the most overcrowded prison in Flanders. In October 2026, reporters gained ten days of near-unrestricted access to the facility, where [around 150 detainees are held in a space designed for 84](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/14/pano-gevangenis-mechelen-overbevolkt/). The resulting report painted a picture of detainees sleeping beside toilets, sometimes five people in a 13-square-metre cell, alongside drug problems and severe staff shortages.

## Government Measures Fall Short, Critics Say

The federal government has not been idle. In March 2026 it reached an agreement to fit convicted persons serving sentences of up to 18 months with electronic ankle bracelets, and in May the Council of Ministers approved a package of measures against overcrowding. Justice Minister Annelies Verlinden (CD&V) said it was the goal that the Chamber vote on steps before the summer so their effects could be felt by autumn.

Critics, however, argue that such measures do not address the underlying structural causes. When it served its notice of default in June, the [League](https://mensenrechten.be/nieuwsberichten/persbericht-liga-voor-mensenrechten-stelt-federale-regering-in-gebreke-wege) said experts had given the ankle-bracelet scheme little chance of success. The organisation is calling for imprisonment to become a measure of last resort, stronger alternatives to detention, and better management of releases where it can be done responsibly.

## What the Case Could Change

The significance of the lawsuit lies in its ambition. Previous rulings have established that conditions in specific prisons breach human rights; a ruling on the system as a whole could impose a binding structural obligation on the state and set a deadline for reform, something piecemeal cases have not achieved.

The political stakes are high. Overcrowding has become a politically charged issue, with reporting suggesting that Verlinden has been isolated within the government over the dossier. A court ruling would shift pressure onto the federal authorities and could force the pace of reform.

## What to Watch

The immediate questions are whether a court will accept jurisdiction over a claim that targets the system rather than a single institution, what remedies it might order, and on what timeline. A legal victory would not empty the cells overnight, but it could convert years of repeated condemnations into an enforceable mandate. The government's response to the summons — and whether it accelerates its own reforms to defuse the case — will shape the next phase of a debate that has, for now, moved from the prison corridor to the courtroom.
