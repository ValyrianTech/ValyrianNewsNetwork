# Kennedy Center Ticketmaster Switch Raises Ethics Concerns

The John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts is planning to replace its long-standing not-for-profit ticketing platform with Ticketmaster, a move that is drawing conflict-of-interest scrutiny because the institution's former president was simultaneously a paid board member of Ticketmaster's parent company, Live Nation, while the arrangement was being negotiated, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/12/nx-s1-6031461/kennedy-center-ticketmaster-conflict-of-interest).

The federally chartered cultural institution in Washington, D.C., has for decades relied on Tessitura, a not-for-profit ticketing and customer-relationship system developed by the Metropolitan Opera in 1995. The Center was among its earliest users. Now it intends to move to Ticketmaster, the commercial giant owned by Live Nation — a company a federal jury determined this year had operated as an illegal monopoly.

## The conflict at the center

At the heart of the concern is Richard Grenell, whom President Trump installed as the Kennedy Center's president in February 2025 after Trump himself became the institution's chairman. A few months later, in May 2025, Grenell was [elected to Live Nation's board of directors](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/live-nation-entertainment-elects-richard-grenell-to-board-of-directors-302461061.html), a post that came with a salary and stock.

According to Live Nation's SEC filings, Grenell was paid $260,205 for his board work in 2025 and received 102 shares of company stock, then worth roughly $14,200, upon joining. The Kennedy Center's most recent tax filings show that Grenell was paid nothing as its president — meaning his compensation from Live Nation vastly exceeded what he earned running the arts institution. Grenell departed the Kennedy Center in March 2026.

Richard Briffault, a Columbia Law School professor who specializes in government ethics, told NPR that holding fiduciary duties to two entities at once — particularly when one stands to benefit from a deal with the other — would typically raise red flags.

"This is literally conflict of interest," Briffault said. "If you have a duty of loyalty to an entity in which you're an officer ... and at the same time you have a financial stake in another entity that does business with, and benefits from, the business with the entity on whose board you sit — that's a problem."

The Kennedy Center declined to comment on Grenell's concurrent positions or his financial interests in Live Nation and Ticketmaster, and NPR's attempts to reach Grenell were not answered.

## Competing accounts of the timeline

The Kennedy Center says the move to Ticketmaster was not driven by Grenell. In a statement, the institution said discussions have been underway since 2024 — during the Biden administration and before Grenell's appointment — and that it "began evaluating Ticketmaster in the spring of 2024 and determined it had outgrown its previous ticketing system. The agreement followed standard procurement review."

Ticketmaster offered a similar account, saying the Center's own ticketing staff first approached the company in February 2024. "We categorically deny that anyone pressured the Kennedy Center to choose Ticketmaster," the company said in a statement to NPR.

But a second highly placed current employee and a former employee told NPR that Ticketmaster was never under serious consideration by the Center's leadership in 2024. A former senior executive disputed the notion that negotiations were underway at that time. "Ticketmaster could say they were trying to talk to us, but they absolutely were not negotiating with us," the executive said, adding that the Center's staff and Tessitura system gave it the capacity to manage its own data and customers.

The conflicting accounts — the institution and Ticketmaster versus anonymous current and former staff — are central to the propriety question, because they determine whether the ticketing decision took shape before or during Grenell's tenure.

## A decision now shaped by a newcomer

Leadership at the Center has changed dramatically over the past year. Matt Floca replaced Grenell as president in March 2026; he had previously served as the Center's head of facilities and lacked experience in artistic direction, fundraising or arts administration. In October, the Center named Rick Canny, a longtime California-based talent agent who worked with 1980s and '90s rock acts, as its new artistic director.

NPR obtained a March 2026 email in which Canny — then still a consultant — told the Center's chief financial officer that he had taken a leading role in the ticketing deal: "I've been advising on this for almost a year. I've shaped many or most of the terms of the deal and I'm reviewing the final agreement tonight."

Canny also blamed the union representing the Center's box office workers for impeding the plan. "The unions got in the way and they [Ticketmaster] could no longer make money with us. With care and time, they can come back," he wrote.

Current and former employees told NPR that a deal of this kind would ordinarily have been the president's responsibility, but was being shaped instead by the artistic director. Canny did not respond to NPR's attempts to reach him.

## Labor and antitrust backdrop

Earlier this fall, the Kennedy Center [laid off its IATSE union box office workers](https://iatse.net/new-kennedy-center-layoffs-permanently-eliminate-box-office-jobs-iatse-files-new-charges/) after its main building closed for a planned two-year renovation. The union said it was preparing unfair labor practice charges and grievances, arguing the Center was using the temporary closure to permanently eliminate jobs.

The switch also lands against a fraught antitrust record. In April 2026, a federal jury in Manhattan [found that Live Nation and Ticketmaster operated as a monopoly](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/15/nx-s1-5786715/live-nation-ticketmaster-antitrust-verdict-monopoly) that harmed consumers and overcharged ticket buyers — a verdict that came after the Justice Department settled its own antitrust case against the company for $280 million in March without requiring a divestiture of Ticketmaster. Live Nation has denied operating a monopoly and has said it will appeal unfavorable rulings.

## Why the platform choice matters

The decision is not merely administrative. Tessitura bundles ticketing with fundraising, membership, customer service and education functions — an all-in-one system tailored to nonprofit arts organizations. Ticketmaster, by contrast, "is one-dimensional, and it's really designed for commercial presenters," said Steve Jacobson, founder of the consulting firm JCA, which advises nonprofit arts clients.

Replicating Tessitura's functions, Jacobson told NPR, would be an expensive prospect. For a nonprofit that depends on a mix of ticket sales and donations, keeping patron data and outreach in one place is "really important," he said.

## What's next

The immediate practical effect is already visible: as of Nov. 11, the Center was offering only a submission form for information about buying tickets to the Kennedy Center Honors, the December gala that this year will celebrate Paul Anka, David Foster, Andy Garcia, David Mamet and Barbara Mandrell. The Center has not explained how or when tickets will be made available.

Several questions remain open. It is unclear when the ticketing switch will be completed or on what contract terms, what the Center's board knew about Grenell's Live Nation compensation during negotiations, and whether any federal or D.C. ethics oversight body will examine the arrangement. The layoffs of unionized box office workers, meanwhile, could yet produce legal challenges.

For an institution that bills itself as the national center for the performing arts, the episode raises a broader question that extends well beyond ticketing: how a publicly chartered cultural landmark weighs private financial ties against its public mission.
