# Gas Dips Below $4, but Inflation Stays Stubbornly High

American drivers caught a small break at the pump this week, but it arrived alongside a reminder that the broader cost-of-living squeeze is far from over. The national average price of regular gasoline fell below $4 a gallon on Tuesday for the first time since July, even as a government report showed consumer prices rising faster than forecast last month.

The two developments — relief on fuel and stubborn inflation elsewhere — capture the contradictory economic picture facing U.S. households and complicating the Federal Reserve's next move.

## Relief at the Pump

According to [AAA](https://gasprices.aaa.com/for-the-first-time-since-july-national-average-drops-below-4/), the national average fell below $4 a gallon for the first time since July, dropping to roughly $3.90 as crude oil prices declined, refineries switched to cheaper winter-blend fuel, and seasonal demand eased. Even so, AAA noted that the current average remains higher than normal for this time of year, making this the most expensive November on record for gas prices.

As [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/12/nx-s1-6034518/inflation-consumer-prices-economy-gas-prices), the price of regular gas fell 38 cents over the past month to a national average of $3.98. "Temperatures are getting colder," Patrick De Haan, petroleum analyst for GasBuddy, told NPR. "People who have boats and RVs are probably done for the year. I would say, by Thanksgiving, so long as we don't get any wild cards geopolitically, the national average could be $3.75 a gallon."

Even with the decline, gas remains at a record high for this time of year — roughly a dollar a gallon higher than when the U.S. went to war with Iran. Diesel has fallen more slowly, with AAA putting the average at $6.15 a gallon, down about 13 cents from a month ago but still elevated because Russian refinery damage from the war in Ukraine has curtailed global supplies.

## Inflation Runs Hot

The cheer at the gas station was tempered by the October Consumer Price Index, which showed consumer prices rising 0.4% from the previous month on a seasonally adjusted basis — a larger increase than economists had expected and the third straight month of outsized gains, according to the [Labor Department report](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/cpi.pdf).

Rising housing costs accounted for about a third of the monthly increase, and grocery prices climbed sharply as well. The report notably lacked the usual year-over-year comparison, a casualty of last year's government shutdown, which idled price-checkers so October 2025 prices were never recorded. The Cleveland Federal Reserve estimated before the release that prices were up 3.7% from a year earlier — outpacing average wage gains of just 3.1%, meaning the typical worker is losing buying power.

## A Fed Under Pressure

The inflation reading landed days after the U.S. midterm elections, in which affordability concerns drove unusually heavy turnout — 49.1% of eligible voters, the second-highest midterm participation in more than a century. It also intensified debate inside the Federal Reserve over how aggressively to tighten monetary policy.

Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee called the report "another distressing report — borderline disturbing," adding, "We've got a real inflation issue on our hands that has been developing. We're going to have to deal with that," [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/11/goolsbee-october-inflation-fed-rates-disturbing-distressing.html).

The central bank raised rates to a range of 3.75% to 4.00% in September and held steady in October. Fed Governor Christopher Waller has signaled openness to a half-point increase in December, while Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan has said it "may be possible to move more rapidly this winter than the FOMC has moved so far this fall." Market futures now unanimously price a December rate hike.

## Costly Diesel, Costly Goods

The elevated cost of diesel is bleeding into other prices. RBC Capital Markets economist Mike Reid noted that higher freight costs are being passed through to imported goods. "We've seen it show up notably in goods that are heavily imported — apparel, recreation goods, IT equipment," he told NPR. Rising diesel is also pushing up heating oil costs in the Northeast, though mild fall weather has contained the pain so far.

## What to Watch

The coming weeks will test whether the drop in gasoline proves durable. Thanksgiving travel could be the most expensive ever at the pump, and any geopolitical shock could reverse the recent slide. Meanwhile, all eyes turn to the Fed's final policy meeting of the year in December, where officials must weigh a cooling fuel market against an inflation rate that refuses to cooperate — a balancing act with direct consequences for household budgets heading into the holiday season.
