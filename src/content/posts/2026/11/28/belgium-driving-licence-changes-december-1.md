# Belgium's December 1 Driving Test Changes, Explained

Candidates sitting the category B (car) practical driving test in Belgium face a set of modifications from December 1, most notably around vehicle checks. According to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/785433/article/2026-11-28/du-changement-pour-le-permis-de-conduire-des-ce-1er-decembre-en-belgique), learners will no longer have to demonstrate how to check the engine oil level, and examiners will stop asking about brake fluid or coolant. The changes, announced by the driving-test centres' sector federation, apply to the practical exam and take effect at the start of the month.

## What actually changes

The reform is more modest than some headlines suggest. Here is the short list of adjustments.

**Engine checks trimmed.** From December 1, a candidate will no longer have to show how to verify the engine oil level. The examiner will also stop posing questions on brake fluid and coolant. The changes are set out in the new Flemish examination brochure for the category B practical test, [DOC.133-BB-N](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/604a00a4df74a7000318621d/6ae45afd2d2294d548da5017_Doc%20133-BBN_V.pdf), published by GOCA Vlaanderen, the sector federation of Flemish driving-test centres, and take effect on December 1, 2026.

**Tyre knowledge broadened.** Tyre checks remain. Candidates must still know how to check tyre wear and where to pour windscreen-washer fluid. A new requirement has been added: the candidate must be able to point to where the recommended tyre pressure is stated. That information may sit in the vehicle handbook, on a sticker on the door pillar, or inside the fuel-filler flap.

**U-turn rule tightened.** During the manoeuvre of turning a car around in a narrow street, the candidate may no longer use adjacent side streets, or a widening at the end of a dead-end, to complete the turn.

**A stop/exit formality dropped.** When asked to stop the car, get out and get back in, the candidate will no longer be required to lock the vehicle during the operation.

## Why the framing matters

The change drew breathless coverage elsewhere. [Sudinfo](https://www.sudinfo.be/id1231811/article/2026-11-27/denormes-changements-au-permis-de-conduire-rien-ne-sera-plus-pareil-lexaminateur) headlined the story, referring to "huge changes" and declaring that "nothing will be the same." Read closely, however, the changes are procedural refinements rather than an overhaul of how the test works. The examiner will still run the same broader assessment of a candidate's ability to drive safely, and no route or evaluation criteria have been fundamentally rewritten.

There is also a matter of scope. Belgian driving-licence testing is organised regionally. GOCA Vlaanderen governs the Flemish examination centres, while Wallonia and Brussels maintain their own arrangements. The December 1 adjustments are introduced through the Flemish examination framework. As the automotive site [VoitureBelgique](https://www.voiturebelgique.com/examen-pratique-flandre-brochure-decembre-2026/) pointed out in a detailed comparison of the two brochures, the changes carry a "portée flamande uniquement" — Flemish scope only — even though French-language coverage presented them as applying "in Belgium."

VoitureBelgique also cautioned that removing or rewriting a paragraph from the exam brochure does not automatically mean a test has been abolished. In practice, the underlying skills — such as keeping a car roadworthy — remain part of responsible driving even where examiners no longer prompt for them.

## The electric-vehicle logic

One part of the reform carries symbolic weight. Removing the engine-oil check aligns with a driving landscape increasingly shaped by electric vehicles, which have no engine oil to check. The change acknowledges that mechanics once central to driver training are becoming less relevant to modern cars, while the new tyre-pressure requirement formalises a more practical, safety-relevant skill that still matters for every vehicle on the road.

Readers commenting in the wake of the announcement picked up on this. One noted that with electric cars on the rise, some electrical knowledge would make more sense for drivers. The Federation's own logic, as reflected in the measured coverage of the change, follows the same modernising impulse.

## The bigger picture

Belgium's regulatory calendar often bundles unrelated measures onto the first day of each month, and this Le Soir roundup is no exception. Alongside the driving-licence tweaks, the December 1 package includes a temporary EU import quota on Chinese hybrid and plug-in hybrid cars, new drug-testing procedures for prisons, an expansion of the National Bank's securities-accounts register, and a one-percentage-point rise in some consumer-credit ceilings.

For learner drivers, the practical takeaway is narrow but useful. Anyone preparing for a December test should download the correct brochure version and check it corresponds to the exam date — not just the download date. Practise finding the recommended tyre-pressure figure for the actual vehicle you will drive, and rehearse the stop, exit and re-enter sequence in full.

A separate Flemish rule, meanwhile, is due from January 1, 2027, when passing the theory test at a Flemish centre becomes a condition for sitting the practical test in Flanders. Candidates whose exam date crosses into the new year should reconfirm their version of the rules.

## What to watch

The December changes are unlikely to affect whether most candidates pass the practical test. Their real significance lies in direction: they show a testing regime gradually shedding mechanical checks that no longer match the cars people drive, and sharpening the safety-relevant knowledge that does. Whether Wallonia and Brussels follow with equivalent revisions of their own — and how quickly — remains the question worth watching.
