# UN Talks on 'Killer Robots' End Without a Ban

Five days of United Nations negotiations in Geneva ended in the early hours of Saturday, 21 November, without an international ban on lethal autonomous weapons systems — the weapons often called "killer robots." Instead, the 128 states party to the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) agreed only to begin "elaborating" a possible international instrument over the next two years, with a target date of 2028.

The outcome leaves unresolved the core question of how the world will regulate weapons that can select and attack targets using artificial intelligence, without a human giving the final order to fire. Nearly 100 countries had pushed for negotiations on a legally binding ban, but major military powers holding the technology resisted binding commitments — a result that left campaigners and human rights groups deeply disappointed.

## A consensus system that favors the status quo

The structure of the talks goes a long way to explaining the result. The CCW operates by consensus among its 128 High Contracting Parties, meaning a single country can block any agreement. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/21/vn-bereiken-overeenstemming-tijdens-conferentie-over-killerrobot/), the countries that possess the technology — including the United States and Russia — opposed binding arrangements. [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/killer-robots-talks-at-un-end-with-no-legally-binding-ban/a-80008584) reported that Israel, another arms-producing power, was also among those resisting the mandate.

That consensus rule meant the conference could only move as far as the least willing major power would allow. Rather than a prohibition, delegates settled on a procedural step: an agreement to "launch the elaboration of an international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons systems, which should be conducted over the next two years," as the conference's Swiss chair, Julien Thöni, put it.

Thöni framed the result as a victory for diplomacy, hailing "concrete results" delivered even in "today's deeply divided world." Over the coming two years, delegates are expected to work out which autonomous weapons could be prohibited under international humanitarian law and which might remain permitted but subject to rules.

## A chorus of disappointment

Campaigners saw the agreement very differently. Stop Killer Robots, a global coalition of more than 300 organizations, called it a "watered-down mandate that fails to chart a clear path towards a legally binding instrument."

"Over 95 states expressed their support for a treaty," the coalition said, according to AFP. "In the final hours, a small number of states weakened the mandate introducing uncertainty about the legal status of the final document."

The group has warned that the technology is not waiting for diplomats. It says autonomous weapons systems are already reaching the battlefield and have been tested or deployed, including in the war in Ukraine. Human Rights Watch likewise described the outcome as "disappointing," VRT NWS reported.

The frustration was echoed inside the negotiating room. Frederico Meyer, Brazil's ambassador and a proponent of stricter rules, offered a blunt assessment of the pace of the process: "Technology evolves faster than diplomacy. Diplomats are very slow."

## Pressure from the top

The diluted result came despite unusually strong high-level appeals. As talks opened on Monday, 16 November, UN Secretary-General António Guterres urged delegates to move toward negotiating a legally binding instrument with "clear prohibitions and restrictions."

"New technologies, including autonomous weapons systems, present new humanitarian, legal, ethical and security challenges," Guterres said, according to [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/11/1168810). "I urge you to seize this moment. Take action to prevent the use and spread of these merciless weapons."

Days later, UN human rights chief Volker Türk issued a last-minute appeal for states to agree a treaty. "A machine must never have the power to decide whether a human being survives or dies," Türk said, calling for human judgement and responsibility to remain at the centre of any decision involving the use of force. Pope Leo XIV and the International Committee of the Red Cross had also backed a ban.

Guterres had hoped formal negotiations on an international accord would begin in 2026. That goal was not met.

## Why the stakes are rising

The regulation of autonomous weapons has become more urgent as AI advances and as conflicts — most notably in Ukraine — demonstrate how quickly new systems reach the field. The concern is not only ethical but legal: international humanitarian law requires combatants to distinguish between civilians and fighters, ensure proportionality, and take precautions in attack. Critics argue that machines cannot reliably make those judgements, and that accountability becomes murky when an algorithm, rather than a person, selects a target.

The broader normative picture is contradictory. In December 2023, UN General Assembly Resolution 78/241 affirmed that lethal autonomous weapons must respect the laws of war; it drew support from 152 countries, with four against and 11 abstentions. That broad agreement in principle, however, has never been translated into binding rules — precisely because the states that build the most advanced systems are reluctant to constrain them.

The CCW itself is one of the cornerstones of international disarmament, having for more than 40 years set rules to reduce suffering from landmines, improvised explosive devices, incendiary weapons and explosive remnants of war. But its consensus-based model has long struggled with fast-moving technologies, and the LAWS file is now the clearest test of whether that model can still deliver.

## What to watch

The two-year window to 2028 is the next milestone, but the mandate agreed in Geneva is procedural rather than substantive: it commits states to "elaborate" an instrument without settling whether that instrument will be legally binding. With the US, Russia and Israel still unwilling to accept binding limits, the structural obstacles that blocked a ban this year remain in place.

For campaigners, the risk is that the technology outpaces the talks. Stop Killer Robots and Human Rights Watch are expected to keep pressing their case through the next two years, while the clock ticks toward 2028. Whether that deadline produces a treaty with teeth — or another agreement to keep talking — may depend on whether the major military powers that stayed the hand of the conference decide that the cost of inaction has finally become too high.
