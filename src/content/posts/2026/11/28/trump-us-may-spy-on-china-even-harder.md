# Trump Says US May Spy on China 'Even Harder'

US President Donald Trump has acknowledged that American intelligence agencies spy on China — and suggested Washington may conduct even more aggressive espionage than Beijing. The remark, made in response to a reporter's question, effectively inverted the standard US framing of Chinese espionage and conceded the symmetrical nature of great-power intelligence competition.

According to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34580813), the Chinese-language news outlet that first reported the comments in the pipeline's source material, Trump said people "always say China spies on US intelligence, but the US is also spying on China's intelligence — these operations have gone on for many years, and maybe the US spies even harder."

## A Candid Concession

The fullest version of the quote, carried by [Sina News](https://news.sina.com.cn/c/2026-11-27/doc-inkaewtv0953413.shtml) via the state-affiliated China News Service, captures the exchange more completely. In his own words, translated from the Chinese reporting:

> "People always say they (China) may be spying on our intelligence — but we spy on their intelligence just the same, and maybe we spy even harder than them. That's just how it is; it's been like this for many years."

The comment was reportedly made on the evening of 26 November, local time, and was syndicated widely by Chinese outlets — including Sina, Sohu, Tencent News and NetEase — through 27 and 28 November. The Paper dates the remark to 27 November, a discrepancy likely reflecting Beijing-time publication.

The candour is striking because it undercuts a long-standing rhetorical asymmetry. Washington frequently casts Chinese espionage as a one-sided threat, but Trump's phrasing treated mutual espionage as routine and established practice — "that's just how it is."

## Not a First

The "we spy on them too" framing is not new for Trump. He has used similar language repeatedly through 2026, including when dismissing reports about Chinese intelligence activity. The remark nonetheless landed with particular weight because of its timing and its clarity: it was a sitting US president, on the record, describing American intelligence gathering against China as comparable to — or possibly exceeding — Beijing's efforts against the United States.

Chinese state and social media largely received the comment as an implicit admission of American double standards, with commentators noting that great-power intelligence competition is routine and that the US "spies globally yet accuses others." For Chinese audiences, the candour was read as both revealing and self-incriminating.

## The Geopolitical Backdrop

The remark came at a delicate diplomatic moment. Just days earlier, Trump had embraced President Xi Jinping at the APEC summit in Shenzhen, where the two leaders pledged cooperation. But as [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2026/11/27/trump-embraced-xi-then-slapped-new-tariffs-on-china-whats-his-game-plan) reported, the goodwill did not last a week: the administration then unveiled a sweeping new tariff regime under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, targeting 14 major trading partners and hitting China hardest with a fresh 20 percent levy — and a threatened 40 percent if talks break down.

Beijing responded by warning it "reserves the right" to retaliate with steeper tariffs, calling the measures "a typical act of unilateralism that severely disrupts the international economic order" and a violation of WTO rules. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera interpreted the tariffs as an effort to rebuild leverage ahead of the next Trump-Xi meeting, scheduled for the G20 Leaders' Summit in Miami in December.

The espionage remarks fit within that broader posture. They also coincided with a live intelligence subplot: the ongoing US-Iran conflict and reports of foreign intelligence support to Tehran. During a Thanksgiving online call to US troops, Trump made a similarly candid comment on intelligence sharing, saying that "a lot of people always say Russia provides intelligence to Iran, but we provide far more intelligence than they do."

## Analysis: Symmetry, Not Asymmetry

The significance of the admission lies less in the fact of US espionage — an open secret in intelligence circles — than in its public acknowledgement by the president himself. By framing spying as mutual and long-standing, Trump moved the conversation away from the moral asymmetry often invoked in US political rhetoric and toward a recognition of intelligence competition as a structural feature of great-power rivalry.

That framing carries risks. For Washington, it complicates efforts to portray Chinese espionage as uniquely threatening. For Beijing, it offers a convenient talking point: the US, in Trump's own words, spies "even harder." And for the broader relationship, it adds another layer to an already combustible mix of trade confrontation, summit diplomacy and intelligence friction.

## What to Watch

Whether the White House walks back or clarifies the remark remains unclear; no official US statement was found in available reporting, and Beijing has issued no specific diplomatic response beyond routine media coverage.

The nearer test is December's G20 summit in Miami, where Trump and Xi are due to meet again. Both sides have signalled they want to discuss far larger issues — AI, nuclear weapons, and the wars in Iran and Ukraine — than trade or espionage. But the candid remarks serve as a reminder that, beneath the summit pageantry, the intelligence contest between the world's two largest economies continues unabated — and, by the president's own account, in both directions.
