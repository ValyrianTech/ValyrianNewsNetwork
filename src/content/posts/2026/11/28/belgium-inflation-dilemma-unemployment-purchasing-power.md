# Belgium's inflation dilemma: jobs or purchasing power

Belgium's inflation rate eased only marginally to **5.09% in November 2026**, down from 5.26% in October and 4.69% in September, according to the official [Statbel](https://statbel.fgov.be/nl/nieuws/de-inflatie-bedraagt-509) release. But behind that slight cooling lies a structural problem that one of the country's most respected economic historians says leaves policymakers with an uncomfortable choice: "We will have to choose between two evils: more unemployment or less purchasing power."

The warning comes from Erik Buyst, a professor emeritus of economic history at KU Leuven, in an interview with [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/28/wat-we-niet-kunnen-leren-uit-vorige-inflatiegolven/) on Radio 1's *De Ochtend*. With prices up roughly 5% over one year and about 25% over five years, Buyst argues that Belgium is living through inflation waves not seen in 40 to 50 years — and that the tools it once used to escape them are no longer available.

## An energy shock with a Belgian twist

The headline figure masks a sharper reality in energy. Energy product inflation reached **33.69%** in November, contributing 2.96 percentage points to overall inflation. Natural gas rose a striking 65.6% year-on-year, heating oil 38.1% and electricity 25.6%, according to the same Statbel release. Core inflation, which strips out energy and unprocessed food, was a gentler 2.47% — down from 2.80% in October.

Buyst draws a direct parallel with the oil shocks of the 1970s, when OPEC embargoes and the Iranian revolution sent energy costs soaring. Those global shocks, he says, are being amplified in Belgium by a distinctly national mechanism: automatic wage indexation. "Price increases translate immediately into higher wages. Those wage increases in turn push prices back up. We call that the famous wage-price spiral," he told VRT NWS. "In that sense, we see that inflation in Belgium generally runs higher than in neighbouring countries."

The system, born in the mid-1950s, is now roughly 70 years old. "It has become a fetish in the Belgian economy. But I fear we won't be able to escape it," Buyst said.

## Why the 1980s playbook no longer works

Buyst's historical argument is that the last comparable inflation crisis was tamed by a combination of forces Belgium can no longer fully command. Part of it was luck: a reverse oil shock in the mid-1980s, driven by overproduction and disunity within OPEC, sent prices falling. Central banks then raised interest rates sharply to cool demand. And crucially, Belgium devalued the franc.

"That is of course no longer possible today, because we have the euro. It is an important instrument we have lost. So we will have to do it all ourselves," he said. Without the exchange-rate escape valve, Belgium must instead reform its own supply side — a far slower and more politically painful path.

Buyst points to two priorities: cutting dependence on foreign energy and — more controversially — revisiting nuclear power. "We may have phased out that energy source somewhat too bravely. I think we should re-examine that," he said, pointing also to offshore wind in the North Sea, which he notes has suffered significant delays. On fossil fuels, he argues for diversifying supply lines beyond the Middle East and Russia toward North Africa, the United States and possibly Venezuela.

## The index: a double-edged sword

The heart of Buyst's argument lands squarely in Belgium's most explosive political debate. The De Wever government's budget table has sought some **€1.1 billion** in index-related savings, including a €400 million reform to be negotiated by employers and unions and roughly €700 million in additional measures, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/26/deze-voorstellen-over-de-index-waren-de-druppel-voor-vooruit/). Proposals to shift public servants, pensioners and benefit recipients onto the private-sector PC 200 system — indexing once a year on 1 January rather than quarterly — proved the "straw that broke the camel's back" for the socialist party Vooruit, whose chairman Conner Rousseau threatened to walk away from budget talks.

Buyst endorses more flexibility in the system, while conceding its protective value. "It is a double-edged sword. But we must be realistic: we cannot be holier than our neighbours. If they use a different system, we will constantly lose competitiveness. Ultimately we pay the price for that, with company closures," he said — and, he adds, with higher unemployment. He suggests firms with strong profits and productivity gains could absorb more indexation, while struggling sectors index more slowly.

## Analysis: a trade-off, not a solution

Buyst's framing is analytically honest but politically combustible. By presenting the choice as "two evils," he acknowledges that there is no painless exit from Belgium's inflation problem — only a question of who bears the cost. Indexation defenders, including Vooruit and the unions, see the system as essential protection for purchasing power, particularly for lower-income households most exposed to energy-driven price spikes. Employers and some economists echo Buyst's concern that above-neighbour inflation erodes competitiveness.

The research behind the interview is a single economist's analytical perspective, delivered on a public broadcaster's morning programme rather than a balanced debate; indexation's defenders were not given a counter-argument in the segment. Readers should weigh it as informed opinion rather than settled fact.

What is not in dispute is the arithmetic. With the smoothed health index at 102.25 points and the next pivot index (spilindex) for public-sector pay and social benefits set at 102.29 — just 0.04% away — the Federal Planning Bureau expects a crossing in December 2026. That would trigger automatic increases for many, even as the government scrambles for savings.

## What to watch

The coming weeks will test whether Belgium can thread Buyst's needle. Three things to watch: whether the Groep van Tien social partners reach an index-reform deal by year-end; whether the De Wever coalition holds together over the politically toxic question of index savings; and whether a December spilindex crossing reignites the wage-price spiral the economist warns about. For now, Belgium's households face the sharper end of the trade-off — a rising cost of living, and a policy debate that offers protection only at the price of competitiveness, or competitiveness only at the price of purchasing power.
