# China Touts 'National Chessboard' as Growth Engine

China's flagship state newspaper has made the case that binding the country's regions and its cities and countryside into a single coordinated strategy — the metaphor of a "national chessboard" — is unlocking large-scale development potential, as part of a series tracking implementation of Beijing's eight key economic priorities for 2026. The commentary, published in [People's Daily](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34581144) on 28 November and written by Wang Hao, is the fifth installment in a "implementation watch" series devoted to the eight tasks set at the December 2025 Central Economic Work Conference.

## Context: The Eight Tasks and the Fifth Priority

Beijing's leadership laid out the eight key economic tasks for 2026 at the Central Economic Work Conference held on 10-11 December 2025, a gathering that also set the year's guiding orientation of seeking progress while maintaining stability and improving quality and efficiency. As summarized by financial outlet [Hstong](https://www.hstong.com/news/detail/25121207300144646), the conference detailed the "five musts and eight tasks" that frame the 2026 economic agenda, with 2026 designated the opening year of the 15th Five-Year Plan.

Task five of the eight is to "adhere to coordinated development and promote urban-rural integration and regional linkage." The new commentary is squarely focused on that priority, presenting it less as a standalone policy than as an organizing principle for the entire economy.

## A Window Through Shenzhen

The article opens in Shenzhen, framing the southern tech hub and wider Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area as a lens onto national coordination. It points to the Shenzhen-Zhongshan Link keeping traffic high and to the newly opened Huanggang Port as symbols of a region where "roads connect, cities blend, and hearts are linked." It then widens to national efforts, describing the deep integration of physical infrastructure with institutional coordination. The "6 axes, 7 corridors, 8 channels" backbone of the national comprehensive transport network, it reports, is more than 90% complete, alongside the world's largest express delivery network and the rapid buildout of water, power-grid, and computing networks.

On the softer side, the piece cites cross-region government services such as the Sichuan-Chongqing "one-door, two-place affairs" arrangement, Guangdong's "Greater Bay Area collaborative innovation 2.0," and a "Beijing research, Tianjin-Hebei manufacturing" division of labor, arguing that institutional reform is breaking down administrative barriers between regions.

## Weighing the Urban-Rural Balance

The commentary turns to agriculture and rural life with a set of headline figures: summer grain output exceeded 300 billion jin for the first time, rural retail sales of consumer goods rose 2.2% year-on-year in January-October, and the urban-rural per-capita income ratio narrowed in the first three quarters, with central and western regions generally growing faster than the east. It also highlights new urbanization, noting the push to grant urban residency to rural migrants and the growth of tourism to county towns.

These points echo the series' January launch article, published by [People's Daily](https://www.peopleapp.com/column/30051172654-500007298307) and written by Yu Jingxian, which laid out the reference data behind the task. That piece quoted President Xi Jinping's rationale for the strategy: "Only when urban-rural and regional coordinated development is realized can the space for domestic circulation be broader and its quality better." It cited 19 city clusters carrying 75% of the population and contributing roughly 85% of GDP, a year-end 2024 urbanization rate of 67%, and 62 "100-billion-yuan counties" — local economies with GDP above 100 billion yuan — as evidence of county-level vitality.

## Analysis: Turning 'Development Gaps' into Momentum

The argument rests on a deliberate reframing: regional disparities, long a source of concern, are recast as untapped energy. The November piece speaks of "development gaps" whose potential is gradually being converted into the momentum of coordinated growth, and of stacking four strategies — regional coordinated development, major regional strategies, the main functional zone strategy, and new urbanization — so their effects compound rather than compete.

This is the logic that ties coordinated development to the broader push for "common prosperity" and an expanded domestic market. By narrowing income and infrastructure gaps between coast and interior and between city and countryside, policymakers hope to release new consumer demand and deepen economic resilience at a time of external headwinds. The task is thus both a social goal and a demand-side growth strategy.

The commentary is best read as an official achievement narrative rather than independent reporting: it is a morale piece in a government series, and it emphasizes successes while passing over persistent challenges such as regional fiscal strain, rural aging, and uneven county development. Yet the underlying statistics draw on official sources including the National Bureau of Statistics, and the framing is internally consistent with the January launch article.

## What to Watch Next

With 2026 the opening year of the 15th Five-Year Plan, the state media's message is that coordination is a durable organizing theme, not a one-year campaign. The commentary closes by invoking the ambition of playing "the national chessboard of development" well, contending that steadily improving the balance between town and country and among regions "will surely accumulate strong momentum for realizing Chinese-style modernization."

For observers, the key question is whether the emphasis on soft connectivity — shared public services, mutual recognition of standards, and broken-down administrative barriers — can keep pace with the easier wins of hard infrastructure, and whether the narrowing gaps celebrated in official data translate into lasting gains in rural and interior incomes.
