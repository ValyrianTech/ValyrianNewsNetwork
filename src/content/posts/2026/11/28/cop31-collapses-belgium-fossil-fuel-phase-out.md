# COP31 Collapses as Belgium and 20+ Nations Fight for Fossil Phase-Out

The COP31 climate summit in Antalya ended without an agreement on Sunday 22 November, after Belgium and more than 20 like-minded countries fought to reinstate an explicit commitment to phase out oil and gas, and the European Union formally rejected a final draft that did not even mention fossil fuels. It was only the third time in the 31-year history of the UN climate process that a summit has closed without a deal — after The Hague in 2000 and Copenhagen in 2009.

## A "Delivery COP" That Delivered Nothing

The Turkish resort city was meant to host a "delivery COP," one focused on implementing rather than on new promises. Instead, the conference ran into overtime and became the longest COP ever, as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/eu-verwerpt-akkoord-dat-voorligt-we-kunnen-geen-beslissing-ste~1794235749946/) reported, surpassing the 44-hour overrun of Madrid in 2019. Technical texts were endorsed, but the overarching political decision collapsed for lack of consensus.

The core conflict was familiar. Three years ago in Dubai, countries committed for the first time to "transition away" from fossil fuels. At Antalya, that language quietly disappeared from the draft presented by the Turkish presidency, which instead retained a reference to carbon capture and storage (CCS) and set no figure for climate finance.

## Belgium Raises Its Voice

As negotiations entered their final stretch, Belgium joined a coalition of around 20 countries demanding that the phase-out of oil, gas and coal be restored to the final text. According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/cop31-a-antalya-la-belgique-et-une-vingtaine-de-pays-haussent-le-ton-pour-reinscrire-la-sortie-du-petrole-et-du-gaz-dans-le-projet-d-accord-11818457), the group announced it would — or already had — established national roadmaps to turn the commitment into concrete action. A separate letter from France, Panama and the Solomon Islands demanded a "clear transition away from fossil fuels" and "immediate clean electrification."

Belgium's federal Climate Minister, Jean-Luc Crucke, refused to concede defeat mid-negotiation. "The final text is not yet approved. It changes hour by hour and it remains a demand of Europe... So we will fight to the end," he said.

In the end, the fight failed. When the EU — speaking through Irish Climate Minister Darragh O'Brien, in his capacity as holder of the rotating EU Council presidency — formally rejected the revised text, the summit's fate was sealed. "We will not support an agreement that does not even mention fossil fuels, the main cause of climate change. We will not accept a step back in time," O'Brien said. "And we will not accept a climate conference that greenwashes fossil fuels."

Panama, the Marshall Islands, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu and Norway followed. COP president Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister, then confirmed there was "no consensus" and that the decisions were "not adopted."

## "A Step Backwards"

Crucke did not mince words on the outcome. "The text that was submitted to us last night is unacceptable. It is not simply a text that lacks ambition: it is a step backwards. It turns its back on science, stays silent on the main cause of global warming and would lock us durably into our dependence on fossil fuels," he later said.

The Belgian minister also fired a warning at those who used carbon capture as a justification for prolonging hydrocarbons. "Carbon capture is necessary for certain industries that are difficult to decarbonise... But it cannot become an alibi to prolong our dependence on oil, gas and coal," he said.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres voiced the frustration of those who had hoped for more. "I share the deep frustration of everyone who hoped governments would respond to what the climate crisis demands," he said, describing fossil fuels as "the engine of the climate crisis." "Those who profit from the destruction of our planet should never dictate the terms of the fight to save it," he added.

France's La Première and the wider Belgian press watched the proceedings with mounting alarm. A [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/784283/article/2026-11-23/l-urgence-climatique-se-heurte-au-mur-de-la-cop31-antalya) editorial likened the summit's indecision to Winston Churchill's 1936 critique of European leaders who were "resolved to remain irresolute" in the face of a gathering peril.

## Why It Fell Apart

The standoff had several roots. Saudi Arabia and Russia led a bloc that blocked any reference to a fossil fuel phase-out, with Russia warning of "energy shortages, price shocks and energy poverty." The EU, meanwhile, held firm against a text it said rolled back the Dubai commitment.

An unprecedented co-presidency complicated matters: Turkey served as formal president and host while Australia led the negotiations — an arrangement analysts say weakened the process. Both countries remain heavily dependent on coal, and Turkey plans to triple oil production to one million barrels a day by 2030. A signature COP31 objective — the so-called 35x35 goal of reaching 35% electricity in the global energy mix by 2035 — was demoted in the final draft to an optional tool, and critics noted it said nothing about how that electricity is generated.

Climate finance was another fault line. Developing countries sought $120 billion by 2035 for adaptation; the text contained no figure, only an agreement to "keep talking." Saudi Arabia championed that grievance while defending CCS against EU criticism that it amounted to greenwashing.

## A Process Under Strain

The failure reverberated beyond Antalya. Al Gore called for "drastic reform" of the COP process, and Australian opposition parties demanded the resignation of Energy Minister Chris Bowen, who had served as lead negotiator. Crucke stressed that the process must survive — but change. "The COP must continue... But a system where a few countries can do everything to block an agreement, that cannot last," he said.

Belgian climatologist Jean-Pascal van Ypersele, a former IPCC vice-chair, called the outcome "symbolic of the current geopolitical situation," in which the United States was largely absent, China stayed in the background and the EU talked ambitiously but did not always act consistently. Still, he saw a possible upside. "I think it could create an electroshock. Maybe it is not even such a bad thing to give the process a new impetus and make a fresh start on a better basis," he said.

UNFCCC Executive Secretary Simon Stiell acknowledged the process had fallen short, but placed it in longer perspective: before Paris, the world was heading for 3.5–4°C of warming; now it is on track for around 2.6°C. "That is still not where we need to be," he conceded, adding that past setbacks had always been followed by comebacks.

## What's Next

Attention now turns to COP32 in Ethiopia, one of the least developed countries and highly exposed to climate impacts — and to the crossing of the 1.5°C threshold, which scientists now consider all but certain. Despite the no-deal, a coalition of some 30 countries, Belgium among them, is pressing ahead with national roadmaps to exit fossil fuels — a "coalition of the willing" approach that some see as the future of climate diplomacy.

Greenpeace's Jasper Inventor warned of the cost of inaction. "The failure to agree is very painful for people on the frontline of the climate crisis, such as the Pacific island states," he said. "As always, it is the most vulnerable communities who pay the price for this failure."

For Belgium, the summit leaves a bitter taste and a clear message. As Crucke put it during the final hours: "Our ambition does not depend on the permission of those who want to keep the world dependent on fossil fuels." The question for Antalya's aftermath is whether that ambition can be turned into a binding outcome — or whether the world's climate architecture will keep stalling at the wall of consensus.
