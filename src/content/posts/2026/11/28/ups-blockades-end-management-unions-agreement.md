# UPS Blockades End as Management, Unions Reach Deal

The blockades at parcel-delivery giant UPS came to an end on 28 November after management and the unions concluded a social agreement overnight, drawing an eight-day industrial dispute to a close. The picket lines at the company's sites in Lummen (Limburg) and Diegem (Flemish Brabant) were lifted immediately and work resumed, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/28/akkoord-bij-pakjesbedrijf-ups-blokkades-in-lummen-en-diegem-wor/).

The deal ends a standoff that had held back hundreds of thousands of parcels during one of the busiest delivery periods of the year — but it does not reverse the job cuts at the heart of the dispute, and the unions are already reframing the fight as a campaign for tighter regulation of the wider parcel sector.

## What the agreement covers

Workers voted to approve the draft protocol agreement reached overnight, and the pickets were dissolved at once. "We have been able to put a proposal on the table for the staff with sufficient compensation for the dismissals," Hans Elsen of the Christian trade union ACV said, as quoted by [TVL](https://www.tvl.be/nieuws/staking-voorbij-na-sociaal-akkoord-bij-ups-184540). "The staff approved it, after which the picket posts were lifted."

The concrete content of the agreement has not been made public, and the precise compensation levels remain unconfirmed. UPS said only that normal service had resumed. "All our activities are running normally again," the company stated. "We are fully committed to offering our customers the reliable service they expect."

The joint union statement struck a similar note of closure while pointing to the work still to be done: "The workers have approved the agreement. As a result, the picket lines are being lifted immediately and work is resuming."

## Eight days of disruption

The dispute had been building since June 2026, when UPS informed its works council that it intended to cut up to 525 of roughly 800 Belgian jobs, replacing permanent staff with cheaper subcontractors and freelancers, as [VRT NWS reported at the time](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/06/23/ups-wil-tot-525-banen-schrappen/). The move angered unions, coming a week before the summer holidays and forming part of a broader global retrenchment — UPS had announced 20,000 job cuts worldwide in April 2025, [VRT NWS noted](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/04/29/ups-schrapt-20-000-banen-wereldwijd/).

The action escalated in late November. On 20 November, workers began blocking subcontractors around Diegem, holding back some 45,000 parcels, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/20/werknemers-ups-blokkeren-onderaannemers-45-000-pakjes-tegengeh/), with [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/783894/article/2026-11-20/les-travailleurs-dups-bloquent-des-sous-traitants-pres-de-diegem-45000-colis) reporting the same figure. Blockades soon spread to the UPS centres in Diegem and Lummen.

By 24 November the unions reported up to 50,000 parcels blocked per day, with an accumulated backlog of 200,000 to 300,000 parcels, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/24/ups-pakjes-staking-hinder-pakjesachterstand-geblokkeerd-diegem-l/). Earlier in the strike, [TVL](https://www.tvl.be/nieuws/meer-dan-55000-pakjes-geblokkeerd-door-aanhoudende-staking-bij-ups-184420) had put the figure at more than 55,000 parcels blocked at its peak snapshot.

## What was won — and what was not

The unions secured improved compensation for dismissed workers, but they did not obtain a reduction in the 525 planned job losses. Throughout the dispute they had invoked the so-called Renault Law, which obliges large employers planning collective redundancies to consult workers and negotiate a social plan, and had pushed to return to the "first phase" of that law — genuine negotiation over the scale of the cuts. The agreement concerns severance and compensation terms, not the preservation of the posts.

The timing of the settlement reflected pressure on both sides: federal conciliation talks convened at the FPS Economy, the mounting economic cost of a growing parcel backlog during peak season, and the approach of the end-of-year rush all weighed toward a deal.

## The fight moves to the legislature

With the industrial action over, the unions have pivoted to the political arena. Elsen warned that the sector's structural problems run far beyond UPS. "There is much more employment in the sector, more and more parcels are being delivered," he said. "But they are delivered by very poorly paid self-employed people, partly also undeclared. The group of people who are paid correctly, who can live off their job, is getting smaller and smaller."

The unions are demanding a "Postal Law 2.0" to tighten rules on bogus self-employment, undeclared work and exploitation among parcel subcontractors. The current framework dates to the 2018 [Postal Law](https://www.ejustice.just.fgov.be/eli/wet/2018/01/26/2018010510/justel), which governs how postal services are organised. Elsen argued that policy must "encourage employers to pay people correctly and hire them on permanent contracts."

## What to watch

Two questions hang over the settlement. The first is logistics: with 200,000 to 300,000 parcels backlogged, neither side had clarified how or when the accumulated deliveries would be cleared — a pressing concern for businesses and consumers relying on UPS in the run-up to the holidays. The second is political: whether the union campaign for a stricter Postal Law gains traction will determine whether the UPS dispute proves a turning point for labour standards across Belgium's parcel sector, or merely a truce.

As the unions put it, parcel deliverers and warehouse workers "form the indispensable links in the logistics chain behind e-commerce" and "have the right to safe, healthy and correct working conditions." How that principle is translated into law is now the central question.
