# Stoclet Palace Caught in Family Legal Feud

One of Brussels' most celebrated architectural landmarks is now at the centre of a legal battle inside the family that owns it. Minority family shareholders of the company that manages the Stoclet Palace — a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a masterpiece of Viennese Art Nouveau — have filed a dissolution action before the French-speaking Enterprise Court of Brussels, according to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/785426/article/2026-11-28/le-palais-stoclet-pris-dans-un-conflit-judiciaire-entre-les-actionnaires), citing reporting by L'Echo. The plaintiffs argue that the company's coffers are empty and that the passage of time is taking too heavy a toll on the building, making a sale "inevitable."

The majority side insists that operations are continuing normally and that new projects are under development.

## A Total Work of Art

Commissioned by the banker Adolphe Stoclet and designed by the Viennese architect Josef Hoffmann, the palace was built between 1905 and 1911 on the Avenue de Tervueren in Woluwe-Saint-Pierre. It is widely regarded as a synthesis of the Vienna Secession's idea of the *Gesamtkunstwerk* — a total work of art — bringing architecture, interior design and fine art into a single ensemble.

Its interior is an exceptional treasure: mosaics by Gustav Klimt, decorations by the painter Koloman Moser, and works by Fernand Khnopff, including the celebrated Klimt dining-room frieze. Privately owned to this day, the palace is managed by a family company, the Compagnie Immobilière S.A.S.

## The 37.5 Percent Revolt

The dispute centres on that company. Some shareholders holding 37.5 percent of the shares believe the firm is financially "exsangue" — drained — with no prospects for what they describe as a heritage jewel. According to [L'Echo](https://www.lecho.be/culture/architecture/le-palais-stoclet-au-c-ur-d-un-conflit-entre-les-actionnaires-familiaux/10707287.html), no family shareholder appears either able or willing to recapitalise the company or to buy out the shares of other family branches.

The minority shareholders had already triggered the so-called "sonnette d'alarme" (alarm-bell) procedure at the 2024 ordinary general meeting — a mechanism in Belgian company law that lets a quarter of the votes cast decide on dissolution. They say the majority side bypassed that vote and simply carried on. The minority then turned to the Brussels Enterprise Court to have the company's dissolution formally declared.

For its part, the Compagnie Immobilière S.A.S. states that "activities are continuing normally and that the company is developing new projects."

## Two Decades of Legal Battles

The shareholder dispute is the latest chapter in a much longer saga. For roughly twenty years, the palace has been the subject of litigation that once pitted the family against the Brussels Region rather than against itself.

The first skirmishes date to 2005, when the Region sought to classify the palace's movable furnishings, which include masterpieces by Klimt and Khnopff. Three of Baron Stoclet's granddaughters issued a rare statement insisting that the house and its contents "were never considered by our grandparents and parents as a museum or an object of curiosity." The case reached the Court of Cassation, which in 2013 ruled in the Region's favour, finding that classifying the furniture was not prejudicial.

More recently, the family contested the creation of an immersive 3D model of the palace, built by researchers from ULB's architecture faculty from public archives and shown at the Cinquantenaire Museum in 2023. An agreement was eventually reached. In parallel, Ans Persoons (Vooruit), Brussels' Secretary of State for Heritage, championed an ordinance requiring the palace to open to the public 15 days per year. As [L'Echo reported](https://www.lecho.be/culture/architecture/les-heritiers-stoclet-ne-veulent-pas-ouvrir-le-palais/10656561.html) in April 2026, the heirs challenged that ordinance before the Constitutional Court, arguing it violated their right to property and individual freedom. Their lawyer, Michel Delnoy, opened his plea with a blunt question: "Who would accept being obliged to open their home to the public, forced to receive strangers under imposed arrangements? No one." The case was taken under deliberation.

That legal front left the palace effectively closed to the public, as Le Soir noted in June. The Region has paid just over €1 million in subsidies for works since 2007.

## A Heritage Awaiting a Project

The internal rupture is what makes this week's development significant. While the historic conflict was heirs versus Region, the palace's future direction is now contested *inside* the owning family itself.

Indeed, the absence of a shared plan has been flagged publicly for months. In July 2026, heritage chronicler [Paul Grosjean published an open letter](https://www.forbes.be/fr/palais-stoclet-une-lettre-ouverte-a-boris-dillies-pour-rendre-a-bruxelles-son-joyau-unesco/) to Brussels Minister-President Boris Dilliès in Forbes Belgique, urging that the palace be turned into an instrument of regional influence ahead of Belgium's bicentenary. He wrote that the palace "lacks a genuine project, for lack of consensus among the descendants of Adolphe and Suzanne Stoclet" — a remark that now reads as a premonition of the open family conflict.

## Where the Money Meets the Marble

At its core, the shareholder action is a financial manoeuvre dressed in corporate law. By invoking the alarm-bell procedure and then seeking dissolution, the minority is trying to force one of three outcomes: recapitalisation, a buyout, or a sale.

Each carries complications for a UNESCO-listed property. This is an extraordinarily expensive asset — delicate Klimt friezes, mosaics and fragile materials — that generates no tourism revenue and has not been inhabited since Baroness Anny Stoclet's death in 2002, apart from its concierges. If the court declares the company dissolved, the palace or the company's assets could be liquidated, raising the question of who could step in: the Region, a museum, a foundation, or a private collector. Any buyer would also inherit the unresolved 15-days-per-year opening obligation and the pending Constitutional Court ruling.

## What to Watch

The Enterprise Court's handling of the dissolution action will determine whether the palace faces a forced change of hands. Several questions remain open: the actual financial position of the Compagnie Immobilière, the precise holdings and identities of the majority ownership, and whether the Constitutional Court will uphold the public-opening ordinance. For now, a building celebrated as a total work of art remains caught between the impulse to preserve it exactly as it is and the growing pressure — from within its own family — to let it go.
