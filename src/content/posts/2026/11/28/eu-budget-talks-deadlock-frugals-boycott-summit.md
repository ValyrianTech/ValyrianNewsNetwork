# EU Budget Talks Deadlock as Frugals Boycott Summit

European Union leaders left a Brussels summit without agreement on the bloc's next seven-year budget, after a group of net-paying "frugal" states boycotted the small-group talks designed to break the deadlock and a second day of negotiations was scrapped. The failure leaves the EU's 2028-2034 Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF) stalled, with talks now pushed to a December summit and several leaders openly questioning whether the year-end deadline can be met.

The special European Council, held on 26-27 November, had been convened explicitly to find a breakthrough on the budget, which sets the EU's spending ceiling and priorities for seven years. The current framework expires at the end of 2027.

## A summit built to fail

The meeting was plagued by tensions, reproaches and boycotts, according to [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/27/a-lot-of-political-theatre-eu-summit-ends-in-disarray-as-leaders-cling-to-red-lines), which reported that leaders "split in opposing camps, refused to abandon their red lines and openly questioned whether a deal could be reached by December."

Council President António Costa tabled a compromise worth roughly €1.78 trillion over seven years — a cut of about 10%, or €204 billion, from the European Commission's original proposal of nearly €2 trillion. The plan equals about 1.07% of EU gross national income, below the current budget's 1.12%; once Covid-era repayments are subtracted, it falls to around 0.97%.

To coax concessions, Costa split the summit into small groups of geographically mixed leaders, with several rounds planned through midnight. The format backfired. The seven frugal states — Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden — closed ranks, complained the arrangement unfairly pressured them, and refused further small-group sessions, demanding a return to plenary. Austria and Sweden skipped later group meetings; Denmark also dropped out of a bilateral session.

## Two camps, one gulf

On one side sit the net contributors, led by Germany, who want the budget cut further. On the other, the 17 "Friends of Cohesion" — mostly central, eastern and southern net recipients — resist cuts to agriculture and cohesion funds.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz told reporters late at night that the reduction "will not remain at €204bn. It will be less," adding that "the reduction compared to the commission proposal must be several hundred billion euros," according to [EUobserver](https://euobserver.com/252027/eu-leaders-leave-brussels-with-no-budget-deal-piling-pressure-on-christmas-summit/). Merz also stressed that "the European Union comprises of 27 member states, not 23 or 24."

French President Emmanuel Macron, who occupies a middle position, called the €204 billion cut a "strategic error" and warned he would not sign an "unbalanced agreement." France is demanding full protection of its €66.25 billion share of the Common Agricultural Policy, insisting that "not a single euro of what France currently receives must be missing."

Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni set the tone early, telling reporters a deal was "light years away" and rejecting the year-end target. "I don't have a time limit. I have specific conditions," she said. Dutch Prime Minister Rob Jetten, a staunch frugal, was equally dismissive: "There's no need to hurry. For me, there's no deadline."

## The election calendar looms

The pressure to conclude by 31 December is driven less by procedure than by politics. Macron warned that after that date he would "no longer be in a position, politically or democratically, to reopen discussions on the budget," as France enters a period too close to its spring 2027 presidential election, in which far-right leader Marine Le Pen — who has vowed to cut France's EU contribution — is competitive.

Spain, Poland and Italy also face votes within 12 months. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez was absent from the summit because of national elections that weekend, delegating his vote to Macron.

As [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/27/eu-budget-2028-2034-deadlock.html) reported, the negotiations are unfolding against a backdrop of surging government borrowing costs, elevated inflation and sluggish growth across much of the continent, alongside concerns about rising budget deficits in several member states. Failure to meet the year-end target risks deepening the political deadlock at the heart of the talks.

The summit had also been meant to address a proposed €45 billion loan to Ukraine, backed by joint debt, and the renewal of European Central Bank leadership, with Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos selected to succeed Christine Lagarde. But the budget fight consumed the agenda, and the Ukraine loan — opposed by Slovakia, Hungary and the Czech Republic — was pushed to December.

## Analysis: a near-impossible balancing act

Costa faces a structural dilemma. To satisfy the frugals he may need to trim the headline figure further; to keep the 17 Friends of Cohesion and France on board he must protect agriculture and cohesion spending. Squaring that circle is what stalled this summit, and nothing on the table resolves it.

Two conditions must ultimately be met: unanimous approval by all 27 member states and a majority in the European Parliament — at least 361 of 720 votes. Parliament has signalled it will not be a rubber stamp. "Parliament is consistent and proposes more investments, [the] Council is inconsistent and proposes cuts," Romanian MEP Siegfried Mureşan said, warning that "prime ministers also cannot dictate to the European Parliament to adopt a budget that is too small, and which does not equip Europe for the future."

Costa, for his part, insisted the process was on track. "This meeting was a necessary step in the process," he said. "Everybody knows that we cannot fail Europe, and we need to have a new budget from the 1st of January 2028." One diplomat offered a blunter reading: "You need to have a bad European Council to have a good one. It was a lot of political theatre."

## What's next

All eyes now turn to the European Council on 17 December, where Costa is expected to bring a revised proposal. If the year-end deadline slips, the deal likely falls to the second half of 2027 under Ireland's EU presidency — a scenario France warns would unfold under "much more pressure" amid its presidential campaign.

The core question remains unresolved: can 27 member states with incompatible red lines, and an election calendar closing in, agree the shape of Europe's spending before the current budget expires? On the evidence of Brussels this week, the answer is not yet.
