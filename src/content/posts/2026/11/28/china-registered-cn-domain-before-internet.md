# How China Registered .cn Before It Even Had the Internet

On November 28, 1990, China secured its place in the global address system when the country-code top-level domain **.cn** was recorded in the international registry. The anniversary is being marked as a milestone in China's internet history—but the popular story of a single national triumph obscures a more unusual reality: the domain was registered from Germany, by a German professor acting on Chinese authority, at a time when China had not yet connected to the global internet.

According to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34571855), which republished the anniversary account sourced from CCTV News, the date is tied to Qian Tianbai, revered as the "Father of China's Internet," who is said to have registered the .CN domain on China's behalf and helped establish the country's first CN domain name server abroad.

## A Registration Before a Connection

The commemorated date is real, but it marks an administrative milestone rather than a single signing ceremony. As a detailed oral history published by the [Internet Society of China](https://www.isc.org.cn/article/13069.html) recounts, the November 28 date is the one recorded in the IANA/InterNIC registry—the international body that assigned domain names. The application itself was filed on November 26 and formally approved on December 3, in the same batch as Egypt (EG), Hungary (HU), and South Africa's UNINET project (ZA).

The driving force behind the application was Wang Yunfeng, a professor at the Institute for Computer Application (ICA) under China's ordnance industry. At a meeting with German professor Werner Zorn at the University of Karlsruhe on October 10, 1990, Wang decided that "CN"—drawn from the international abbreviation "CHN"—should represent China online. Fearing that delay would leave China behind, he formally authorized Zorn to register the domain on the country's behalf.

## The German Hand in China's Digital Identity

Zorn, who had helped send China's first email in 1987, became the practical architect of the registration. He sent a preliminary reservation request to InterNIC on October 19, received confirmation four days later that "CN" was available, and formally applied on November 26. He listed Qian Tianbai—who had recently joined the CANET networking project—as the administrative contact, with the ICA as the owning institution.

The technical contact proved more delicate. Because China was not yet connected and Zorn felt it inappropriate to list himself, he recorded the technical contact as the University of Karlsruhe's computer science department. The result, as the ISC oral history notes, was a rare arrangement: a national domain administered by Chinese hands but operated technically by a German university department.

Zorn's role has been described in Chinese accounts as akin to that of Norman Bethune—the Canadian physician revered in China for his selfless service—working without expectation of reward. In a 2004 letter to CNNIC marking the anniversary, he closed with a Latin blessing: "Vivat, Crescat, Floreat!"—may you and China's internet live, grow, and flourish.

## A Server Abroad, Then at Home

With China still outside the global network, the .cn name server began running at the University of Karlsruhe in January 1991, maintained by Zorn's team. It would remain there for more than three years. China achieved a full functional internet connection only on April 20, 1994, and on May 21 of that year the .cn server was finally set up domestically at the CAS network center that would become CNNIC. Karlsruhe stopped resolving, and the domain came home. Qian Tianbai remained administrative contact, while Qian Hualin took over the technical role.

The numbers tell the story of what followed. When the server returned to China in 1994, it held only about a dozen domains, almost all tied to scientific research. By 2008, .cn had grown to more than 12 million registered domains, making it one of the largest country-code domains in the world.

## Contested Credit and a National Asset

The anniversary is now firmly part of China's internet lore, but historians of the period are careful about attribution. While The Paper's account centers on Qian Tianbai, CNNIC's own chronicle credits both Wang Yunfeng and Werner Zorn for the registration, and the ISC oral history makes clear that the filing was a genuinely cross-border effort. Popular retellings have also credited Qian with China's first email of 1987—an achievement that documentary evidence attributes to a collective effort involving Li Chengjiong, Wang Yunfeng, and Zorn.

Qian's documented contribution lay elsewhere: in liaison, administration, advocacy, and the later institution-building of CNNIC and CSTNET. He died of a heart attack in 1998 at the age of 53, before the domain he helped steward became a national strategic asset.

## What's Next

Today .cn is managed by CNNIC under the oversight of the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, and it sits at the center of China's push for digital sovereignty and its "network power" agenda. The anniversary is less a celebration of one person than a reminder of how China's place in cyberspace began—with foresight, a borrowed server, and a partnership that crossed borders long before the network itself did.
