# Houston Police to Reduce ICE Cooperation After Midterms

Houston Mayor John Whitmire signed an executive order on Friday directing the Houston Police Department to sharply reduce its contact with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, a shift he explicitly tied to this week's midterm election results. Under the new policy, HPD officers will contact ICE only when a person with a civil immigration warrant is also charged with a jailable offense, according to [Houston Public Media](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/city-of-houston/2026/11/06/566458/houston-mayor-john-whitmire-announces-hpd-will-reduce-ice-cooperation-following-midterm-results/).

"I do this to begin a healing process, reduce fear and anxiety across our community," Whitmire said at a press conference. "I also do this knowing that elections matter."

## A Reversal of Course

The order marks a dramatic swing in a policy fight that has consumed Houston City Hall for most of the year. In April, Whitmire joined several city council members in an effort to curtail HPD's coordination with federal immigration agents, giving officers discretion over whether to contact ICE about civil warrants. Texas Gov. Greg Abbott intervened, threatening to revoke more than $110 million in public safety funding, and the council reversed the measure, instead requiring officers to detain immigrants for a "reasonable" period while awaiting ICE.

The earlier retreat was driven by money. Before the council vote to walk back the ordinance, Whitmire's staff circulated a memo detailing more than $260 million in direct appropriations to Houston from the Republican-controlled Texas Legislature in 2025, and the mayor warned that a protracted fight with the state would cost the city future funding.

## What the Order Actually Changes

The executive order draws a careful distinction between civil and criminal matters. Officers will still contact ICE when someone with an administrative immigration warrant is arrested for a jailable offense, and the order does not prevent ICE from conducting its own federal operations in Houston. HPD does not participate in a 287(g) agreement that would let local officers perform federal immigration enforcement functions, according to Police Chief Noe Diaz.

"If our police officers pull over someone for a minor traffic infraction and they write a citation, that person receives a citation and goes about their day," Diaz said. "HPD will no longer contact ICE if they have an administrative warrant and they are not arrested."

The policy change follows reporting that HPD's interactions with ICE had grown contentious. According to the department's first two quarterly reports, officers contacted ICE nearly 200 times between April and September, including at least 36 cases in which HPD handed people directly to ICE. Some of those encounters followed calls for emergency help, including a suicide attempt and a domestic violence incident.

## Community and Political Reactions

The change was welcomed by immigrant advocates who had pressed the city for months. "Today Houston won. Today our community can breathe easier," said Cesar Espinosa, executive director of FIEL Houston. Sergio Lira of LULAC described a flood of calls from residents asking, "Why now? Why not sooner?" but added, "it's never too late for our city to start healing."

City Council Member Alejandra Salinas, who spearheaded the April ordinance, praised the mayor and chief for "finally listening to the calls of community." But the timing drew pointed criticism even from allies. City Controller Chris Hollins said simply: "Protecting Houstonians is every elected leader's responsibility, before and after an election."

The Houston Police Officers' Union, which was not consulted, reacted with alarm. "We were completely blindsided by this," said union president Douglas Griffith, who warned that officers could be exposed to liability should someone released later commit a crime.

Federal officials pushed back sharply. A Department of Homeland Security spokesperson told [KPRC 2](https://www.click2houston.com/news/local/2026/11/07/sanctuary-politicians-dhs-fires-back-at-houston-mayors-decision-to-limit-hpd-cooperation-with-ice/) that the agency remains "disappointed that sanctuary politicians seek to limit cooperation with ICE," invoking the Supremacy Clause and warning that federal officers would maintain "a more visible presence" in the city. ICE, in a social media post, accused Whitmire of "directly advocating for policies that protect some of the worst criminals in America."

## Analysis: A Calculated Bet on a New Political Landscape

Whitmire's decision is less a sudden conversion than a recalculation of risk. For a year, the mayor balanced his long-standing opposition to Texas's SB 4 against the city's dependence on state and federal grants — a tension he acknowledged candidly. "Sometimes, as a mayor, I have to do not my personal views, but what I know to be in Houston's best interest," he said. "We cannot run the city government without state and federal grants."

What changed is the political math. The midterms delivered sweeping Democratic gains across Texas, including the governor's office, which Whitmire said he believes will take a more receptive approach to the city's position. By acting now, the mayor is testing whether the funding leverage that once forced his retreat has weakened under new state leadership. He framed the move as both principle and prudence: "I understand there's risk. I will take that risk and I know I'm doing right."

The order leaves several questions open. It is unclear whether state or federal officials will challenge it legally, particularly given the union's concerns about SB 4 compliance. How much ICE contact will actually decline remains to be seen, and the measure's durability depends on whether the incoming state government follows through on a softer posture.

For now, Houston has repositioned itself in the national debate over local immigration enforcement — and Whitmire has signaled that, in his view, the election has given him room to act.
