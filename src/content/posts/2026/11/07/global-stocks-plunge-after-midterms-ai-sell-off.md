# Global Stocks Plunge After Midterms on AI Sell-Off

Global stock markets fell sharply after the U.S. midterm elections, driven by a violent sell-off in technology and artificial intelligence shares as investors weighed whether a new Democratic-controlled Congress and freshly elected state leaders will slow the nation's AI infrastructure buildout. The Nasdaq 100 plunged 6.3% on Thursday, its worst single-day performance since the Covid-19 pandemic, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/markets/global-markets-tech-stocks-ai-midterms-democrats-asia-europe-rcna607794). The broader S&P 500 fell 4.4%, wiping out roughly $2.8 trillion in paper wealth.

## Context: A Political Shock Meets an AI-Fueled Market

The trigger was Tuesday's midterm elections, in which Democrats won sweeping victories at both the federal and state levels — including candidates who had pledged to rein in AI expansion and data-center development. Most prominent was Gina Hinojosa, who won the Texas governor's race in a state that ranks among the largest data-center markets in the country. Investors had initially pushed shares higher on Wednesday, relieved that the elections had not left control of Congress undecided, but sentiment shifted sharply the following day as the implications sank in.

"Markets are being punished according to their dependence on the U.S. AI infrastructure cycle," Charu Chanana, chief investment strategist at Singapore-based SAXO Markets, told NBC News.

The AI boom has become an outsized driver of U.S. equity gains and economic growth, helping the economy post 3.4% annualized growth in the third quarter of 2026, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/05/nx-s1-6031987/stock-markets-ai-midterm-elections). Any threat to the demand-fueled growth narrative can rattle investors whose valuations rest on continued AI capital spending.

"There is some concern regarding AI regulation, and fears that there could be far more regulations coming to AI technology, as well as a pushback on the buildout of data centers across the country," said David Busch, chief investment officer at Trajan Wealth.

## The U.S. Rout, in Numbers

The Thursday sell-off was severe and broad. Individual AI and semiconductor names were hit hard, with [LiveMint](https://www.livemint.com/market/stock-market-news/how-us-midterm-election-results-ai-sell-off-impacted-us-stock-market-nikkei-225-kospi-taiwan-and-china-data-details-11793943446082.html) reporting that NVIDIA fell 9.26%, AMD dropped 17.26% and Micron tumbled 17.46%. Elon Musk's companies were also among the biggest decliners; Tesla and SpaceX fell particularly sharply, with investors fearing the billionaire — who spent heavily backing unsuccessful Republican candidates — could face Democratic scrutiny when the new Congress is seated in January.

U.S. markets partially steadied on Friday. The Nasdaq 100 closed up 1.1% and the S&P 500 finished 0.3% higher, though a strong October jobs report raised the odds the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates at its December meeting, pushing stocks off their highs earlier in the day.

## Asia Bears the Brunt

The pain rippled across Asia, where hardware-heavy markets are most exposed to the U.S. AI supply chain. Japan's Nikkei suffered its worst week since 2020, falling as much as 6% on Friday and more than 20% from its recent high, entering bear-market territory. South Korea's Kospi tumbled more than 8%, triggering a temporary marketwide trading halt; memory suppliers Samsung and SK Hynix plunged more than 10% before recovering some losses. In Taiwan, home to chip giant TSMC, the benchmark TAIEX index dropped 9%.

"This matters enormously for Asia because much of the hardware supporting America's AI ambitions is manufactured here," Chanana said. Tai Hui, J.P. Morgan's APAC chief market strategist, told NBC News he was "not surprised to see the magnitude of correction today following the sharp correction in the U.S."

China offered a partial exception. Its tech-focused Star 50 and AI Index slumped as much as 5% before recouping some losses, and mainland and Hong Kong equities largely shrugged off the rout, reflecting lower direct exposure to the U.S. data-center supply chain. "There is a legitimate argument that parts of China's AI equity bubble have already burst," Chanana said, adding that Beijing treats AI and semiconductor self-sufficiency as strategic national priorities, allowing investment to continue for reasons beyond immediate profitability.

## Analysis: Correction or the End of the Boom?

Some analysts viewed the sell-off as overblown — a sign of froth coming off an overstretched market rather than the end of the AI investment cycle. "This is a stock market that is trying to figure out the right valuations for the AI trade, which is grappling with a swift sentiment shift after the midterm elections," said Alex Guiliano, chief investment officer at New Jersey-based Resonate Wealth Partners. Economist and investor Ed Yardeni called the declines "just speed bumps" that could ultimately help keep the AI bubble from overheating.

Still, the scale of the reaction underscores how much of the market's value now hinges on continued AI capital spending — and how vulnerable it is to political risk. Voters' backlash against data centers, driven by concerns over water and energy use, has turned into a political force that now threatens the permitting and buildout pipeline for some of the largest technology companies, including Meta and Alphabet.

## What's Next

Much depends on how aggressively the incoming Congress and new state leaders move to scrutinize AI infrastructure projects. Until it becomes clearer how much data-center construction is truly threatened, volatility is likely to continue. The Fed's December rate decision is a fresh variable, and average Americans — many of whom own AI shares through retirement accounts — remain exposed to a prolonged rout. For now, markets are in a holding pattern, waiting to see whether the political shift translates into real constraints on the AI boom.
