# Brussels Eateries Fight No-Shows With Deposits, Blacklists

Brussels restaurateurs, increasingly fed up with customers who reserve a table and never arrive, are turning to deposits, credit-card blocks and even informal blacklists to protect their margins. The measures range from the mundane to the legally dubious, and they reflect a deeper erosion of booking etiquette that the sector says is getting worse every year.

The issue was thrust into public view by Philippe Limbourg, owner of the restaurant Gueuleton in Sint-Pieters-Woluwe, who took to Facebook to publicly name and shame customers who failed to honour their reservations. "Congratulations to Davinia W., Hugo B. and Amory B.! This week you join the very exclusive club of customers on the no-show blacklist," he wrote, as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/07/van-voorschot-tot-blacklist-restaurants-gaan-strijd-aan-tegen-n/) reported.

## An expensive problem

Empty tables are not just an annoyance. They are direct lost revenue, compounded by staff costs and food wasted on preparation. Limbourg estimates his restaurant loses up to 8,000 euros in turnover every month because of no-shows. "When five customers don't come, the loss easily amounts to around 500 euros," he told [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/lifestyle/food/2026/11/03/face-a-la-pratique-du-no-show-des-restaurateurs-sorganisent-la-liste-de-clients-blacklistes-circule-tant-a-bruxelles-quen-wallonie-HHARBRCM3BAG5H7IBTOJJNCOHQ/).

The scale of the problem is borne out by the reservation platform Zenchef, the largest in Belgium. According to Benelux manager Paul Slootmans, no-shows rose 40 percent between 2022 and 2025, with the steepest increase in the most recent year, and the trend for 2026 confirms the pattern. "The problem is significant especially in big cities like Antwerp, Brussels and also Bruges," Slootmans told [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/40-procent-meer-no-shows-in-restaurants-zo-lossen-deze-chefs-dat-op-mensen-weten-dat-ze-bij-ons-altijd-wel-een-plekje-vinden~a80a603c/). "In cities there are three times more no-shows than elsewhere in Belgium."

Fine dining feels it most acutely. Zenchef data show gastronomic restaurants record a no-show rate about 1.7 times higher than casual establishments, a function of fixed menus, limited covers and preparation that must be calibrated to exact numbers. "Even with a credit-card guarantee, where we reserve an amount in advance, people still don't come," said Tatiana Litvine, manager of La Villa Lorraine and La Villa in the Sky. "I see that as a lack of respect. That's why we keep a list: anyone with a no-show in one of our restaurants goes on it."

## The blacklist problem

The most controversial response is the informal blacklist. Limbourg's personal list contains about 30 names, which he exchanges informally with some 20 like-minded restaurateurs across Brussels and Wallonia, producing a combined list of roughly 100 bad customers. Those on the list risk having reservations refused at multiple establishments.

David Debin, owner of the pasta restaurant Ricotta & Parmesan and vice-chair of Horeca Brussels, told [BRUZZ](https://www.bruzz.be/actua/economie/van-voorschot-tot-blacklist-restaurants-gaan-strijd-aan-tegen-no-shows-2026-11-07) that sharing such lists between businesses is, purely legally, not permitted under GDPR privacy rules, though keeping one's own list is in principle allowed. "But we fully understand that colleague Limbourg has had enough, because we all experience this ourselves as restaurateurs," he said. "It is happening more and more and it is a major concern for the sector. Respect has disappeared, and not only in hospitality. I hear the same complaints from hairdressers and doctors."

Debin points to a particular habit that compounds the damage: some customers book several restaurants for the same evening and only decide on the night, leaving the others with empty tables. "Frivolous and disrespectful, because it means the other restaurants are left with an empty table."

## From walk-ins to credit-card blocks

Beyond blacklists, restaurants are experimenting with a range of measures. Some have simply abandoned reservations altogether. Au Vieux Saint Martin on the Sablon runs its kitchen non-stop from midday to 11 pm and takes no advance bookings except for a separate room. Debin notes this works only where there is heavy footfall; for restaurants outside the city centre, it is far harder.

Others enforce punctuality. At Le Tournant in Ixelles, a table is guaranteed only for 15 minutes past the reservation time, and Debin's Ricotta & Parmesan and Rooftop 58 use the same method. "But it is not always simple," he conceded. "If customers arrive half an hour late and have lost their table, they often react angrily."

Deposits are another route. The Anderlecht grill restaurant Le Becue charges variable deposits, from 15 euros for a table for two up to 60 euros for a table of eight to 12. A variant is the credit-card block, similar to a hotel-room reservation: Old Boy in Ixelles blocks 40 euros per customer, charged only in the event of a no-show. At the gastronomic end, Humus x Hortense, which holds a Michelin star, deducts 230 euros per person from the card if a reservation is not honoured, and La Villa in the Sky uses credit-card blocking as well.

Debin cautions that deposits have their limits. "For a Michelin-starred restaurant that can work, but at a classic brasserie customers won't accept it," he said. "Yet oddly enough they do accept having to pay for a festival ticket a year in advance. It's a matter of mentality. Unfortunately there is no easy solution to the problem."

## A wider erosion of commitment

The no-show surge fits a broader pattern of reservation fatigue, as some chefs describe it: customers dislike booking far ahead, and instant online platforms make it frictionless to reserve at several places and decide later. Sam D'Huyvetter, chef of Bask and Taberna Bask in Ghent, sees the other side of the coin. "I fully understand that people feel like eating out on the night itself," he told HLN. "In hospitality we could be a bit more flexible about that again." His restaurants keep only 60 percent of tables available for reservation, releasing the rest in the week before or on the night, a system that leaves them rarely troubled by no-shows.

Not everyone can afford that flexibility. Marie Van Cauwelaert, chef of Spice Bank in Antwerp, recounted a particularly galling case. "Last week people came by at noon to ask if they could please get a table that evening, and in the evening they didn't come," she said. "I can't accept that. Everything was ready for them and I had to say no to other people."

## What to watch

The measures raise as many questions as they answer. The informal blacklists sprouting between restaurateurs sit in a legal grey zone: a business may keep its own record, but sharing customer names across independent establishments without a legal basis is problematic under GDPR. No enforcement action or formal regulator ruling has been reported, so the practice is likely to continue until tested, or until a customer challenges it.

For now, the sector's options look like a patchwork: deposits where customers accept them, walk-ins where location allows, and strict cut-off times everywhere in between. What unites them is a shared diagnosis from Debin: the problem is less about technology than about a fraying sense of commitment. With margins thin and costs rising, Belgian restaurateurs are no longer willing to absorb the cost of a customer who simply never turns up, and they are increasingly prepared to say so, publicly and by name.
