# Trump Names Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller to Top Staff Role

President Donald Trump announced Friday that White House chief of staff Susie Wiles would be leaving her role this week and would be replaced by deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/live-blog/rump-taps-stephen-miller-chief-staff-wiles-cabinet-midterm-elections-rcna607914). The reshuffle installs one of the administration's most prominent figures atop the senior staff as the president recalibrates his political strategy — just days after Democrats flipped both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections.

The announcement was made via Trump's Truth Social platform. Miller, one of the president's longest-serving advisers and a principal architect of his hardline immigration agenda, previously served as deputy chief of staff.

## A Defiant Pick After a Defeat

The decision follows midterm elections in which Democrats flipped the House and Senate, with NBC News projecting Democrats could hold 259 House seats next year — far beyond the 218 needed for a majority. In the Senate, Democratic candidates also flipped seats in South Carolina, Texas, Maine and Ohio, cementing a Democratic majority in the upper chamber.

The appointment is widely read as a signal of confrontation rather than conciliation. As chief of staff, Miller will manage the president's staff, schedule, and access to the Oval Office — placing him at the center of the coming dynamic with a Democrat-led Congress expected to assert its oversight authority.

## What It Means

The chief of staff post is traditionally the senior-most position in the White House, responsible for imposing order on a sprawling staff and controlling who reaches the president. Elevating Miller — a loyalist known for ideological combat — signals that Trump intends to fight rather than accommodate the new congressional majority.

The move also carries political risk. Miller's prominence on immigration could energize the Democratic base that just delivered the president a rebuke, while complicating any effort to strike bipartisan deals. Yet Trump has hinted at openness to cooperation: in a preview clip from a CBS "60 Minutes" interview set to air Sunday, he said he could go six months without insulting Democrats if it meant getting a deal done. Asked whether he could refrain from insults for six months to secure a deal, Trump responded, "Very easily."

## What to Watch

The two developments — a combative staffing choice and a stated willingness to cut deals — have left the administration's direction genuinely uncertain. In the coming weeks, attention will focus on how quickly the new Congress moves to launch investigations, how Miller staffs and structures the White House operation, and whether the administration pursues deals with Democrats or digs in for confrontation. Trump has also signaled that more Cabinet departures could follow, telling NBC News in comments tied to the "60 Minutes" interview that further changes are possible — suggesting the reshuffle may not be over.
