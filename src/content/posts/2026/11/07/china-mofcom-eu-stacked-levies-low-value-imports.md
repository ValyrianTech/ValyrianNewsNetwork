# China Voices High Concern Over EU Stacked Levies

China's Ministry of Commerce said it is "highly concerned" about the European Union's newly stacked charges on low-value imported goods, urging Brussels to weigh the impact of its rules and keep the door open to cooperation on cross-border e-commerce. The response, delivered by ministry spokesperson Huang Ling at a regular press conference on 5 November and carried by [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20261105/59dfce3d7424459b8fb7d02fea97f9e2/c.html), marks Beijing's first public comment on a fee regime that took full effect four days earlier and now hits a trade flow overwhelmingly dominated by Chinese platforms.

## What the EU Is Now Charging

The dispute centers on two distinct charges the bloc applies to parcels worth 150 euros or less arriving from outside the EU. Since 1 July 2026, a 3-euro temporary customs duty has been applied per item, replacing the long-standing de minimis duty exemption that had shielded low-value consignments from tariffs. According to [European Commission guidance](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/news/guidance-and-legal-text-temporary-flat-fee-low-value-imports-which-will-apply-until-1-july-2028-2026-06-08_en), the flat fee is levied per tariff-classified item rather than per parcel, runs under Council Regulation (EU) 2026/382, and will remain in force until 1 July 2028, when normal tariff rates are due to apply.

On top of that duty sits a 2-euro per-item customs and handling fee, in force since 1 November 2026 and formally confirmed in the Commission's updated e-commerce guidance in October. The Commission classifies it as a processing and compliance fee, not a customs duty, intended to cover the cost of customs clearance and product inspections. Together, the two charges are what Chinese state and industry media describe as stacked levies, a framing reflected in MOFCOM's statement.

The EU's rationale rests on scale and compliance. The Commission [estimates](https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/news/e-commerce-150-eur-customs-duty-exemption-threshold-be-removed-2026-2025-11-13_en) that roughly 5.9 billion low-value items were shipped directly from third countries to EU consumers in 2025 without paying customs duties, and that EU-wide inspections in 2025 found over 60% of checked cosmetics, toys, electronics and similar products failed EU standards. Brussels frames the measures as non-discriminatory and designed to level the playing field between direct-mail e-commerce and traditional retail.

## Beijing's Calibrated Pushback

Huang's remarks were measured but pointed. "Cross-border e-commerce, as a new business form and new model of international trade, provides diversified choices for global enterprises and consumers," she said, adding that China hopes the EU will "cautiously assess the impact of the related policies and create favorable conditions for China-EU cooperation in e-commerce and other fields."

The tone is notable for what it omits: no threat of retaliation, no announcement of a WTO complaint, and no reciprocal measure. That restraint is consistent with Beijing's preference for keeping trade friction in negotiation channels, but it also reflects the asymmetry of the dispute, in which Chinese platforms are the primary target and Brussels holds the regulatory pen.

## Why the Charges Bite

The commercial stakes for China are substantial. Chinese data compiled by [163.com](https://c.m.163.com/news/a/L37OUS7R053469RG.html) puts the volume of sub-150-euro parcels entering the EU in 2024 at 4.6 billion, of which roughly 91% originated in China, flowing mainly through AliExpress, Temu and SHEIN. The new regime also shifts compliance burdens directly onto sellers: e-commerce platforms can now be designated as the importer of record, and Product Identifiers became mandatory from 1 November to improve traceability.

The effects are already visible. Swedish customs data, cited by the [EU-China Chamber of Commerce](http://www.ccceu.eu/2026-08/11/c_5089.htm), showed parcels from outside the EU to Swedish consumers fell nearly 70% year-on-year in July 2026, the first month the 3-euro duty applied, drawing hundreds of consumer complaints. One Stockholm resident told Swedish Radio it was strange that ordinary people buying small things are restricted, while noting that governments and companies also source from China.

## Analysis: A Test Case for Trade De-escalation

The e-commerce parcel dispute is a compact illustration of the wider 2026 China-EU trade dynamic: friction that is real, growing, and yet still managed rather than escalated. The Commission insists the rules are origin-neutral, and formally they are. But with more than nine in ten affected parcels originating in China, the measures are widely read in both capitals as targeting Temu, SHEIN and AliExpress, and Beijing knows it.

The more consequential question is where the fee level goes next. European textile and industry bodies have lobbied to raise the handling fee from 2 euros to 10, arguing the current level does not cover customs costs. A five-fold increase would sharply raise per-parcel costs for Chinese direct-mail sellers (Chinese industry analysis estimates the combined duty, fee and VAT already add roughly 5 to 8 euros per parcel) and could push Beijing toward a firmer response.

## What to Watch

Three signals will shape the next phase. First, whether MOFCOM moves beyond an expression of concern to concrete action, such as a WTO challenge or a reciprocal measure, particularly if the fee rises. Second, how platforms restructure, with EU-based warehousing and local entities likely to absorb more volume and blunt the charges' impact. Third, whether member-state and consumer backlash, visible in Sweden's parcel collapse and complaints, softens EU resolve or is instead cited as proof the loophole is closing.

For now, Beijing has chosen counsel over confrontation. Whether that holds depends on Brussels' next move on the fee.
