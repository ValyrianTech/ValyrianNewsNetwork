# China Condemns First US Sanctions on Firms Over Cuba

China's Ministry of Commerce on November 7 condemned the United States for imposing sanctions on two Chinese companies over their alleged support for Cuba's military, demanding that Washington "correct the erroneous practice as soon as possible" and reserving the right to retaliate. The action marks the first time the US has sanctioned Chinese firms specifically on Cuba-related grounds, opening a new front in an already tense bilateral relationship.

## A First-of-Its-Kind Designation

The dispute traces back to November 5, when the US State Department announced a package targeting eight entities, two individuals, and five vessels as blocked property, according to an [official fact sheet](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesman/2026/11/further-sanctions-on-the-cuban-regimes-foreign-military-support-and-illicit-revenue-networks-fact-sheet/). The designations were made under Executive Order 14404, the Cuba sanctions framework that President Trump signed in May 2026.

Among those listed were two China-based firms: the China National Electronic Import and Export Company (CEIEC), a state-owned enterprise that exports defense electronics, and Beijing H&C Technologies Co., Ltd, a private subsidiary of China Electronics Technology Group Corporation that sells surveillance and security equipment. The State Department alleges both firms worked with the Cuban arms procurement entity Sociedad Mercantil Duna S.A. to supply components and equipment for Cuba's Ministry of Revolutionary Armed Forces in 2026.

On the same day, the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control extended the order's reach to four additional Cuban economic sectors — manufacturing, technology, electronics, and marine.

## Beijing's Rebuke

In a formal written statement reproduced by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34425625), a MOFCOM spokesperson framed the designations as improper extraterritorial overreach. "The US, on the grounds of involvement of a third party, has taken unilateral sanctions against Chinese companies, which violates international law and basic norms of international relations. China firmly opposes this," the spokesperson said.

The ministry went further, accusing Washington of a broader pattern of economic pressure. "Recently, the US has escalated sanctions and suppression against China, intensively introducing multiple discriminatory restrictive measures, seriously harming the rights and interests of Chinese companies, departing from the important consensus reached by the two heads of state, and undermining the stability of China-US economic and trade relations."

The statement concluded with a warning: "China urges the US to correct the erroneous practice as soon as possible, and reserves the right to take necessary measures to safeguard its legitimate rights and interests."

The response followed comments from Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun a day earlier, who told a press conference that China's cooperation with Cuba is "open and aboveboard." According to [Global Times](https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202611/1373795.shtml), Guo said Beijing "firmly opposes illegal unilateral sanctions and so-called 'long-arm jurisdiction' that have no basis in international law and are not authorized by the UN Security Council," and vowed to "take all necessary measures to resolutely protect the legitimate rights and interests of Chinese enterprises."

## The Legal Architecture Behind the Move

E.O. 14404, issued May 1, 2026, built on a national emergency declared earlier that year and created a new list-based sanctions regime operating alongside the longstanding Cuban Assets Control Regulations. As [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/06/us-expands-cuba-sanctions) explained in a legal analysis, the order targets those operating in key Cuban sectors — energy, defense, metals and mining, financial services, and security — and notably expands secondary sanctions risk to foreign financial institutions.

Crucially, the order authorizes designating foreign persons who provide material support to the Cuban government. That authority is what allowed Washington to reach Chinese suppliers, a step that earlier Cuba sanctions confined largely to Cuban, Russian, and Venezuelan actors.

The broader package also targeted Cuba's oil-shipping network and state-owned import entities, and named Any Rodriguez Lastres, daughter of the sanctioned GAESA president — a signal that Washington intends to pursue family networks to prevent evasion.

## Analysis: A New Pressure Point

The designation lands at a delicate moment. As the [Associated Press reported](https://www.usnews.com/news/politics/articles/2026-11-05/us-escalates-economic-campaign-against-cuba-and-targets-china-based-suppliers), Secretary of State Marco Rubio cast the campaign as aimed at a ruling elite "hoarding the island's energy, shipping, and technology resources for its own enrichment." The Cuba action comes amid heightened US-China friction and follows other 2026 disputes over sanctions tied to Russia and Xinjiang-related entity listings.

For Beijing, the core objection is legal and strategic. Chinese officials argue that sanctioning firms for dealings with a third country amounts to improper long-arm jurisdiction, a recurring talking point against US secondary sanctions. MOFCOM's explicit reservation of the right to take "necessary measures" raises the prospect of countermeasures under China's Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law or its Unreliable Entity List.

Both targeted firms fit a profile already familiar to US regulators — CEIEC was previously sanctioned by the Treasury in 2020 over support for the Maduro regime, and Beijing H&C operates in surveillance and security exports, a sector that has drawn sustained American scrutiny.

## What to Watch

Whether Beijing moves from rhetoric to concrete retaliation is the central question. So too is whether more Chinese firms will be swept up as E.O. 14404's sectoral reach expands, and how the designations affect China-Cuba defense trade going forward. For now, the episode demonstrates how a Cuba-focused sanctions program has become a vehicle for broader US-China economic pressure — and how quickly a regional policy can become a global flashpoint.
