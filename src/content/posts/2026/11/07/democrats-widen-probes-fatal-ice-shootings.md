# Democrats to Widen Probes of Fatal ICE Shootings

Empowered by sweeping midterm victories that handed them control of Congress, Democratic lawmakers are preparing to expand congressional investigations into fatal shootings by federal immigration agents — a push that will test how far the legislative branch can go in holding U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement and Customs and Border Protection accountable for their use of force.

With majorities in both chambers, Democrats can now issue subpoenas compelling testimony from top Trump administration officials and demand internal documents, escalating scrutiny that until now had been limited to symbolic "shadow hearings," [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/07/nx-s1-6029659/democrats-investigate-fatal-ice-shootings).

## The Deaths That Galvanized a Backlash

The investigation push follows a string of killings that turned ICE enforcement into one of the most politically charged issues of the 2026 campaign. During the Minneapolis operation known as "Operation Metro Surge," federal agents shot and killed two U.S. citizens weeks apart: Renée Good, a mother of three, on January 7, and Alex Pretti, a 37-year-old Veterans Affairs ICU nurse, on January 24.

The Trace, a nonprofit newsroom tracking gun violence, has [documented](https://www.thetrace.org/2025/12/immigration-ice-shootings-guns-tracker/) at least nine people killed in shootings connected to federal immigration enforcement since Trump took office again in 2025, along with dozens of nonfatal shootings and incidents in which agents drew weapons on bystanders, protesters and journalists.

Video evidence in the Pretti case became a flashpoint. As multiple news organizations reviewed the footage, Pretti was holding a cell phone when agents confronted him; one agent removed a gun from his holster roughly a second before another opened fire, and two agents fired at least ten rounds in about five seconds. The administration had initially described Pretti as an agitator and, without evidence, a domestic terrorist — characterizations that drew criticism even from Republican gun-rights groups.

Federal officials have given little indication that they are making progress on investigating the deaths. The Department of Homeland Security did not respond to NPR's request for the status of those investigations.

## What Subpoena Power Unlocks

The midterm results changed the calculus. Democrats' victories — driven in significant part by public backlash to the raids and shootings — mean committee gavels and subpoena authority now rest in their hands.

That could mean compelling testimony from figures at the center of the deportation strategy, including White House Border Czar Tom Homan, former Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, former Border Patrol Commander Gregory Bovino, White House Chief of Staff Stephen Miller and current DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin. Lawmakers can also demand internal records such as use-of-force reports, training materials and field orders.

"They can dissect these individual shootings, and really unravel the story of what happened," said Naureen Shah, who leads the ACLU's immigration policy work. She argued the new authority lets Congress press top decision-makers on fundamental questions: What orders were given to officers in the field? What was their training? Who decided to portray the victims as threats?

"It's like we can turn the faucet on and actually find out so much more," Shah said. "How do you have that reckoning if you don't actually have the ability to force people to explain themselves?"

Democratic leaders have signaled they intend to move quickly. Rep. Jamie Raskin of Maryland, expected to chair the House Judiciary Committee, said Congress will use "every tool" at its disposal and that it is time for ICE to "start answering to the American people." Rep. Robert Garcia of California, in line to lead the Oversight Committee, offered a two-word message to the administration — "Lawyer Up" — in a [statement](https://oversightdemocrats.house.gov/news/press-releases/incoming-oversight-chairman-robert-garcia-statement-after-democrats-secure-majority-in-the-us-house-of-representatives) after Democrats secured the House. Rep. Bennie Thompson of Mississippi, expected to chair the Homeland Security Committee, [demanded](https://democrats-homeland.house.gov/news/correspondence/thompson-demands-dhs-comply-with-all-document-preservation-requirements) that DHS "comply with all document preservation requirements."

## The Limits of a Majority

For all the new leverage, Democrats face hard constraints. Undoing or clawing back ICE funding would require a veto-proof majority they do not have, and a president who has shown little appetite for holding federal agents to account remains in office.

"You still have a president who has shown a complete lack of interest in accountability for federal agents and in fact, a great deal of knee-jerk defensiveness about their conduct," said Rachel Moran, a professor at the University of St. Thomas School of Law.

The White House has framed the coming investigations as political theater. A spokesperson told NPR that if Democrats "choose to pursue sham investigations and radical left policies, they are choosing to work against the American people," adding that "we still control the federal government and all of its powers." DHS general counsel James Percival dismissed the effort as "political shenanigans" while saying the agency was prepared to "deal with this nonsense."

Those confrontations are likely to end up in court, with subpoena fights and possible contempt proceedings testing the boundaries of congressional authority against executive resistance.

## A Reckoning — or a Stalemate?

Even short of legislative victories, Democrats argue that building a public record has value. Evidence surfaced through hearings could feed state criminal investigations. Minnesota officials have been battling the federal government over access to evidence in the Good and Pretti killings, and a fuller congressional record could strengthen those efforts.

The political backdrop is unmistakable. Polling this year found that most Americans believed ICE had gone [too far](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/05/nx-s1-5699413/poll-trump-ice-immigration-economy-approval), and the midterm backlash was especially pronounced in Texas, where Democrats swept statewide races. For the families at the center of the story, the stakes are deeply personal.

Brent Ganger, Renée Good's brother, said learning more about his sister's death would be painful to hear, "but I think it's also super important for us to understand what happened that day. I don't want history to keep repeating itself."

He described watching, after his sister was killed, as agents went on to kill others — a sequence he thought her death might have prevented. On election night, he said, he was scrolling through his phone when he saw a photo of his sister's memorial, now covered in "I Voted" stickers.

"I was very grateful to see the repudiation of this administration from the country," he said. "It felt very supportive of my entire family."

What comes next will hinge on whether subpoenas produce testimony and documents — or a prolonged legal war. Either way, the deaths in Minneapolis have moved from tragedy to a test of whether Congress can force the machinery of federal enforcement to explain itself.
