# He Lifeng, Bessent Hold Call as China Airs Trade Concerns

Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng held a video call with US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer on the evening of November 6, Beijing time, in the latest high-level engagement between the world's two largest economies. According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/leaders/20261107/ebbfb492652949189aee6a26a748bf1a/c.html), the two sides conducted "candid, in-depth and constructive" exchanges on economic and trade issues of mutual concern, with China expressing "solemn concern" over recent US trade-restriction measures against Chinese goods.

The call underscores a relationship that remains simultaneously cooperative and contested: both sides are keeping the consultation channel open even as they press their respective leverage, and a looming truce deadline now sets the calendar for any progress.

## What Was Said

Beijing's readout, published by Xinhua on the morning of November 7, framed the conversation around implementing the "important consensus" reached by the Chinese and US heads of state at their meetings in Beijing and Washington, as well as the outcomes of prior economic and trade consultations.

The two sides agreed, under the strategic guidance of both presidents, to continue giving full play to the China-US economic and trade consultation mechanism, to strengthen cooperation, properly manage differences, and push relations toward "sustained, stable and sound development." Beijing said the goal is to help build "a China-US constructive strategic stability relationship based on respect, fairness and reciprocity."

Notably, the Xinhua report does not name the "Busan trade agreement" directly, though the US side did. Washington's readouts were more conditional and enforcement-oriented.

## Washington's Message: Deliver on Commitments

Bessent, posting on X, said he and Greer spoke with He about the US-China economic and trade relationship and the Busan trade agreement. As [Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20261107-9962035) reported, Bessent said he stressed that it is "crucial for China to fully and immediately honor its commitments" under the Busan agreement, expressed concern about China's "recent moves," and made clear that "the pace of trade negotiations will depend on concrete results."

Greer, in a statement issued by the US Trade Representative's office, said he stressed that China must take concrete actions to implement past commitments, "especially purchases of US agricultural products and the supply of rare earths and other critical minerals." He said the Trump administration will continue to seek a balanced trade relationship with China, monitoring China's delivery on past commitments while securing lasting benefits for American farmers, ranchers, manufacturers and workers.

The divergence in tone is itself telling. Beijing's framing is cooperative and mechanism-driven; Washington's is transactional and contingent on delivery. The two readouts agree on the basic facts of the call but emphasize different priorities.

## The Friction Beneath the Diplomacy

The call comes amid fresh tensions. On November 5, China's Ministry of Commerce announced it would continue to levy anti-subsidy duties on n-propanol (NPA) imported from the United States for five years, effective November 6. The same day, at its regular press conference, the ministry said China is open to expanding purchases of US agricultural products, but only on the precondition that the previously agreed "300-billion-for-300-billion" reciprocal tariff-reduction arrangement is implemented, and it called on Washington to jointly lower tariffs and reduce trade barriers.

On the US side, Reuters reported in early October that the Trump administration had slowed approval of export licenses for aircraft parts to China and limited parts supplied to Chinese planemakers, seeking to turn China's dependence on American aviation suppliers into trade leverage. Late in October, the US Treasury also unveiled proposed rules expanding outbound investment restrictions.

## Background: The Busan Truce

The underlying framework is the one-year trade truce reached in October 2025 in Busan, South Korea, where President Xi Jinping and President Donald Trump agreed to a pause in the tariff escalation. In September 2026, during Xi's visit to Washington, the two countries agreed to cut tariffs on $30 billion of each other's non-sensitive goods and to extend the truce, originally due to expire November 10, 2026, to January 10, 2027, buying time for further negotiations.

That "300-for-300" arrangement is now the pivot of the dispute. Beijing ties agricultural purchases to its implementation; Washington ties the pace of talks to China's delivery on agriculture and rare earths.

## Analysis: Managing Differences, Not Resolving Them

The call reflects a "managing differences" phase rather than a breakthrough. Both sides reaffirmed the consultation mechanism while restating firm and differing positions. Beijing's stated grievance — US trade restrictions — and Washington's stated demands — agricultural purchases and rare-earth supply — are the same issues that have defined the relationship for more than a year.

For Beijing, the emphasis on a "constructive strategic stability relationship" signals a preference for predictable, structured engagement over confrontation. For Washington, the insistence that "the pace of trade negotiations will depend on concrete results" signals that the truce is not open-ended: the January 10, 2027 deadline is the operative calendar, and further extension is likely to be conditioned on verifiable Chinese action.

## What to Watch

Three questions now matter most. First, whether the "300-for-300" reciprocal tariff cut is actually implemented, unlocking Chinese agricultural purchases. Second, whether China's rare-earth and critical-mineral supply meets Washington's expectations. Third, whether either side escalates with new restrictions before the January 10 truce deadline.

For now, the channel remains open — a modest but meaningful signal that both capitals still prefer negotiation to rupture. Whether that preference translates into delivery on the ground is the test the coming weeks will set.
