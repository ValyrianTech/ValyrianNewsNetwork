# Belgian Archdiocese Urges Churches to Cut Energy Costs

The Archdiocese of Mechelen-Brussels has sent a letter to all parishes in its territory recommending measures to curb soaring energy bills, including bundling services into the most efficient churches, lowering thermostats and asking congregants to donate more. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/07/aartsbisdom-mechelen-brussel-roept-op-om-te-besparen-op-energie/), the guidance reflects mounting financial pressure on parishes and church fabriques — the local bodies that manage church buildings and their budgets — as energy prices remain high.

## Context

The intervention from Belgium's metropolitan archdiocese is the latest sign that religious institutions across Europe are being forced to adapt their operations to a sustained period of elevated energy costs. Many historic church buildings are poorly insulated and feature high ceilings, making them expensive to heat, particularly during the winter months. Those costs fall directly on parishes and the fabriques that own and maintain the buildings.

The archdiocese, one of Belgium's eight Catholic dioceses and its metropolitan see, oversees two cathedrals — St. Rumbold's in Mechelen and St. Michael and St. Gudula in Brussels. Its guidance was distributed internally to parishes rather than issued as a public press release, underscoring that the recommendations are meant as practical operational advice rather than a headline announcement.

## Key Recommendations

The letter lays out a series of concrete measures aimed at trimming consumption without disrupting community and liturgical life. Among them, VRT NWS reports, parishes are advised to choose the most energy-efficient church or churches within a single pastoral zone for weddings and funerals. Sunday services, meanwhile, could be bundled into one church so that parishes avoid heating multiple buildings at once.

Where a church has access to a chapel or a room that is easier to heat, the archdiocese recommends holding winter services there by preference. Parishes are further encouraged to lower the heating temperature by one degree or more, to draw up a general energy plan for each pastoral zone, and to switch to more LED lighting.

## The Donation Appeal

Perhaps the most notable element of the guidance is its financial dimension. The archdiocese wants parishes to encourage congregants to give more money during services — for instance, by communicating how much it concretely costs to heat a church over a single weekend. The specific euro figure is framed as a communication tool to help worshippers understand the real cost of maintaining their buildings, though it is not quantified in the reporting.

The appeal reflects a delicate balancing act for church leaders: seeking to keep religious and community activities running while asking an already-committed base of worshippers to shoulder a larger share of the financial burden.

## A Wider European Trend

The Belgian guidance echoes steps taken elsewhere in Europe. In October 2023, the German Diocese of Hildesheim became the first German diocese to recommend that parishes avoid heating church buildings altogether where possible, capping heating at 5 to 9 degrees Celsius when necessary and testing electric seat cushions to warm worshippers rather than the buildings themselves, as [Otheo reported](https://www.otheo.be/nieuws/kerken-niet-meer-verwarmd-duits-bisdom-test-elektrische-kussens).

That approach illustrates the range of options available to religious institutions, from modest efficiency tweaks to more radical changes in how sacred spaces are used during the coldest months. The Mechelen-Brussels recommendations sit toward the more moderate end of that spectrum, prioritizing consolidation and efficiency over abandoning heating entirely.

## Analysis and Implications

The guidance signals that the energy crisis is not only a household and industrial concern but one that reaches into the fabric of community life. Churches often serve as gathering points that extend well beyond weekly worship, and decisions about which buildings to heat and which services to consolidate can have knock-on effects for local communities, particularly in rural areas where a parish church may be a village's most significant shared space.

For the fabriques, the measures offer a measure of relief but do not address the underlying structural challenge: aging, inefficient buildings whose maintenance costs are unlikely to fall. The call for larger donations may help bridge shortfalls, but it also highlights the financial vulnerability of institutions that depend on voluntary giving.

## What's Next

How faithfully parishes implement the advice will likely vary by zone and by the condition of local buildings. The archdiocese has framed the letter as guidance rather than mandate, leaving individual parishes to weigh the trade-offs between cost savings and the accessibility and tradition of their worship spaces. What to watch in the months ahead is whether the recommendations translate into measurable savings — and whether other Belgian dioceses follow Mechelen-Brussels in issuing similar guidance of their own.
