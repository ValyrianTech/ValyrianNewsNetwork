# Huo Zhijing Named Head of Beijing People's Art Theatre

Huo Zhijing has been appointed deputy party secretary and director of the Beijing People's Art Theatre, one of China's most prestigious state theater institutions, replacing veteran actor Feng Yuanzheng, [The Paper reported](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34422015). The change was announced at a cadre meeting at the theatre on November 6, following a decision by Beijing's municipal party committee and government.

The appointment marks a return to an administrator-led model for the institution after four years under its first working actor to hold the top post, and it places a film-and-propaganda official at the helm of China's flagship spoken-drama company.

## A theatre at the heart of Chinese drama

Founded in 1952 under the directorship of playwright Cao Yu, the Beijing People's Art Theatre — known colloquially as "Ren Yi" — has long served as a showcase for modern Chinese spoken drama, or *huaju*. Its prestige is national, and its leadership appointments carry political as well as artistic weight. As a state institution tied to the municipal party and government apparatus, its top leadership is named through the standard cadre-announcement process rather than a commercial board decision.

According to The Paper, the November 6 meeting was attended by municipal leaders, officials from the Municipal Organization Department and the Municipal Propaganda Department, and members of the theatre's leadership team, mid-level cadres, and representatives of performers and staff.

## From vice director to the top job

Huo Zhijing is a familiar figure at the theatre. Public records show she previously served as vice director of the Beijing People's Art Theatre before moving to the Beijing Municipal Party Committee's Propaganda Department as deputy director and to the head of the Beijing Municipal Film Bureau.

In that role, she was also vice chair and secretary-general of the Beijing International Film Festival organizing committee, appearing at industry forums and speaking publicly on the city's film-consumption voucher programs — including a 33-million-yuan initiative in 2025. Her background suggests a director likely to emphasize media partnerships, film-theatre crossover, and outreach beyond the traditional stage.

## Feng Yuanzheng's tenure ends

Feng Yuanzheng joined the theatre in 1985 and, in 1989, was invited to study acting at the drama department of the West Berlin Higher School of the Arts, where he specialized in the Grotowski method. He built a celebrated stage career in productions including *Tea House*, *Football Club*, *Soulmate*, *The Player*, and *Sima Qian*.

He was formally appointed director on September 8, 2022 — the theatre's fifth leader in its roughly 70-year history and its first working actor to hold the title. During his tenure, he worked to strengthen performer training by restoring the theatre's trainee-class model and cooperating with acting graduates from the 2019 and 2022 cohorts, and he oversaw the theatre's 2026 international theater season.

He had also weighed in on the place of technology in the arts, remarking that artificial intelligence, however advanced, "can never dominate people."

## What the change signals

The shift from an artist-director to a culture-administrator-director fits a broader pattern in China's state cultural sector, in which leaders are selected for a combination of artistic familiarity and administrative, propaganda, or media experience. Huo Zhijing's prior service inside the theatre gives her institutional knowledge, while her years in the film bureau and propaganda department bring a policy and outreach orientation that may reshape programming and audience strategy.

The announcement did not state a reason for Feng Yuanzheng's departure. Retirement eligibility had been raised in discussions around his 2022 elevation, when he was widely reported to have planned to retire before being named director.

## What to watch

Observers of China's theater scene will be watching for signals of Huo Zhijing's programming direction — particularly whether her film-industry background translates into closer collaboration between the capital's stage and screen sectors. Her early public appearances and the theatre's next season announcements will offer the first indications of how the institution's artistic and political mandates will be balanced under its new leadership.

For now, the change is procedural in tone but symbolically significant: at an institution where a working actor once broke the mold, leadership has returned to a seasoned cultural administrator.
