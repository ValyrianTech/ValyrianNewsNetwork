# Activist Helps Block $5B Maryland Data Center

A grassroots activist who lived less than a mile from the site of a proposed $5 billion data center campus in Prince George's County, Maryland, became an unlikely force in pausing the project — a case study in the growing national backlash against hyperscale computing facilities.

Taylor Frazier McCollum launched a petition that gathered more than 25,000 signatures, helping to pressure county officials into a series of moratoriums on data center development, [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/07/nx-s1-6007770/data-centers-activist-moratorium) reported. The campaign culminated in a two-year countywide moratorium approved by the Prince George's County Council on July 8, 2026.

## A vacant mall and a $5 billion plan

The project would have risen on the former Landover Mall site, a roughly 80-acre expanse that has sat mostly empty since the mall closed in 2002. In 2024, the developer that built the original mall won county approval for a data center campus totaling about 4.1 million square feet.

For Frazier McCollum, the news landed like a personal affront. She had been living in her grandfather's home nearby after an eviction from her apartment.

"I was like, 'Oh, hell no,'" she told NPR. "Why would you want to bring water-polluting, diesel-generating data centers right down the street from where people live, work, play? It just didn't make sense to me."

Her petition and organizing helped spark widely attended protests and drew together an ideologically broad coalition — socialists, environmentalists, residents angry about rising electric bills, and neighbors new to activism. Local media dubbed her "the Landover girl."

## From petition to policy

The pressure built through 2025. In September of that year, dozens rallied against the project ahead of a county task force meeting, with several council members speaking out against it, [WTOP](https://wtop.com/maryland/2025/09/dozens-rally-against-data-center-project-at-abandoned-mall-site/) reported.

"Yes, we need to develop the site, but are we willing to take the bottom of the barrel offer from a company that cares nothing about our community?" Frazier McCollum asked the crowd at the time.

County Executive Aisha Braveboy imposed a temporary moratorium on data center permit reviews that September, extending it several times to give a county task force time to issue recommendations. The council's two-year moratorium, sponsored by Chair Krystal Oriadha and member Tom Dernoga, followed. Seven members voted in favor, one abstained, and three were absent, [The Baltimore Banner](https://www.thebanner.com/politics-power/local-government/prince-georges-county-council-data-center-moratorium-CKGTDP5ZNFBFRNF3NGTTA5UARI/) reported.

Oriadha framed the measure as a pause rather than a permanent prohibition. She supports a full ban on data centers but wanted to halt the zoning process while the county debates the facilities' future.

"It's important we continue to have those conversations," she said. "I think this moratorium is a compromise that allows that to happen."

## An economic tug-of-war

The fight exposes a difficult tension in Prince George's County, a majority-Black jurisdiction grappling with fiscal strain. Six Flags America closed in 2025, the Washington Commanders announced plans to move to Washington, D.C., and thousands of federal workers have lost jobs. The county faces a budget deficit.

Data centers promise tax revenue that officials badly need. Council member Wanika Fisher, who supports moving some projects forward, pressed that point.

"Our county has an economic future that we have to grapple with," she said. "I don't see where the money is coming from. I think we have to have a real conversation. And I'm not saying that a data center is the answer, but I'm saying it's worth the hard discussion."

Business leaders warned against the moratorium's length. Alexander Austin, president and CEO of the Prince George's County Chamber of Commerce, told the council, "A two-year moratorium is too long."

The July meeting also featured unusual theatrics: a group of pro-data-center speakers in matching neon "Face the Future" caps and T-shirts testified in support of the facilities. Some opponents questioned whether the group had been paid to appear.

"There's big industry," Oriadha said. "There are paid individuals that can come out here every day to speak, and they're paid by people that don't live here."

## A national movement takes shape

The Maryland fight is one front in a much wider conflict. Across the country, roughly 500 moratoriums on data centers have been imposed, according to one estimate cited by NPR, as communities weigh the energy, water, and land costs of the artificial intelligence boom against the promise of tax revenue and jobs.

For her part, Frazier McCollum has broadened her activism beyond Landover. With support from longtime environmental organizer Staci Hartwell and others, she has carried her message to Black communities resisting data center growth in Atlanta, Memphis, and as far away as Zambia.

"I feel like so many people are depending on this fight to be successful," she said. "Not just for myself, but even across the country, to set precedent for other people that are fighting against hyperscale data centers."

Her father, Lacy McCollum Jr., casts the struggle in civil rights terms.

"We're talking about the right to water, the right to clean air, the right to a place to live. That's everybody's fight, right?" he said. "We needed the Medgar Everses. We needed the Martin Luther Kings. We needed the Malcolm X's. People like Taylor, they jump out here so the people behind them get to make progress."

## What's next

The Landover project is paused, not dead. The two-year moratorium blocks the planning department from considering or approving data center applications until the council enacts regulations, leaving open the possibility that development could resume if the pause lifts.

Frazier McCollum now wants a full ban on the project and others like it. In the meantime, she is pitching alternative uses for the vacant site — a sports complex, affordable housing, or heritage tourism — while acknowledging that none would be as lucrative as a data center campus.

Some supporters have urged her to run for office. She says she wants to pay off debt, resolve traffic tickets, and find a home first, but does not rule it out.

"Now is not maybe the moment for it, but I do have aspirations to run for something," she said.
