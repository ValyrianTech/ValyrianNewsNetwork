# Politicians Slam Duchatelet Over Elderly Care Remarks

Belgian politicians from across the political spectrum have sharply condemned businessman and former senator Roland Duchatelet after he suggested the government could find budget savings by reconsidering expensive end-of-life care for the elderly. The remarks, made on the Play talk show 'De tafel van Ruben', triggered outrage from party leaders who described the proposal as "disgusting" and "completely crazy," according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/roland-duchatelet-ouderenzorg-besparen-begroting/).

The controversy erupted as Belgium's federal government searches for 10 billion euros in savings to bring the budget in order. One debated option is cutting the fiscal and social benefits enjoyed by professional football clubs and players—a route Duchatelet opposed.

## The 'Forbidden Discussion'

Instead of targeting football's tax advantages, Duchatelet proposed what he called the "forbidden discussion," arguing that the final year of life costs the social security system around 10,000 euros per person and that savings could be found by reconsidering costly end-of-life treatments as the population ages.

"I don't mean you should tell people they must die," Duchatelet said. "But when people are very old and would rather die, we keep giving them medication and operating on them. But that is a sacred cow."

He linked his proposal to an apparent savings potential of 10 billion euros, calculating "10,000 euros times 100,000 inhabitants." VRT NWS noted that the arithmetic is incorrect—the figure comes to 1 billion euros, not 10 billion.

## Cross-Party Outrage

CD&V chairman Sammy Mahdi delivered one of the sharpest rebukes, calling it "the most distasteful idea to get the budget in order" and drawing a comparison to authoritarian measures under Adolf Hitler.

"With all due respect, but how deranged can you be to make such statements?" Mahdi said in a video on Instagram. "It is simply disgusting."

Vooruit chairman Conner Rousseau, writing on Facebook, accused Duchatelet of lobbying to preserve tax breaks for professional footballers while proposing that people die earlier to sustain those benefits. "Are you serious, Roland? I almost fell off my chair," Rousseau wrote, calling the position "completely crazy."

Peter Mertens, a member of the Chamber for the PVDA, responded sarcastically: "If grandma would just want to die a year earlier, please!" He contrasted Duchatelet's appearance on Forbes' billionaires list this year—with an estimated fortune of 1.5 billion dollars—against the businessman's opposition to a wealth tax.

Gwendolyn Rutten, a Flemish Parliament member for Anders, described the remarks as nostalgia for "a time of classes and ranks" and "the horror of dehumanisation," calling it "a sad, powerless discourse."

## A Sensitive Debate Beneath the Outrage

While the reaction was overwhelmingly critical, the row also exposed a genuine and sensitive debate about how Belgium funds long-term care for its ageing population and how it approaches end-of-life treatment. Belgium has among the world's most liberal euthanasia laws, making discussions about end-of-life spending especially fraught.

Some voices offered a measure of nuance even while rejecting Duchatelet's framing. One social media commenter quoted by VRT NWS argued that the insinuation was "misplaced and immoral" but that the debate about extending life regardless of quality and cost is "legitimate in itself." Others agreed that patients who are exhausted and wish to die should not simply be kept on medication.

The episode also reflects a broader political dynamic in which Duchatelet's billionaire status and his advocacy for business-friendly tax regimes are turned against him by left and centre parties. Duchatelet has a history of provocative public statements, including a February 2026 appearance in which he made a claim about CO2 and climate change that was later fact-checked.

## What to Watch

As the federal government continues its search for 10 billion euros in savings, the underlying questions Duchatelet raised—how to balance fiscal sustainability with dignified care for the elderly—are unlikely to disappear, even if his specific proposal has been widely rejected. The political fallout may also intensify scrutiny of the tax advantages for professional football, the very benefits Duchatelet sought to defend.
