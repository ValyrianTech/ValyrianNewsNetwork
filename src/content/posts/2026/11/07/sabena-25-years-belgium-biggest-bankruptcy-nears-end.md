# Sabena: 25 Years On, Belgium's Biggest Bankruptcy Nears End

Exactly 25 years after the collapse of Sabena, the liquidation of Belgium's national airline — the largest corporate failure in the country's history — is finally approaching its closing chapter. Christian Van Buggenhout, the court-appointed curator who has overseen the wind-down since November 2001, says the case can be formally closed next year. "Next year we can really close it," he told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/faillissement-sabena-na-25-jaar-nu-echt-bijna-afgehandeld-zegt/).

The quarter-century saga has recovered more than a billion euros for creditors — a result far above what a conventional fire-sale would have yielded — and offers a rare case study in how to extract value from a corporate wreck rather than simply selling it off.

## A crown jewel that fell hard

Sabena, founded in 1923, was Belgium's flag carrier for most of the 20th century, a pioneer in aviation and a trendsetter on African routes, as a [VRT NWS retrospective](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/kijk/2026/10/23/faillissement-sabena-25-jaar-luchtvaart/) recounts. Yet the airline had struggled financially since the 1970s, surviving on repeated state support and a series of failed alliance attempts, according to [Wikipedia's account](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faillissement_van_Sabena) of the collapse.

The end came on 7 November 2001, when the Brussels commercial court declared Sabena bankrupt. The last flight, SN 690 from Cotonou via Abidjan, landed at Brussels Airport that day as employees formed a human chain around the aircraft. More than 7,500 people lost their jobs. The collapse was driven by chronic unprofitability, the failed partnership with Swissair — which held 49.5% and pulled the plug amid its own post-9/11 crisis — and the broader aviation downturn that followed the September 11 attacks.

Van Buggenhout was appointed curator that November and has held the role ever since.

## The slow road to recovery

The curator's defining choice was to keep businesses running rather than sell assets immediately. "You have to drain the cup to the bottom," he said of the deliberate, drawn-out approach.

The results are striking. Had everything been sold at once, only about €190 million would have been available to distribute. Today the pool stands at €1.05 billion.

The baggage-handling operation, valued at €3 million at the time of the bankruptcy, was continued and eventually sold for €55 million. Engines were leased out rather than dumped on the market, and a hotel in Kinshasa, Congo — the Memling — was operated for nearly 25 years before being sold at a strong price.

"A curator's task is to create added value, continue and restructure businesses," Van Buggenhout said. "That naturally takes a lot of time and means you are actually an entrepreneur, with all the risks that entails."

A parallel effort targeted the debt itself. The declared liabilities of just over €3 billion were whittled down through hundreds of court challenges contesting inflated creditor claims. "One of your tasks as curator is to contest those claims, because creditors often exaggerate," he said. The debt was eventually reduced to just over €1 billion.

## Where the money went

The largest share went to the people who lost their jobs. Some €500 million in employee severance has been paid out in full.

What remains are supplier debts: €600 million in total, of which €250 million has been repaid, leaving about €350 million outstanding. Van Buggenhout noted that it is unusual for creditors in a bankruptcy to recover anything at all.

"Practically everything has been sold," he said, "except the problem dossiers." A parcel of land in Congo is now before the Court of Cassation, at the finish line, and a procedure in Switzerland may yet yield further funds.

A few years ago, Van Buggenhout predicted the case would be wrapped up by 2024 — a target that slipped. "But next year we will really be able to close it, I believe. We are now truly in the end phase. Only a few procedures remain."

## A lesson in liquidation

The Sabena case has become something of a reference point in Belgian insolvency practice, cited in commentary on more recent collapses such as the 2024 Van Hool bankruptcy. Its central argument — that preserving and restructuring operating businesses can recover far more than a quick asset sale — cuts against the instinct to wind up failed companies as fast as possible.

Critics have accused Van Buggenhout of dragging the process out. He counters that the goal was always to extract maximum value and give creditors what they were entitled to. The numbers lend weight to his case: more than five times the recovery of an immediate sale.

The liquidation was also complicated by Sabena's global footprint — subsidiaries, airline stakes across Africa, and real estate in Brussels, Kigali and Kinshasa — as well as international legal battles with Switzerland. A Belgian complaint at the International Court of Justice over the Lugano Convention saw claims of more than €2 billion reduced to an award of €82 million.

## What's left to watch

The formal closure, expected in 2027, would end one of Europe's longest-running corporate wind-downs. Two questions remain open: whether the outstanding supplier debts are recovered further, and whether the Swiss procedure or the Congo land case produces additional funds.

Van Buggenhout, for his part, frames the quarter-century as a duty discharged. "The law says you must manage as a good steward," he said. "I think we have been a very good steward for the creditors."

"It is also a passion," he added. "I myself spent four years in Congo for this. You have to be prepared to give a lot — but the result is there too."
