# Waregem lab Maenhout hit by cyberattack, data theft unclear

A clinical laboratory in the West Flemish town of Waregem was the victim of a cyberattack last week that paralysed at least part of its operations and left it unable to process blood samples, according to [HLN](https://www.hln.be/waregem/klinisch-laboratorium-maenhout-uit-waregem-slachtoffer-van-cyberaanval-niet-duidelijk-of-gegevens-zijn-buitgemaakt~a7975455/). It remains unclear whether patient or company data were stolen, and the company has declined to say whether the disruption has been resolved.

The victim, Klinisch Laboratorium Maenhout, confirmed the incident implicitly: it does not deny the hacking but has offered no further comment. A hacker collective that has previously targeted other companies is reportedly responsible, though the group has not been publicly named.

## What is known

According to HLN's regional reporting, the attack seriously disrupted the laboratory's computer systems. The consequences were concrete and immediate: blood samples could no longer be processed, forcing the lab to call in other laboratories from the region to absorb the outage.

The hackers demanded a ransom in exchange for restoring access to the systems. Reportedly, Maenhout did not pay. Several key questions remain open at the time of writing — most importantly whether personal or medical data were exfiltrated, whether the problems have since been fixed, and whether the incident has been reported to Belgian authorities.

Maenhout is not a small operator. Founded in 1979 by clinical biologist Patrick Maenhout, the laboratory performs more than 5,000 analyses a day and is accredited under ISO 15189 and ISO 17025 standards. It runs a dedicated blood-draw centre and an online results portal used by general practitioners — the kind of externally facing system that widens an organisation's attack surface. The attack arrived only weeks after the lab completed the acquisition of the medical laboratory activities of Labo Medische Biologie in Destelbergen, an expansion of its Flemish footprint.

## Why laboratories are attractive targets

Clinical laboratories sit on precisely the kind of data that ransomware operators prize: blood test results, biomarkers and personal identifiers, all tied to identifiable patients. That combination of operational dependency and sensitive information gives attackers two levers — the threat of downtime and the threat of exposure — to pressure a victim into paying.

Belgian cybersecurity expert Eddy Willems, speaking to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/01/13/cyberaanval-az-monica-antwerpen-expert/) after an earlier hospital attack, explained the logic. Medical data, he said, are "sacred to us and we prefer to share them with as few people as possible" — which is exactly what makes them valuable to criminals. He estimated that healthcare institutions face between 2,000 and 3,000 cyberattack attempts every day.

Willems also noted that in roughly half of successful attacks, a ransom is ultimately paid, which reinforces the criminal business model. Recovery, where an organisation lacks a solid backup plan, can take "days to weeks."

## Part of a wider pattern

The Maenhout case fits into a difficult year for Belgian healthcare IT. In January 2026, the AZ Monica hospital in Antwerp was hit by ransomware, forcing the cancellation of more than 70 operations and the evacuation of seven critical patients; electronic patient records only became accessible again about three weeks later. In April, a cyberattack on an online care portal at AZ Delta in Roeselare, traced to a supplier, again put hospital systems in the spotlight. Earlier, a ransomware attack on the Algemeen Medisch Laboratorium in Hoboken — a lab that at the time ran thousands of COVID tests a day — demonstrated the same lab-targeting model.

Waregem itself has recent experience of industrial-scale cybercrime. The forklift-parts multinational Thermote & Vanhalst (TVH), headquartered in the town, previously endured several months of disruption following an attack, HLN noted.

## The regulatory stakes

As a processor of patient medical data, a Belgian clinical laboratory falls under GDPR and national data-protection rules. Where a personal-data breach is likely, organisations are generally expected to assess the incident and, if necessary, notify the Belgian Data Protection Authority and potentially the Centre for Cybersecurity Belgium. Whether such notification has occurred in this case has not been disclosed. The unresolved question of whether data were stolen is central to that obligation — and to the laboratory's reputational exposure.

## What to watch

Three things will determine how this story develops. First, forensic findings on whether patient data left Maenhout's systems — the difference between a disruptive outage and a serious privacy breach. Second, confirmation that systems have been restored and that results are flowing normally through the lab's doctor-facing portal. And third, whether the unidentified hacker collective is linked to the wider ransomware campaigns that have struck Belgian healthcare this year.

For now, the clearest takeaway is structural rather than local: a mid-sized, accredited laboratory in a Flemish town of 38,000 has joined the list of Belgian care providers whose core operations can be halted by a single intrusion. The question is no longer whether such attacks will happen, but how quickly the sector closes the gaps they exploit.
