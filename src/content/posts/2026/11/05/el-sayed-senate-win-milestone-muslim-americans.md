# El-Sayed's Senate Win Is a Milestone for Muslim Americans

Abdul El-Sayed did not campaign on the prospect of making history, but he savored it on Wednesday when it arrived. The Michigan Democrat becomes the first Muslim elected to the U.S. Senate, defeating Republican Mike Rogers in a contest that was roiled by a wave of anti-Muslim rhetoric, [The Associated Press reported](https://apnews.com/article/abdul-el-sayed-muslim-american-islamophobia-senate-a3c3a0a3a3779326afe4856687ad3abb).

"They tried to demonize people because of how they pray," El-Sayed told the AP. "And Michigan — the whole Michigan — clapped back. People don't want that. They're sick of it."

In his victory speech, the former Wayne County health director and physician stated his full name: "My name is Abdulrahman Mohamed El-Sayed." It was a pointed repudiation of attacks aimed at his identity, and a moment that resonated deeply with Muslim Americans across the country.

## A Historical Reversal, 25 Years in the Making

The AP framed the result against the long shadow of Sept. 11, 2001, and its aftermath, which pulled anti-Muslim sentiment into the forefront of American life. For a community that grew up under intense scrutiny, the victory carried symbolic weight beyond a single Senate seat.

"Having grown up in a post-9/11 world, being Muslim, being Arab myself, never in a million years did I think we would see what we saw today," said Yasmin El-Sayed, no relation to the senator-elect, who brought her daughters to a Detroit watch party and teared up after the speech.

The campaign's hostile climate was not incidental. Trump called El-Sayed a "jihadist" who was "on the side of the terrorists," while dozens of Republicans in Congress formed a Sharia-Free America Caucus, casting Islam's religious code as a threat to the U.S. Constitution. At the GOP's midterm convention in Dallas, Sen. Ted Cruz of Texas drew some of the loudest cheers with the declaration that "Sharia law has no place in America."

## Victors See a Broader Verdict

For advocates, the outcome was about more than a single office. The result "represents a historic turning point for American Muslim political engagement and a historic defeat for the politics of hate," said Nihad Awad, national executive director of the Council on American-Islamic Relations.

"Anti-Muslim extremists, Israel lobby groups and even the president of the United States used every smear in the book to paint Dr. El-Sayed as a Muslim boogeyman," Awad said. "None of it was true, and none of it worked. Anti-Muslim hate was on the ballot, and it failed."

Mohamed Gula, CEO of the Muslim American advocacy group Emgage Action, tied the moment to representation — and to the generations that will follow. "Our children, years from now, will be able to run for office," he said, "to be seen as who they are."

## Voters Backed a Platform, Not Just an Identity

Notably, supporters and local leaders were careful to stress that voters were motivated by policy as well as representation. Abdullah Hammoud, mayor of Dearborn, a city near Detroit with a sizable Arab American population, said the win reflected a coalition built on ideas.

"What a beautifully American story," Hammoud said. "One of our brightest and best earned the support of the majority of Michiganders who participated in yesterday's election. And regardless of the direction in which he prays, they're sending him to the U.S. Senate."

Hammoud pointed to Dearborn's earlier support for independent Sen. Bernie Sanders of Vermont, who is Jewish and caucused with Democrats, as evidence that residents back candidates for their platforms rather than their faith.

## A Rejection of Hate Rhetoric — With Caveats

Analysts read the result as a signal that anti-Muslim attacks are not the political weapon their architects assume. "What I find especially exciting is that El-Sayed won in a political climate where being visibly Muslim can still be a liability," said Asma Uddin, a professor at Michigan State University's College of Law. His victory "gives us real reason for optimism. It suggests that anti-Muslim attacks don't necessarily carry the political weight that those deploying them assume they do."

Gula also pointed to Republican losses in Texas, where Gov. Greg Abbott lost his reelection bid and Attorney General Ken Paxton was trounced by Democrat James Talarico in a Senate race. Both had led the anti-Sharia campaign, which Gula cited as further evidence that voters rejected hate-based politics.

"I saw this as not just Muslims standing against Islamophobia as never before," Gula said. "I saw Americans saying hate will not be used as a campaign issue ... we are not going to accept hate."

## What to Watch

The victory places El-Sayed among a small but growing group of Muslim Americans in national office and hands him a platform on health care, affordability and foreign policy. Whether his win proves a durable shift in how anti-Muslim rhetoric is received at the ballot box — or a result shaped by Michigan's particular demographics and coalition — is an open question. What is clear is that a community long cast as a national security concern now has a voice in the world's greatest deliberative body, and a new generation of candidates who can point to it.

"I didn't run to be the first Muslim senator," El-Sayed said. "I ran to be the next senator of the state of Michigan who happens to be Muslim. But I also know that democracy is constantly about the conversation about who we are, who we want to be."
