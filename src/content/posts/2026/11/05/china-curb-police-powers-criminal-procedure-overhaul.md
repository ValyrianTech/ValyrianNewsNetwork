# China Moves to Curb Police Powers in Legal Overhaul

China has proposed a sweeping revision of its Criminal Procedure Law (CPL) that would explicitly prohibit the presumption of guilt, restrict a contentious police surveillance measure, and tighten safeguards against improper detention and asset seizures. The draft amendment was submitted Nov. 2 for a first reading at the 25th session of the Standing Committee of the 14th National People's Congress (NPC), according to the official Xinhua News Agency, as [reportedly](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-11-05/china-moves-to-curb-police-powers-in-criminal-procedure-overhaul-102501203.html) by Caixin.

The proposal marks the fourth revision of the law since its adoption in 1979 and the first since 2018. Because the CPL governs the boundary between state coercive power and individual rights, it is often dubbed China's "little constitution"-making any change to it a significant signal about the direction of the country's legal system.

## What the Draft Would Change

The amendment contains 55 provisions, including 13 new articles, according to the [Chinese-language Caixin report](https://china.caixin.com/2026-11-04/102500851.html).

**Ban on presuming guilt.** The draft would write an explicit prohibition on the presumption of guilt into the law's general provisions. Existing law lacks such an express principle, though it already includes safeguards-prosecutors bear the burden of proof, suspects cannot be compelled to incriminate themselves, and courts must acquit when evidence is insufficient. Placing the principle in the general provisions would give it elevated legal standing.

**Restrictions on residential surveillance.** The controversial practice of "residential surveillance at a designated location" (RSDL)-a form of de facto house arrest at unknown sites that rights advocates have long criticized-would be limited to national-security or terrorism cases. Other suspects without a fixed residence would be subject to alternative, legally authorized measures. The draft's explanatory notes frame the change as restoring residential surveillance to its intended role as an alternative to detention.

**Detention reform.** Grounds for extending police detention would be narrowed. The current 37-day pre-arrest maximum has been widely criticized as ripe for abuse. Under the draft, a 30-day extension would remain available only for major suspects in serious cases involving criminal groups, cross-border offenses, economic crimes, national security, or terrorism. Prosecutors reviewing arrest requests would have to question suspects, hear from defense lawyers, and regularly reassess the necessity of detention. Detainees must be released or placed under alternative measures once detention approaches the likely prison sentence.

**Property seizure reform.** A new chapter would overhaul how authorities handle seized assets, a response to years of complaints about "profit-driven enforcement," in which police are accused of pursuing seizures for revenue. Confiscated criminal proceeds would go to the state treasury unless legally returned to victims, and agencies would be barred from diverting or spending the funds or tying them to their budgets. Authorities must distinguish lawful from illicit assets, corporate from personal property, and suspects' property from that of relatives and third parties. Seizures must leave families their basic living expenses, and productive assets can remain in reasonable use where proceedings are not compromised.

**Defense lawyer rights.** The proposal would end so-called "placeholder defenses," in which appointed legal-aid lawyers continue to represent a defendant even after the person retains their own counsel. Detention centers would be required to ensure lawyers actually meet clients within 48 hours. Legal aid would expand to suspects facing at least three years' imprisonment in ordinary-procedure cases, and duty lawyers would receive explicit rights to case information, client meetings, and file inspection.

**Plea leniency safeguards.** Under the guilty-plea leniency system, written admissions would require simultaneous audio and video recording, and courts would verify voluntariness and authenticity. Mandatory interrogation recordings would expand to cases carrying possible sentences of at least three years, covering entire interrogations and preserved without editing.

**Minors and foreign defendants.** Minor victims would qualify for legal aid, and questioning must protect privacy and avoid repeated interviews. Foreign suspects and defendants would receive fair treatment, with qualifying evidence from international cooperation permitted and countermeasures authorized against improper foreign criminal jurisdiction.

## Why It Matters

The reform responds to years of advocacy from scholars and legal practitioners over abuses associated with RSDL, profit-driven enforcement, and the use of criminal procedure to interfere in economic disputes.

Shen Chunyao, director of the NPC Legislative Affairs Commission, said the reform targets abuses that "seriously undermine judicial fairness," citing the misuse of residential surveillance at designated sites, profit-driven enforcement, criminal probes into economic disputes, and appointed lawyers blocking defendants' choice of counsel, Caixin reported.

The changes build on earlier signals of a shifting approach. Caixin reported in March 2026 on plans for a major CPL overhaul and in November 2025 on tightened rules for the controversial detention system-both precursors to the current draft.

## What to Watch

The draft faces multiple readings before any final passage, and its provisions may change. Two issues will determine its real-world impact.

First is enforcement. Past CPL provisions have sometimes been unevenly applied in practice, and whether the new RSDL restrictions and the 48-hour lawyer-access requirement are honored will be the key test of the reform's credibility.

Second is the scope of carve-outs. The "national security" exception for RSDL and detention extensions could leave substantial discretion in politically sensitive cases-a concern likely to shadow the amendment through its remaining legislative readings. How narrowly that exception is defined may prove as consequential as the reforms themselves.
