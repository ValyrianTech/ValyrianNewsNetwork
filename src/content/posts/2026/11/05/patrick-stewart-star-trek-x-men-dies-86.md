# Patrick Stewart, Star Trek and X-Men Icon, Dies at 86

Sir Patrick Stewart, the classically trained English actor who brought Shakespearean gravitas to the bridge of the USS Enterprise and the halls of the X-Mansion, has died at age 86. He died Wednesday in Los Angeles following what his spokesperson called "a brief and poignant journey" with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy Body Dementia — diagnoses that had not been previously disclosed, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/patrick-stewart-dies-8b38054ff74d4187a32158d78e345ad7).

Best known to millions as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in "Star Trek: The Next Generation" and Professor Charles Xavier in the "X-Men" films, Stewart was among the most recognizable and versatile actors of his generation — a man who moved, as the [BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cye0l23lx15o) put it, "effortlessly between Star Trek and Shakespeare."

## A Stage Actor First

Before he ever commanded a starship, Stewart commanded the Shakespearean stage. Born Patrick Hewes Stewart on 13 July 1940 in Mirfield, West Riding of Yorkshire, he grew up in a working-class household in northern England, the son of a decorated but traumatized Dunkirk veteran and a mother active in amateur dramatics who encouraged his early love of performance.

He left school at 15, briefly worked as a journalist and obituary writer at the Dewsbury Reporter, then won a scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theatre School. By 18 he had lost all his hair to hereditary alopecia — a blow to a self-conscious teenager who feared it would cost him his looks, but one that ultimately became part of his unmistakable presence.

Stewart spent roughly a decade and a half with the Royal Shakespeare Company from 1966, appearing in more than 40 productions and rising from supporting parts to starring roles as Shylock, Prospero, Henry IV and Mark Antony, according to the [AP's appreciation of his stage career](https://apnews.com/article/patrick-stewart-shakespeare-theater-appreciation-9fe4ffe6024a270aad3a456ae26a11b7). He won three Olivier Awards, including one for playing Claudius in "Hamlet" opposite David Tennant, and earned his sole Tony Award nomination for a 2007-08 "Macbeth" that critics hailed as definitive.

"Nearly 50 years I've waited," Stewart told the AP in 2008 of finally tackling Macbeth. "I learned it when I was 14. I memorized all the soliloquies just because I thought they were great."

## The Picard Breakthrough

Stewart was 47 and largely a character actor when he was cast as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in 1987. A producer had spotted him lecturing at his wife's Shakespeare class in Los Angeles. Stewart knew nothing of the original "Star Trek" and doubted the new series would last, signing a six-year contract mainly to stabilize his finances.

"Everybody told me it would be a flop," he recalled. "A friend of mine said, 'Get a suntan, make some money, go home.'"

It didn't flop. "Star Trek: The Next Generation" ran seven years and 178 episodes and spawned four feature films, making Stewart a household name and a wealthy man. He deliberately infused Picard with Shakespearean formality, modeling the character on "Henry IV."

"For huge numbers of people I will always be Jean-Luc Picard, and that's fine," he later said. "I really believe we created an admirable individual."

In 2000 he took on the role of Professor Charles Xavier in the original "X-Men" film, a part described as essentially playing a telepathic Picard in a wheelchair. He appeared in the original trilogy and returned to the character across multiple films, including 2017's "Logan" and 2022's "Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness." His final on-screen appearance, as Xavier in Marvel's "Avengers: Doomsday," is slated for release this Christmas.

## A Galaxy of Tributes

News of Stewart's death prompted an outpouring from friends, colleagues and co-stars. Ian McKellen, his close friend and frequent stage partner who officiated Stewart's 2013 wedding to singer-songwriter Sunny Ozell, remembered him as rooted in his Yorkshire identity.

"Before everything else, Patrick was defined by being born and bred in Yorkshire, the proud county in the north of England which he loved to the end," McKellen said. "He wore his heart on his sleeve, which made him a man to rely on, to admire and in my case, love."

William Shatner, the original Captain James T. Kirk, called it "a heartbreaking week." "From the stage to the bridge of the Enterprise, he brought intelligence, grace, and a deep sense of humanity to everything he did," he said. "Captain Picard stood for the best of us, and so did the man who played him." The tributes were compiled by the [AP](https://apnews.com/article/patrick-stewart-dead-tributes-reactions-5d771fd5af52070e23cf081b31754393).

Hugh Jackman, Stewart's "X-Men" co-star, wrote: "We lost one of the greats of stage and screen today. And, in my book, a tremendous friend and man. I learned so much from him. I will miss you, Patrick Stewart, all of my days."

George Takei, who played Hikaru Sulu, said Stewart "took the starship Enterprise to places it had never gone before. He inspired a whole next generation of star gazers... Rest in the heavens, Captain."

Marvel Studios praised Stewart for bringing Charles Xavier to life "with extraordinary grace, intelligence, and humanity, defining the character for generations of fans." Kevin Feige, the studio's president, called it "a true privilege to know him and to work with him."

## A Painful Week for Star Trek

Stewart's death came just days after the loss of Walter Koenig, who played Ensign Pavel Chekov on the original 1960s "Star Trek" series and died Sunday at 90. The twin losses struck hard at a fan base devoted to a franchise built on optimism, as the [AP chronicled](https://apnews.com/article/star-trek-patrick-stewart-walter-koenig-death-fcf834160945365b00f310f299ec4ef7).

Only two members of the original cast now remain: Shatner and Takei. "None of us will be around forever," Shatner said in a 2008 interview, "but 'Star Trek' will."

## A Life Beyond the Screen

Stewart's legacy extends well beyond his famous roles. A lifelong Labour Party supporter and political activist, he was also a patron of Refuge, a British domestic abuse charity, drawing on a childhood scarred by his father's violence against his mother. He wrote candidly about that history in his 2023 memoir, "Making It So."

He won a Grammy in 1996 for his spoken-word recording of "Peter and the Wolf" and was knighted by Queen Elizabeth II in June 2010 for services to drama. Late in life he became an unlikely internet favorite, posing in a lobster-suit bath and delighting fans with his humor. During the COVID-19 pandemic he recorded daily readings of Shakespeare's sonnets, introducing the Bard to a new generation.

Stewart is survived by his wife, Sunny Ozell, and two children from a previous marriage, daughter Sophia and son Daniel.

## What to Watch For

Beyond the personal loss, Stewart's death marks the close of an era for a franchise in transition. "Star Trek" devotees are already looking ahead to his posthumous turn in "Avengers: Doomsday" this Christmas — a final curtain call for an actor who, as McKellen put it, was "the most respected and beloved actor of his generation." London's West End theaters plan to dim their lights next Tuesday evening in his memory.
