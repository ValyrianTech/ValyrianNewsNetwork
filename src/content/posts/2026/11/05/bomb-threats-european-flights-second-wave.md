# Bomb Threats Hit 15+ European Flights in Second Wave

A fresh wave of bomb threats disrupted passenger air travel across Europe on Thursday, the second such episode in a single week, prompting fighter-jet escorts, emergency diversions and on-board searches at airports from France to Bulgaria. Among the affected flights was a LOT Polish Airlines service from Warsaw to Brussels, which landed safely at Zaventem after Belgian federal police concluded that no additional measures were required, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/).

At least 15 flights operated by Aer Lingus, Finnair, Ryanair, Wizz Air, LOT and Aegean Airlines received threats, according to [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/live/cwjdmnrg94ezt), though counts varied during the day as reports came in. The Associated Press put the figure at [more than a dozen](https://apnews.com/article/flight-diverted-france-bomb-threat-dublin-rome-6585ff565ee1ccb9f19b73074aa2fec4). No explosives or suspicious items were found aboard any inspected aircraft.

## The Brussels angle

The threat to LOT flight LO235 arrived before its scheduled landing in Zaventem. "We are informed about a suspicious message on a flight with destination Brussels Airport," the airport's press office said. "After investigation by the Federal Police, no additional measures appeared to be necessary. The aircraft has since landed safely."

A LOT spokesperson said the crew conducted an on-board inspection in accordance with established procedures and found no suspicious items, adding that a post-landing inspection by security services found "no dangerous or suspicious items." Belgian police are investigating whether the LO235 threat is linked to earlier incidents this week. Brussels Airport, site of the 2016 attacks, has historically been a high-profile target, making the measured official response notable.

## Fighter escorts and diversions

Elsewhere, the alerts triggered far more dramatic responses. An Aer Lingus Airbus A320 flying from Dublin to Rome declared an emergency, turned near Paris and was escorted by French military aircraft to the Evreux air base in Normandy. The French prime minister's office said all 151 passengers and six crew members were brought to safety and that bomb disposal teams inspected the plane.

In Italy, Italian Air Force jets escorted a Finnair flight from Helsinki into Rome Fiumicino, where bomb disposal experts cleared the aircraft. Bulgarian MiG-29 fighter jets took off from Graf Ignatievo Air Base to intercept and escort three Ryanair flights bound for Bulgaria; two landed in Sofia and one in Plovdiv. In Romania, nearly 200 passengers were evacuated from a Wizz Air flight from Milan to Bucharest while an explosives disposal team inspected the aircraft.

Czech police reported threats against four Prague-bound flights, with pyrotechnic inspections returning negative results. The investigation was taken over by the National Centre against Terrorism, Extremism and Cybercrime. In Denmark, police and the bomb disposal service responded to threats against two flights at Copenhagen Airport without finding suspicious objects.

## A pattern of cheap disruption

The episode follows a similar wave on Tuesday, when at least seven flights were targeted and no explosives were found, [VRT NWS reported at the time](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/vliegtuig-noodlanding-polen-bommelding/). No group or individual has been definitively attributed responsibility.

Officials have cautioned against concluding the incidents are coordinated. "It is too early to draw conclusions on whether the reported incidents are linked," EU Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas said. "National investigations remain ongoing." UK Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander described the events as "malicious hoax threats," saying established processes were being followed.

The only apparent claim of responsibility surfaced via Rome airport authorities, who received an emailed threat signed on behalf of a supposed group calling itself "Fighters of the Male State," framing the action as targeting a "feminist regime." The group is not an established organisation and the claim has not been authenticated.

Aviation safety expert Marco Chan, a senior lecturer at Kingston University and former Cathay Pacific pilot, told BBC Verify that hoax threats are seen by some experts as "possibly part of a pattern of cheap, deniable disruption." "The cost to the sender is almost nothing... That asymmetry is the point of the tactic, whatever the motive," he said. Still, he cautioned that "three days of incidents is too small a sample to separate co-ordination from contagion."

## What to watch

The threats come amid heightened geopolitical tension and a broader climate of "hybrid" security incidents across Europe, including a cyberattack on supermarket refrigeration systems, drone incursions into European airspace and the hacking of emergency alert apps. No evidence has publicly linked the bomb threats to Russia or any state actor; such attribution remains speculative and unconfirmed.

For now, the practical question is whether the wave subsides or escalates. Airlines and airports continue to treat each threat as real, a costly but necessary default. As Finnair put it: "Threats are almost always unfounded" — but the system cannot afford to assume so. The coming days will show whether investigators can trace a pattern, or whether Europe is facing a drawn-out season of low-cost disruption.
