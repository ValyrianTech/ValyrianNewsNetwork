# Gennez Pulls Trainee Doctor Over Patient Safety

Flemish Welfare Minister Caroline Gennez has personally terminated a young doctor's specialist training at Antwerp hospitals, ruling that patient safety was at risk—a first for a Flemish minister, [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/jonge-arts-uit-opleiding-gezet-zas-uza/) reported. The trainee, who disputes all allegations, has appealed the decision to Belgium's Council of State.

The case marks the first time since record-keeping began that a Flemish welfare minister has had to break a deadlock between two expert committees—and the first time one has ruled against a trainee's continued training on patient-safety grounds. It has thrown a spotlight on how Flanders supervises its trainee physicians and balances clinical training against the risk to patients in their care.

## A training path that went wrong

The doctor graduated from KU Leuven in 2021 and spent two years specializing in clinical biology before switching to pathological anatomy—the branch of medicine in which tissue and cell samples are analysed to diagnose disease, including cancer. He trained at Antwerp University Hospital (UZA) and, from October 2025, at ZAS Middelheim, according to [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/voor-het-eerst-zet-gennez-jonge-arts-uit-opleiding-voor-patientveiligheid-arts-vecht-beslissing-aan~af313aab/).

His supervising professors logged 15 cases of possible errors. Among them: tumor tissue from a child left in decalcification fluid for more than a week, making the tumor structure almost impossible to assess; a delayed tissue analysis that kept a patient under anaesthesia longer than necessary; and a misdescribed tumor size that led to an initial underestimation of severity, affecting the planned treatment.

The mentors also reported that the doctor appeared drunk on duty, and pointed to a separate serious incident now subject to a complaint at the public prosecutor's office and the Order of Physicians. The precise nature of that incident has not been disclosed. The doctor and his lawyers deny every allegation.

ZAS said it acted to protect patients. "When there are signals, as in this file, that the quality or safety of care may be compromised, we take additional measures to protect patients and guarantee continuity of care," the hospital network said in a written response.

## Two committees, two verdicts

The case followed Flanders' standard two-tier review. The recognition committee of the Flemish Department of Care voted unanimously to end the training. The doctor appealed to the Advisory Committee for Specialist Physicians and General Practitioners, which sided with him—citing positive internship evaluations and a series of procedural complaints—and recommended he continue elsewhere, "with a fresh look and without prejudice."

His defence argued that negative evaluations were recorded months after the relevant interviews, and that two supervising physicians who had filed complaints also sat on the recognition committee and took part in deliberations on their own complaints. The Department of Care denies that the mentors joined those deliberations.

Because the two bodies reached opposite conclusions, the decision fell to Gennez. She followed the recognition committee, citing eight extensive evaluation interviews, the 15 cases and the risk to patient safety. "The decision was not taken lightly, but came after several remediation trajectories and evaluation moments, during which the doctor's functioning was extensively investigated and assessed," said department spokesperson Anneleen De Sadeleer.

## Why the intervention matters

Since 2017, seven physician training programmes in Flanders have been terminated for poor functioning, according to department figures cited by [De Specialist](https://www.despecialist.eu/nl/nieuws/gennez-stopt-zelf-opleiding-van-jonge-patholoog-wegens-gevaar-voor-patientveiligheid.html). In every previous case both committees agreed, so no minister ever had to step in.

The split verdict is what forced Gennez to act—and it exposes fault lines in the system. It raises questions about how trainee evaluations are documented, how conflicts of interest are handled when complainants sit on review panels, and how far a minister should be able to override an appeals body that found procedural flaws. The Council of State's eventual ruling, which could take up to 18 months, may clarify where the balance of power lies between elected officials and professional training committees.

For patients, the immediate signal is that patient safety outranks a trainee's right to complete a programme when the two collide. For the small world of Flemish specialist training, the precedent is subtler: a minister has now shown she will overrule an appeals committee—and both trainees and their supervisors will be watching how that power is used next.
