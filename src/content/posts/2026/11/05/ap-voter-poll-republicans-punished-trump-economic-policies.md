# AP Poll: Voters Punished GOP Over Trump's Economy

President Donald Trump was "publicly humbled by inflation" in Tuesday's midterm elections, as rising prices drove a blistering set of Republican losses across House, Senate and gubernatorial races, according to [The AP Voter Poll](https://apnews.com/article/economy-trump-midterms-inflation-ai-wealthy-healthcare-f8dbe8800549340c8a75d956302c126b). Roughly two-thirds of the approximately 100,000 voters surveyed said Trump's own policies had fueled inflation and made the economy worse—sharply higher than the roughly half of 2022 midterm voters who said the same of Joe Biden's policies. Democrats swept both chambers of Congress, ushering in divided government and setting the stage for a new era of oversight in Washington.

## Why Voters Blamed Trump

The survey, conducted by SSRS for a consortium including the AP, ABC News, CBS News, CNN, FOX News Media, NBC News and Versant, paints a picture of a president who campaigned with "a decidedly deaf ear." Voters watched prices begin to climb with Trump's "chaotic rash of tariffs," and energy costs spiked after he launched a war with Iran in February. Consumer prices are up 3.7% year-over-year, compared to a 3% inflation rate when Trump began his second term. According to the [SSRS methodology statement](https://ssrs.com/wp-content/uploads/2026-Voter-Poll-Methods-Statement-Nov-3-8PM.pdf), the full-sample margin of sampling error is plus or minus 0.6 percentage points.

Trump's argument that the fault lay with his predecessor "didn't land." At one of his final rallies in Iowa, he insisted, "I've done a great job. Prices are coming way down." On Wednesday, he told reporters the Iran war "drove the gasoline up a little bit short-term," but defended the conflict as necessary to stop Iran's push for nuclear weapons. "If I had to do it again, I'd have to do it again," he said.

The cost of living was the overwhelming priority. About 9 in 10 voters called it a "crucial" or "major" factor in their congressional vote, and about 6 in 10 of that group backed Democratic House candidates. Voters from California to Mississippi broadly agreed that Trump's tariffs on foreign imports have done more harm than good.

## An Economy That Felt Worse Than It Looked

A central paradox of the result is that the underlying economy was stronger than many Americans perceived. The S&P 500 stock index was up more than 10% for the year, and unemployment stood at a healthy 4.2%. Investors greeted the Democratic wins positively, with stocks rising on Wednesday.

Yet voters were dominated by price levels, not aggregate growth. Voters don't share Trump's optimism about artificial intelligence, which he renamed "super intelligence" in federal documents and stoked by refusing to codify more aggressive oversight. About 6 in 10 voters said AI will do more to hurt society than help—fearing job displacement or a financial bubble—though only about one-quarter called it a "crucial" factor.

Voters also perceived an economy tilted toward the wealthy. About 7 in 10 said the economy favors wealthy people, while only about 3 in 10 said it is fair to everyday Americans. Tech billionaires became frequent White House guests, and the concentration of wealth unsettled voters across income levels.

## A Trap for the President

Voter sentiment tracked closely with paycheck size. For those earning less than $50,000 a year, about 6 in 10 said they were worse off than two years ago. Higher-income voters felt better about the economy—but that created a different problem: About 4 in 10 households earning more than $200,000 named democracy as their top issue, roughly even with the economy, and voters focused on democracy broke overwhelmingly for Democrats.

Those concerns were fed by a governance style in which Trump, without congressional approval, laid off thousands of federal workers, altered federal spending and imposed hundreds of billions of dollars in new tariffs that the Supreme Court earlier this year ruled illegal. A slim majority of voters said government corruption was a crucial factor in their vote. Most—about 6 in 10—said Trump's priorities were misplaced and that he did not fight for people like them. About 7 in 10 said Congress is not doing enough to provide checks and balances on the presidency, including about 4 in 10 Republicans.

## What's Next

Democrats now face a mandate that is clearer about what voters rejected than what they embraced. Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer acknowledged as much: "Many of the folks who voted for us yesterday are still skeptical, and that's OK." House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries, poised to become the first Black Speaker when the new Congress convenes Jan. 3, called the result a "stunning rebuke of MAGA extremism."

There is no guarantee that a Democratic Congress can bring inflation down on its own; progress would likely require bipartisan cooperation or a stable economy that lets the Federal Reserve ratchet down rates. Democrats are preparing a 100-day affordability agenda alongside aggressive oversight, with investigations and even impeachment on the table. On the Republican side, Speaker Mike Johnson reportedly will not seek reelection as House GOP leader, opening an internal reckoning over a party that misread an angry, price-conscious electorate. Trump, facing the prospect of gridlock, told Politico, "I don't want to be just sitting there for two years vetoing everything."
