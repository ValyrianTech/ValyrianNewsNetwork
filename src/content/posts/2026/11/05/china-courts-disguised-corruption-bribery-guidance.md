# China Targets 'Disguised' Corruption in Bribery Guidance

China's Supreme People's Court and Supreme People's Procuratorate on November 5 released a set of six model cases on punishing what they call "new and hidden" forms of duty-related crime — a judicial push to standardize how courts and prosecutors identify corruption wrapped in the appearance of legitimate business. The notice, dated November 2 and published via the procuratorate's official account, was [reprinted by Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/legal/20261105/40131af24ea148179cfb5ad111358346/c.html) and state media.

The release introduces no new statute. Instead, it operationalizes a directive from the Fifth Plenary Session of the 20th Communist Party of China Central Committee to "enrich effective methods for preventing and combating new and hidden corruption," using concrete, anonymized cases to teach investigators, prosecutors and judges how to see through increasingly sophisticated schemes.

## What 'new and hidden' corruption means

In Chinese anti-corruption discourse, the term describes graft deliberately disguised to evade detection — illicit transfers of benefit dressed up as market transactions, salaries, or investments. According to a [CCTV commentary on the Plenum](https://news.cctv.cn/2026/11/01/ARTIdyrOZxl61bjP8LlVbt58261101.shtml), the Party has committed to "integrating the advancement of not daring to corrupt, not being able to corrupt, and not wanting to corrupt" as the basic policy of its anti-corruption struggle, with a renewed emphasis on systematic, root-cause treatment.

The six cases map directly onto the typologies officials have flagged: shadow companies and shadow shareholders, the government-business "revolving door," deferred or option-style benefits tied to future gains, proxy shareholding, and name-lending arrangements.

## Six cases, one principle

Each case follows a standard template — keywords, facts, handling, "model significance" and relevant law — and each illustrates the same underlying doctrine: courts look past lawful-looking wrappers to ask whether a benefit was obtained through official power, whether there was real work and real risk, and whether the gain corresponds to the official's conduct.

**Bribery via a manufactured business opportunity.** A former trade-center Party secretary surnamed Wang helped a construction company win a convention-complex project. In return, the company set up his daughter in a steel-trading business — providing premises, capital, startup funds and staff — then made it a designated supplier and let it pocket the margin. The company made RMB 11.55 million without substantive operations or risk. Wang was sentenced to 13 years and fined RMB 2.2 million.

**Bribery via pre-IPO shares.** A former district deputy head surnamed Wu received a RMB 3 million "strategic placement" quota in a power-electronics company before its STAR Market listing, with a pledge that he would not lose money. After the listing, the bribe-giver returned his RMB 3 million plus RMB 3.67 million in profit. The ruling established that the expected returns of a high-appreciation strategic placement share qualify as "property" under criminal law.

**Bribery via no-show salaries.** A former vice president of a state-controlled bank surnamed Zhang agreed to collect a salary at an investment company after resigning — drawing RMB 3.89 million while attending only one board meeting. He also arranged for his unqualified wife to serve as an independent director, where she received RMB 400,000 while barely participating. He was sentenced to 12.5 years.

**Embezzlement via sham litigation.** A sub-district Party secretary surnamed Liang conspired in a demolition project to have the office double-pay already-settled compensation and fabricate documents for 27 households, then had a company sue the office and win a court judgment worth RMB 2.63 million. Prosecutors conducted their own supplementary investigation, exposed the forged forms, and moved to correct the fake lawsuit via a retrial recommendation.

**Bribery via a specially created post.** A former state-owned group chairman surnamed Zhou had an auto group create executive positions for a specific relation, paying RMB 11.74 million — RMB 8.15 million above the highest comparable vice-president salary — plus a promised RMB 15 million bonus. Because the relation did real but lesser work, the court deducted a reasonable salary benchmarked to comparable executives and treated only the excess as the bribe. Zhou was sentenced to 13 years and fined RMB 2.4 million.

**Bribery via handed-over projects.** A hospital president surnamed Gao steered procurement bids to five businesspeople, who then did all the preparatory work on seven profitable projects before handing them to a company controlled by his son. That company made RMB 2.49 million without substantive operation or market risk. Gao was sentenced to 10 years and four months.

## A coherent valuation rulebook

The unifying contribution is methodological. Across the cases, the bribe amount is calculated consistently: where there was no real work at all, the entire sum counts; where a beneficiary did genuine but lesser work, only the excess over a reasonable benchmark counts; where profit flowed through a shell, the actual illicit profit is the measure.

This "substance over form" approach gives lower courts and procuratorates a teachable standard, addressing one of the practical difficulties in hidden-corruption cases — pinning down exactly how much was illicit when money moves through apparently legitimate channels.

## Beyond conviction

The Liang case also signals the procuratorate's expanding role beyond prosecution. In it, prosecutors ran their own supplementary investigation, corrected a sham civil judgment, and issued a governance recommendation to the local justice bureau over lawyer-oversight gaps — embodying the "handle one case, govern a whole sector" philosophy the notice urges.

All six defendants received prison terms ranging from 4.5 to 13 years with substantial fines, and none appealed — a detail the release uses to convey deterrence.

## What to watch

No new law accompanied the guidance; it relies on existing Criminal Law provisions and 2007 Supreme Court and Procuratorate opinions on bribery cases. The open question is whether this presages a formal judicial interpretation codifying the "actual profit" and "excess-over-benchmark" valuation methods, and whether lower courts will apply them uniformly.

Timed days after the Fifth Plenum and its strict-governance decision, the release reads as both a practical handbook for the judiciary and a political signal — reassuring the public and warning officials that the anti-corruption campaign is adapting to the methods of those it pursues.
