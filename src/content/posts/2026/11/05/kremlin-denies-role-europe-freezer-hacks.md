# Kremlin Denies Role in Europe Freezer Hacks

The Kremlin on Thursday rejected as "absurd accusations" claims that Russia was behind a wave of cyberattacks that remotely sabotaged refrigeration and freezer systems at supermarkets across Europe, forcing retailers to destroy tonnes of spoiled food, according to [HLN](https://www.hln.be/buitenland/kremlin-ontkent-russische-betrokkenheid-bij-cyberaanvallen-op-diepvriezers-in-europese-supermarkten-absurde-beschuldigingen~a90633ba/). The denial came as investigators across the continent examined how hackers turned commercial cooling systems into weapons of disruption — and as no European authority had yet formally named a culprit.

## What Happened

Over several days, with the main wave hitting on Monday 2 November, attackers remotely manipulated refrigeration and freezer controls at supermarkets in multiple European countries, in some cases reportedly raising temperatures to 50°C (122°F). According to the [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/europe-supermarkets-freezers-cyberattacks-a8ea5b30d67da47957769f29101fe686), stores in Sweden, Belgium, Luxembourg, Ireland and Estonia were among those affected, forcing some to dispose of ruined food.

Among the chains hit were Delhaize in Belgium and Luxembourg, Carrefour in Belgium (15 of its more than 700 stores), Intermarché and Ikea in Belgium, Coop and ICA in Sweden, Kesko in Finland, SuperValu in Ireland and Selver in Estonia. Delhaize spokesperson Sarah De Meester said the impact was contained: "In the affected stores, the range of fresh and frozen products is temporarily somewhat limited. Our distribution centers are operating normally, and there has been no disruption to deliveries to our stores."

The disruption was not confined to supermarkets. On Monday, French blood banks were also targeted, with cold-storage units holding blood products remotely manipulated. The French Blood Establishment (EFS) confirmed the incident on Wednesday, saying no blood products were lost after alarm systems detected the anomalies quickly, [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/04/blood-bank-fridges-cyberattacks.html) reported.

The attacks echo an earlier wave on 12 October, when hackers took control of refrigeration systems in hundreds of French supermarkets and independent stores, sending temperatures soaring.

## The Kremlin's Response

Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov told journalists on Thursday: "These are absurd claims... As always, there is no substantiation, no evidence. And yet such unfounded accusations are made against our country, as if Russia is involved everywhere."

Moscow has a long-standing pattern of denying involvement in cyber-operations and sabotage, and its rejection does not by itself confirm or refute any role. Crucially, no European government, the EU or any national cybersecurity agency has officially attributed the attacks to Russia or to any state actor.

## Competing Claims of Responsibility

On Wednesday, two pro-Russian hacktivist collectives — NoName057(16) and Z-Pentest — claimed responsibility via Telegram, publishing what they said were 2,500 lines from a database of Danish refrigeration manufacturer Danfoss, including login credentials and passwords. According to [Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/security/russian-hackers-hacked-european-refrigeration-systems/), the posts have since disappeared and the claims could not be independently verified.

"The problem is not with 'evil hackers,' but with the systemic negligence of European and American operators who have ignored basic cybersecurity requirements for years," Z-Pentest wrote. NoName057(16) said it was releasing the data sample "to prove that we have the database."

Yet the actual role of the two groups remains uncertain. NoName057(16) is primarily known for distributed denial-of-service attacks, not industrial control systems, raising doubts about whether it could have carried out the sabotage. Observers have suggested the groups may simply be seeking the spotlight.

## How the Hack Worked

The attack was notable for its simplicity. According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/02/europe-supermarket-fridges-freezers-who-hacking-why), attackers exploited internet-exposed appliance management software and weak passwords, remotely switching evaporators to defrost mode to raise temperatures. No malware was required — leaving what one expert described as "no trace of a weapon at the crime scene."

The vulnerability is systemic. Commercial refrigeration software is connected to the internet for remote monitoring, but is often left unpatched for long periods because patching requires taking equipment offline — a real problem for food retailers. Analysts estimate around 13,000 refrigeration systems across Europe still run vulnerable, outdated software.

"The two most plausible attribution scenarios for me are some kind of state-based signalling and demonstration of capability or some kind of lone-wolf hacker that is conducting these attacks for their own personal reasons," said Rafe Pilling, senior director for threat intelligence at the cybersecurity firm Sophos.

## The Attribution Question

The evidence remains circumstantial. Cybersecurity expert Xavier Bellekens, CEO of the Scottish firm Lupovis, estimated a 95% probability that a Kremlin-backed group is behind the attacks, citing similarities to digital infrastructure previously used in sabotage linked to the Russian government. The European Commission has described the attacks as "a deliberate attempt to disrupt our food supply chain and undermine consumers' confidence in it."

But the case for skepticism is real. The simplicity of the hack is well within reach of lone actors or opportunistic hacktivists. The claiming groups' channels vanished and their claims are unverified. And Russian denials of such allegations are routine and expected.

## A New Front in Hybrid Warfare?

The incidents are being viewed through the lens of what Western intelligence chiefs describe as a Russian campaign of sabotage across Europe — ranging from cyber-attacks to arson — that is likely to intensify. If attributable to Russia or its proxies, the attacks represent an escalation: a low-cost, high-visibility tactic aimed squarely at civilian populations.

A parallel is under investigation in the United States, where the Pentagon is examining a possible similar cyberattack on refrigeration at military base commissaries in early August.

## What to Watch

The central question is not whether the attacks happened — they demonstrably did — but who is responsible, and whether that can be proven. European authorities have convened a crisis response involving ENISA and Europol, and the EU faces pressure to formally attribute responsibility and to compel manufacturers and retailers to secure internet-exposed industrial controls. Whether further waves follow, and what Danfoss says about the alleged credential leak, will shape the next phase of a story that has already exposed how fragile the continent's cold chain has become.
