# China's Rail Renumbering Begins, New Tickets Nov 11

China is set to implement the first phase of a nationwide reform to its railway train-numbering system, with new-number tickets going on general sale from November 11 and the new prefixes taking effect for passenger trains at midnight on November 25, [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261105/bad380f84e854348949b137244b690d8/c.html) reported. The initial batch covers conventional-speed passenger trains, tourist trains, and freight services, involving more than 4,700 trains across the network.

According to state-owned operator China State Railway Group (CR), freight trains will adopt the new numbering at 18:00 on November 24, one day before passenger services switch over. High-speed and other EMU trains are being held back for a second batch scheduled for late December.

## The Prefix Reorganization

At the center of the change is the newly issued 2026 edition of the *Train Number Allocation Regulations*, which replaces rules in force since 2014. For conventional-speed passenger services, three letter prefixes will now be used: **Z**, **N**, and **V**.

The **Z** prefix, read as the English letter, denotes trains with a maximum speed of 160 km/h and retains numbers in the Z1–Z1998 range. The **N** prefix, also read as an English letter, designates 140 km/h express services and replaces the old **T** prefix, covering N1–N1998. The **V** prefix covers 120 km/h conventional trains, absorbing the forms **K** and plain-number services, with subranges for express (V1–V5998), ordinary express (V6001–V6998), and slow trains (V7001–V7998).

The **Y** prefix stays intact for tourist trains, with Y1–Y998 for tourist service-line trains and Y1001–Y9998 for charter services.

## Freight Faces the Biggest Overhaul

Freight is the area most visibly changed. The old **X** prefix — historically meaning parcel or mail service — will be retired entirely and replaced with **B** for block trains (班列), read as "ban." Rather than a simple relabeling, the freight numbering has been broken down by product family, with speed-ordered number ranges assigned to China-Europe and Central Asia block trains, multimodal services, intercity services, and bulk direct trains. Rail planners said the objective is to let shippers match a train number to a service type and speed tier at a glance.

Heavy-haul, coal-direct, and oil-direct trains will keep their five-digit unlettered numbers, with dedicated ranges for each category. In total, more than 4,700 passenger and freight trains are affected in this first round.

## Why the System Is Being Rebuilt

The reform is being driven by a looming arithmetic problem rather than a cosmetic preference. Under the 2014 regulations, **G**-prefix high-speed trains consumed more than 90% of their allocated number ranges during peak holiday periods, [IT Home](https://www.ithome.com/1/013/840.htm) reported. To relieve that pressure, IT Home noted, the total number of prefixes available for EMU trains has been expanded from four to seven, increasing the pool of allocatable train numbers by roughly 40%.

The underlying growth is striking. As commentator Hu Bo noted in [Xinhua Daily](https://news.10jqka.com.cn/20261026/c681059161.shtml), the number of 300–350 km/h high-speed EMUs grew 209% between 2014 and 2025 — swelling demand for a number pool that was already near its limit.

A new **X** prefix has been introduced for Fuxing trains operating above 350 km/h — a placeholder for the next generation of high-speed rail. The **T** and **K** prefixes that conventional trains are losing are not disappearing; they are being repurposed for 300 km/h and 200–250 km/h EMUs respectively in the second batch.

Writing in the same Xinhua Daily column, Hu Bo described the shift as more than a cosmetic change. "This is not just swapping a letter, but reserving an identity in advance for next-generation high-speed rail," he wrote, adding that the reform will let ordinary travelers identify a train's class at a glance.

## What Travelers Need to Watch

Running sections and timetables remain essentially unchanged; the first batch is a relabeling exercise, not a rescheduling. The risk is transitional. Because tickets are sold 15 days in advance, the same physical train could display two different numbers on the 12306 booking platform during the changeover. CR said the 12306 app will carry banners on its homepage and query pages, with stations reinforcing the message via broadcast, notice boards, and electronic displays.

First-batch tickets go on sale progressively from November 11. On the Beijing–Shanghai railway, which operates a trial advance-booking service, new-number tickets are released from November 6. Freight customers can check new designations through the 95306 logistics platform, which carries no impact for trains already in transit. On-board services, including light luggage delivery, the Railway Pass QR code, and online meal ordering, remain unchanged.

## What's Next

The second and more consequential batch lands in late December, when the high-speed network — including X, G, T, and K services — is renumbered, along with D, C, and S trains. For now, travelers booking journeys from mid-November onward should verify both the train number and the timetable before purchase, as the same service may appear under its old and new designations during the overlap. CR has completed the rule revisions, signal-software upgrades, and safety evaluations required for the switch, and said the first batch is ready to operate.
