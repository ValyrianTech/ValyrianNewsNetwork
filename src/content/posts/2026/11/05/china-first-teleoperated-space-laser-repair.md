# China Tests First Teleoperated Space Laser Repair

China has completed what its researchers describe as the world's first extravehicular laser "minimally invasive" maintenance experiment, using a remotely operated device to perform precision cutting on the exterior of its Tiangong space station. The test, reported by [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261105/c38da29c88bc4fe8a84b8be64df6609f/c.html) and originally published by Science and Technology Daily, marks a step toward routine on-orbit servicing of long-lived space infrastructure.

The device, called the extravehicular laser maintenance apparatus, was jointly developed by Professor Huang Yu's team at the School of Mechanical Science and Engineering at Huazhong University of Science and Technology (HUST) and the Shanghai Institute of Space Power-Sources.

## What the experiment achieved

The in-orbit test successfully conducted millimeter-level precision cutting of large exterior equipment, with the laser processing capability reaching micron-level — tens of micrometers — precision, according to the official account. The team said it validated a proprietary space on-orbit maintenance technology system.

Crucially, the operation was performed by teleoperation from the ground rather than by astronauts during a spacewalk. Earlier Chinese exterior repairs — the first in March 2024 when the Shenzhou-17 crew repaired the Tianhe core module's solar wings, and a second in August 2026 by the Shenzhou-23 crew — relied on manual, hands-on work by astronauts outside the station.

"For a space station operating over a long period, the regular maintenance and high-precision repair of extravehicular equipment are the lifeline of safe operation and the normalized operation of facilities," Huang said.

## Overcoming three technical bottlenecks

Translating ground-based precision laser equipment into a space-borne tool required advances in three areas, according to Huang: adaptability to the extreme vacuum, strong radiation and severe temperature swings of space; millimeter-level high-precision positioning; and micron-level laser precision cutting.

The team developed an "8+2"-axis multi-degree-of-freedom fine-adjustment mechanism, a three-degree-of-freedom precision capture mechanism, and an advanced multi-level visual guidance system. Together these enabled intelligent, high-precision positioning and micron-level laser thermal-effect cutting of exterior targets under complex spacecraft attitudes.

The team also compressed the entire research-and-development cycle — from conceptual design and a prototype to flight-qualified hardware — into about one year, a rapid-turnaround model it described as "one-step" flight-model qualification.

## Why it matters

Space debris travels at typical speeds of 7 to 10 kilometers per second. Millimeter-sized fragments can degrade solar wings and reduce power output, while centimeter-sized objects can penetrate spacecraft shells and damage tanks and pipelines. That threat is a principal reason exterior equipment on long-duration stations needs periodic inspection and repair.

A validated teleoperated laser capability supports routine maintenance and life extension of space assets, a field known as on-orbit servicing. It also points toward debris-mitigation approaches, in which precision cutting could trim or repair damaged structures. Because the operation is ground-controlled, it reduces reliance on risky astronaut spacewalks.

## A claim awaiting independent confirmation

The "world's first" characterization is asserted by Chinese state media and the research team. No independent international confirmation of the claim was identified in available reporting, and the precise scope of the milestone should be read as the developers' own framing: the first teleoperated precision laser cutting conducted in the space environment.

Public disclosures remain high-level. Specific cut dimensions, the exact hardware targeted, and the full mission parameters were not released.

## What to watch

The development fits a broader push by China to build out capabilities for operating and maintaining assets in orbit, alongside its university-institute collaboration model. Key questions remain: how quickly the technology could scale from repair tasks toward operational debris removal, and whether follow-on missions will release technical data that allow outside experts to assess the results.

For now, the experiment shows that industrial-grade precision manufacturing is edging into the orbital environment — a capability whose value will grow as more spacecraft spend longer in space.
