# Belgium Budget Crisis: Talks Resume as Wallonia Reacts

Belgium's federal government remained in limbo on Thursday as N-VA chair Valerie Van Peel arrived at the Royal Palace for the final audience in King Philippe's round of consultations over the collapsed budget talks — and left without a word to waiting reporters. The deadlock, which prompted Prime Minister Bart De Wever to offer his government's resignation earlier this week, has exposed fresh fault lines between the country's Dutch- and French-speaking communities.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/valerie-van-peel-nva-aangekomen-op-paleis-voor-gesprek-over-b~1793247532380/), Van Peel gave no comment on arrival or departure, underscoring the tension surrounding the talks. She was the last of the coalition party chairs to be received by the King, who has held De Wever's resignation "in beraad" — under consideration — while he meets each party leader in turn.

## How the crisis unfolded

The government's inner cabinet broke up in the early hours of Monday 2 November without agreement on a multi-year budget, after a marathon overnight session failed to bridge the gap. De Wever travelled to the Palace that morning to tender his resignation, meeting the King for more than 90 minutes.

Rather than accept it, King Philippe launched consultations with the chairs of the coalition parties spread across the week: Yvan Verougstraete (Les Engagés) and Sammy Mahdi (CD&V) on Tuesday, Conner Rousseau (Vooruit) and Georges-Louis Bouchez (MR) on Wednesday, and Van Peel (N-VA) on Thursday. De Wever is due back at the Palace on Friday at 10:30, when it is widely expected the King will refuse the resignation and send the parties back to the negotiating table.

## What is blocking a deal

The dispute centres on roughly €10 billion in budgetary consolidation and how it should be achieved. The centre-right parties and the Flemish socialist Vooruit are split over the balance between spending cuts and new revenue, with a particular flashpoint over contributions from large fortunes and the healthcare budget.

Sammy Mahdi of CD&V arrived at the Palace visibly furious, warning there was no room for political games. Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem cautioned that Belgium risks drifting toward "Greek situations," while Vooruit's Conner Rousseau insisted any agreement must be "fair" and that "everyone must do their part." MR leader Bouchez maintained his hard line against new taxes, arguing the answer lies in controlling public spending and reviving growth.

## Wallonia's divided view

The crisis has also sharpened the debate in French-speaking Wallonia. As [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/sommigen-juichen-het-falen-van-regering-de-wever-toe-hoe-kijkt-wallonie-naar-de-regeringscrisis~a7760e59/) reported, some in the south openly welcome the failure of the De Wever government — a striking reflection of how the impasse is deepening the country's linguistic divide. The outlet's framing quote, drawn from RTL journalist Christophe Deborsu and political commentator Alain Gerlache, captured the mood: "Door de impasse is De Wever toch wat van zijn glans verloren" — because of the impasse, De Wever has lost some of his shine.

Yet Walloon politics is itself split. Paul Magnette, leader of the Francophone socialists (PS), ruled out rescuing a "dying government," saying on RTL-TVi that he would not come to the rescue of a dying government and that if the government cannot govern, it should return to the voters. Les Engagés, by contrast, explicitly wants extra revenue from large fortunes, while the liberal MR refuses new taxes — an internal Francophone rift that mirrors the national deadlock.

## Economic pressure mounts

The financial stakes are rising. Jean Deboutte, director of Belgium's Federal Debt Agency, said the failure of talks had widened the spread between Belgian and German bonds by about ten basis points early in the week — "100 percent" a consequence of the failed negotiations. Belgium's borrowing costs stand at their highest level in roughly 14 years, and the OECD has repeatedly urged the country to bring its budget under control.

For a second consecutive year, Belgium is preparing to run on "provisional twelfths" — an emergency mechanism under which the state spends one-twelfth of the previous year's outlays each month, because a full budget cannot be passed in time.

## What to watch

Friday's royal audience is the pivotal moment. Should King Philippe refuse the resignation, as expected, the parties will face renewed pressure to reach a compromise — though several figures have warned the exercise may amount to little more than a delay of execution. Les Engagés' Maxime Prévot has pegged the odds of new elections at "50/50," and analysts caution that a snap vote could cost six to eight months of coalition formation while doing nothing to ease the underlying budget arithmetic.

For now, the government survives — but, as one commentator put it, it hangs by a silk thread.
