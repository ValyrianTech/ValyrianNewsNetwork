# Greenhouse Gases Hit Record Highs as CO2 Nears 427 ppm

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases reached unprecedented highs in 2025, the UN's weather agency reported, locking the planet into further warming for decades and widening the gap between the physical state of the atmosphere and international climate goals.

In its annual Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22, released in Geneva on 27 October 2026, the [World Meteorological Organization](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high) said the globally averaged surface concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2) was **426.8 parts per million (ppm)** in 2025 — or **153% of the pre-industrial baseline of 1750**. Concentrations of methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O), the second and third most important long-lived greenhouse gases tied to human activity, also set new records.

The bulletin was published ahead of the COP31 UN climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye, running from 9 to 20 November 2026, where negotiators are seeking to turn climate commitments into concrete action.

## 'Another year, another new record'

"Another year, another new record. Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies," WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett said.

"The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue. CO2 has a very long life-time and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries."

Speaking to journalists in Geneva, Barrett stressed the compounding nature of each incremental increase. "Every additional part per million matters because it traps additional heat," she said, as [UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/10/1168677) reported. "In effect, we are supercharging the ocean and the atmosphere. That means that heat waves, tropical cyclones, and rainstorms are intensifying faster than our historical baselines prepared us for."

## Concentrations, not emissions

The bulletin measures the amount of gas that has accumulated in the atmosphere, not the annual flow of new emissions. This distinction is central to understanding why records keep falling even as some countries make progress on cutting emissions: about half of all CO2 emitted each year remains in the atmosphere, while the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans.

Because CO2 persists for centuries, today's concentrations commit the planet to warming that will outlast any near-term mitigation effort. WMO reported that when the bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 level measured by its Global Atmosphere Watch network was 377.5 ppm — compared with roughly 427 ppm today. Growth rates have roughly tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from about 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year in the 2011–2020 decade.

From 2024 to 2025, the global average CO2 concentration rose by **2.8 ppm** — above the decadal average but below the exceptional surge of the previous year.

"The more modest growth rate from 2024 to 2025 reflects recovery of the land carbon sinks and reduced fire carbon emissions in 2025 after the strong 2023/2024 El Niño, which reduced the efficiency of the biosphere through more drought, drier vegetation and more wildfires," said WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova.

She cautioned that the respite is likely to be brief. "A 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," she said. "We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026 because of fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño which will impact land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024. The risks of reduced CO2 uptake by the ocean are also increasing in our changing climate."

## Methane and nitrous oxide

The bulletin also detailed record concentrations of the two other major long-lived greenhouse gases.

Methane reached **1,948 parts per billion (ppb)** in 2025 — **267% of the 1750 level**. It accounts for about 16% of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases but has a far shorter atmospheric lifetime than CO2. Roughly 35% of methane comes from natural sources such as wetlands, while about 65% is anthropogenic — from cattle, rice farming, fossil fuel extraction, landfills and biomass burning.

Nitrous oxide reached **339.1 ppb**, or **126% above 1750**. It is the third most important long-lived greenhouse gas and stems from both natural sources and human activities such as biomass burning, fertiliser use and industrial processes.

The warming effect of long-lived greenhouse gases, as measured by the NOAA Annual Greenhouse Gas Index, increased by 56% between 1990 and 2025 — with CO2 responsible for about 81% of that increase, known as radiative forcing.

## Two overlooked gases

Beyond the headline figures, the bulletin flagged two potent but less abundant trace gases tied to the energy transition that could undermine its climate benefits: sulphur hexafluoride (SF6) and molecular hydrogen (H2).

SF6, used mainly as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, has risen steadily over three decades in step with global electrification. It has an atmospheric lifetime of more than 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly **24,300 times that of CO2** — and its increase in 2025 was a record high.

Hydrogen concentrations rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024. Hydrogen is not itself a greenhouse gas, but it influences atmospheric chemistry and has a strong indirect warming effect. As it is increasingly used as an energy storage medium for hard-to-decarbonise sectors such as steelmaking and shipping, leakage from production facilities, pipelines, storage tanks and fuel cells poses a growing concern.

"Both H2 leakage and SF6 persistence represent potential threats that could undermine the climate benefits of the energy transition," WMO warned.

## A widening gap with climate goals

The bulletin's findings land against a stark policy backdrop. Days before its release, the UN Environment Programme's [Emissions Gap Report 2026](https://unric.org/en/unep-emissions-gap-report-2026-1-5-degree-overshoot/) concluded that overshooting the 1.5°C Paris target is now unavoidable, shifting the focus toward minimising and reversing the overshoot.

The WMO data provides the physical evidence behind such warnings: concentrations are at their highest in the industrial era and still rising, not stabilising. As [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779336/article/2026-10-27/une-nouvelle-annee-un-nouveau-record-la-concentration-gaz-effet-de-serre-atteint) reported in its AFP-sourced coverage, the three principal greenhouse gases all set records in 2025 — a message the agency timed deliberately for the negotiators gathering in Antalya.

COP31 opened on 9 November under the presidency of Murat Kurum, who said the 1.5°C goal "remains our guide." The summit convened amid low expectations after previous conferences were criticised for producing little agreement on cutting emissions, even as extreme heat in Europe in summer 2026 was estimated to have killed tens of thousands.

## What's next

The bulletin's central message is one of locked-in momentum. Even under rapid future emissions cuts, the CO2 already in the atmosphere will continue to trap heat for decades and centuries, driving more intense heat waves, storms and rainfall.

With a powerful El Niño developing and ocean carbon uptake at growing risk, WMO expects concentrations to climb again in 2026. Whether the negotiators in Antalya can translate that scientific reality into accelerated action — and close the gap between the atmosphere's trajectory and the goals set to restrain it — is the question the coming fortnight must answer.
