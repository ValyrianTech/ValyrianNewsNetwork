# CPAS Need €210M More After Unemployment Reform

Belgium's public social welfare centres (CPAS) say they need roughly €210 million in additional federal funding to absorb the fallout from the government's unemployment reform, arguing the money set aside for the task was "considerably underestimated." The shortfall surfaced in the second 2026 budget adjustment, examined in the Finance Committee of the Chamber of Representatives, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-budget-necessaire-a-ete-considerablement-sous-estime-les-cpas-ont-besoin-de-210-millions-d-euros-supplementaires-suite-a-la-reforme-du-chomage-11813484).

The €300 million originally earmarked to help the centres cope with an influx of people losing their unemployment benefits has already been outstripped: €302 million was paid out in just the first nine months of the year.

## A forecast that missed reality

The mechanism of the shortfall is a single, off-target assumption. The government estimated that about one-third (33%) of people excluded from unemployment would turn to a CPAS for a *revenu d'intégration* — the integration income known as the RIS. In practice, the number of new beneficiaries is "clearly higher" than 33%, the average cost per case is higher, and spending began earlier than anticipated, the budget's *exposé des motifs* (statement of grounds) states.

"The necessary budget was considerably underestimated," the document reads. "The number of new beneficiaries is clearly higher than the 33% forecast, the average cost is higher, and spending began to be committed faster than expected. In the first nine months, €302 million has already been paid and, based on the latest figures on entries and average cost, the deficit is estimated at €208 million." The headline figure of €210 million reflects rounding.

## Why a CPAS file costs more than a benefit cheque

The gap reflects a structural difference between the two systems. Unemployment benefits are a near-automatic entitlement based on a person's work history; they take no account of household income, home ownership or savings. The RIS, by contrast, is a *residual* right: before granting it, a CPAS must exhaustively investigate every other resource — assets, savings, alimony, other household income — and, since 1 March 2026, globalise the household's income. That makes each file far more labour-intensive and costly to administer.

The reform itself has been rolling out since 1 January 2026, with the time limit on benefits taking effect on 1 March 2026. From that date, unemployment benefits are capped at a maximum of two years: anyone who worked at least one year in the last three years is entitled to up to one year, with each additional four months worked adding a month of entitlement, and those who worked at least five years can claim up to 24 months.

## Warnings that went unheeded

The centres say they saw this coming. As early as March, the Federation of CPAS estimated the reform could cost them nearly €1 billion (€995 million) between 2026 and 2029, uncompensated by the federal government — a projection that itself assumed only one-third of excluded people would seek a CPAS, as [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-reforme-du-chomage-pourrait-couter-pres-d-un-milliard-d-euros-aux-cpas-d-ici-2029-non-compenses-par-le-federal-11689203). That breakdown included about €631 million in missing RIS payments, €170 million in uncompensated social aid and €194 million in unbudgeted staffing costs.

In May, the three regional federations representing the centres — the VVSG for Flanders, the Federation of CPAS de Wallonie and Brulocalis for Brussels — [urgently demanded](https://www.rtbf.be/article/reforme-du-chomage-les-cpas-demandent-d-urgence-des-mesures-de-soutien-temporaires-face-a-l-afflux-de-dossiers-11719969) temporary support measures from federal Social Integration Minister Anneleen Van Bossuyt (N-VA). Dorothée Klein, president of the CPAS of Namur and of the Federation of CPAS, put the strain in blunt terms.

"CPAS are asking for time, money and a little respect, especially for the social workers," she said. "The so-called official figures given about the reform do not correspond at all to what we are living on the ground," she added, calling for "reliable monitoring."

Staffing is a central concern. "We're reaching social workers who each manage 110, 120 files. Obviously it's not in every CPAS, but it exists," Klein said. Myriem Amrani, president of the CPAS of Saint-Gilles in Brussels, described the burden of the 1 March revision of household income as "a colossal administrative overload."

## A mounting ledger

The €210 million demand is only the latest marker in a widening divergence between the Arizona coalition's cost forecasts and the field experience of the centres. By mid-October, RTBF reported that there are now more people relying on CPAS than there are unemployed people in Belgium. A Constitutional Court ruling in September validated the reform, with the exception of the time limit for the long-term unemployed. Wallonia, home to a higher concentration of excluded jobseekers, bears the largest burden: 83,025 people were projected to be excluded from unemployment in the region by July 2027.

The federal compensation formula compounds the structural problem. For people newly entering the RIS system, the state promised to reimburse CPAS 100% in year one, 90% in year two, 80% in year three and 75% thereafter — a sliding scale that leaves permanent, unfunded costs in place.

## What to watch

The immediate question is whether Minister Van Bossuyt and the government will grant the full €210 million in the second 2026 budget adjustment, or only part of it. Just as consequential is whether the government accepts the CPAS demand for a transparent, joint monitoring system to replace the disputed 33% assumption — a mechanism the centres describe as essential to tracking a reform whose real costs are still unfolding.

If take-up keeps exceeding one-third, the earlier €1 billion estimate could itself prove too low. And with the federal government already contending with a broader political crisis, the budget adjustment is set to test whether the reform's fiscal promises can survive contact with the reality on the ground.
