# China's AI Diagnosis Boom Raises New Healthcare Questions

For a growing number of patients in China, the first stop on a trip to see a doctor is no longer a search engine but an AI chatbot. "Doctor, AI says I have this disease — is it right?" has become an increasingly common opening line in outpatient clinics, according to a [report by the Economic Information Daily](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20261110/91fcf1c6b72b437ca9f4d0c39bbd70a3/c.html), the business outlet of the state-run Xinhua news agency. Patients now walk into consulting rooms, pull out their phones and recite an AI-generated verdict before describing their own symptoms — a habit experts say brings both convenience and new risks.

AI-assisted diagnosis, marketed as a fast, low-threshold complement to public health education and self-screening, is spreading quickly. By March 2026, AI-native apps in China had reached 440 million monthly active users, according to QuestMobile data cited by [China News Service](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/jk/2026/10-21/10718618.shtml), turning large language models into a primary gateway to health information. But doctors warn that over-reliance on machine conclusions can produce one-sided judgements, delay proper treatment and lead to inappropriate medication.

## A phenomenon rooted in scarce medical resources

The trend has multiple drivers. "Many people want to understand, before a visit, what disease their symptoms might correspond to, which department to register for, how to choose a doctor and the general treatment approach," Chen Biao, an attending physician in the otology department at Beijing Tongren Hospital, told the Economic Information Daily. Young and middle-aged people are the main users, but Chen said more elderly patients, people with pronounced health anxiety and patients with rare or hard-to-diagnose conditions are also increasingly turning to AI.

Liu Yuanli, a State Council counselor and tenured professor at the Peking Union Medical College's School of Health Management and Policy, said AI health tools offer preliminary symptom checks, triage advice and health education with lower privacy concerns. "Behind this phenomenon is the reality of rising public health literacy coexisting with scarce high-quality medical resources," he said, noting that people hope to save the time and cost of in-person visits and ease the anxiety caused by information gaps.

A dual-end survey by Fudan University's Institute of Aging illustrates how far the practice has spread. Roughly seven in ten surveyed doctors said they had encountered patients who used AI to research health information, with 36% seeing such cases two to four times a month and 24% four or more times a week, according to China News Service. Separately, Beijing Daily [reported](https://www.workercn.cn/c/2026-10-29/8928804.shtml) that 20% to 30% of patients mention AI during consultations, according to multiple outpatient doctors.

## Convenience does not mean accuracy

The risks are most acute where patients cannot speak for themselves. Yang Ye, deputy director of pediatrics at Beijing Hepingli Hospital, described a six-year-old girl with a history of bronchial asthma whose parents fed "allergy, asthma, cough" into an AI tool. It returned a diagnosis of an acute asthma attack and recommended nebulizer treatment, so the family insisted on taking only nebulizer medication home. When Yang examined the child, she found not only wheezing but localized crackles — a sign of pneumonia. The parents initially refused blood tests and a chest X-ray. Two days later the child developed a fever and rapid breathing and was diagnosed with pneumonia.

Pediatrics is nicknamed the "mute specialty" because infants cannot accurately describe symptoms, Yang explained. AI "cannot see or hear" and cannot perform a physical exam. Parents, meanwhile, instinctively select information that confirms their own guesses, creating a "one-sided input, reinforced guess" loop.

Such cases are not isolated. A 2024 study in *JAMA Pediatrics* tested ChatGPT-3.5 against 100 real pediatric cases and found a clinical diagnosis error rate of 83%, the newspaper reported. Chen of Beijing Tongren Hospital added that blind faith in AI can drive over-medicalization: probabilistic models recommend large numbers of tests that are often unnecessary, heightening anxiety, wasting personal spending and crowding out public medical resources.

Liu attributed the public's cognitive bias to three compounded psychological effects — a transfer of technology authority, anchoring, and an illusion of being informed — in which people mistake standardized AI output for an absolutely objective answer and selectively trust information that matches their own assumptions.

## A role for AI, but clear boundaries

China's policy direction is to welcome the technology while drawing firm lines around it. The 15th Five-Year Plan outline calls for the orderly promotion of digital-intelligent technology in assisted diagnosis, precision medicine, health management, insurance services and care for the elderly and disabled, the newspaper noted. "AI-assisted diagnosis is an inevitable direction for medical digitalization; we should not give up eating for fear of choking," Liu said. In a smart healthcare system, he argued, AI's core role should always be to "assist decision-making, empower medical staff and serve the public."

AI can independently handle health education, symptom organization, triage guidance and report interpretation, Liu said. But diagnosis, prescriptions and treatment plans — which involve life safety and medical liability — must be carried out by licensed, accountable clinicians who can dynamically assess a patient's condition. "Writing a prescription is a legally responsible medical act," said Li Zixiao, vice president of Beijing Tiantan Hospital. "AI can provide diagnostic support and suggestions, but the prescription must still be issued by the attending physician and manually verified."

The regulatory framework has been built up steadily. The 2022 trial rules on internet-diagnosis supervision state that AI software may not impersonate or replace a physician in providing diagnosis, and strictly prohibit AI from auto-generating prescriptions. In November 2024 the National Health Commission issued a reference guide outlining 84 AI application scenarios, including intelligent pre-consultation, assisted decision-making and assisted treatment. In October 2025, the commission, the National Development and Reform Commission and other departments issued implementation opinions deploying AI across eight areas, from grassroots care to traditional Chinese medicine and public health.

Liabilities are also being clarified. Experts interviewed by [People's Daily](https://tech.cnr.cn/gstj/20260123/t20260123_527503526.shtml) agreed that the medical institution is always the primary responsible party in AI-assisted diagnosis. Deng Yong, a professor of health law at Beijing University of Chinese Medicine, said current law broadly treats medical AI as a product: if a technical fault causes an erroneous decision, the device or agent's provider generally bears liability, and the institution may seek recourse. He recommended refining regulations and strengthening approval and supervision of AI medical products.

## The communication squeeze

The rise of AI is also reshaping the doctor-patient relationship. Wang Yue, a professor at Peking University's School of Medical Humanities, said the traditional doctor-centered model of communication is facing "collapse" and argued doctors must shift from a paternalistic stance to a more collaborative one. "Rather than dislike patients who study up, cultivate patients who study up," he told China News Service, suggesting doctors help patients filter AI information and explain which parts apply.

That takes time. With consultations often lasting about five minutes, doctors struggle both to explain discrepancies with AI and to complete proper clinical work. A 2018 study in *The Lancet* covering 136 tertiary hospitals and nearly 28,000 patients identified an eight-minute "inflection point": below eight minutes, each added minute markedly raised the probability of a positive patient evaluation. Hubei province's health commission has piloted a "sufficient consultation" scheme limiting experts to about 30 slots per half-day so that average communication time reaches six minutes or more.

The survey data suggests most patients still defer to their doctors: when AI and doctors disagree, 43% follow the doctor directly and 35% mainly follow the doctor but want an explanation. And most doctors are not hostile to the technology — 65% were neutral on patients using AI, while 16% explicitly opposed it. "Bringing AI to a doctor is often not out of distrust," said Shen Qi, a professor at Fudan University's Institute of Aging, "but a genuine wish to understand one's condition." If doctors explain the difference clearly, she said, it can actually strengthen trust in the treatment plan.

## What to watch

Experts broadly agree that AI's most valuable uses lie in preparation and understanding — organizing a medical history, drafting questions to ask, or explaining terminology in a prescription — rather than in making decisions. A Beijing resident who kept a 10,000-word health diary over six years recently fed it, along with new symptoms, into the DeepSeek model, which advised taking an elderly relative to a fever clinic; the man was diagnosed with a viral infection the same night, Beijing Daily reported.

Still unresolved are the device-approval standards for AI diagnostic products and how liability will be assigned in practice. Liu proposed a three-pronged approach: teach the public to distinguish "reference" from "diagnosis," strengthen disclaimers and risk warnings in products, and clarify the attributes, market access and liability boundaries of AI medical products. The task ahead, experts say, is not to ban the technology but to build a "human-machine collaboration" model in which AI is more accurate, more transparent and more compliant — and where the final call always rests with a human doctor.
