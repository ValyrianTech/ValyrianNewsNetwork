# Bomb Threats Disrupt 30+ European Flights in a Week

A wave of hoax bomb threats forced emergency landings, fighter-jet escorts and airport searches across at least 16 countries this week, disrupting more than 30 civilian flights across Europe, including a Polish airliner bound for Brussels. No explosives were found on any aircraft, and authorities have stressed that the incidents remain under investigation, with no confirmed link to any single actor.

The disruption peaked on Thursday, 5 November, when at least 25 passenger flights were affected in a single day, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/). It followed a smaller cluster on Tuesday, when seven planes were searched or diverted, and an incident on Monday involving a flight from London Luton to Sofia that was diverted to Budapest.

## Belgium directly affected

Belgium found itself at the centre of the scare. A LOT Polish Airlines flight from Warsaw to Brussels landed safely at Zaventem airport after receiving a threat. Belgium's federal police conducted a risk analysis and concluded that "no additional measures were needed," the airport's press office said. LOT said an onboard security check and further searches in Brussels found "no hazardous or suspicious items."

A second Belgian-linked incident involved a Ryanair flight from Charleroi to Pescara in Italy. According to VRT NWS, a note bearing the word "bom" (bomb) was found in the aircraft toilets at 13:13 local time; the alarm was lifted once the threat was confirmed false, and the flight proceeded to Italy.

## Diversions and fighter jets across the continent

Elsewhere, the threats triggered dramatic precautions. An Aer Lingus Airbus A320 flying from Dublin to Rome was diverted to a French military air base at Évreux, roughly 100 kilometres west of Paris, and was intercepted and escorted by French fighter jets. The French authorities said the 115 passengers and six crew members were unharmed, according to [NOS](https://nos.nl/artikel/2638119-opnieuw-bommeldingen-europese-vluchten-vliegtuig-breekt-vlucht-naar-rome-af) — a figure that differs from VRT's earlier report of 157 occupants aboard.

A Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome was escorted by Italian air force jets into Fiumicino, where searches found nothing. Aegean Airlines and Ryanair flights landed at Milan Malpensa; a Wizz Air flight from Milan to Warsaw diverted to Vienna; and three Ryanair flights bound for Sofia were affected, two landing in the Bulgarian capital and a third diverted to Plovdiv. Czech police investigated four planes landing in Prague and rated the reports of "low relevance."

In Bucharest, Romania's intelligence agency reported two additional threats on Thursday, including a KLM flight to Tel Aviv with a stopover in the Romanian capital and an earlier Wizz Air flight from Milan carrying nearly 200 people. None of the aircraft was found to contain explosives.

## A group claims responsibility, but officials counsel caution

An anti-feminist group calling itself "the Male State" claimed responsibility for some of the emailed threats. According to [Nine.com.au](https://www.nine.com.au/world-news/uk-europe/europe-flights-bomb-threats-multiple-flights-forced-to-divert-20261105-p61buy.html), which reported with the Associated Press and CNN, the group emailed bomb threats to a logistics company working with Rome Fiumicino Airport, naming two flights bound for the Italian capital. "You're all complicit in a criminal plot, we want to annihilate you for this," the message read in Italian. "We, the fighters of the Male State, with our actions will leave a mark on history. Your feminist regime will never forget it." The message also warned that "the explosion will occur in 60 minutes" and that "your skies will turn red."

The name likely refers to a Russian organisation, Muzhskoi Legion, according to the BBC. Male State is a Russian far-right, ultranationalist movement founded in 2016 by Vladislav Pozdnyakov, who was convicted in 2018 of inciting hatred against women; the group later became a prominent online supporter of the war in Ukraine.

Despite the claim, EU officials cautioned against drawing firm conclusions. "It is too early to draw conclusions on whether the reported incidents are linked," said Apostolos Tzitzikostas, the EU Commissioner for Sustainable Transport and Tourism, speaking to reporters in Dublin. "Any threats against civilians are unacceptable. National investigations remain ongoing." He added that an EU-level aviation security information-sharing mechanism had been activated, but that "we don't have information yet whether this is a cyberattack."

UK Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander described the events as a series of "malicious hoax threats."

## The shadow of hybrid warfare

The sheer scale of the disruption fed growing concerns about so-called hybrid attacks against Europe. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/05/bomb-threats-european-flights-airports-disruption-explainer) noted, fake threats occur daily, but the multiplicity of this week's incidents was described as unusual. Sofia and Milan each recorded six incidents, followed by Rome with five and Prague with four; Ryanair was the hardest-hit airline, with eight reported cases. More than 27,400 flights were scheduled across Europe on Thursday, according to Eurocontrol, meaning the affected flights were a tiny fraction of overall traffic.

Italian Defence Minister Guido Crosetto framed the hoaxes within a broader pattern of destabilisation. "It seems to me that every day now, from refrigerators to blood supplies in France, to these fake alarms, there's someone who has an interest in stoking tensions," he said. "It's called 'under-threshold warfare' because it's below the attribution threshold. It's done that way on purpose, so no one can pin the blame."

The threats came amid a series of other disruptive incidents across Europe in late 2026, including cyberattacks targeting supermarket refrigeration systems in several countries, suspected sabotage and arson, and reported drone incursions near Russia and Ukraine. European officials have repeatedly warned that Russia, and to a lesser degree Iran, conducts operations designed to destabilise and sow fear while remaining below the threshold of clear attribution. The Kremlin has dismissed such accusations: spokesperson Dmitry Peskov called it "absurd" to blame Moscow for related incidents, citing "such baseless accusations against our country, implicating it in everything."

## Why threats are so disruptive

Even a false alarm carries real costs. Joji Waites, director of flight safety at the British Airline Pilots' Association, explained how airlines grade threats as "red" (credible), "amber" (partly credible) or "green" (not credible). "Usually if a plane is still on the tarmac that will mean an evacuation, and if it is in the air the priority will be to land the plane as soon and as safely as possible," he said.

The ready availability of airline schedules, aircraft types and technical specifications online allows hoaxers to craft detailed, credible-sounding threats — something a generation ago would have required insider knowledge. Diversions to military bases or quiet airfields, fighter-jet escorts and full security searches are expensive and highly disruptive, but airlines say they have little choice when facing any remotely credible threat. "Safety is the number one priority for the industry and we take all threats seriously," said Nick Careen, senior vice president for operations, safety and security at the International Air Transport Association.

## What to watch for next

National investigations continue across more than a dozen countries, and it remains unclear whether a single actor is behind the entire campaign or whether the threats are a mix of coordinated action and copycat hoaxes. Some incidents — including the Brussels LOT flight and the Charleroi toilet note — may be unrelated to the "Male State" emails. As VRT aviation journalist Gianni Paelinck put it, "I think we can gradually speak of a pattern."

For travellers, the practical picture is reassuring: every incident this week was confirmed to be a false alarm. The deeper concern is strategic. If the goal of such campaigns is to create disruption and erode confidence rather than to cause casualties, then the measure of their success may lie less in found explosives than in the chaos, cost and fear they generate across Europe's open societies.
