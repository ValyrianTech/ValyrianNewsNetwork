# Two Flemish Writers Build Bestsellers Without a Big Publisher

As Boektopia, Flanders' largest book fair, arrives in Antwerp, two picture books that topped the children's charts at its previous stop came from no major publishing house. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/razend-populair-op-boektopia-zonder-grote-uitgeverij/), the self-published titles *In de puree* by Bieke Biesbrouck and *Wilma Wonder en de circusbaby* by Hanne Luyten ranked in the top three best-selling books for children aged 0 to 6 at the fair's previous edition in Kortrijk.

## Two writers, two paths to independence

Hanne Luyten founded her own publishing house, Het Hannekesnest, in 2021 after writing three books for another publisher. The experience left her curious about the parts of the process she had never controlled. "At a certain point you hand over your manuscript," she told VRT. "I became so curious about that creative process that I decided to take the reins myself. Actually it happened to me rather than being planned — there was never a grand strategy."

Bieke Biesbrouck's route was more unusual still. She and her husband Mathieu Haspeslagh launched Höngry in 2021 around a concept the major houses had already turned down: making fruit and vegetables appealing to young children. "The big publishers found it not commercially interesting," she said. "That's why we started creating games and books ourselves."

## Investing in the unknown

For all the freedom it offers, self-publishing is far from a low-cost entry point. Both writers stress the upfront capital required — illustrations, printing, storage — with no guarantee the public will respond. "Can anyone start a publisher? Probably, but you have to dare to invest," Biesbrouck said. "That requires enormous capital, with no guarantee the books will be liked."

Luyten echoed the uncertainty around print runs. "In advance you don't know if a title will catch on," she said. "The larger the print run, the lower the cost per book. Even after five years it stays exciting."

The workload, both warn, is frequently underestimated. "You're a jack-of-all-trades," Biesbrouck said. "You're logistics manager, brand manager, contact person for the printer and also a writer." Luyten called it a full-time job: "It's much more work than it seems at first."

## The advantages of holding the rights

Yet the model pays off in ways a traditional contract often does not. Luyten points to the international rights she now controls entirely. "Three of our titles will appear abroad in the coming years," she said. "In total we have sold international rights seven times: twice for Brazil, three times for South Korea, once for France and once for Turkey. I don't have to consult anyone about that."

Biesbrouck argues that independence also extends a book's commercial life. Traditional publishers, she notes, organise their year around three key release periods — spring, summer and autumn — so a title that does not sell quickly is soon pushed aside by the next catalogue. "With your own publishing house you can give your books attention and keep them relevant much longer," she said. "You invest in a book, but it also gets a longer chance to earn something back."

## Community as the new gatekeeper

What replaces a publisher's marketing engine, both women suggest, is a mix of recognisable brands, direct audience relationships and physical presence. Luyten describes building "a very loyal community" over the years, one that now includes readers whose own children have outgrown her target age but who still buy the books as gifts. "The publishing trade can sometimes be a lonely profession, but thanks to my community I feel well surrounded," she said.

Biesbrouck emphasises breadth alongside digital reach. "We are not famous Flemish celebrities. We really started from zero," she said. "Social media certainly plays a role, but it's also important to be stocked in various bookshops and to give many readings. That way you're not dependent only on your channel or the algorithm."

## What's next

Their books' showing in Kortrijk — Biesbrouck's title held a top-three place across four consecutive days — comes as Boektopia moves on to its Antwerp leg. "Last year we had the best-selling picture book of the whole of Boektopia," Luyten said. "A place in that top three is a fantastic confirmation that people still manage to find us."

The broader significance reaches beyond two authors. As production tools, direct sales and social platforms lower the barriers to reaching readers, the traditional publisher looks less like a prerequisite and more like one option among several — provided an author is willing to shoulder the capital, the risk and the workload. VRT's profile is built largely on the authors' own accounts and offers no independent sales figures beyond the fair's charts, so the durability of the model outside celebrated cases remains an open question. For now, though, two women who started from zero are proof that a bestseller no longer requires a big name behind it.
