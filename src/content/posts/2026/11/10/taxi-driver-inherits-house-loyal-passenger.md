# Taxi Driver Inherits House From Loyal Passenger

For years, Joeri Cools drove Annie around the Ghent region as her taxi driver. Somewhere along the way, the two struck up a friendship that spanned very different worlds. This week, Cools learned just how much that bond meant to her: Annie, who had no heirs, left him her house in her will without ever telling him, [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/10/oud-taxichauffeur-joeri-erft-huis-van-vaste-klant-het-kwam-uit/) reports.

"It actually fell out of heaven," Cools told VRT NWS's Radio 2 Oost-Vlaanderen. On Monday, coincidentally his birthday, he sat at the notary's office to sign the papers.

## A Bond Built in Traffic Jams

Annie was a longtime regular of Cools's taxi. She had studied social work and spent decades in Brussels campaigning for better housing for students. Cools, by contrast, grew up in a difficult home situation.

"I also have a social past, but the other side of the coin," he said. "Through my stories and by listening to Annie's stories, a close bond developed between us. In a traffic jam you can easily talk to each other for a quarter of an hour or 20 minutes. When you see each other often, things become more relaxed."

That closeness was the foundation of the bequest. Annie drew up her will in the early years of their taxi relationship and never mentioned it to Cools, who went on driving her for years afterward.

"It never occurred to me that she would have done that. She never said anything about it. But apparently she handled it very consciously," Cools said.

## A Tsunami of Feelings

Cools learned of the inheritance around the same time he heard that Annie had died. She had moved into a care facility around the coronavirus period and died of old age, leaving no heirs.

"A tsunami of feelings went through my head. I couldn't sleep for two nights over it," he recalled. Eventually he called the notary to ask how to proceed.

The former driver says he ferried thousands of customers over the years and always tried to put the passenger first. "Not the taxi meter was central, but the customer," he said. Being rewarded this way, he added, feels like an honour. Via the radio, he wanted to thank Annie one last time: "I hope she can listen along from somewhere. I want to express my sincere thanks to Annie for this beautiful gift."

## A Windfall Heavily Taxed

The gift, however, comes with a substantial bill. Because Cools is not a relative of Annie, he falls into Belgium's "others" category — the highest inheritance-tax bracket. Under the Brussels rates cited by VRT NWS, he would pay 40% on the first €50,000, 55% on the portion between €50,000 and €75,000, 65% between €75,000 and €175,000, and 80% on everything above that.

For a home valued at €500,000, that works out to roughly €358,750 in inheritance tax — nearly 72% of the property's value. Rates for non-relatives are lower in Flanders, ranging from 25% to 55%. Which region collects the tax depends on where the deceased had her fiscal domicile for the longest period in the five years before death; the article's use of Brussels rates suggests Annie's ties lay there, consistent with her decades of work in the capital.

Cools does not have to pay the sum out of pocket. He can sell the inherited home and use the proceeds to settle the tax bill.

## A Reform That Does Not Reach Him

Annie fits the profile of a recent Flemish reform designed to help people exactly like her. Since 1 January 2026, single people without a partner or (grand)children in Flanders can designate heirs in their will to inherit up to €100,000 at the low rates of 3% and 9%, as [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/10/03/erfbelasting-vlaamse-regering-eerste-fase/) when the measure was announced. The favourable scheme does not apply in Brussels — which, on the article's account, is the regime Cools faces.

The story thus carries a quiet public-information message beneath its warmth: the tax treatment of a gift depends heavily on where the deceased was fiscally resident, and the gap between regions can be steep. For Cools, the inheritance remains, above all, an honour — a testament to years of conversation across the seats of a taxi.

## What's Next

Having signed the papers, Cools now faces the practical question of settling the tax — likely by selling the home. The case also illustrates a broader point for Belgian policymakers: as the number of childless singles grows, the rules on passing assets to non-relatives are increasingly consequential. Flanders has begun to adapt. Whether other regions follow is an open question.
