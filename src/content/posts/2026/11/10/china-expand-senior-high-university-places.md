# China to Expand Senior High and University Places

China will increase the supply of places at ordinary senior high schools and higher education institutions during the 15th Five-Year Plan period (2026–2030), a senior planning official said on 10 November, as the country confronts a demographic squeeze that has left its education system with shrinking lower-level rolls but fast-growing demand at the top. Huang Weiru, deputy director-general of the Social Development Department at the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC), told a press conference in Beijing that the government would expand capacity "through multiple channels" to keep the senior-high entrance bottleneck from tightening further, according to [Xinhua](https://education.news.cn/20261110/c86c56a4895347748e205917251c5660/c.html).

## A Demographic Reversal

The announcement rests on an unusual demographic moment. Huang noted that China's kindergarten, primary- and junior-high-school-age populations have already peaked, while the senior-high and university-age cohorts are still expanding — a structural mismatch that is forcing planners to reallocate resources upward. During the 14th Five-Year Plan (2020–2025), combined enrollment in kindergarten, primary and junior high school fell by nearly 17 million, she said, a decline many families have felt in the form of easier access to preschool and primary places, as [Lianhe Zaobao](https://www.zaobao.com.sg/news/china/story20261110-9973626) reported from the same briefing.

That decline is a direct consequence of a falling birth rate. Xinhua's companion reporting, cited by Zaobao, puts total kindergarten, primary and junior high enrollment at about 189 million in 2025 — even as the number of births fell to its lowest level since at least 1949 and the population aged 65 and over surpassed 223 million. Huang acknowledged that some surplus preschool and compulsory-education facilities can now be shared across school stages within a county, used to advance smaller-class teaching, or converted into venues for labor and aesthetic education.

## Expanding the Upper Tiers

On senior high schools, Huang said the NDRC would continue to support the construction of teaching buildings, dormitories, canteens and sports grounds — investments intended both to improve conditions and to add physical capacity. "Anxiety about being unable to attend ordinary senior high school due to insufficient places is expected to be further eased," she said.

For higher education, the plan emphasizes breadth as well as quality: supporting qualified localities to establish several new "new-type research universities" (新型研究型大学), building a batch of high-level vocational undergraduate schools with distinctive characteristics, and expanding high-quality university resources through multiple channels. The stated aim, Huang said, is to give students "more choices."

## Three Principles of Adaptation

Huang framed the strategy around three principles of adaptation. First, resources must adapt to the downward trend in the total school-age population — meaning optimization of preschool and compulsory-education allocation. Second, they must adapt to structural change in the school-age population — the senior-high and higher-education expansion. Third, they must adapt to regional differentiation, so that equal access to education is better guaranteed for all urban and rural groups.

The approach follows an established trajectory. Reporting earlier this year described a "three-stage school-age population peak" (三阶段学龄人口排浪式达峰) strategy under which China would prioritize ordinary senior-high construction and expand quality undergraduate capacity — the 10 November briefing appears to operationalize that commitment.

## Why It Matters

The push addresses two pressures at once. On the supply side, it responds to a demographic wave passing through the system — cohorts born during an earlier period of higher births are now reaching senior-high and university age, even as younger cohorts shrink. On the demand side, it answers persistent public anxiety over the high-stakes "zhongkao" entrance exam and intense competition for university places, a concern that has shaped household decisions on where to live and how much to invest in tutoring.

The same press conference, as part of a broader social-development briefing, also covered childcare and elderly care — reflecting the NDRC's mandate over public-service investment. Officials pushed to extend comprehensive childcare service centers to the prefecture-city level, develop integrated childcare and kindergarten services, and prohibit arbitrarily adding fee-charging "specialty classes." On elderly care, the NDRC aims to raise nursing-type beds to 73% of the total and expand integrated medical-and-elderly care.

## What to Watch

The announcement did not include specific numerical targets — the number of new senior-high places, the number of new research universities, or the funding attached. How central and local governments will finance the campus construction and new institutions remains unspecified. Nor is it clear how the elevation of vocational undergraduate schools will interact with the traditional academic track and with graduate employment outcomes. For now, the signal is directional: China intends to shift education investment from the shrinking bottom of the system to its still-growing top, betting that broader access at the senior-high and university levels will both calm parents and build the talent base the 15th Five-Year Plan envisions.
