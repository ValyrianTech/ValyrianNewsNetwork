# Gas Dips Below $4, But Inflation Stays Stubbornly High

Americans are finally catching a break at the gas pump, but that relief is being offset by stubborn cost pressures across the rest of the economy. The national average price of regular gasoline dipped below $4 a gallon on Tuesday for the first time in three months, falling to $3.98 — a drop of 38 cents over the past month, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/10/nx-s1-6034518/inflation-consumer-prices-economy-gas-prices). At the same time, consumer prices rose 0.4% in October from the previous month, a hotter-than-expected reading that underscores how deeply affordability concerns have taken root, [according to the Labor Department](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/cpi.pdf).

## A Tale of Two Indicators

The divergence between falling fuel costs and rising overall prices captures the central economic tension heading into the holiday season. Gasoline's decline stems from a straightforward combination of forces: falling crude oil prices, refineries switching to a cheaper winter fuel blend, and the typical seasonal drop in demand.

"Temperatures are getting colder," said Patrick De Haan, a petroleum analyst for GasBuddy. "People who have boats and RVs are probably done for the year. I would say, by Thanksgiving, so long as we don't get any wild cards geopolitically, the national average could be $3.75 a gallon."

But the broader cost-of-living picture remains far less encouraging. Rising housing costs accounted for about a third of the monthly increase in the Consumer Price Index, and grocery prices also climbed sharply during the month.

## Why the Relief Is Partial

Even with the recent decline, gasoline remains near a record high for this time of year — roughly a dollar a gallon higher than when the United States went to war with Iran. That conflict, which began in late February, pushed oil toward $100 a barrel and continues to shape energy markets. This Thanksgiving's holiday drive could be the most expensive ever for many families.

Diesel prices tell an even starker story. The AAA average for diesel sits at $6.15 a gallon, down only about 13 cents from a month ago. The slow decline reflects the combined toll of the U.S.-Iran conflict and Russia's war in Ukraine, which has damaged Russian refineries and dried up a major source of global diesel supply.

"When a country like Russia isn't exporting diesel, that is very impactful for the global market," De Haan said. "So diesel prices – they will come down but they will still remain very elevated."

Elevated diesel costs ripple outward. Heating oil, a close cousin of diesel, remains expensive in the Northeast, and higher freight costs are feeding into the price of imported goods. "You're starting to see higher freight costs get passed through," said economist Mike Reid of RBC Capital Markets. "We've seen it show up notably in goods that are heavily imported — apparel, recreation goods, IT equipment."

## A Data Gap Clouds the Annual Picture

Tuesday's report came with an unusual limitation: it does not include the typical year-over-year comparison. Last year's government shutdown idled hundreds of government price-checkers, so prices from October 2025 were never recorded for comparison.

To fill the gap, the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland estimated ahead of the release that consumer prices in October were up about 3.7% from a year earlier. That would outstrip average wage gains of roughly 3.1%, meaning the typical worker is losing buying power — a dynamic that helped drive heavy turnout in last week's midterm elections, in which 49.1% of eligible voters participated, the second-highest total for any midterm in more than a century.

## The Fed's Next Move

The inflation data lands as the Federal Reserve weighs whether to tighten monetary policy further. In September, the central bank [raised its benchmark rate by 25 basis points](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/16/fed-rate-decision-september-2026.html) to a target range of 3.75%–4%, its first increase since 2023.

In an October speech, Fed Governor Christopher Waller [signaled that additional hikes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/waller20261008a.htm) may be warranted, arguing that persistent inflationary forces — from energy shocks to the artificial intelligence buildout — could prove more lasting than previously assumed. "I am concerned that the recent acceleration in inflation … will lead consumers, investors, and price-setting businesses to revise up their expectations for future inflation," Waller said.

## What to Watch

For consumers, the weeks ahead hinge on two variables: whether gasoline continues its slide toward De Haan's $3.75 forecast, and whether housing and grocery costs begin to cool. For policymakers, the question is whether a still-solid labor market can absorb further tightening without tipping the economy into a slowdown.

The mixed signals — cheaper fuel but pricier everything else — are likely to keep the affordability debate at the center of American politics well beyond the midterms. As long as wages trail inflation, the squeeze on household budgets is likely to persist, even as one of the most visible prices at the pump finally ticks lower.
