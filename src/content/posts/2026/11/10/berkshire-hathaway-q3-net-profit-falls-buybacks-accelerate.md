# Berkshire Net Profit Falls 28% as Buybacks Accelerate

Berkshire Hathaway reported a 28% decline in third-quarter net profit on Saturday, even as the conglomerate more than doubled its share buybacks and continued to build one of the largest cash reserves in corporate history. Net profit attributable to shareholders fell to $22.112 billion from $30.796 billion a year earlier, according to the company's [official quarterly report](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/qtrly/3rdqtr26.pdf).

The headline drop, however, masks a more nuanced picture beneath the surface — one in which the sprawling operating empire assembled over six decades by Warren Buffett continues to hum along even as portfolio swings and a crucial leadership transition reshape how investors view the company.

## The Accounting Behind the Decline

The profit decline was driven almost entirely by investment results, not the underlying businesses. Investment gains fell nearly 50% to $9.407 billion from $17.311 billion a year earlier, as [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/07/berkshire-hathaway-q3-2026-earnings-buybacks.html) reported.

Under U.S. generally accepted accounting principles, unrealized gains and losses on Berkshire's vast equity portfolio flow directly through net income each quarter. That makes the company's headline profit figures notoriously volatile. Berkshire itself has long cautioned against reading too much into them, reiterating in its report that the investment gains or losses in any given quarter are "usually meaningless" and that the resulting net income figures "can be extremely misleading to investors with limited understanding of accounting rules," as [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34429766) noted.

A more revealing metric — operating profit, which strips out portfolio swings and which Buffett long regarded as the truest gauge of Berkshire's health — fell just 5.8% to $12.705 billion from $13.485 billion.

## A Tale of Two Businesses

The operating results revealed a sharply divided conglomerate. The railroad unit BNSF posted after-tax profit of $1.729 billion, up about 19% year over year, while manufacturing, service and retail operations earned $4.042 billion, up 12%. Berkshire Hathaway Energy contributed $1.612 billion, an 8% increase.

Insurance, historically the engine at the heart of Berkshire's model, was the weak spot. Underwriting profit plunged 43% to $1.352 billion from $2.369 billion, while insurance investment income slipped 14% to $2.734 billion — a reminder that even the most disciplined underwriters are not immune to a softer pricing cycle.

## Buybacks Signal a Scarcity of Targets

Perhaps the most telling development was the surge in share repurchases. Berkshire bought back roughly $9.6 billion of its own stock in the third quarter, a single-quarter record that more than doubled the $4.5 billion repurchased in the second quarter. Year-to-date buybacks now total about $14.4 billion.

The acceleration under CEO Greg Abel — who took the helm following Buffett's departure from the role — sends a clear signal. When Berkshire's own stock looks like the best available use of capital, management tends to buy it. The aggressive pace suggests a scarcity of attractively valued acquisition targets that meet the company's exacting standards, making Berkshire itself, in the words of The Paper's analysis, "perhaps the most worthwhile asset to invest in right now."

At the same time, Berkshire returned to being a net seller of equities, offloading about $426 million in stocks during the quarter. That reverses a first half of 2026 in which it was a net buyer of $11.625 billion — a stretch briefly read as a sign that the company was ready to deploy its cash hoard.

## The Alphabet Bet and the AI Reckoning

The investment portfolio remained concentrated, with five holdings — Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America and Coca-Cola — accounting for 65% of the equity portfolio's fair value. But the newest of those positions proved a drag.

Berkshire first built a stake in Alphabet in the third quarter of 2025, vaulting the Google parent into its top-ten holdings almost immediately. In June 2026, it committed $10 billion to Alphabet's $80 billion equity raise — $5 billion in A-shares at $351.81 and $5 billion in C-shares at $348.20. Those shares have since fallen nearly 25%, weighing heavily on third-quarter investment results.

The cash pile, meanwhile, kept climbing. Cash and equivalents rose 3% to $377.435 billion as of Sept. 30 from $365.514 billion the prior quarter, though core cash — excluding rail and utility holdings and Treasury bill payables — edged down 1.9% to $352.3 billion.

## What the Market Sees

Berkshire's stock has been a rare bright spot in a turbulent stretch. Class A shares are up more than 12% over the past month, while the S&P 500 has fallen 5.5% over the same period. Year to date, Berkshire has gained 13% versus the index's 7.8%.

The outperformance reflects two forces: defensive capital rotating out of an increasingly volatile technology and AI trade, and a gradual easing of investor anxiety over the post-Buffett era. With Abel now firmly in command, record buybacks and a fortress balance sheet, Berkshire is positioning itself as a port in the storm rather than a growth engine.

Whether that posture pays off depends on what comes next. A more attractively priced market could finally put that $377 billion war chest to work. Until then, Berkshire appears content to bet on the one company it knows best — itself.
