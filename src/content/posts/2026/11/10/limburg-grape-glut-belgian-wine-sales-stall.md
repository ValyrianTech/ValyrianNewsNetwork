# Limburg Grape Glut as Belgian Wine Sales Stall

An ambitious project to convert Limburg fruit growers to winegrowing is buckling under large surpluses and stalled retail sales, [VRT NWS reports](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/06/limburgse-wijnbouw-krijgt-klappen/). Six years after the scheme launched, converted growers are sitting on unsold harvests while the initiative's architect insists the model can still work — a dispute that exposes a widening split in Belgium's young wine sector.

## A pivot born of a boycott

The project dates to around 2020, when Russia's restrictions on imports of Limburg pears — part of a boycott on EU agricultural products that began in 2014 — pushed the region's fruit growers to look for alternatives. Professor of economics Ghislain Houben, of UHasselt and Jerom's Winery, convinced a group of local growers to uproot part of their orchards and switch to viticulture.

The growers planted disease-resistant hybrid grape varieties, known as PIWI, which need fewer crop-protection products and typically yield higher volumes — pitched as both an ecological and an economic alternative.

## Supermarket sales stall

For the past two years, surpluses have been piling up. The expected smooth sales through major supermarket chains such as Delhaize and Carrefour have failed to materialise.

"As a Belgian winegrower you are the first victim in the supermarket," said Stijn Avermaete, the largest affiliated grower in the consortium at 20 hectares. "For stores only the numbers count, and the profit margin on Belgian wine is much smaller than on French or Spanish wines." He added that Colruyt has planted its own vineyards, further squeezing third-party producers. Avermaete still believes in the model and opposes hasty uprooting.

Quality and processing have compounded the problem. Growers delivered their grapes to Jerom's Winery, where Houben's sons handled processing. "They were actually too good and accepted all harvested grapes, including those of somewhat lesser quality," said Borgloon fruit grower Jos Claes. "Sugar content was not always optimal, and mixing everything together meant the result was not always top quality. That did the image no good." Claes still has his grapes pressed at the winery but now sells his wine directly himself.

One grower, who asked to remain anonymous, was blunt: the economic model has not succeeded for now, and the investments have not yet been recouped. Still, he argued that taking risks is part of the fruit grower's trade — with new apple varieties, too, it does not always work the first time.

## Houben defends the model

Houben blames a mix of high Belgian production costs, strong foreign competition and a lack of chauvinism among Belgian consumers. "Traditional wines are slowly reaching the end of their life cycle and the government is forcing us toward more sustainable cultivation," he said. "I believe more in hybrid grape varieties every day."

He compares the image problem to a start-up phase: in the Champagne region, companies build their reputation over six generations, whereas in Belgium this is only the first. Growers must build the brand "by choosing originality and innovation, not by being copycats of traditional regions," he argued.

## A global downturn, but not everyone is hurting

The consortium's troubles sit against a broader slump. According to [VRT NWS's reporting on global wine data](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/04/16/wereldwijde-wijnconsumptie-daalt-waarom/), worldwide wine consumption fell to its lowest level in six decades, and Belgian consumption dropped notably as young people increasingly favour cocktails, mocktails and alcohol-free alternatives.

Yet older, established estates barely feel the dip. "Cheap bulk wines are under heavy pressure, but for quality products with a story and established family businesses, this is a wave we can absorb just fine," said Joyce Van Rennes of Wijnkasteel Genoels-Elderen.

Karel Henckens of wijndomein Aldeneyck in Maaseik sees no drop in demand and is even planting additional vines. "If we want to move forward in Limburg, we must all focus on quality and identity from our terroir, not on producing bulk wines," he said. Henckens is himself a former apple and pear grower who switched to wine in 2002 and helped establish the Maasvallei Limburg appellation — a precedent that the pivot can succeed when built on terroir rather than volume.

## What to watch

The Limburg case lays bare the tension between two strategies: a volume-oriented, hybrid-variety model aimed at supermarket shelves, and a quality-focused, terroir-driven model aimed at premium channels. Belgian wine's greatest weakness lies precisely where the consortium placed its bets — in retail, where margins are thin and buyer power is decisive.

How the growers dispose of their accumulated stock, whether Jerom's Winery tightens its quality standards, and whether the cooperative model can be reformed will determine if the Houben experiment survives — or becomes a cautionary tale about timing a market that turned against it.
