# Recorder Recovered From Florida Jet Crash That Killed 9

Federal investigators said Monday they have recovered the cockpit voice recorder from a business jet that plunged from the sky amid severe weather in Florida last week, killing all nine people on board — a potential key piece of evidence as they work to explain why the Learjet 60 broke apart midair. The National Transportation Safety Board said the recorder was found at the site of initial impact and is being evaluated at its Vehicle Recorders Laboratory in Washington, D.C., according to a statement on its [official investigation page](https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/Pages/ERA27MA026.aspx).

It was not immediately known whether the device was damaged or was functioning during the accident flight. From photos the agency posted online, the recorder's orange-painted metal shell appeared to be in decent shape — an encouraging sign for investigators.

## What the Recorder Could Reveal

The device, if it holds usable audio, could fill in critical gaps about what the two pilots said and heard in the final moments as the aircraft encountered turbulence, ice formed on the wings and control was lost. Aviation safety expert Jeff Guzzetti told The Associated Press that recovery is no guarantee of usable data, noting that a cockpit voice recorder pulled from a medical transport plane that crashed in Philadelphia in January 2025, killing seven, later turned out likely not to have functioned for years.

"It's very plausible that any info on the recorder survived," Guzzetti said. "If they can recover it, and it's from the accident flight, that will be a huge help to investigators."

Even with a working recorder, the public may learn only so much: federal law bars the NTSB from releasing cockpit voice recordings, and only relevant transcript portions are typically made public at a hearing or in the factual docket.

## How the Flight Unraveled

The Learjet 60, registered as N722BD and operated by Aurora Aviation doing business as Upper Jets, departed Austin-Bergstrom International Airport in Texas bound for North Eleuthera in the Bahamas. About 12:50 p.m. local time on Sunday, Nov. 1, it slammed into the ground along Military Trail near Greenacres in Palm Beach County, erupting in a fireball beside a mobile home neighborhood and narrowly missing homes, [NBC News and The Associated Press reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/investigators-recover-voice-recorder-jet-crash-florida-killed-9-rcna608510). One person on the ground suffered a leg injury, and roughly 600 people from 77 mobile homes were displaced.

According to the NTSB's on-scene briefings, the crew reported light clear ice, moderate turbulence and altitude deviations of about 700 feet, and was cleared to climb. Less than 30 seconds later, a transmission announced a descent, followed by a mayday call amid an overspeed. The aircraft dropped from roughly 35,250 feet to 8,750 feet in a little over a minute.

Three pieces of the jet's left elevator — the tail surface that controls pitch — were found 1.3 to 3.8 miles from the crash site, confirming the plane began coming apart in the air before impact. NTSB Member Tom Chapman said knowing the elevator was lost is "a very important piece of evidence in terms of pointing us towards other things to investigate," adding that at the speeds involved, "I don't know that it would've been effective even if it had still been attached."

The Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office identified all nine victims on Nov. 4: pilots Jeffrey Gregory, 55, and Nathalia Filgueiras, 20, along with passengers Adam Robinson, 45; Helen Sharp, 40; their children Emma, 4, and Charles, 2; Rachel Reed, 38; Rowan Charmi Fehr Toews, 38; and Ana Maria Ramirez Cetina, 24.

## The Central Question: Weather

Investigators have said the weather is a major focus. A central puzzle is why the jet appeared to steer around a large storm cell only to head directly into a rapidly developing smaller one minutes before the descent. The NTSB has not confirmed whether the aircraft flew through an active thunderstorm.

About 20 NTSB investigators — covering systems, structures, recorders, weather and air traffic control — worked the scene, and the agency said excavation of the impact crater is now complete. Crews recovered components from both wings, both engines, the flight deck and tail assemblies, some of which were sent to Washington laboratories for closer inspection.

## What's Next

The NTSB has assigned no cause. A preliminary report, which is factual rather than conclusive, is typically due within about 30 days. A final determination generally takes 12 to 18 months and can run longer for complicated cases — a significant timetable given that the agency's roughly 400 workers are juggling more than 1,200 active investigations.

Investigators will review maintenance records and any history of problems with the aircraft, and will interview officials at Upper Jets as well as air traffic controllers and airport personnel in Austin. Whether the jet carried a flight data recorder — which captures far more parameters than audio — remains an open question; under FAA rules, a recorder of that type is generally required only on larger aircraft.

For the families and the displaced residents of Greenacres, the recorder represents the clearest near-term hope for answers. Whether it delivers them will depend on what the Washington lab finds — and on how much the charred, high-speed plunge into the ground left intact.
