# China Targets AI-Driven Health and Science Rumors Online

The Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) has launched a nationwide, two-month special campaign to root out science and health misinformation online, placing particular emphasis on content generated or amplified by artificial intelligence. The "Qinglang · Rectification of Science Popularization and Health-related Online Rumors" action, announced in a [notice](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2026-11/06/c_1795719652932799.htm) dated 6 November, will run roughly through early January 2027 and forms the latest installment in the regulator's long-running "Qinglang" ("Clear and Bright") enforcement series.

According to the CAC, the campaign aims to "further strengthen the rectification of prominent online-rumor problems in the science-popularization and health field and to foster a clean and upright online space."

## Why It Matters

Health and "science popularization" content has become one of China's most lucrative misinformation spaces, driven by wellness marketing, supplement sales and so-called wellness influencers who often funnel users into private chats and group messages to evade platform oversight. The campaign is part of a coordinated 2026 enforcement wave that has already taken aim at AI abuse, deepfake impersonation and fake online personas.

## Four Priority Targets

The CAC announcement specifies four categories of violations. The first covers the fabrication of livelihood and health rumors — packaging ordinary food and supplements as "miracle drugs," promoting "cure-all" folk remedies, and exaggerating the side effects of medicines and everyday products to manufacture anxiety. The regulator cited circulating false claims such as "long-term blood-pressure medication makes blood vessels brittle," "blood donation harms the body" and "crystallized honey is inedible" as examples.

The second category targets the impersonation of professional identities. This includes posing as medical professionals, inventing titles such as "National Class-1 Health Manager," staging white-coat photo shoots in hospitals and clinics, and packaging fake experts who peddle pseudo-scientific concepts like "quantum empowerment" or "cosmic energy."

The third — and perhaps most consequential — category addresses the abuse of AI to generate health-science rumors. The CAC flags the use of AI to mass-produce homogenized text, images, audio and video; the use of AI face-swapping and voice-cloning to steal the likeness and voices of well-known experts for fake science content and livestream selling; the inducement of AI large models to generate false "mortality rates," "survival rates" and fabricated scientific data; and the use of AI to forge "authoritative announcements" and official health advisories.

The fourth category covers the fabrication of recovery cases and exaggerated efficacy used to defraud consumers through private domains. The CAC describes "personal experience," "patient recovery stories" and "ancestral secret recipes" used to claim "100% effective" or "never relapses," complete with rigged before-and-after images and chat screenshots designed to lure users into private messages and group chats.

As [IT之家](https://www.ithome.com/1/023/409.htm) reported, the campaign singles out AI misuse as a headline concern — a sign that health misinformation, an old problem, is now being treated as a distinct AI-governance challenge.

## How Enforcement Will Work

The CAC outlined three governance measures. First, authorities will step up investigation and punishment of violating accounts and institutions, strengthening identity and credential verification for medical-science accounts. Such accounts must clearly display their qualification information and may no longer use vague profile labels like "a certain hospital physician" or "a certain field expert" to imply authority. The source of medical-science information must also be strictly labeled.

Second, platforms must strengthen management of key channels — prohibiting the promotion of health-science rumors in rankings and recommendation slots, building keyword and typical-case libraries, expanding the pool of authoritative science content, and reinforcing the reach of debunking information through pop-ups and private-message reminders.

Third, platforms are urged to enhance their AI-prevention capabilities, improving their ability to detect, identify and handle AI-generated or synthesized health-science rumors, strengthening deepfake authentication of face-swapping, voice-cloning and virtual-avatar imagery, and achieving automated identification of content impersonating professionals.

A CAC official stressed that local cyberspace departments must "earnestly fulfill their territorial management responsibilities" and strengthen inter-departmental coordination, while urging platforms to conduct self-inspection and periodically publish typical cases to reinforce deterrence.

## Broader Context

The campaign builds on a series of 2026 crackdowns. Earlier in the year, the CAC deployed the second phase of its "Rectification of AI Application Chaos" action, which by early September reported clearing more than 5.61 million illegal items and handling over 49,000 accounts. In late September, it launched a two-month campaign against short-video fake personas, naming AI impersonation among five priority problems. On the same day the health-rumor campaign was announced, the CAC also reported a separate AI-impersonation enforcement round targeting fake public figures, with more than 47,000 items cleared and 3,961 accounts handled.

The [National Online Rumor Refutation platform](https://www.piyao.org.cn/20261109/1eb1119df6a046c8b8b1ce31e3c27965/c.html), run under Xinhua, republished a digest on 9 November featuring the campaign as a public reminder.

## What to Watch

Beyond removing content, the campaign signals a shift toward structural platform accountability — credential-verification and source-labeling regimes that go further than simple takedowns. Key questions remain: whether platforms will face named cases and penalties during and after the two-month window; what concrete metrics authorities will publish on accounts handled and content cleared; and how the credential rules for medical-science accounts might evolve into a formal licensing or labeling standard. The campaign's explicit focus on private chats and elderly consumers also signals regulatory attention to fraud that migrates off public feeds into harder-to-police spaces.
