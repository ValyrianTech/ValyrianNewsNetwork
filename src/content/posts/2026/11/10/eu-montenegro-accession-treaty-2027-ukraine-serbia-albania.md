# EU Eyes Montenegro Treaty by Summer 2027

The European Union expects to be in a position to sign an accession treaty with Montenegro in June 2027, setting the Balkan country on course to become the bloc's 28th member state in 2028 — its first enlargement since Croatia joined in 2013. The target, confirmed by EU Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos in an exclusive interview with [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/europese-unie-wil-in-juni-volgend-jaar-toetredingsverdrag-ondert/), raises a sharper question: if Montenegro is nearly there, where do the other candidates — Ukraine, Serbia and Albania among them — actually stand?

The answer, set out in the European Commission's 2026 Enlargement Package and confirmed in its [annual enlargement report](https://www.dw.com/en/eu-says-montenegro-on-track-to-become-blocs-next-member-state-by-2028/a-79855011), is that there are now four frontrunners — Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine — each with an indicative roadmap for the year ahead, while Serbia is being offered what Brussels calls a "fresh start" after last month's elections.

## Montenegro: the finish line in sight

Montenegro has provisionally closed 21 of its 33 negotiating chapters. Subject to member-state agreement, it could close the remaining chapters by the end of this year, according to the Commission's [2026 Enlargement Package](https://enlargement.ec.europa.eu/news/2026-enlargement-package-shows-reform-commitment-delivers-progress-towards-eu-membership-2026-11-04_en). If negotiations wrap up this year, the accession treaty could then be signed in June 2027.

"Montenegro is on its way to becoming the next new member of the EU," Kos told VRT NWS. "The accession treaty is already being prepared extensively." The Commission has already presented the financial package for Montenegro's accession, and work on the treaty itself is described as "well underway."

Even if the treaty is signed next summer, membership would not follow immediately. The text must be ratified by all 27 member-state parliaments — a process expected to take roughly a year. That points to accession in 2028. Kos is unfazed by the small size of the country, home to roughly 630,000 people: "Actually Montenegro is a small country with roughly as many inhabitants as Antwerp. I am confident we will get the population on board."

## The Croatia complication

The main outstanding risk is a bilateral dispute with neighbouring Croatia, an EU member that has been blocking the closure of negotiating chapters since mid-2026. Zagreb has circulated an eight-point list of grievances, most rooted in the 1990s Yugoslav wars, as [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/19/montenegro-moves-to-settle-croatia-disputes-threatening-its-eu-membership-bid) reported.

They include the preservation of a memorial plaque honouring Croatian victims of the Morinj detention camp; the naming of a Kotor swimming pool after a water-polo player alleged to have served as a guard there; a request to help locate the remains of 14 Croatian war victims; compensation for families unlawfully deprived of property; and a land and maritime border dispute, of which only about 200 metres remain contested on land.

Montenegro has moved to address several of these, passing legislation protecting historical sites and a law barring sports facilities from being named after people who died less than 40 years ago. But Croatian Prime Minister Andrej Plenković has insisted progress has been absent: "There are issues which we have signalled to our Montenegrin neighbours for years... In the last two years, there has been no progress whatsoever."

Germany has intervened to press for a swift settlement. Chancellor Friedrich Merz said: "Montenegro has fulfilled all the requirements. There are meetings to draw up the final treaty. There are still a few smaller bilateral issues... but all of them can be solved." Berlin fears that allowing bilateral grievances to block the process could set a damaging precedent for other candidates.

## Albania and Moldova: the quiet frontrunners

Beyond Montenegro, the Commission identifies Albania and Moldova as making strong headway. Albania aims to conclude negotiations by the end of 2027 and has opened its last remaining cluster and met interim rule-of-law benchmarks, enabling the provisional closure of four chapters. The Commission calls its 2027 target "ambitious but achievable."

Moldova, a candidate only since 2022, has opened four clusters over the past year and met the conditions to open all remaining ones. Its objective of closing negotiations by early 2028 is likewise described as "ambitious but achievable." Brussels is watching closely for Russia's reaction and for the country's own internal divisions between pro-EU and pro-Russian sentiment.

## Ukraine: five years, war permitting

Ukraine has opened four clusters and met the conditions to open all remaining clusters, with the Commission urging Kyiv to accelerate reforms on the rule of law, democratic institutions and public administration. Its stated objective is to close negotiations by the end of 2028.

Kos is cautiously optimistic about a longer horizon. "It is possible that Ukraine joins the EU within 5 years," she said. "Ukraine is fighting for survival right now, but we set the bar just as high for them as for the other candidate countries. And that is not easy." She added: "I believe that should be possible if we help Ukraine. Although that will also depend on how long the war lasts."

## Serbia: a political earthquake and a fresh start

Serbia presents the most striking shift. The Commission's report records no substantive progress over the reporting period, citing democratic backsliding, low alignment with EU foreign policy and societal polarisation. But the recent parliamentary elections — won by a student-led movement that defeated long-time leader Aleksandar Vučić — have changed the political calculus.

"After Montenegro they were actually the furthest along, until under Vučić's government steps were taken backwards in recent years," Kos said. "I have already received them here in Brussels. If they mean it seriously and really want to carry out major reforms, then things could suddenly move very fast for Serbia."

## The wider picture

Commission President Ursula von der Leyen framed the package as a turning point: "The significant progress achieved by some candidates over the last year allows us to turn the promise of enlargement into reality... Step by step, we are building a more complete, stronger Union." High Representative Kaja Kallas was blunter: "Enlargement is a direct investment in Europe's security."

Not everyone is advancing. North Macedonia still needs constitutional amendments; Bosnia-Herzegovina is disengaging from its EU path amid divisive rhetoric; Kosovo has no candidate status; and Türkiye and Georgia remain furthest away, with Georgia's process "de facto halted."

Kos likened the moment to the EU's 2004 "big bang," when ten countries joined at once. "I feel that more and more European leaders realise it is important to bring all these countries on board, if we want to protect ourselves in these geopolitical times."

## What to watch

Three things will determine whether the timetable holds. First, whether Montenegro can close its remaining chapters by the end of 2026 — and whether Croatia lets it. Second, whether Serbia's new government turns its electoral mandate into genuine reform, which could reorder the queue. Third, whether Ukraine's war-battered administration can keep pace with a reform agenda the Commission insists is no softer than for any other candidate. The June 2027 treaty date is a political target, not a guarantee — but for the first time in more than a decade, the EU's door is visibly open.
