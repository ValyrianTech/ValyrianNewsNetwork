# Femina Goes to Haenel, Goncourt to Jaenada

French novelist Yannick Haenel has won the Prix Femina for *La solitude des professeurs est infinie* ("The Solitude of Teachers Is Infinite"), a memoir-novel drawn from his years teaching in the Paris suburbs, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576). The award, announced on 4 November 2026 in Paris, came a day after France's most prestigious literary prize, the Goncourt, went to Philippe Jaenada for *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel*. Together, the two honours close the autumn *prix littéraires* season that dominates the French cultural calendar each November.

## A book born from grief and protest

The Femina jury, made up of twelve women, chose Haenel over Emma Marsantes for *N'efface pas mes cercles* and Lucie Rico for *Mais que fait ce sang sur le pull de Jennifer ?*, both of whom also received votes. Accepting the prize at the Dior headquarters in Paris, the author dedicated his book "to the teachers, to the high school students and to the middle school students," RTBF reported — a gesture made as student protests were sweeping the country.

Published in August, *La solitude des professeurs est infinie* follows Jean Deichel, the author's double, as a young trainee French teacher arriving at a middle school in Mantes-la-Jolie, west of Paris. The novel charts the stress, the urge to pass on knowledge, and the trials of the profession. Haenel writes of the "joy" a teacher feels "when it works, when a face lights up fleetingly, when a pupil shows understanding, when a lesson succeeds, or a paper is excellent." But he also recalls spending his time "calming things down, defusing, neutralising: whatever the pedagogues say, it is violence that comes first in middle schools; and being a teacher means stemming its unleashing."

Haenel said he felt the need to write about "the solitude of teachers" after the killings of the French teachers Samuel Paty and Dominique Bernard, both murdered in separate attacks that shook the country's education system.

## From classroom to literary laurels

After studying in Rennes, Haenel, now 59, taught during the 1990s before turning to writing. He won the Prix Interallié in 2009 for *Jan Karski* and the Prix Médicis in 2017 for *Tiens ferme ta couronne*, an unclassifiable novel blending literature and cinema in which the American filmmaker Michael Cimino appears. "I have two passions in life: cinema, especially American cinema, and literature," he said at the time. "I tried with this book to satisfy my cinephilia with sentences." He is also a columnist for the press, including the satirical weekly *Charlie Hebdo*.

The Femina's other awards went to the American writer Laura Kasischke for *Baignade surveillée* (Femina étranger, or foreign fiction) and to Jennifer Tamas for *Barbe Bleue contre-enquête* (Femina essai, or essay).

## Jaenada takes the Goncourt

The Femina ceremony followed the Goncourt, which on 3 November crowned Philippe Jaenada for *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel* (Flammarion), as [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/goncourt-2026-le-prestigieux-prix-a-ete-attribue-a-philippe-jaenada-pour-son-livre-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel-11809864). The Goncourt remains France's best-known literary award and typically delivers a substantial sales boost to its winner. In the same week, the Renaudot prize went to François-Henri Désérable for *Le Palais* (Gallimard), [RTBF noted](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-prix-renaudot-2026-attribue-a-francois-henri-deserable-pour-le-palais-11809873).

Jaenada, the Goncourt laureate, has also drawn attention for his stance on artificial intelligence, defending what he described as a moderate and playful use of the technology — a remark that landed as the publishing world debates AI's role in writing.

## Analysis: a season of teaching and technology

This year's laureates reflect two concerns running through contemporary French letters. Haenel's win elevates the figure of the teacher at a moment of national unease over schools, student protest and the memory of attacks on educators. His book turns the classroom into a subject of literary weight, treating pedagogy not as backdrop but as a test of endurance and connection.

Meanwhile, the Goncourt's choice of Jaenada — and his openness about using AI — places the prize at the centre of a broader conversation about authorship and tools. For a prize long treated as a bellwether of literary taste, that framing is significant: the Goncourt winner is not merely a bestseller-in-waiting but a figure whose public positions shape debate.

The pairing also underscores the structural rhythm of the French system. The Femina, founded in 1904 by the magazine *Femina* and awarded by an all-female jury, and the Goncourt, first given in 1903, are announced within days of each other each November, generating a burst of media coverage and bookstore traffic that no other European literary market matches.

## What's next

With the major prizes now settled, attention turns to sales, translations and the longer afterlife of the winning books. Haenel's novel will be watched for how it resonates beyond France, given its rootedness in the French school system and the memory of Paty and Bernard. In 2025 the Femina went to Nathacha Appanah for *La Nuit au cœur* (Gallimard), and the tradition of the November season shows no sign of loosening its grip on French cultural life.

For readers outside France, the twin awards offer a compact map of where the country's literary establishment is looking: toward the classroom, toward questions of violence and care, and toward the uneasy border between human authorship and machine assistance.
