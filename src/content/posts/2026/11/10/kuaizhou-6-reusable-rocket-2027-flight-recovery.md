The Kuaizhou-6 reusable liquid-fueled rocket is expected to complete its maiden orbital flight and first-stage recovery in 2027, its developer said, a move that would push a company long tied to small, expendable solid rockets into China's increasingly crowded reusable-launch race.

Chairman Zha Xiongquan of Kuaizhou Aerospace Space Technology Co., Ltd. announced the target in a keynote address at the main forum of the 2026 Commercial Space Forum, held in Wuhan, Hubei, on 9 November, according to [Science and Technology Daily](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261110/445c4dd3f0614f98b1fa962f85cd962b/c.html), which was republished by Xinhua. The company said it is accelerating development of the reusable rocket and expects to achieve both orbit and booster recovery within 2027.

## A Shift from Solid to Liquid Reuse

The Kuaizhou-6 would carry a payload capacity of up to 20 tonnes, positioning it in the medium-lift class. By the mid-point of China's 15th Five-Year Plan — roughly 2028 to 2029 — the company plans to complete a changeover to a 140-tonne-class liquid engine and enter large-scale market service, which it said will further improve lift capacity and the engineering maturity of reusability.

The plans represent a strategic turn. The Kuaizhou family has, to date, been built around solid-fueled, expendable rockets operated by what was long known as CASIC Rocket Technology Co., a subsidiary of the state-owned China Aerospace Science and Industry Corporation (CASIC). The operational fleet includes the Kuaizhou-1A small launcher and the Kuaizhou-11, which can carry about one tonne to a 700-kilometre Sun-synchronous orbit.

That solid-rocket business remains active. On 17 September 2026, a Kuaizhou-11 rocket launched the Tianyi-51 and Tianyi-52 satellites from Jiuquan — the seventh flight of the Kuaizhou-11 and the 40th flight of the Kuaizhou series overall, the [Wuhan Economy and Information Technology Bureau](https://jxj.wuhan.gov.cn/xwzx_9/tpxw/202609/t20260918_2849459.html) reported. The bureau confirmed that the Kuaizhou-11 is developed by Kuaizhou Aerospace Space Technology, with a one-tonne capacity to a 700-kilometre Sun-synchronous orbit.

## Corporate Restructuring and a Hybrid Model

The announcement comes after a year of corporate change. Zha said that in 2026 Kuaizhou Aerospace completed an equity restructuring, forming "a new development pattern of a state-capital main body, central-local joint ownership and market-oriented operation," describing it as a practice of a "new type of nationwide system" for coordinating resources. On 31 July 2026, the company was officially renamed Kuaizhou Aerospace Space Technology Co., Ltd., dropping the CASIC/Sanjiang name and adopting its rocket brand as its corporate identity.

Zha also outlined a parallel product: the Kuaizhou-12, a 10-tonne-class solid-liquid hybrid rocket that is also slated to make its maiden flight and enter the market in 2027. He said its launch cost would be no higher than 20,000 yuan per kilogram, roughly US$2,800 per kilogram.

## A Crowded and Fast-Moving Field

The Kuaizhou-6 target lands in a sector where Chinese firms have recently crossed key reusability milestones. On 19 August 2026, the privately developed Zhuque-3 rocket completed China's first land-based, landing-leg vertical recovery of an orbital-class first stage, touching down near Minqin in Gansu province after lifting off from the Dongfeng commercial space test zone, the [Beijing municipal government](https://www.beijing.gov.cn/ywdt/gzdt/202608/t20260820_4829664.html) reported. That came about 40 days after the state-developed Long March 10B conducted China's first controlled first-stage recovery and the world's first sea-net recovery demonstration, giving the country a differentiated "sea versus land" recovery matrix.

Reusable rockets promise to reduce launch costs and increase flight frequency — a combination increasingly seen as essential to deploying planned satellite megaconstellations. After Zhuque-3's first flight in December 2025, its developer took just over eight months to achieve a successful recovery, far faster than SpaceX's Falcon 9, which took more than five years between first flight and first recovery, according to [Chao News](https://news.hangzhou.com.cn/zjnews/content/2026-08/22/content_9286614.htm). Zhuque-3's stated aim is to cut domestic launch prices from roughly 100,000 yuan per kilogram to about 20,000 yuan per kilogram.

Demand for low-cost, high-frequency launch is mounting. China has published 65 commercial satellite constellation plans and filed nearly 80,000 low-orbit satellites with the International Telecommunication Union, while the Guowang and Qianfan constellations — the latter of which has entered a key deployment phase — plan to place some 28,000 satellites in orbit between 2024 and 2035, Chao News reported.

## Analysis: Ambitious, but Unproven

The 2027 target, if met, would mark a remarkably fast transition for a company that has specialised in small, expendable solid rockets. But the timeline is a forward-looking corporate plan rather than a demonstrated capability: the Kuaizhou-6 has not yet flown, and achieving a first-stage recovery on a maiden orbital flight would be ambitious even by the standards of more experienced operators. Industry practice often separates a rocket's first flight from its first recovery, and reusable liquid rockets require mastery of variable-thrust engines, precision landing guidance, thermal protection and rapid turnaround — areas where Chinese firms have only recently shown success.

The precise propellant combination and architecture of the Kuaizhou-6 have not been confirmed in this reporting. The plan to switch to a 140-tonne-class engine by the mid-15th Five-Year Plan implies an initial engine of lower thrust, and earlier public exhibitions showed a liquid-oxygen/methane "Kuaizhou-6 (C)" concept. The company's approach emphasises standardised, modular, building-block development of low-cost medium-lift rockets that leverage existing mature technology and production capacity.

The corporate restructuring adds both opportunity and uncertainty. State backing from Wuhan could accelerate delivery, while the loss of the CASIC/Sanjiang name signals a sharper commercial orientation — and, likely, financial pressure — as the company competes with better-established reusable players.

## What to Watch

Investors and industry watchers will look for the first concrete tests of the Kuaizhou-6 — engine firings and hover or landing demonstrations — as evidence that the 2027 timeline is achievable. Two questions will shape the outcome: whether Kuaizhou Aerospace can translate its established solid-rocket manufacturing base into liquid-reuse capability, and whether the crowded Chinese market can absorb another medium-lift reusable vehicle on the projected schedule. The Kuaizhou-12 hybrid rocket's 2027 first flight, meanwhile, offers an earlier, lower-cost test of the company's ability to hit its stated milestones.
