# Moscow and Beijing Build a Strategic Arctic Alliance

Russia and China are moving toward a highly strategic rapprochement in the Far North, building what is billed as a "Polar Silk Road" that links Chinese shipping ambitions to Russia's Arctic transport corridor. According to [RTBF's "Vu d'Europe" analysis](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719), the partnership spans port development, maritime research, container-terminal automation technology and shipping along the Northern Sea Route (NSR) — a convergence that is alarming Western capitals and has pushed the European Union to adopt a revamped Arctic strategy.

## A warming region, a widening opening

The driver is climate change. Melting ice is opening previously inaccessible maritime routes and exposing vast resources, a dynamic the piece frames as both an economic opportunity and a geopolitical flashpoint. Per USGS estimates cited by RTBF, the Arctic holds nearly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and 13% of its undiscovered oil, alongside rare-earth metals. As [Defence24](https://defence24.com/geopolitics/cold-war-at-the-top-of-the-world-the-militarisation-of-the-arctic-and-the-sino-russian-alliance-on-the-northern-sea-route) notes, the real geostrategic game-changer is the NSR, which cuts transit times between Asia and Europe by 30–40% compared with the Suez Canal — a route whose appeal grows as Middle East shipping is disrupted.

## The Patrushev visit and the terms of cooperation

The rapprochement was on display in October, when Nikolai Patrushev, an adviser to Vladimir Putin, visited China, according to [Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/). Discussions covered linking China's Maritime Silk Road with Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor, developing Russian ports, exchanging container-terminal automation technology and conducting joint maritime research. The two countries signed a key Arctic cooperation agreement in 2023, and Chinese entities now co-finance major extraction projects such as Yamal LNG while expanding their own fleet of nuclear-powered icebreakers.

The economic logic is clear: China is Russia's largest trading partner, and bilateral trade exceeded US$200 billion for a third consecutive year in 2025. Russia recorded 37.9 million tonnes of cargo traffic on the NSR in 2024, a record high. In 2026, Russian oil exports via the Arctic rose nearly 50% year-on-year, with about 20 million barrels delivered to Asia between July and mid-October, [Modern Diplomacy reported](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/10/19/russia-ramps-up-arctic-oil-exports-as-global-shipping-crisis-disrupts-trade-routes/), citing Reuters. Gazprom Neft plans to increase deliveries by roughly 35% this year, and Putin has claimed freight could double to 70 million tonnes a year by the end of the decade, aided by Rosneft's Vostok Oil project.

## Two different motives

Each side is pursuing its own interests. Russia wants to deliver more oil and gas to China and escape Western sanctions imposed over its war in Ukraine. China wants an alternative maritime route to reduce its dependence on the Malacca Strait — a narrow passage the US Navy could blockade in a Taiwan contingency — and to gain access to Russian Arctic resources and infrastructure. In its 2018 Arctic Policy, Beijing presented China as a "near-Arctic state" with legitimate interests in shipping, resources, research and governance.

"China aspires to the status of a global superpower. One of the attributes is domination, or at least presence, in geopolitically important areas of the world, and the Arctic is unquestionably one of them," said Vlastislav Bříza, a security expert at Charles University, as quoted by RTBF.

## The military dimension

Beneath the commercial layer sits a harder security reality. Russia controls about 53% of the Arctic coastline, and its Northern Fleet — home to strategic ballistic-missile submarines — is based on the Kola Peninsula. Moscow has reactivated Soviet-era bases and deployed Bastion coastal defence, Arctic-adapted S-400s and hypersonic-missile-armed interceptors, with the declared aim of turning the NSR and adjacent waters into a controlled zone.

"Russia is expanding its military presence in the North. Moscow has reopened Soviet bases and is testing new weapons in the region," EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas warned, adding that "drone incursions, GPS jamming and sabotage of undersea cables are undermining Arctic security." The head of Swedish military intelligence, Thomas Nilsson, told The Times that China "will use" Russia to gain a foothold in the Far North.

The West's physical capability lags. The United States has only two ageing operational icebreakers, while Russia has more than forty, including Arktika-class nuclear vessels; the EU has about ten. "Without the physical capability to operate in ice-covered waters, Washington's declarations in support of freedom of navigation in the Arctic remain little more than diplomatic notes," Defence24 argued. NATO's "Arctic Sentry" framework, launched in February 2026, is meant to unify patrols and intelligence-sharing, and with Finland and Sweden's accession seven of the eight Arctic Council members are now NATO members.

## A more cautious reading

Not all analysts share the alarm. In a [skeptical assessment for East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/21/china-adapts-to-a-colder-arctic-reception/), Hudson Institute senior fellow Liselotte Odgaard writes that China's partnership with Russia is "relatively narrow," that proposed Chinese investments have been blocked by Arctic states, and that NSR commercial activity has "fallen short of expectations." There is, she notes, "no public evidence" that China routinely deploys submarines in Arctic waters — though its expanding research, satellite and seabed-mapping activities represent a gradual accumulation of dual-use capabilities.

RTBF's own text carries this caveat, noting that Suez traffic "remains without common measure" to the Arctic route, which is hampered by ice, high insurance premiums, thin infrastructure and a short season. The analytical tension between an urgent strategic threat and an over-hyped corridor runs through the entire story.

## Europe's answer

The EU adopted a new Arctic strategy on 20 October 2026, shifting focus from climate to security. "The Arctic has changed dramatically, and the world has changed, especially since Russia's aggression against Ukraine," said Commissioner Jozef Síkela, who prepared the strategy, noting the region is warming up to four times faster than the global average. Commission President Ursula von der Leyen put it starkly: "The Arctic is one of the places where the tectonic plates of geopolitics collide."

The plan calls for bolstering the European icebreaker fleet, expanding surveillance of Russia's "shadow fleet," creating a new Centre for Maritime Security extending monitoring to the North Atlantic and Arctic, and building a "sovereign and secure" Greenland–Europe subsea cable within a pan-Arctic project that would also link Canada, Japan and South Korea. It also backs the IRIS2 satellite constellation to compete with Starlink — steps that, as [Le Monde noted](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2026/10/20/eu-unveils-new-arctic-strategy-in-face-of-us-russia-threats_6760261_4.html), appear aimed at reducing dependence on Washington. The Centre for Maritime Security will not be operational immediately, however; according to the [Kyiv Independent](https://kyivindependent.com/eu-to-bolster-tracking-of-russian-shadow-fleet-and-hybrid-attacks-with-new-arctic-strategy/), Brussels must first consult member states on who will contribute and how.

## What to watch

The gap between ambition and reality is the key uncertainty. Will the Polar Silk Road deliver trade volumes that match its strategic billing, or remain a seasonal, high-cost niche? Will China's dual-use research translate into a military presence the West fears? And can Europe turn a security-heavy strategy into operational capability — new icebreakers, a working maritime centre, a sovereign satellite network — before the Arctic's strategic balance hardens further? For now, the direction is clear even if the pace is not: the Far North has become one of the central arenas of great-power competition.
