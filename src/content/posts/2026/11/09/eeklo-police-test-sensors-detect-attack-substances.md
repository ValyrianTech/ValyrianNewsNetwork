# Eeklo Police Test Sensors to Detect Attack Substances

Police in the Belgian town of Eeklo are installing three fixed sensors that, in a later phase, should be able to detect dangerous chemical substances after a terrorist attack or explosion, as part of a European research project. The police zone Meetjesland Centrum — which covers Eeklo, Kaprijke and Sint-Laureins in East Flanders — was selected as the sole Belgian test site for the EU-funded Horizon Europe project BEHOLDER, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/07/politie-eeklo-sensoren-terroristische-aanval-proefproject/).

The devices are being placed at the train station, on an industrial estate and next to a school. "The project is not really intended for accidents, but rather for terrorist attacks or explosions," said Johan Verschaeve, project leader and information security officer for the police zone.

## From air quality to hazardous substances

The current devices measure only air quality. In a subsequent phase, new sensors should be able to detect dangerous chemical substances or nuclear threats, as well as explosives — the core of the research project.

The sensor sites were deliberately chosen to differ. At the station, the sensor can register the influence of diesel trains; at the industrial estate and the school, the project team wants to study which changes in air quality the sensors can perceive.

The three sensors are connected to the police zone's existing wireless network, which is used today for its cameras and is considered unique because it relies on a former military communications tower for microwave links. That network covers the entire Meetjesland Centrum zone.

Measurement data appears on a digital map. The fire brigade also gets access to the information and helps determine from which readings an alarm should later be triggered. A local AI model is meant to analyse the data and detect anomalies, defects and false alerts.

## Evolving into a smart-city network

The police already tested the sensors last summer during the Herbakkersfestival in Eeklo, mainly to check whether measurement data could be sent correctly to a central server. The devices are now getting a permanent location.

In a later test phase, the police want to carry sensors on drones and install them on their vehicles, allowing emergency services to gather live information about possible hazardous substances during an intervention.

"The network is evolving from a camera network into a kind of smart-city network in which we can integrate sensors," Verschaeve said.

## Part of a wider European project

BEHOLDER is a Horizon Europe project developing an integrated, IoT-based detection system for chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear and explosive (CBRN-E) threats, according to the [Politecnico di Milano](https://www.deib.polimi.it/eng/european-projects/details/572). Launched in June 2025 and running for three years, the initiative brings together 14 partners from seven European countries, coordinated by the Dutch Institute for Safe & Secure Spaces (DISSS). By its final stage in May 2028, it aims to deliver a scalable, interoperable platform to improve real-time detection, assessment and response capabilities.

The project is built around a vendor-neutral ecosystem that integrates with existing urban infrastructure such as smart-city platforms, without compromising the look and feel of public spaces. A key element is the creation of open standards for CBRN-E data, alongside AI-based anomaly detection and real-world pilot deployments in urban settings.

The Belgian police zone is one of the consortium members, alongside organisations including the Municipality of Eindhoven, the University of Warsaw and the International Union of Railways. Earlier reporting by [HLN](https://www.hln.be/eeklo/politie-meetjesland-centrum-onderzoekt-hoe-ai-grote-evenementen-veiliger-kan-maken~a2c4be1f/) noted the AI-driven threat-detection testing during a crowded event in Eeklo.

## What's next

According to the police zone, the European project is expected to run for another year and a half. The infrastructure built for it in Meetjesland Centrum will remain afterwards.

The zone hopes the technology will later be usable by other police zones and security services. Within BEHOLDER it works with universities, technology companies and European cities — a collaboration that, if the pilot succeeds, could shape how European towns screen public spaces for hidden threats long after the research project formally ends.
