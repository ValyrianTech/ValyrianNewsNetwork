# China Launches 29th Batch of Satellite Internet Satellites

China launched the 29th group of satellites for its satellite internet low-orbit constellation early on 9 November 2026, sending the payload skyward aboard a Long March 12 rocket from the Hainan Commercial Spaceport and reporting a complete mission success. The launch marks another step in Beijing's drive to build a large-scale space-based broadband network.

## The Launch

According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261109/dc25f9a14d5a477ca8d5d1c96c7a5fdc/c.html), the rocket lifted off at 04:24 Beijing time from the Hainan Commercial Spaceport in Wenchang, Hainan Province. The satellites entered their predetermined orbits and the mission was declared a complete success (圆满成功). The Xinhua dispatch bylined by reporter Chen Kaizi was brief and did not name the satellite manufacturer or constellation operator.

Those details came from [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/ZhengZhi/2026_11_09_907570.shtml), citing the official WeChat account of the China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation (CASC). The satellites were developed under the overall responsibility of CASC's Fifth Academy, the China Academy of Space Technology (CAST), which is a leading centre of Chinese satellite design and manufacturing.

The launch was also the 676th flight of the Long March rocket family, according to Guancha. The Long March 12 is China's first 4-metre-diameter, single-core launch vehicle, standing roughly 62 metres tall, with a payload capacity of no less than six tonnes to a 700 km Sun-synchronous orbit. It can be fitted with either 4.2-metre or 5.2-metre fairings to support single- and multi-satellite missions to different orbits.

## A Constellation in the Making

Guancha reported that the Group 29 satellites belong to the China SatNet GW constellation, an operator known formally as China Satellite Network Group Co., Ltd. Networking began on 16 December 2024 with the launch of the "low-orbit Group 01" satellites.

The constellation is planned to comprise approximately 13,000 satellites deployed in two orbital layers, aimed at building a high-speed communications network covering the globe. The figure is cited in Chinese space media as a planned or aspirational target rather than a confirmed tally of deployed spacecraft, and Chinese reporting does not disclose the exact number of satellites carried in each group.

Low-Earth-orbit (LEO) satellites circle the planet at altitudes of roughly 200 to 2,000 km, far closer than traditional geostationary communications satellites. That proximity brings lower latency, broader coverage and higher transmission rates, but each satellite covers only a limited footprint, so continuous wide-area service requires very large numbers of them.

## The Manufacturing Shift

Beyond the launch itself, this mission also reflected a broader change in how China builds satellites. According to [KuaiTech](https://news.mydrivers.com/1/1164/1164130.htm), this was the final satellite internet low-orbit launch conducted by the Fifth Academy in 2026.

The development team used an integrated, modular common platform design and drew on the Fifth Academy's intelligent production line in Tianjin to achieve standardised, batch production. That approach differs markedly from the older model of building individual satellites with long, customised lead times, and it reflects the industrial logic needed to sustain a 13,000-satellite build-out: continuous manufacture, launch and maintenance at scale.

KuaiTech reported that the team also faced challenges including simultaneous development of multiple satellites, parallel tasks at different stages, and tight delivery deadlines. The satellites will now undergo in-orbit network-access testing before entering service, and the Fifth Academy said it would continue to refine its low-orbit satellite platform and boost its batch-manufacturing capacity to support future constellation construction.

## What It Means

Routine as the Group 29 launch may appear, it fits a wider strategic pattern. China's satellite internet programme is positioned domestically as a strategic infrastructure project and as a counterpart to Western mega-constellations such as SpaceX's Starlink. The effort is bound up with the competition to secure orbital slots and radio spectrum, and with ambitions to provide sovereign broadband connectivity.

The Hainan Commercial Spaceport, a commercially oriented facility designed to increase China's launch cadence, has become a regular departure point for these constellation missions. A steady cadence of launches, backed by batch manufacturing, is what separates a real mega-constellation from a paper plan.

## What to Watch

Attention now turns to the network-access testing of the Group 29 satellites and to how quickly China SatNet can scale its launch rhythm through the remainder of the year. The gap between the roughly 13,000-satellite ambition and the number actually in orbit remains the decisive variable. For now, each new group of low-orbit satellites brings China a step closer to a globally competitive space-based broadband system.
