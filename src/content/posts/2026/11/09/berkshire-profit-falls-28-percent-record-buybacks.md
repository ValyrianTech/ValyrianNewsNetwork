# Berkshire Profit Falls 28% as Buybacks Hit Record

Berkshire Hathaway reported a 28 percent decline in third-quarter net profit, but the bigger story was what the conglomerate did with its money: it bought back a record amount of its own stock, turned into a net seller of equities, and pushed its cash reserves higher. The results, released on the evening of November 7, offer the clearest picture yet of how the company is being run under CEO Greg Abel, who succeeded Warren Buffett.

Net profit attributable to shareholders fell to $22.112 billion from $30.796 billion a year earlier, according to [Berkshire's official quarterly release](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/qtrly/3rdqtr26.pdf). But the drop was driven almost entirely by the accounting of Berkshire's vast stock portfolio, not by the underlying businesses.

## A Fall Driven by Investment Gains, Not Operations

Investment gains shrank to $9.407 billion from $17.311 billion, a decline of nearly 46 percent. Under US accounting rules, unrealized swings in Berkshire's equity holdings flow straight through net income, making the headline figure highly volatile. Berkshire itself has long warned against reading too much into it, reiterating in the release that quarterly investment gains or losses are "usually meaningless" and can be "extremely misleading" to investors unfamiliar with accounting rules.

Operating earnings — the metric Buffett long called the truest gauge of the business — fell a more modest 5.8 percent to $12.705 billion from $13.485 billion. Three of Berkshire's four main non-insurance segments grew. The railroad BNSF posted after-tax profit of $1.729 billion, up about 19 percent; manufacturing, service and retail earnings rose roughly 12 percent to $4.042 billion; and Berkshire Hathaway Energy climbed about 8 percent to $1.612 billion.

## Insurance Is the Weak Spot

The drag came from insurance. Underwriting profit tumbled 43 percent to $1.352 billion from $2.369 billion, and insurance investment income slipped 14 percent to $2.734 billion. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/07/berkshire-hathaway-q3-2026-earnings-buybacks.html) reported that the pullback in underwriting reflected pressure in the insurance business, with GEICO among the units under strain. For a company whose insurance engine has long been its profit center, the softness is the one operational concern worth watching into the fourth quarter.

## The Buyback Signal

Berkshire repurchased about $9.6 billion of its own stock in the third quarter — more than double the roughly $4.5 billion bought back in the second quarter and a single-quarter record, according to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34429766). Buybacks totaled just $2.35 billion in the first quarter, and the nine-month tally reached about $14.4 billion.

The acceleration carries a clear message. As The Paper put it, the move signals that high-quality acquisition targets meeting Berkshire's standards remain scarce — and that Berkshire itself may be the most worthwhile asset to buy right now. In effect, management is choosing to return capital to shareholders rather than deploy it into large deals.

That caution extends to the equity portfolio. After net buying roughly $11.6 billion of stocks in the first half of the year, Berkshire turned into a net seller in the third quarter, shedding about $426 million. Its top five holdings — Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America and Coca-Cola — remained unchanged and together account for about 65 percent of the portfolio's fair value.

One position has weighed on results. Berkshire made a $10 billion strategic investment in Alphabet during the company's $80 billion equity raise in June, buying shares at $351.81 and $348.20. Alphabet shares have since fallen roughly 25 percent, denting Berkshire's investment gains — a reminder of the risk in its uncharacteristically large bet on the AI trade.

## Cash Keeps Climbing

Berkshire's cash and equivalents rose 3 percent to $377.435 billion from $365.514 billion at the end of the second quarter. Excluding rail and utility cash and T-bill payables, cash holdings stood at $352.3 billion, down 1.9 percent from the prior quarter. Even as it returns billions to shareholders, the company is still sitting on one of the largest cash piles in corporate America.

## A Defensive Posture Pays Off

Paradoxically, the defensive stance has been rewarded. Berkshire Class A shares are up more than 12 percent over the past month, while the S&P 500 has fallen 5.5 percent, and the stock has gained 13 percent year to date versus 7.8 percent for the index. That outperformance comes as investors rotate out of high-flying technology and AI names into defensive value.

The relative strength also suggests the market is growing more comfortable with the Buffett-to-Abel transition. The leadership handover had been a cloud over the stock through 2025 and 2026; analysts quoted by The Paper say that anxiety now appears to be fading.

## What to Watch

Three questions will shape the next few quarters. First, whether insurance underwriting keeps weakening — and how much of the softness is specific to GEICO. Second, whether the record buyback pace holds, or whether the third quarter was a one-off as cash approaches record highs. And third, how the Alphabet bet and broader AI volatility feed through to reported investment gains.

For now, the quarter tells a coherent story: a conglomerate with few attractive places to put its money is choosing to buy itself, hoard cash, and wait. Whether that patience reflects prudence or a thin opportunity set is the debate Abel will have to answer in the quarters ahead.
