# Palantir Hires Jon Tester as Congress Scrutiny Looms

Palantir Technologies has hired former Democratic Senator Jon Tester of Montana as an advisor, a move that signals the data-analytics company is ramping up its Washington influence operation as Democrats prepare to take control of Congress in January. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/09/nx-s1-6029641/palantir-congress-jon-tester-investigations), the hire comes as Palantir braces for intensified scrutiny over its roughly $2 billion in federal contracts and its role in federal immigration and data programs.

Tester, who lost his 2024 reelection bid after an 18-year Senate career, characterized his arrangement as advisory rather than lobbying. "My agreement with Palantir is advisory in nature, it is not lobbying," he said.

The move reflects a broader pattern: technology companies are hiring former Democratic lawmakers and aides to make their case to incoming Democratic majorities in the House and Senate, reversing two years of Republican control that Tester described as a time of disappointment.

## Why It Matters

Palantir, co-founded by Peter Thiel and led by CEO Alex Karp, has grown into one of the most valuable technology firms in the world, with a market valuation cited at more than $350 billion. Its business is heavily reliant on federal contracts concentrated in the Departments of Defense, Homeland Security, and State, with additional work for Agriculture, Veterans Affairs, and Health and Human Services.

That reliance makes the company acutely sensitive to congressional oversight — and Democrats are preparing to turn up the heat. Senator Ron Wyden of Oregon, along with more than 30 Democratic lawmakers, previously demanded answers about Palantir's Department of Homeland Security contracts and whether its tools are used to surveil Americans. Palantir denies conducting or enabling mass surveillance of U.S. citizens, saying it does not gather or manipulate data but instead creates tools for agencies and companies to organize their own information.

Wyden's office says he intends to press again as chairman of the Finance Committee. "Senator Wyden has a number of questions about Palantir's work to facilitate the Trump-Vance abuse of government data that he intends to ask again as chairman of the Finance Committee," his staff said. "If Palantir is not responsive next year, compulsory processes will be on the table."

## A Surveillance Debate

The scrutiny has been sharpened by a series of reports. In May 2025, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2025/05/01/nx-s1-5372776/palantir-tech-contracts-trump) on Palantir's rising role in the Trump era, including a $30 million ICE contract to build a tool providing near real-time visibility into migrant movement. More than a dozen former Palantir employees criticized that contract, arguing it violated the company's own code of conduct.

Earlier in 2026, court filings indicated Medicaid officials improperly shared data on millions of people with ICE, which then shared it with Palantir, according to a [July 2026 NPR report](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/17/nx-s1-5898504/ice-medicaid-palantir-data). The revelation added fuel to long-standing accusations — which Palantir denies — that its tools enable surveillance of Americans.

Tester, a longtime critic of the PATRIOT Act, addressed that tension directly. "I wanted to do away with the Patriot Act because it was about privacy... This is an organization that believes in privacy," he said, arguing that Palantir does not build large databases or trade private data.

## The Political Calculus

The hire lands at a delicate moment for Palantir's leadership. Karp describes himself as a liberal and has been critical of Democrats on immigration and artificial intelligence. In a 2025 Axios interview, he said: "I would say to my Democratic friends... You have to grow a spine because a lot of these people actually agree with me in private." Meanwhile, Thiel, the company's co-founder, is a longtime Trump backer — a dynamic that has made Palantir a lightning rod in partisan debates over government data.

Critics of the influence strategy argue that hires like Tester's are less about persuasion than about buying time. "I think at the end of the day, these companies have an inordinate amount of money and they will try and put it wherever they can in order to stave off any real accountability," said Aya Ibrahim, a senior fellow at the AI Now Institute and former State Department official.

Tester, for his part, is betting that a Democratic Congress will moderate the aggressive immigration enforcement tactics that drew controversy. "It is going to make a difference as far as the policies that are being advocated for... I don't think you're going to see the kind of aggressive tactics that were taken forth by ICE moving forward," he said. He added that "perception is reality in Washington, D.C. and in politics in general... But the fact is, I think Palantir has got a good story to tell, and they need to tell it. And hopefully folks will be receptive to it."

## What to Watch

When Democrats formally take control of Congress in January 2027, Palantir will face its most consequential oversight test to date. Wyden's threatened compulsory processes — subpoenas and other formal demands — could force the company to disclose details of its government work that it has so far kept private. Whether Tester's advisory role can soften that scrutiny, or whether it becomes a symbol of the influence economy critics decry, will shape how the company navigates its next chapter in Washington.
