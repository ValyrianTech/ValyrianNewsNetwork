# China Enacts Cross-Border Anti-Corruption Law

China's legislature has passed a dedicated cross-border anti-corruption law that, for the first time, converts corporate anti-corruption compliance from policy guidance into binding statutory duty—and asserts Beijing's control over how corruption allegations involving Chinese entities are investigated on its soil.

The Standing Committee of the 14th National People's Congress approved the Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law at its 25th session on Nov. 6, and the legislation took effect upon promulgation, according to [Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-11-09/china-adopts-cross-border-anti-corruption-law-mandates-corporate-compliance-102502593.html). The 47-article, six-chapter law consolidates previously scattered rules on punishment, prevention and countermeasures into a single framework.

## A Decade in the Making

The law is the culmination of a legislative track that stretches back years. China's Criminal Law was amended in 2011 to criminalize bribing foreign public officials, and in 2014 the Central Anti-Corruption Coordination Group established an office for recovering fugitives and assets abroad. The Anti-Cross-Border Corruption Law was formally listed as a "first-class project" in the NPC's legislative plan in 2023 and endorsed at the Third Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee in 2024, [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/09/first-reading-of-chinas-anti-cross-border-corruption-law-what-companies-with-global-operations-need-to-know) noted. A first draft was submitted for reading in August 2026, followed by a public comment period that closed in September.

## What the Law Covers

Under the law, cross-border corruption spans five categories of conduct: Chinese citizens and companies—including branches and subsidiaries—bribing foreign or international public officials; overseas parties bribing Chinese officials or state entities; other duty-related crimes committed abroad by Chinese nationals, such as embezzlement and abuse of power; corruption in which conduct occurs abroad but consequences land in China; and suspects fleeing overseas with illicit assets.

The scope reaches further than the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, Morgan Lewis noted, because it also captures the *acceptance* of bribes by Chinese officials and reaches duty-related misconduct in the private sector.

## Compliance Becomes a Legal Duty

The most consequential change for business is the compliance mandate. "For the first time, the law explicitly establishes anti-corruption compliance obligations for companies conducting cross-border business, turning policy requirements into statutory duties," Caixin reported. Firms must build compliance management systems, investigate and remediate violations, control corruption risks, oversee third parties and provide anti-corruption training. They are also required to report suspected cross-border corruption to authorities—a mandatory self-reporting obligation.

Officials from the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection and the National Commission of Supervision said the legislation embeds preventive compliance in law, encouraging systems "grounded in Chinese conditions and aligned with international practice."

## Guarding Jurisdiction

The law also stakes out jurisdictional territory. It bars foreign institutions, organizations and individuals from conducting anti-corruption investigations or other enforcement activities in China without approval—directly or through intermediaries—and prohibits domestic parties from providing overseas parties with evidence or related assistance without approval. It further provides a basis for invoking China's Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law and blocking statutes against what Beijing calls the "unjustified extraterritorial application" of foreign anti-corruption laws.

Morgan Lewis noted that the provision appears to target *law enforcement* activities specifically, so an internal investigation by a foreign parent into its own China subsidiary should theoretically fall outside its scope—but cautioned that how the rule is interpreted in practice "bears watching."

## An Enforcer With Reach

A national coordination mechanism led by the National Commission of Supervision will bring together foreign affairs, public security, judicial administration, finance, commerce, anti-money laundering, auditing, state-owned assets, securities regulation and cyberspace authorities. The law authorizes the use of big data and artificial intelligence to integrate oversight information, subject to protections for state secrets, trade secrets and personal privacy, and tightens oversight of officials holding foreign citizenship or overseas residency.

## Why It Matters

China has more than 50,000 enterprises overseas, is a major trading partner of over 160 countries and regions, and has signed Belt and Road cooperation documents with more than 150 countries and over 30 international organizations. As those overseas assets have grown, so has the corruption risk confronting "going out" firms—pressure that officials say the new law addresses by making integrity a source of competitiveness.

For multinationals, the law creates a more demanding—and potentially conflicting—compliance environment. Companies navigating both Chinese and Western regimes will need to reconcile heightened disclosure and self-reporting duties with the expectations of U.S. and European enforcers. As Morgan Lewis put it, the law signals "a new era of regulatory scrutiny and compliance expectations for companies engaged in international operations."

## What to Watch

Several questions remain open. How authorities interpret the bar on foreign investigations—particularly regarding internal corporate probes and shared-service arrangements—will shape how multinationals operate. The scope of the mandatory self-reporting obligation, the enforcement priorities of the National Commission of Supervision, and the interplay between this law and existing sanctions and anti-corruption regimes in the U.S. and EU will all determine how heavy the new compliance burden proves to be.

For now, the message to companies with cross-border operations is clear: the era of voluntary anti-corruption best practice in China is over. Compliance is now a legal obligation—and Beijing intends to enforce it.
