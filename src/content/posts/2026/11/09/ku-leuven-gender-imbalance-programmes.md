# Two in Three KU Leuven Programmes Show Gender Imbalance

Two out of every three bachelor's programmes at KU Leuven, Belgium's largest university, carry a gender imbalance, according to the institution's own Gender Equality Report, first reported by the student magazine *Veto* and covered by [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/09/ku-leuven-universiteit-gender-onevenwicht-kloof-mannen-vrouwen/). The finding points to a persistent and, in some respects, deepening divide between men and women across fields of study — even as women now make up a clear majority of the student body.

## How the imbalance is defined

KU Leuven classifies a programme as imbalanced when more than two-thirds of its students share the same sex. By that measure, the extremes are stark. In the bachelor's programme in Speech-Language and Audiology Sciences, 99 percent of students are women. At the other end, Industrial Sciences and Computer Science are each roughly 90 percent male.

Rather than evening out, the report suggests the gap is entrenching itself. Programmes that were historically male-skewed are becoming more male, and disciplines long dominated by women are becoming more female — a polarisation the university describes as a self-reinforcing pattern.

## A widening gap over three decades

The historical comparison is perhaps the most striking element of the report. In the academic year 1992-1993, the oldest period for which data is available, only one-third of programmes were imbalanced. Over roughly three decades, that share has doubled.

The shift occurred alongside a major demographic change: women now account for 55 percent of KU Leuven students, up substantially from the early 1990s. But that overall growth in female enrolment has not translated into a more even spread across disciplines.

## 'Half the talent pool'

Tom Van Puyenbroeck, KU Leuven's Vice Rector for Student and Diversity Policy, frames the issue less as a question of numerical balance than of lost opportunity. The university, he said, mainly wants to avoid a situation in which students steer clear of a programme because they believe too many students of the other sex are enrolled.

"That is a shame for both the student and society," he said. "A student will exclude in advance programmes that might suit him or her. As a programme, you then only appeal to half the talent pool."

Van Puyenbroeck places the root cause well before university. "As a university, we come as the last step in a societal process, where children pick up roles and stereotypes," he said. The report points to the classic socialisation dynamic — girls given dolls and seen as caring, boys given toy cars and introduced early to technology — shaping study choices years later.

## Measures short of quotas

KU Leuven has signalled that it favours softer, structural measures over hard quotas. It has worked to increase the number of female professors — an imbalance it says is being redressed and improving each year — and is reviewing how it presents its programmes, down to the imagery in brochures. Showing machines, versus showing people performing technical tasks, can make a meaningful difference, the university notes.

Even renaming can shift enrolment. The programme formerly known as Agricultural Sciences was renamed Bioscience Engineering and gradually attracted more female students; it now sits at roughly 50/50.

Role models remain central. "We must show students that even in male-oriented programmes — to call them that — women do graduate, work and teach," Van Puyenbroeck said. "In 'female' programmes the opposite applies, of course. Such role models are very important."

## When imbalance may be a good thing

The report is careful not to treat every imbalance as a problem. In healthcare, where medicine has for decades been male-dominated and medical practice has largely been tailored to male bodies, a female majority may help correct a historical inequality. Medicine, Dentistry, and the Biomedical and Pharmaceutical Sciences now range from 60 to 78 percent female.

Birgitte Schoenmakers, a professor at KU Leuven, sees that as a positive development. "We know that more women in a programme or profession ensure that more attention is paid to female perspectives," she said. She has little patience for warnings of a "feminisation" of the profession. "Then I think: does that matter?"

Van Puyenbroeck agrees that balance in every field is not the goal. "We are certainly not aiming to achieve balance in every programme," he said. "We mainly want to avoid young people letting opportunities slip... we must be careful that a discipline does not look at the world with a predominantly male or female gaze."

## An open question

Why the imbalance has grown in recent years has not been researched; Van Puyenbroeck said that was not the report's purpose, describing the university as the last link in a social chain that is difficult to straighten entirely. The report also acknowledges the limits of binary data, noting that "gender goes broader than that binary given."

For now, the numbers make one thing clear: as KU Leuven has grown more female overall, its individual disciplines have, in many cases, drifted further apart.
