# Trump's 60 Minutes Interview: Key Takeaways After GOP Rout

Days after Republicans suffered a sweeping midterm defeat, President Donald Trump sat down with CBS News' 60 Minutes for a wide-ranging interview, standing by his handling of the economy and the Iran war while conceding "bad messaging" on the economy and admitting his immigration crackdown was "too tough" at times, according to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/10-takeaways-from-trump-60-minutes-interview/).

The interview, conducted by correspondent Ross Douthat and aired Sunday, November 8, was Trump's first major sit-down since Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in a rebuke that cost Republicans more than 40 House seats and at least seven Senate seats.

## Why Republicans lost

Trump told 60 Minutes that voters "didn't like the war" with Iran or the soaring gas prices it triggered. But he argued the primary reason for the losses was that "I was not on the ballot" — an explanation he has offered repeatedly in recent days.

"I think they didn't vote, really. I think they didn't vote. I think in some cases, they didn't like the candidates. And that's, you know, their prerogative," he said.

Exit polling underscores the depth of the GOP's challenge: about 63% of midterm voters disapproved of Trump's job performance, and 44% said one reason for their vote was to express opposition to him.

## On the economy: 'bad messaging, maybe by me'

Trump defended his economic stewardship, pointing to a stronger-than-expected jobs report released the morning of the interview. "We're doing numbers that have never been done before. 401(k)s are at their all-time high by a lot," he said.

But exit polling tells a different story: three-fourths of midterm voters viewed the economy as poor or not so good, and 70% of that group voted for Democratic House candidates. Two-thirds believed Trump's tariff policies hurt the economy.

Pressed on why his message didn't resonate, Trump conceded: "I think it was bad messaging, maybe by me, but I would talk about it all the time. So I don't know, how much can I talk about it?" He predicted Americans would "notice it in the next two, three, four months."

## The Iran war and Vance's diagnosis

The morning after the election, Vice President JD Vance argued on social media that "the real reason we lost is obvious to almost everyone" — the unpopular Iran war. Trump said he told Vance to "write what you want" and that he doesn't disagree with the diagnosis "other than one thing."

The president said that if he had to do it again, he would still strike Iran, calling it an "emphatic yes" and arguing Iran's conventional military has been degraded and its nuclear program set back.

"I think historically, this will be a very good decision. Is it a good decision for today and tomorrow? Maybe not? But I have to go with what's right for our country," he said.

Despite his defiance, Trump said he is not looking to escalate the war now that the midterms are over: "Well, I'm not looking to. I don't think there's any reason to." He also said he believes Iran is close to striking a deal, with Qatar mediating.

## Immigration: 'I think it was too tough'

Immigration delivered one of the interview's most striking moments. While Trump gave himself "an A+ for closing up the border," he acknowledged that deporting people with good jobs who committed only a single violation — entering the country illegally — had hurt him politically.

"I think it was too tough," he said. The admission came as exit polling showed 58% of voters believe the administration's immigration actions went too far, and as Latino voters in battlegrounds like Texas shifted back to Democrats.

The concession sits uneasily alongside Trump's decision to install Stephen Miller — widely viewed as an architect of the administration's hardline immigration enforcement strategy — as White House chief of staff. Trump said he would "love to do something on immigration" with congressional Democrats.

## Personnel changes and a new chief of staff

During the interview, Trump hinted at impending firings: "I do, yeah. Little bit. I love my Cabinet, but I'll be doing maybe a few changes." One hour later, the White House announced that chief of staff Susie Wiles would depart and be replaced by Miller.

Trump also defended Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth against Democratic calls for impeachment. "We had two wars, we won both. We won Venezuela, we won Iran," he said.

## Pardons, 2028 and 'hating' his enemies

Trump said he intends to issue "tremendous numbers of pardons" before leaving office, joking that "anybody that got within a thousand miles of the Oval Office" could get one. He said he does not expect to attempt a self-pardon but called the idea "certainly not unreasonable."

On 2028, he said he would "probably" but "not necessarily" endorse a primary candidate, praising Vance, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and "some senators that are excellent."

In one of the interview's most candid exchanges, Douthat asked whether Trump could forgive his political foes to get into heaven. "The answer is no. I want to be honest, no," Trump said, adding: "I probably cannot do that, no, because I do hate some of my enemies and always will."

He also weighed in on UFOs, saying military pilots have "told me things that are very strange."

## What's next

The interview offered a president who is simultaneously defiant and quietly adjusting — defending the Iran war and the economy while conceding missteps on messaging and immigration. The tension between that rhetoric and his decision to elevate an immigration hardliner as chief of staff will define the next phase of his presidency, as the White House braces for Democrat-led congressional investigations.

Whether Trump's "too tough" admission translates into an actual softening of policy — or whether Miller's promotion signals the opposite — is the central question. For now, Trump is betting that history will vindicate the choices voters just punished.
