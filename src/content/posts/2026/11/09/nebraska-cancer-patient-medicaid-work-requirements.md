# Cancer, Then a Medicaid Cut: Nebraska's Work Rules Test

Eli McCreary, a 64-year-old Omaha resident, was fighting kidney cancer when a local hospital told him he had lost his Medicaid coverage and would need to pay out of pocket for care unless he met a new work requirement, according to a [KFF Health News investigation published by NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/09/nx-s1-6032732/trump-medicaid-health-nebraska-work-insurance) on November 9, 2026. His case offers an early, human measure of Nebraska's decision to become the first state to enforce Medicaid work requirements under President Trump's signature One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

The episode sits at the center of a sharp dispute: state officials say the rollout has succeeded and that McCreary suffered no gap in coverage, while McCreary, patient advocates and attorneys contend the rushed implementation produced confusion and misinformation that disrupted care for a seriously ill man.

## What happened to McCreary

McCreary said he had just been diagnosed with cancer in his right kidney when a hospital operated by CHI Health called to say he was no longer insured under Medicaid and that his appointments would be canceled unless he paid out of pocket. When he phoned a call center for help, he said a representative told him the program now required him to hold a job.

"You're required to do work to stay on," McCreary recounted being told. "And you haven't fulfilled your work requirement."

According to documents reviewed by KFF Health News, McCreary has struggled with drug addiction for decades, was discharged from a substance use treatment program in March and was diagnosed with kidney cancer in June. He also has diabetes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

"I don't understand what job I can get," he said. "I can't walk. I can hardly breathe. I can't move around. What job am I going to get?"

He said he canceled a CT scan and other tests and delayed a cancer-related appointment with a physician who travels to Omaha. "I missed my window of opportunity with him for another month," McCreary said.

CHI Health spokesperson Taylor Miller declined to answer questions about his experience, saying in a statement that the system is "committed to helping patients understand their health care coverage and access to care, including available financial assistance resources."

## The first state out of the gate

Nebraska launched its work requirements on May 1, 2026 — eight months before most states are set to do the same — after Gov. Jim Pillen vowed to be first. In December, Pillen said in a video alongside Mehmet Oz, the Trump administration official overseeing Medicare and Medicaid, that state officials "certainly appreciate President Trump and his whole administration for delivering this key reform."

"We're excited to be able to be the first to make it happen," the Republican governor said.

About 70,000 adult Medicaid recipients in Nebraska are subject to the rules, which require proof of 80 hours a month of work or a qualifying activity unless the person qualifies for an exemption. Federal law outlines exemptions that include being too ill to work — a category that covers people with a serious medical condition or a substance use disorder.

Attorneys and policy professionals who work with Medicaid enrollees said McCreary's treatment history and health conditions should have exempted him.

State officials defend the rollout. Jeff Powell, a spokesperson for Nebraska's Department of Health and Human Services, called it "very successful," pointing to [state data](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/early-medicaid-work-requirement-insights-from-nebraska/) showing 86% of affected applicants met the requirement or qualified for an exclusion and 93% of existing enrollees kept their coverage.

Powell also disputed McCreary's account, maintaining he experienced "no lapses in coverage" and was disenrolled only after turning 65, aging out of Medicaid and into Medicare before the work requirements could apply.

## Cost, confusion and longer waits

The rollout has been costly. Nebraska budgeted $8.2 million over two years for contractors — Boston-based Public Consulting Group and Atlanta-based Covendis — to help administer the changes, according to state documents obtained through a public records request. The state listed at least a half-dozen contractors costing more than $250,000 each, including one person whose compensation exceeds $1.8 million. Federal taxpayers are expected to bear much of the burden; Congress allocated $200 million in one-time funding, and Washington covers up to 90% of state IT system change costs.

Sarah Maresh, a program director with the advocacy nonprofit Nebraska Appleseed, called the contractor amounts "shocking."

"When you put in complicated policies like this, there are large corporations making money behind the scenes," she said. "This just further confirms that it's happening in Nebraska quietly."

Advocates say enrollees have been confused and misinformed. Internal documents show that as of early July, most work-requirement denials among applying adults were due to unsubmitted paperwork — a pattern the state called "expected." Average call center wait times nearly doubled between May and July, from about 13 minutes to 26 minutes, and Nebraska fielded more than 101,000 calls in August, a 66% jump from May.

"If people are working, they just don't have time to be sitting on the phone to go through all of this stuff," said Kenny McMorris, CEO of the Charles Drew Health Center in North Omaha. "You can leverage the technology, but you still got to have people there to help."

As of August, roughly 1,100 Nebraskans had been denied benefits because of the work requirements, most for failing to respond to information requests, and total Medicaid enrollment had fallen by roughly 4,600 people since May — a 1.4% drop.

Consultants involved in the launch have been more upbeat. "I really cannot brag enough about how well Nebraska handled this requirement," Latanya Michaux, a senior consultant with Public Consulting Group, said at an August conference, adding that "even though it was not so easy, we definitely had fun standing up the Medicaid work requirement program."

## A test case for the nation

Nebraska's experience is being watched closely because next year the requirements will apply to millions of adult Medicaid enrollees in more than 40 states. The Congressional Budget Office has estimated the broader Medicaid changes will reduce federal spending by more than $900 billion over a decade and leave 5.3 million more people uninsured by 2034.

Gelila Selassie, director of health advocacy for the nonprofit Justice in Aging, warned that the changes compound existing strain on benefits systems. "It's already a shaky system, and now you've added more burdens on top of that," she said.

The political stakes are equally high. With new congressional majorities, Democrats have said they plan to repeal the Medicaid changes in their first 100 hours in office in January 2027 — though it is unclear whether they can succeed, since Trump could veto such a move.

For his part, McCreary is now enrolled in Medicare, with Medicaid covering some of his costs, and was able to reschedule his canceled appointments. He had surgery in early November to remove his right kidney. Reflecting on the ordeal, he said he doesn't know whether the confusion was his fault.

"This cancer ain't going to stop just because I ain't got insurance," he said. "It's going to keep working on me."
