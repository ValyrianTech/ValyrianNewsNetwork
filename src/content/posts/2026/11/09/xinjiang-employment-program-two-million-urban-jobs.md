# Xinjiang Touts 2.39M New Urban Jobs Under Five-Year Plan

Xinjiang accumulated 2.392 million new urban jobs and 16.1 million person-times of rural outbound employment during China's 14th Five-Year Plan period (2021–2025), according to a photo essay published by the state-run [Xinhua News Agency](https://www.news.cn/photo/20261109/a309ed3e6017442fa11b6cd4ffefd1e8/c.html) on 9 November 2026. The 22-image feature, titled "Joyful Work in the Tianshan Mountains, Income Growth in Sight," presents the figures as evidence that the region's employment-first strategy and "employment-benefit project" are steadily improving livelihoods.

## The Claims and How They Are Framed

The report attributes the headline numbers to the cumulative results of the past five years, stating that Xinjiang has "continuously implemented the employment-first strategy" and "deeply advanced the employment-benefit project." According to the Xinhua report, local authorities have strengthened vocational skills training systems, extended rural employment service networks, and broadened so-called "new business formats" alongside channels for nearby and at-home work. The agency describes the outcome as "steadily expanding" employment, with "strong" security for key groups and ongoing improvement in job quality and public services.

The figures carry an important methodological caveat. The 16.1 million total measures "person-times" of rural outbound labor (外出务工) rather than distinct individuals. Because a single worker taking on multiple seasonal jobs in a given year can be counted more than once, the cumulative figure is not equivalent to 16.1 million separate people. The report does not disclose how either number was calculated, nor does it offer baseline comparisons against prior planning periods.

## A Mosaic of Sectors and Geography

Much of the essay's persuasive weight comes from its breadth. The photographs span late 2022 to September 2026 and deliberately cover both southern Xinjiang — Hotan, Aksu and the Lop Nur area of Bayingolin — and northern Xinjiang, including Ili, Tacheng, Horgos and Hami. The sectors on display range from silk reeling and cotton-picker manufacturing to desert agriculture, live-animal trading, e-commerce livestreaming and cross-border logistics.

Several captions name individual workers. A "livestock broker," Ma Jun, registers payment details for cattle-selling villagers at a large trading market where roughly 3,000 brokers are said to be active. Farmer Maimaiti Maitisaiyidi is shown digging Cistanche, a desert medicinal plant, on contracted sandy land in Minfeng County. At the Horgos highway port, a cross-border "ferryman," Gao Yun, clears customs while driving China-made commercial vehicles toward Central Asia. Others include a ski-patrol member at a resort, a mulberry-bark paper designer reviving Kucha mural motifs, and a train captain at the Korla passenger section. This use of named individuals and diverse locations is a familiar editorial technique for lending concrete, human texture to a single macroeconomic assertion.

## Analysis: A Communication Product, Not Independent Verification

This is a single-source report drawn entirely from official figures and imagery. It contains no independent data, no dissenting voices and no discussion of wages, job stability, informality or the seasonal precariousness that often accompanies rural outbound labor. The region's contested human-rights and security context — the subject of sustained international scrutiny in recent years — is likewise absent. Beijing categorically rejects allegations of forced labor and mass detention, describing its Xinjiang programs as voluntary poverty-alleviation and employment initiatives; this essay sits squarely within that official framing.

Readers should therefore treat the two headline statistics as government claims rather than independently verified facts. Automated searches did not surface corroborating coverage of the specific 2.392 million and 16.1 million figures outside the original release, suggesting they originate with this official communication. The essay's forward-looking subtitle — "income growth in sight" — signals its promotional intent as much as its news value.

## What to Watch

The reliability of Xinjiang's employment data will matter as Beijing promotes the region as a gateway to Central Asia under the Belt and Road initiative and as the 15th Five-Year Plan period begins. Independent economists and international bodies will be watching whether future disclosures include methodology, headcount-versus-person-times breakdowns, and wage and job-quality indicators. Until such detail is published, the strongest verifiable takeaway is narrower than the headline: state media is actively projecting a narrative of expanding opportunity in Xinjiang, and has assembled a wide-ranging visual case to support it.
