# Belgium Budget Crisis Deepens as Coalition Talks Resume

Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever relaunched federal budget negotiations on Monday, a week after offering his resignation to King Filip over the coalition's failure to agree a multi-year budget. The King refused the resignation and ordered the five majority parties back to the table, with the Premier due to report to the palace on 16 November, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/bart-de-wever-chez-le-roi-demission-refusee-et-nouveau-delai-pour-bart-de-wever-11811665).

## A new negotiating format

Rather than convene the full inner cabinet, De Wever has opted for separate bilateral meetings with each of his deputy prime ministers, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/budget-federal-le-premier-ministre-relance-les-discussions-via-des-entretiens-bilateraux-11812702). In a notable shift, each deputy premier may now bring one additional person — opening the door for party presidents to join talks they had previously been excluded from.

Only the francophone liberal MR has so far shown enthusiasm for that option. Its leader, Georges-Louis Bouchez, made little secret of his wish to sit at the negotiating table himself to keep the budget within the government agreement's limits. Other party presidents were expected to send a chief of staff or another aide instead, signalling confidence in their government representatives.

Individual meetings are also planned for Tuesday. The precise agenda for the coming days has not been announced.

## Pressure on the party presidents

Much now rests on commitments made at the palace. When De Wever left the royal audience on Friday, he carried guarantees that each party president had pledged to the King to make concessions. "That's what the King is for: to make people swallow snakes," RTBF's Bertrand Henne wrote in his analysis of the royal intervention — a vivid metaphor for the painful compromises being forced on the coalition.

The palace communiqué formalised an engagement by the five presidents that their parties are "ready to make concessions, to let the sense of compromise and responsibility prevail," [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/budget-federal-apres-la-crise-c-est-encore-la-crise-11812864). That framing carries a warning: whoever appears inflexible in the days ahead risks looking disloyal not only to De Wever but to the King himself.

## Mahdi urges decisions

Among the coalition partners, CD&V chairman Sammy Mahdi voiced hope that all parties would honour their pledge. "I hope everyone sticks to the promise to make compromises," he said, while acknowledging concern within his party over pensioners and the tax reduction on work. He added a blunt call for leadership: "You just have to realise that at some point you have to cut knots."

## The fault line: wealth versus spending

The deadlock reflects a deeper ideological divide. Vooruit, the Flemish socialist party led by Conner Rousseau, wants the "broadest shoulders" to contribute more through a millionaires' contribution or wealth tax. The MR fiercely opposes new wealth taxation, arguing Belgium is already among the most heavily taxed countries and proposing alternatives such as recovering funds from fraudulent long-term sick leave claims.

The stakes extend beyond a single year. The coalition approved roughly €18 billion in savings to be realised by 2029, alongside hoped-for feedback effects from labour-market reforms, yet still faces pressure to meet EU fiscal rules. Because no full-year budget has passed, the state is again operating on provisional monthly "twelfths" — a symbol of dysfunction De Wever had vowed to avoid.

## What to watch

The 16 November return to the palace is the next hard checkpoint. If the bilateral format fails to break the impasse, De Wever faces a second consecutive year of provisional financing and mounting pressure over Belgium's deficit trajectory. The standoff is also a stress test for the five-party Arizona coalition itself, with the prospect of early elections quietly shaping every calculation.

For now, the crisis that followed the crisis remains unresolved — and the coming week will show whether the King's intervention produced genuine compromise or merely a brief pause.
