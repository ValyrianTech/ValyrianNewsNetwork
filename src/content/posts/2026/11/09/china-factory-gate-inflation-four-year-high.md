# China Factory-Gate Inflation Hits Four-Year High

China's factory-gate inflation accelerated to its fastest pace in more than four years in October even as consumer price growth slowed, official data showed on Monday, widening a gap between upstream costs and the prices households actually pay. The producer price index (PPI) rose 5.7% year-on-year, up from 5.0% in September, while the consumer price index (CPI) climbed 0.7%, easing from 0.9% the previous month, according to [Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-11-09/chinas-factory-gate-inflation-hits-highest-in-more-than-four-years-102502680.html), citing National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) figures.

The figures beat forecasts on the industrial side — a Reuters poll had pointed to 5.3% — but fell short on consumer prices, where economists had expected 0.8%. It was the fastest PPI reading since June 2022, when producer prices rose 6.1%.

## What drove the producer surge

The October split is chiefly an energy story. NBS Chief Statistician Dong Lijuan attributed the upstream jump in part to higher international energy prices, which pushed coal mining and washing prices up a striking 36.6% year-on-year and oil and gas extraction up 35.0%, [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/20261109/9e6e8510df724a538967976b10776dd4/c.html) reported in its official reading of the data. Downstream, refined petroleum products, organic chemical raw materials, coal processing and gas production all posted monthly gains, as utilities stocked up ahead of the winter heating season.

Those energy-linked industries alone accounted for more than 80% of the 0.7% month-on-month rise in producer prices, according to the NBS breakdown — evidence that the surge is a cost-push, import-driven shock rather than a sign of domestic overheating.

A second, more forward-looking driver is technology. As artificial intelligence spreads into industrial upgrading, demand for electrical machinery and computers and electronics edged higher. On the consumer side, the AI effect is visible in prices for data storage devices (up 45.4% year-on-year), tablets (29.3%), computers (23.0%) and mobile phones (16.7%), which together added about 0.26 percentage points to the CPI.

## Why consumers barely feel it

The muted consumer reading is the more revealing signal. Headline CPI was flat month-on-month, and core inflation — stripping out food and energy — eased to 0.8% from 1.0% in September. Food prices fell 2.9% year-on-year, dragged down by a 16.9% slump in fresh vegetable prices and an 8.6% drop in pork, offsetting much of the energy-driven gains elsewhere. Gasoline, up 22.4% year-on-year, alone accounted for about 0.63 percentage points of the CPI increase.

The gap between the two indices reflects a familiar dynamic in China's current cycle: when household demand is soft, firms find it hard to pass upstream costs on to consumers. In practice, that means factories absorb energy-driven cost increases rather than passing them through to the shop shelf.

## The official view: a moderate, productive rise

Beijing's official reading is upbeat. NBS spokesperson Wang Guanhua said China's price moves remain "relatively moderate" compared with major economies, crediting the country's complete industrial system, resilient supply chains, early build-out of new-energy infrastructure, market diversification and timely price-stabilization policies.

Liu Fang, a researcher at the National Development and Reform Commission's Market and Price Research Institute, argued the moderate rise in prices has helped corporate earnings. Profits at industrial enterprises above designated size grew 15.5% year-on-year in the first nine months of 2026, she noted, sustaining double-digit growth through the year. Over the first ten months, the CPI averaged 0.9% and core CPI 1.1%, both up notably from the prior year.

## The debate beneath the headline

Independent economists are more cautious. The disparity between roaring producer prices and subdued consumer inflation points to weak domestic demand that has yet to be fully addressed by policy. Analysts have noted that a prolonged property-sector slump continues to weigh on household confidence and local government revenue, keeping consumption fragile even as exports stay strong — a combination that may explain why Beijing has held off on large new stimulus.

Much of the PPI headline also reflects a base effect. October 2025 saw producer-price deflation as China grappled with industrial overcapacity, so the "highest in four years" framing partly captures the reversal of that earlier weakness rather than a fresh inflationary breakout. Some forecasters expect the energy shock tied to global geopolitical tensions to fade next year, potentially pulling producer prices back toward deflation and leaving consumer inflation subdued.

## What to watch

For investors and policymakers, the October data sharpen a question that has hung over China's 2026 recovery: is the economy genuinely reflating, or is it importing a cost shock while domestic demand stays soft? The answer matters for the pace of any new stimulus, the trajectory of corporate margins, and the yuan. With exports still providing a cushion and AI-related demand adding a fresh, if narrow, price impulse, Beijing appears content to wait — for now. The next few months of producer prices, and whether any of the upstream pressure finally reaches consumers, will be the clearest test.
