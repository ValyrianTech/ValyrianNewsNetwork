# China Extends Tariff Truce With US for Two Months

China will keep a 24% additional tariff on US-origin goods suspended for another two months while retaining a 10% rate, a maintenance move that stretches a fragile trade truce between the world's two largest economies into early 2027. The adjustment, announced Monday by the Customs Tariff Commission of the State Council, takes effect on November 10 and applies to measures set out in Announcement No. 4 of 2025, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20261109/c65e8e4a6ee9493d82109cc5fd22d836/c.html).

Beijing framed the decision as implementing the "outcomes and consensus" reached at the China-US leaders' summit in Washington, casting the continued suspension as a way to give businesses a "relatively stable and predictable policy environment" and to promote the "sustained, stable and positive development" of bilateral economic and trade ties.

## Context: A Truce Built on Incremental Steps

The arrangement extends a process that began with China-US economic and trade talks in Kuala Lumpur in October 2025. The following month, China announced it would keep the 24% additional tariff on US imports suspended for one year while retaining the 10% rate. During their eighth round of consultations in New York and Washington from September 20 to 23, 2026, the two sides agreed to extend the broader Kuala Lumpur arrangements through January 10, 2027, as [Global Times](https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202611/1373924.shtml) reported.

The extension is now mirrored by parallel measures on both sides. China's Ministry of Transport said it would continue to suspend special port fees on US-linked vessels for another two months, until January 10, 2027, and maintain the suspension of plans to investigate the impact on the security and development interests of its shipping, shipbuilding and related supply chains. On the US side, the Office of the US Trade Representative extended 178 exclusions from Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods and prolonged the suspension of measures targeting China's maritime, logistics and shipbuilding sectors through January 9, 2027. The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security separately extended the suspension of a rule expanding export controls to affiliates of certain listed entities through January 10, 2027.

## The Legal Fault Line Beneath the Deal

The central complication is legal. The announcement explicitly notes that the US has "repeatedly stated" it intends to replace tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) — which have been "ruled invalid" — with tariffs under Section 301 of US trade law. China said it will continue to closely monitor Washington's follow-up measures and adjust its corresponding tariff countermeasures "as appropriate."

That language reflects the aftershocks of a February 2026 US Supreme Court ruling that struck down the legal basis for sweeping IEEPA tariffs, forcing refunds of billions of dollars collected from businesses. The administration's pivot toward Section 301 as a replacement authority introduces fresh uncertainty into an arrangement built on predictability — a point Beijing has been careful to flag without abandoning the truce.

## A Signal of Good Faith — With Conditions

Analysts framed the step less as a substantive tariff cut than as a confidence signal. Song Guoyou, deputy director of the Center for American Studies at Fudan University, said the measures showed both countries were translating leader consensus into concrete policy. "These steps will help both sides better fulfill their commitments, strengthen confidence in negotiations and lay a sound foundation for future trade talks," he told the Global Times.

Cheng Dawei, a professor at the School of Economics at Renmin University of China, emphasized the wider significance. "These concrete policy actions provide an anchor of certainty in an increasingly uncertain world," she said.

Yet the calm on tariffs sits uneasily alongside mounting non-tariff friction. The latest moves came days after Vice Premier He Lifeng, China's lead official for economic and trade affairs with the US, held a video call on the evening of November 6 with US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Trade Representative Jamieson Greer. According to a [statement](https://ru.china-embassy.gov.cn/zgxw/202611/t20261107_12055300.htm) from the Chinese Embassy in Russia, the two sides held "candid, in-depth and constructive" exchanges, and China "expressed serious concerns over recent US economic and trade restrictive measures against China."

Song argued that Washington's contrasting moves — implementing commitments where consensus exists while pressing China on technology firms, outbound investment and biotechnology — reflect a "dual approach" aimed at extracting extra leverage. "Such an approach is not conducive to the long-term stability of China-US economic and trade relations," he said. "The US needs to change its mindset and meet China halfway, addressing differences through sincere and constructive dialogue rather than tactical pressure."

## What's Next

The two-month alignment through January 9–10, 2027 creates a clear decision point. Under the September consultations, the two countries also agreed to hold their next dialogue on artificial intelligence by the end of November and the first meeting of their agricultural working group by the end of 2026, with negotiators working toward further trade and investment arrangements ahead of the APEC meetings in Shenzhen.

For businesses on both sides, the immediate takeaway is a planning window rather than a resolution. The truce holds for now — but its durability depends less on tariff math than on whether Washington's Section 301 process and its broader restrictions on Chinese firms stay within bounds Beijing is prepared to tolerate. The next two months will test whether incremental stability can survive the pressures pulling the relationship apart.
