# Democrats Won on Affordability. Can They Deliver?

Democrats captured both chambers of Congress in Tuesday's midterms after a disciplined, months-long campaign centered on the cost of living, turning voter anxiety about grocery bills and household expenses into a decisive political mandate. But as the party prepares to take control in January 2027, it confronts a hard reality: the levers most likely to bring down prices rest largely in the hands of President Donald Trump, who retains the veto pen and has dismissed the affordability crisis as a "hoax."

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/09/nx-s1-6030830/democrats-grocery-bill-affordability), the central question now is whether Democrats can translate their electoral win into tangible relief amid divided government — with Senate Democrats short of a filibuster-proof majority and the president able to block legislation.

## An Affordability Mandate

The scale of voter concern was striking. More than 80% of midterm voters said they worried about the price of necessities like gas, groceries and healthcare, according to the [NBC News Exit Poll](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/democrats-affordability-promise-midterms-trump-rcna607409), and 69% said the US economic system favors the wealthy. A September poll by the Kitchen Table Project and Global Strategy Group found that nearly half of respondents named lowering grocery bills within six months as their single most important goal.

"They are feeling like they are getting squeezed by high prices that don't need to be that high," Lael Brainard, who leads the Kitchen Table Project, told NPR.

House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries, expected to become the first Black Speaker of the House, framed the result as a verdict on the cost of living. "It was a tremendous victory for the American people and a strong rebuke of MAGA extremism, which has made life more expensive for everyday Americans despite their promises to lower the high cost of living on day one," he said.

## The Price Drivers Democrats Don't Control

The forces pushing prices higher are, in large part, policies the White House controls. Inflation rose to 3.7% annually in September 2026, according to the [Bureau of Labor Statistics data reported by NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/economy/inflation-jumped-september-gas-diesel-prices-squeezed-consumers-ahead-rcna603202). The Trump administration's tariffs have raised the cost of imported foods and the imported inputs — machine parts, packaging — that food producers rely on. The US-Israel war in Iran drove energy prices up, raising transport costs, while the closure of the Strait of Hormuz cut off a significant share of global fertilizer trade, pushing fertilizer costs higher. And the president's immigration crackdown has produced labor shortages on American farms.

That dynamic shapes what Democrats can realistically achieve. As Lehigh University political scientist Chris Borick put it to NPR: "I think the president would certainly want to find ways to reduce the cost of groceries. Democrats [also] see that in their interest... The mechanisms, the tools, the levers that would be most readily available to do that — there's quite a bit of distance between the president and Democrats that'll be taking over both the House and the Senate."

## What Democrats Say They'll Do

At the federal level, Democrats' plans begin with rolling back Trump's tariffs, which they argue have raised the cost of imported foods and drinks and made imported production inputs more expensive. Their first 100 hours in power are expected to focus on restoring enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies that expired in 2025 and reversing Medicaid cuts and work requirements enacted in the 2025 Republican "One Big Beautiful Bill Act."

Democrats also intend to reassert Congress' authority over tariffs, force votes on war powers resolutions aimed at ending the Iran war, and use the appropriations process to withhold war funding. Incoming senators have floated their own proposals: James Talarico of Texas wants to defend WIC and SNAP, eliminate tax cuts benefiting the wealthiest, expand first-time homebuyer assistance and suspend the federal gas and diesel tax; Adam Hamilton of Kansas proposes a national ban on "dynamic pricing" in grocery stores; Roy Cooper of North Carolina wants to block food industry mergers; and Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan is pressing for "Medicare for All."

At the state level, where at least 28 states will have Democratic governors and ten legislative chambers flipped, leaders are pursuing their own affordability measures — from allowing municipalities to suspend local sales taxes on groceries, baby formula and diapers, to outlawing "predatory surveillance pricing" and price gouging.

El-Sayed suggested Democrats should pass their agenda and force the president's hand. "I want us to get to the work of passing legislation. If Trump vetoes that, it's on him," he told NPR. "But I think you're going to see a whole lot of Republicans thinking twice about whether they want these Medicaid cuts for example to go through."

## The Math of Divided Government

The structural obstacles are formidable. Even with both chambers, Democrats lack the two-thirds majorities needed to override a presidential veto and the 60 votes needed to break a Senate filibuster. Meanwhile, the party's new majority is ideologically broad — spanning moderates who flipped red-leaning districts in Iowa, Kansas, South Carolina and Michigan, alongside a fresh class of progressives.

That tension is already visible. Some moderates are urging restraint. "We shouldn't overpromise at the risk of underdelivering," Representative Brad Schneider, who chairs the New Democrats caucus, told NPR. And Brian Schatz, the third-ranking Senate Democrat, suggested the boldest changes are "for 2028."

Molly Reynolds, a Congress expert at the Brookings Institution, noted that wave elections tend to remove the other party's moderates, leaving a more conservative Republican conference — and a harder negotiating partner. "When you have a wave election like this, the seats in the other party that get taken out often tend to be the more moderate end of that party," she told NPR.

Democrats are also balancing affordability work against an aggressive oversight agenda. With subpoena power, they plan investigations touching the president, his family, cabinet officials, Elon Musk and Epstein-related bank records, alongside ethics reforms such as a stock-trading ban and limits on super PACs — priorities that compete for political attention and floor time.

## What to Watch

Trump's posture after the losses has been inconsistent. He said he would be willing to work with Democrats — echoing a similar olive branch after the 2018 midterms that produced no meaningful legislation — while also threatening to be "Mr. Veto." Senator Ron Wyden, the top Democrat on the Senate Finance Committee, summarized the party's twin aims: priorities "will be affordability for us, accountability for them."

For voters like Joel Rutherford, a Democratic activist in Macomb County, Michigan, the stakes are concrete. "If the Democrats, hopefully, take the Senate and take the House, they better do something, OK?" he told NPR. "People better see some real change."

Whether that change arrives will depend less on what Democrats pass than on whether they can compel a president from the opposing party to sign it — or persuade voters that the obstacle is him.
