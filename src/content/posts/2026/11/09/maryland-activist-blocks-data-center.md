# A Maryland Activist Helped Block a $5B Data Center

Taylor Frazier McCollum had never organized a campaign before February 2025, when a neighbor told her that a $5 billion data center campus was slated for the former Landover Mall site less than a mile from where she was living in Prince George's County, Maryland. Her response was immediate. "I was like, 'Oh, hell no,'" she recalled. "Why would you want to bring water-polluting, diesel-generating data centers right down the street from where people live, work, play? It just didn't make sense to me." What followed, according to [NPR and WAMU](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/07/nx-s1-6007770/data-centers-activist-moratorium), was a petition, a protest movement, and a countywide pause on new hyperscale data centers — making her an unlikely face of a national backlash now reshaping American politics.

The shuttered Landover Mall, closed since 2002, sits on roughly 90 acres of largely vacant land. In 2024, developer Lerner Enterprises — the D.C.-based firm that built the original mall and is run by the family that owns the Washington Nationals — won county approval for an artificial intelligence "tech park" on the site. As [Slate](https://slate.com/technology/2026/08/ai-data-centers-protests-maryland.html) detailed, the plan called for five buildings across 87 acres, drawing 820 megawatts of power, with construction scheduled for January 2026. Few residents in the majority-Black community nearby seemed to know the project was coming. The county's planning department had approved it quickly, citing the region's need for tax revenue.

## A different kind of activist

Unlike many land-use campaigners defending property they own, Frazier McCollum did not own a home. In 2024 she and her young daughter were evicted from their apartment after her wages were cut; they were living at her grandfather's house when she learned of the data center proposal. She created a petition that eventually gathered more than 25,000 signatures, and began posting videos warning that large data centers elsewhere had driven up utility bills, polluted air and water, and lowered property values.

Her effort collided with a county process that offered little room for public input. At a September 2025 meeting of the county's Qualified Data Center Task Force, residents found that spoken comment was barred — feedback could only be written on Post-it notes stuck to whiteboards. Organizers nevertheless drew crowds that lined up around the block. "I live across the street from this Landover site, and I had no idea they were building this data center there," one attendee told a protest organizer, as Slate reported.

## A countywide pause

The pressure worked. Prince George's County Executive Aisha Braveboy issued an executive order halting the Landover groundbreaking and pausing all new hyperscale data center permits — a move the county council later extended, ultimately stretching the pause to two years. The case became emblematic of a broader shift: communities across the country have prompted an estimated 500 moratoriums on data centers, though estimates vary, NPR reported.

The Landover fight also laid bare a genuine tension. With the project paused, some county officials worry about vanishing tax revenue. Six Flags America closed last year, the NFL's Washington Commanders have announced plans to return to neighboring Washington, D.C., and thousands of federal workers have lost their jobs — all against the backdrop of a county budget deficit. At a September council meeting, member Wanika Fisher, who supports moving some data center projects forward, reminded Frazier McCollum that the county needs development. "Our county has an economic future that we have to grapple with," Fisher said. "I don't see where the money is coming from. I think we have to have a real conversation. And I'm not saying that a data center is the answer, but I'm saying it's worth the hard discussion."

## A national inflection point

The Landover pause coincided with a wider reckoning. In July 2026, New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed an executive order barring new hyperscale data centers using 50 megawatts or more of power for up to a year — the first statewide ban of its kind in the country. "These hyperscale AI data centers consume enormous amounts of power, truly threatening to outpace our grid's capacity," Hochul said. "They drive up costs for local ratepayers, and I refuse to let those costs get passed down to New Yorkers," [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/14/new-york-ai-data-center-ban.html).

The politics soon followed. According to [Foreign Policy](https://foreignpolicy.com/2026/11/05/midterm-results-ai-data-centers-texas-georgia-ohio/), the 2026 midterms served as a partial referendum on the data center boom: 57 percent of voters told NBC exit pollsters that AI would do more harm than good, and 56 percent called the technology a major factor in their vote. Of 41 local data center measures on ballots across 10 states, voters rejected the projects in all but one. Texas Governor Greg Abbott, who had courted the industry, was unseated by a challenger calling for an immediate moratorium.

Analysts caution that pausing is easier than resolving. "It's one thing to impose a pause for a period of time. It's another to actually make use of that time and figure out where to go next," Slate noted — pointing out that only one U.S. city, Monterey Park, California, has passed a permanent data center ban, and that moratoriums often leave existing permits intact.

## What's next

For Frazier McCollum, the fight is no longer just local. With grants secured by longtime environmental organizer Staci Hartwell and others, she has taken her message to Black communities in Atlanta, Memphis, and as far as Zambia, and she now wants a full ban — first on Landover and eventually statewide. "I feel like so many people are depending on this fight to be successful," she said. "Not just for myself, but even across the country, to set precedent for other people that are fighting against hyperscale data centers."

Her father, Lacy McCollum Jr., frames the effort in the language of civil rights. "We're talking about the right to water, the right to clean air, the right to a place to live. That's everybody's fight," he said. "People like Taylor, they jump out here so the people behind them get to make progress."

Some urge her to run for office; she says she wants to stabilize her own housing and finances first, though she has "aspirations to run for something." In the meantime, she is pitching alternatives for the old mall site — a sports complex, affordable housing, or heritage tourism — while acknowledging none would be as lucrative as a data center campus. Whether the county's two-year pause becomes a permanent turning point, or merely a delay, may be the test that determines how far the national backlash can go.
