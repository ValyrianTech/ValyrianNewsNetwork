# Femina Goes to Haenel, Goncourt to Jaenada

French writer Yannick Haenel has won the 2026 Femina prize for *La solitude des professeurs est infinie*, a novel drawn from his years teaching in the Paris suburbs, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576). The award, announced on Wednesday 4 November, came just one day after the Goncourt — France's most prestigious literary honour — went to Philippe Jaenada for *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel*. Haenel had himself been a Goncourt finalist, meaning he lost that prize to Jaenada before claiming the Femina the following afternoon.

## A prize decided by twelve women

The Femina jury, a panel of twelve women, met at the Musée Carnavalet–Histoire de Paris and selected Haenel's novel over finalists including Emma Marsantes and Lucie Rico. The prize was founded in 1904 by women journalists at the magazine *Fémina* as an all-female counterweight to the then all-male Goncourt, and it is traditionally announced the day after its older rival — a rhythm that this year produced a striking one-two for the French literary season.

In the companion categories, the Femina étranger (foreign novel) went to the American writer Laura Kasischke for *Baignade surveillée*, and the Femina essai to Jennifer Tamas for *Barbe Bleue contre-enquête*, RTBF reported. In 2025 the Femina had honoured Nathacha Appanah for *La Nuit au cœur*.

## A novel about the solitude of teaching

*La solitude des professeurs est infinie*, published by Gallimard in late August, follows Jean Deichel, the author's fictional double, as a young trainee French teacher arrives at a middle school in Mantes-la-Jolie, west of Paris. As [franceinfo](https://www.franceinfo.fr/culture/livres/prix-litteraires/la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-de-yannick-haenel-prix-femina-2026-quand-l-enseignement-cessera-t-il-d-etre-un-sport-de-combat_8267690.html) described it, the book is at once an initiation story and a "cry" about a profession under strain.

Accepting the award at Dior's Paris headquarters, Haenel dedicated the book "to the teachers, to the high-schoolers and to the middle-schoolers," as a student protest movement unfolded in France that autumn. The 59-year-old, who studied in Rennes and taught in the 1990s before turning to writing, is the author of *Jan Karski* (Prix Interallié 2009) and *Tiens ferme ta couronne* (Prix Médicis 2017), and writes a column for the satirical weekly *Charlie Hebdo*.

Haenel has said the murders of teachers Samuel Paty in 2020 and Dominique Bernard in 2023, and a rise in attacks on teachers over the past three years, drove him back to his classroom memories. "With all the attacks on teachers that have multiplied over these last three years, I plunged into the obsession," he said, according to franceinfo.

## Jaenada's first-round Goncourt

A day earlier, the Académie Goncourt awarded its prize to Jaenada, 62, on the first round with six votes to three for Sylvain Prudhomme's *De l'autre côté du lac*, [franceinfo reported](https://www.franceinfo.fr/culture/livres/prix-litteraires/philippe-jaenada-a-remporte-le-prix-goncourt-2026-avec-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel_8265806.html). Jaenada succeeds Laurent Mauvignier, the 2025 laureate, and had himself won the Femina in 2017.

*L'Inconnue du quai de Javel*, published by Flammarion, is a sprawling true-crime investigation into the 1949 murder of Louise Cansot, a model much admired by Montparnasse painters, who was found strangled in Paris. Philippe Claudel, president of the Académie Goncourt, said the book "makes visible a woman who had been rendered invisible — a victim, a young victim the police did not much concern themselves with." Jaenada told reporters the prize was "a culmination" and "an honour," adding: "I feel joyful."

The Goncourt carries only a symbolic €10 cheque but reliably transforms an author's fortunes, with more than 500,000 copies of the winning title expected to sell. On the same day, the Renaudot — marking its 100th edition — went to François-Henri Désérable for *Le Palais*, won on the first round by six votes to four, [franceinfo reported](https://www.franceinfo.fr/culture/livres/francois-henri-deserable-remporte-le-renaudot-2026-avec-le-palais_8265878.html).

## Concentration and contention behind the prizes

This year's Goncourt finalists all came from the Madrigall group, France's fourth-largest publisher, which owns Gallimard, Flammarion and Minuit — meaning the group was guaranteed a win regardless of the outcome, a quirk that prompted one franceinfo piece to dub the finale "absurd." The detail points to a deeper story about consolidation in French publishing, where a handful of houses increasingly dominate the prize shortlists.

The 2026 season was also marked by debates beyond the books themselves. Jaenada has publicly endorsed what he calls a "moderate and playful" use of artificial intelligence, and a separate controversy swirled around another novel over alleged AI involvement in its writing. Days after the awards, more than 200 women authors signed an open letter denouncing what they called the "sexism" of the French literary prize system — a pointed backdrop for a Femina whose very origins lie in redressing an all-male prize.

## What to watch

For readers, the practical effect is immediate: the Goncourt and Femina seals typically send winning titles to the top of French bookshop displays and bestseller lists within days. The longer-term questions are institutional. Whether French publishing's prize culture can broaden beyond a dominant few houses, how it reckons with AI, and whether the industry answers the women authors' open letter will shape the seasons ahead. For now, though, the year belongs to two novels rooted in human tragedy — one excavating a forgotten victim of 1949, the other giving voice to the teachers of the present.
