# Belgian Army Lost Two Heavy Sniper Rifles Last Year

The Belgian army lost two heavy sniper rifles belonging to its elite special units in 2025, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/17/belgisch-leger-verloor-vorig-jaar-2-zware-scherpschuttersgeweren/) on Tuesday, citing reporting by Het Nieuwsblad. One is a precision rifle used by the sniper teams of the special forces; the other is a heavier weapon capable of disabling lightly armoured vehicles. How and where the two firearms disappeared is not known, and it remains unclear whether they were stolen or simply lost.

The loss is the latest in a documented pattern of weapons going missing from Belgium's Defence department and police, and it has renewed questions about armoury security, record-keeping and accountability. Defence says it intends to track weapons and other military materiel more closely using new technology such as tags.

## "Very hard to explain"

"This is very hard to explain," Yves Huwart, chairman of the military union ACMP-CGPM, told VRT NWS's "De Ochtend" on Radio 1. He pointed to the complicated logistics involved when soldiers are deployed around the world, where something can go wrong — for instance when "a weapon case momentarily ends up in the wrong direction and ultimately does not arrive where it should."

Huwart stressed that the weapons are heavily secured, both in the armouries and during transport, and that those who handle them are constantly reminded that there is no room for carelessness. "A great many measures are taken, but a zero risk of incidents naturally does not exist," he said. Such a loss happens "relatively rarely, but every time is one time too many." For the soldier and for whoever is responsible for managing the weapon, it amounts to "a kind of reprimand — nobody is happy about it."

Whether the rifles were stolen is unclear. Standard procedure after such a loss is an internal investigation into the cause; if there are indications of theft, the police are called in. Huwart does not know where the weapons are now. "It is quite possible they end up in the wrong hands," he said. "But equally, they may have been lost in some wooded area and we find them again within 20 years."

## The weapons involved

The army uses two rifle types for such missions. The British Accuracy International AX338 is a long-range precision rifle; the shooter must manually reload after each shot. It can hit targets at up to 1,400 metres, is about 1.25 metres long (1.03 metres with the stock folded) and weighs more than 7 kilograms without additional equipment.

The other weapon is the American Barrett M107A1, a semi-automatic rifle firing 12.7 mm rounds — the same calibre as heavy machine guns. It is about 1.44 metres long and weighs roughly 12.5 kilograms without additional equipment. Its heavy calibre allows it to be deployed against lightly armoured vehicles and other materiel. VRT noted that especially the Barrett is notably large and heavy.

## A pattern of losses

This is not the first time weapons have vanished from Defence. Between 2017 and 2021, the army [lost 86 firearms](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2022/06/22/wapens-munitie-verliezen-defensie/) — nearly double the 46 lost in the preceding five-year period — and only about a quarter were recovered, according to figures obtained through a parliamentary question by MP Peter Buysrogge (N-VA). In that same period, five weapons were stolen and ten files were opened for stolen ammunition. Defence emphasised that the annual number of losses was actually stable, noting that since 2020 pepper sprays and batons have also been counted as "lost or stolen weapons."

In 2021, Defence was embroiled in controversy when soldier Jürgen Conings took heavy weapons from a barracks. Conings held far-right sympathies and had made threats against virologist Marc Van Ranst and the government over coronavirus policy. After a month-long manhunt, his body was found in a forest in Dilsen-Stokkem; he had died by suicide. The case became a symbol of security failures at Defence.

The problem extends to the police. A semi-automatic FN SCAR assault rifle of the federal police disappeared from a barracks in Etterbeek — last seen on 24 May 2024, but only confirmed missing in March 2025. A confidential General Inspectorate report later described [deficient internal controls](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/04/17/vernietigend-rapport-na-verdwijning-aanvalsgeweer-fn-scar-stru/), missing logbooks, and access codes that were never changed. A VRT NWS investigation published in April 2026 found that at least [26 firearms were lost or stolen by police](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/01/09/hoeveel-politiewapens-zijn-gestolen-of-verdwenen-niemand-in-bel/) between 2021 and 2025 — a figure that may be an underestimate because of poor registration.

## Tracing versus operational security

Defence's plan to tag weapons and track them more closely may help with transport and other logistical movements, Huwart suggested. But on the ground, he said, it is harder: a signal that traces a weapon can also reveal the position of soldiers. "When soldiers operate in tactical environments, it is precisely not advisable to be able to trace where a weapon is located."

That tension is central to the debate. Better tracking could improve accountability for lost or stolen materiel, but it also carries operational risks for the units that rely on these specialised weapons — units whose very purpose involves operating discreetly.

The criminal dimension is equally serious. As Nils Duquet of the Flemish Peace Institute has noted, legal firearms that disappear often end up in the criminal underworld, and criminals have a strong interest in heavy, military-grade weapons such as a 12.7 mm Barrett or a .338 precision rifle.

## What to watch

The cause of the loss remains undetermined, with an internal investigation the first step and police involvement only if theft is suspected. So far no public accountability has been established for who is responsible or what consequences follow.

The story fits a documented pattern at both Defence and the police: poor armoury controls, missing logbooks and weak central registration. It also arrives at a time when Parliament has repeatedly demanded figures on missing weapons — questions that have themselves exposed how incompletely such losses are recorded.

The key open questions are whether the two rifles will ever be recovered, whether investigators determine they were lost or stolen, and whether Defence's promised tagging technology can close the gap between oversight and operational secrecy. For now, the army can say little more than that the weapons are missing — and that, as its own union representative put it, every such loss is one time too many.
