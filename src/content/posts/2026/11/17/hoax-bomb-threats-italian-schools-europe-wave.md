# Hoax Bomb Threats Hit 6,300 Italian Schools in Europe-Wide Wave

Around 6,300 schools across Italy received an identical hoax bomb-threat email on Wednesday 11 November, prompting evacuations at hundreds of sites, while more than 700 schools in Scotland and Northern Ireland were sent similar threatening messages, according to authorities. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni said checks had turned up no evidence of a "concrete danger", describing the alerts as a strategy to "cause alarm and concern".

The coordinated wave of false threats — swiftly assessed as hoaxes on all sides — is the latest disruption to hit European institutions in a season of hybrid-warfare-style attacks, from flight bomb scares to cyberattacks on supermarket refrigeration systems.

## A single email, thousands of inboxes

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/11/bommeldingen-scholen-europa/), the initial picture in Italy was of more than 300 schools evacuated after the emails arrived. The education ministry later reported that roughly 6,300 of Italy's 7,476 state schools had received the message, though not all were emptied of pupils. Private schools were unaffected, [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwz0k79pr8z2o) reported.

In many cases the alarm lifted as quickly as it arose. Some pupils did not even realise a bomb threat had been issued, assuming the bell had simply signalled an early break. In Scotland, where some schools were briefly evacuated, most lessons continued normally, and authorities in Northern Ireland advised schools to remain open.

Italy's government convened an emergency meeting and Meloni conferred with the intelligence services. "These reports appear rather linked to a strategy intended to cause alarm and concern," she said via Facebook. In Northern Ireland, officials said police did not regard the threats as credible, and Police Scotland said there was "no requirement to evacuate schools or keep children away from education based solely on the content of the emails".

## A recurring signature

Italian media that viewed a copy of the email reported that it also contained threats directed at Meloni, though neither she nor her office commented on them directly. The message was signed by a purported figure named "Mikhail Andropov, leader of the Vilayat of Misoginistan", [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/11/at-least-300-italian-schools-evacuated-after-email-bomb-alerts) reported, citing a copy obtained by Reuters. The same supposed author claimed responsibility for email bomb threats sent days earlier to newsrooms in Ireland and Bulgaria, one of which led Ireland's public broadcaster to be evacuated.

The BBC noted that the emails sent to Scottish schools carried similar wording but referenced "Mikhail Andropov" as the leader of a "Red Owl" project instead — suggesting either a shared template or a copycat variation. The name echoes that of the former Soviet leader Yuri Andropov, though there is no evidence linking the messages to any state.

## Prank, cyberattack or hybrid warfare?

Italian Education Minister Giuseppe Valditara told the La7 television channel that the incidents were probably a "cyberattack rather than a genuine threat". "It is clear that there is a strategy at play," he said. "It is difficult to say right now who might be behind this. Intelligence services are, of course, working to identify them."

But Italy's Defence Minister Guido Crosetto cautioned against assuming a foreign hand. "It is not always the work of other countries or hostile entities," he said, according to VRT NWS. "There are also attention-seekers and madmen who, in times like today, cannot wait to sow terror in a city or a region."

The mundane evidence points in a similarly undramatic direction. One Milan headteacher, Anna Bertato, told Italian media she had checked the sender's email address online and found it "was already classified as spam by a student at a university in Taiwan". Another, Massimo Nunzio Barrella, said the text "seemed almost comical... it was like a message from Martians: 'We have come to annihilate you.' I even had a bit of a laugh."

## Part of a wider campaign

The school threats did not emerge in isolation. As Al Jazeera reported, they came amid rising European concern over hybrid-warfare threats targeting supermarkets, blood banks, air traffic and broadcasters. This month, flights across Europe have been diverted, grounded and escorted by fighter jets in response to a wave of bomb threats. Hackers also raised the temperature of refrigeration systems at supermarkets in Germany, Romania and the UK, mirroring an October cyberattack that swept hundreds of French supermarkets.

On Tuesday 10 November, France summoned Iran's ambassador after accusing Tehran of the supermarket cyberattack — an accusation Tehran rejected as "baseless". Some European officials suspect Russia of orchestrating a broader disruption campaign against countries supporting Ukraine, a charge Moscow denies as a smear. No country has formally attributed the school bomb threats to any state or group.

A mass hoax email had already hit Scottish schools on 19 October, and an extremist misogynistic group that had earlier claimed some threats later denied any link to the false warnings.

## What to watch

The immediate danger appears to have passed, but the episode lays bare a deeper anxiety. An Italian teacher, quoted in a VRT video report, put it plainly: "This is how war now goes. We have to get used to that idea, mentally and psychologically."

Scotland's Education Secretary Mairi McAllan said specialist cyber-crime officers were working to identify the source of the messages, liaising across the UK and internationally. Investigators must now determine whether the wave of hoaxes reflects an organised campaign, a lone prankster with a copied template, or something in between — a distinction with real consequences for how Europe responds to the next disruption.

For now, the schools are open, the pupils are back in class, and the emails have been filed as what they most likely were: a cheap, effective way to spread fear.
