# Supreme Court Rebuffs Trump Appeal on DEI Grant Cuts

The Supreme Court on Monday declined to hear the Trump administration's emergency appeal over its termination of millions of dollars in federal research grants to University of California researchers, leaving intact a lower court ruling that found the cancellations likely violated the First Amendment. No justice publicly dissented from the brief order, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/supreme-court-trump-administration-dei-36d846810d632fba17364355de99efa8).

The procedural loss keeps the administration's DEI-linked funding restrictions on hold for now, though the underlying lawsuit will continue to play out in the lower courts.

## Background

The dispute traces to the first days of President Trump's second term, when he signed executive orders directing federal agencies to cancel grants tied to certain topics, including diversity, equity and inclusion. According to [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/supreme-court-trump-dei-research-grants/), agencies carried out those orders by running keyword searches of grant applications and terminating awards that used words such as "diversity," "climate change" and "gender ideology."

Lawyers for the University of California said the administration cut off more than $324 million in grants to the university system alone between January and June 2025. Grant recipients began receiving termination letters in April 2025, and six UC researchers whose funding came from the Environmental Protection Agency, the National Science Foundation and the National Endowment for the Humanities filed suit.

## The Lower Court Rulings

In June 2025, U.S. District Judge Rita Lin sided with the plaintiffs, concluding the grants were likely terminated en masse based on the perceived viewpoint of the research rather than for legitimate programmatic reasons. She ordered the agencies to reinstate the funding and certified a provisional class of UC researchers.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit upheld the core injunction in May 2026. The panel wrote that the government "has not merely exercised its power to choose the programs it funds but has instead terminated individual grants in existing programs based on the perceived viewpoints of individual speakers."

The administration argued that the government is not obligated to keep funding research that no longer fits its priorities, and the Justice Department [warned](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/26/26-210/419403/20260818155050746_Trump_v_Thakur_petn_final.pdf) that allowing the 9th Circuit ruling to stand would clear the way for taxpayer dollars to fund research promoting DEI initiatives.

## Where the Case Stands

A settlement covering the EPA grants resolved that portion of the dispute. The remaining fight concerns NSF and NEH awards, with roughly $59 million still at issue on grants that expire as late as November 2029.

The Supreme Court's order is a procedural setback for the administration rather than a ruling on the merits. The case returns to the lower courts, and the administration could seek review again after a final judgment.

## A Separate Signal on Implicit Bias Training

The justices issued the grants order the same day they declined to hear a separate First Amendment challenge to California's requirement that continuing medical education courses for physicians include discussion of implicit bias. In that case, *Khatibi v. Yip*, three conservative justices signaled interest in the question.

Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito dissented from the denial, and Justice Neil Gorsuch noted he would have heard the case, according to the [Pacific Legal Foundation](https://pacificlegal.org/press-release/supreme-court-wont-hear-doctors-challenge-to-californias-compelled-speech-mandate-three-justices-disagree/), which represents the plaintiff. "A State cannot regulate its way out of First Amendment scrutiny," Thomas wrote.

The 9th Circuit had held that California regulates medicine so heavily that the courses count as government speech, placing them outside First Amendment scrutiny. Thomas argued that logic misreads Supreme Court precedent.

## Analysis and What's Next

Taken together, the two orders reveal a Court content to let the grant litigation unfold while signaling that at least part of its conservative wing wants to revisit how far states can compel professional speech. Three votes to hear *Khatibi* fell short of the four needed to grant review, but the dissent marks the issue as a live candidate for a future term.

For now, the practical effect is a continuing injunction protecting the remaining NSF and NEH awards. The district court still must handle the pending EPA settlement, which has a preliminary-approval hearing set for December 10.

Beyond this case, the outcome is a marker in the broader legal contest over the administration's use of federal funding as leverage across agencies. Courts have repeatedly been asked to decide where a government's discretion to set funding priorities ends and unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination begins. The Supreme Court declined to answer that question this week, leaving it to lower courts to keep drawing the line case by case.
