# Trump Seeks $50B to Rebuild FAA as Democrats Question Costs

WASHINGTON — The Trump administration formally asked Congress on Tuesday for $50.1 billion to overhaul the nation's aging air traffic control system, days after a router failure triggered the first nationwide grounding of all U.S. flights. But Democrats are demanding to know how billions already appropriated have been spent — and why the price tag keeps climbing.

The request, sent in a letter to key lawmakers and [obtained by The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/nationwide-ground-stop-trump-air-travel-faa-a638c9651f1fabe8f95d20353779ee5b), sets up a politically charged fight over one of the most visible failures of national infrastructure. It unfolds just as Democrats prepare to take control of Congress in January, when scrutiny of the administration is set to intensify.

"Congressional approval of these funds will equip the FAA to deliver to the American people a brand-new air traffic control system that is safer, smarter, faster, and more efficient," the administration's letter states.

## What happened

The crisis began Friday, when a federal contractor replacing a section of old copper wire with new fiber optic cable at a remote FAA facility in El Dorado, Arkansas, caused the system to overload. After the wire was swapped and a new router was turned on, a second problem surfaced at a facility in Amarillo, Texas, cascading into the nationwide ground stop.

The FAA said "excessive data" flooded the system "at a rate of 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds," destabilizing "aircraft communications and radar feeds." The deluge overwhelmed routers that are roughly 25 years old.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the shutdown was necessary because safety is paramount. "Specifically, we had over 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds," he said Saturday. "And what that did was gave us issues with our communication with airplanes, and it also caused some disruption with our radar feeds. And when we see that, your safety is paramount, we shut down the airspace."

Intermittent frequency instability affected as many as 17 of the 22 regional air traffic control centers, according to FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford. Those centers direct planes cruising at high altitudes, above roughly 10,000 feet.

As [AP News explained](https://apnews.com/article/ground-stop-air-traffic-control-0a71f6d3b52f9087105ebea7c527bdd7), the failure was unusually far-reaching because it struck the communications network rather than a single facility. "The thing that was unique about this is that it was a type of failure that wasn't local because it was part of the communication system," said R. John Hansman, an aeronautics professor at MIT. "It propagated around the country."

Planes in the air landed safely using well-known procedures, and the FAA lifted the ground stops by the weekend. But the disruption rippled onward: on Monday, a system outage lasting more than 90 minutes forced United Airlines to delay departing flights, keeping scrutiny high.

## A decades-old problem, and a rising bill

The need to modernize U.S. air traffic control is a decades-old challenge. The system still relies on copper wiring, vintage routers, radars built in the 1980s and, in some cases, floppy disks — technology that predates the modern internet era.

Congress has repeatedly appropriated large sums. Under President Joe Biden, lawmakers approved $25 billion over five years for airport and air traffic control improvements, much of it directed to runways, taxiways and terminals. Then, in 2025, a Republican-led Congress approved $12.5 billion to modernize the FAA's air traffic system, targeting outdated radars and control centers dating to the 1960s. Republicans used the same bill to allocate $170 billion for Trump's immigration crackdown and border security.

The upgrade has two phases. The first, funded by the $12.5 billion, is supposed to be complete by December 2028. Bedford told lawmakers in September that a second phase — new systems to track aircraft and optimize traffic — would cost at least $10 billion more.

The administration's formal request breaks down into $10.1 billion for updating the air traffic control system, with similar amounts for airport infrastructure, terminals at high-impact hubs, five new traffic control centers and improving or replacing up to 140 FAA-owned control towers.

Only about 400 of more than 3,600 routers have been replaced so far, according to Senate Democrats.

## Democrats demand answers

Democrats say they, too, want a modern system — but they are pressing the administration to justify a fresh request when much of the prior funding remains unspent.

"Explain to Congress, again, why they need more money?" Rep. Rick Larsen of Washington, the ranking Democrat on the House Transportation Committee, and Rep. André Carson of Indiana, the top Democrat on a subcommittee with aviation jurisdiction, said in a statement. Rep. Rosa DeLauro of Connecticut, the top Democrat on the House Appropriations Committee, said Congress has shown it is willing to invest.

"But we need a competent partner at the Department of Transportation to carry this out in a sustainable, transparent manner," she said. "Right now, it is clear that we lack such a partner."

The tension sharpened Tuesday when Democrats on the Senate Commerce Committee sent a letter to Duffy demanding root-cause answers and calling for a pause on router replacement until the failure is fully understood. "Identifying the device that failed does not establish why it failed," the letter states. "FAA must investigate further to determine the root cause and report to Congress on how it intends to prevent future issues."

Duffy has countered that Congress restricted how the 2025 money could be used — specifically excluding the FAA's telecommunications infrastructure — and that the earlier appropriation will be spent by the end of 2028.

## The political stakes

President Donald Trump stayed publicly silent for two days after the grounding, weighing in only on Sunday with a post on Truth Social that blamed "old tech" and his Democratic predecessor. As [AP News reported](https://apnews.com/article/nationwide-ground-stop-trump-air-travel-faa-6f58e64d9ced2543aaaf7317ec389d06), Trump later defended his handling of the episode, saying the equipment had "a heart attack" and insisting, "I don't take any responsibility" for Friday's ground stop. "If Congress approves the desperately needed money for Air Traffic Control, we can get it built quickly and efficiently," he wrote. "It will be the BEST SYSTEM anywhere in the World. Much of our current air control system is 50 years old - RIDICULOUS!"

The blame-shifting follows a familiar pattern, and it underscores the political divisions that could widen when Democrats take control of Congress in January. Officials said intelligence agencies found no evidence of a cyberattack, easing fears of hostile action while highlighting the system's fragility.

## What to watch

The fight now moves to a Congress that will look very different in the new year. Defenders of the request note that telecom companies including AT&T, Verizon and Lumen say they are ready to deploy a new network architecture quickly if funded. Critics ask why billions more are needed before the existing money is fully deployed — and before investigators confirm the root cause of Friday's failure.

For the more than 45,000 flights that rely on the system every day, the answer cannot come soon enough.
