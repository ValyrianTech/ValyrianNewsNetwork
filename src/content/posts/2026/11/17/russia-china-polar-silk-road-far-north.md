# Russia and China Build a Polar Silk Road in the Far North

Moscow and Beijing are deepening an Arctic partnership that links China's Maritime Silk Road to Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor, a quiet but consequential rapprochement that is reshaping the geopolitics of the High North and drawing a security-first response from the European Union. According to an analysis published by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719), the cooperation centres on Russian ports, container-terminal automation, joint maritime research and the Northern Sea Route — an emerging "polar Silk Road" that Western capitals are watching closely.

## Why the Arctic matters now

Climate change is the catalyst. Melting ice is opening maritime routes that were previously inaccessible to commercial navigation, while the Arctic seabed is sharpening appetites for resources. Per US Geological Survey estimates cited by RTBF, the region holds nearly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and 13% of its undiscovered oil.

The strategic logic for both capitals is clear. For Moscow, the Northern Sea Route offers a way to deliver more oil and gas to China while circumventing Western sanctions imposed over the war in Ukraine, and to export safely outside European waters. For Beijing, a strengthened polar corridor reduces dependence on the Strait of Malacca and builds redundancy into trade and energy supply chains.

## The Patrushev mission

The recent visit to China by Nikolai Patrushev, an advisor to Vladimir Putin, illustrated the two countries' intent to deepen maritime cooperation in the Far North. According to [Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/), discussions during the October visit focused on articulating China's Silk Road with Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor, developing Russian ports, exchanging container-terminal automation technology and conducting joint maritime research.

Russia's asks were largely commercial: Chinese corporate involvement in the Northern Sea Route, a direct container link between Shanghai's Yangshan port and Russian ports, investment in terminals and Chinese digital expertise in port automation. Moscow also proposed cooperation in shipbuilding, maritime education and research. Beijing described maritime cooperation as a "blue driving force" for bilateral relations.

Cooperation has widened considerably over the past decade. In 2023 the two countries signed a key Arctic cooperation agreement, and Chinese actors now co-finance large extraction projects such as Yamal LNG while expanding their own nuclear icebreaker fleet. In its 2018 strategy, Beijing already presented China as a "near-Arctic State" with legitimate interests in shipping, resource extraction, scientific research and regional governance.

"China aspires to superpower status. One of its attributes is domination — or at least a presence — in geopolitically important areas of the world, and the Arctic is unquestionably one of them," said Vlastislav Briza, a security expert at Charles University's Faculty of Social Sciences, as quoted by RTBF.

## A shorter, riskier route

The Northern Sea Route dramatically shortens distances, especially between Europe and East Asia. The trip from Shanghai to Hamburg takes about 18 days, against 30 to 35 days via the traditional Suez Canal route.

Trade provides the foundation: China is Russia's largest trading partner, and two-way trade exceeded US$200 billion for a third consecutive year in 2025, according to Chinese government data cited by Asia Times. Maritime transport is becoming a strategic extension of the relationship, not merely a means of moving cargo.

The route's appeal is being amplified by disruption elsewhere. With the Suez Canal and the Strait of Hormuz beset by conflict, and tanker freight rates at record levels, Asian refiners are seeking alternatives. In 2026, Russian oil exports via the Northern Sea Route rose nearly 50% year on year — about 20 million barrels delivered to Asia between July and mid-October, [Modern Diplomacy](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/10/19/russia-ramps-up-arctic-oil-exports-as-global-shipping-crisis-disrupts-trade-routes/), citing Reuters, reported. Gazprom Neft plans to raise its Arctic shipments by roughly 35% this year, and Putin has said cargo volumes could double to 70 million tonnes a year by the end of the decade.

Analysts caution, however, that Arctic traffic remains tiny compared with Suez flows, held back by ice, high insurance premiums, sparse infrastructure and a short operating season. The route is a valuable second network, not a replacement for existing chokepoints.

## Western unease and a militarised North

The rapprochement is alarming Western capitals, in part because it unfolds against a backdrop of Russian military build-up. Russia controls roughly 53% of the Arctic coastline and has reopened Soviet-era bases, deploying Bastion coastal-defence systems, Arctic-adapted S-400s and hypersonic-missile-armed fighters, according to the Polish outlet [Defence24](https://defence24.com/geopolitics/cold-war-at-the-top-of-the-world-the-militarisation-of-the-arctic-and-the-sino-russian-alliance-on-the-northern-sea-route).

"Russia is expanding its military presence in the North. Moscow has reopened Soviet bases and is testing new weapons in the region," EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas warned, adding that "drone incursions, GPS jamming and the sabotage of undersea cables are undermining Arctic security."

Sweden's head of military intelligence, Thomas Nilsson, told The Times that China will use Russia to gain a foothold in the High North, and that Moscow — which once resented Chinese influence in the Arctic — now has no choice, because it depends on Beijing to finance its war effort and for dual-use supplies such as microprocessors.

A key Western constraint is physical. As Defence24 put it, NATO's problem "is not geography, but the lack of specialised operational platforms and adequate means to physically express its power in these waters." The United States reportedly has only two ageing operational icebreakers, against more than 40 for Russia, including Arktika-class nuclear vessels. The EU has roughly ten.

NATO has moved to respond through its Arctic Sentry framework initiative, launched in February 2026 to unify maritime and air patrols, intelligence-sharing and exercises. Finland and Sweden's accession means seven of the eight Arctic Council members are now NATO members.

## Europe's security-first turn

The European Union presented its own Arctic strategy on 20 October 2026, built around four priorities: security and resilience, fighting climate change, prosperity, and prospects for Arctic communities. As [Le Monde](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2026/10/20/eu-unveils-new-arctic-strategy-in-face-of-us-russia-threats_6760261_4.html) observed, the bloc has been forced to shift its Arctic focus from climate to security.

"The Arctic has changed dramatically, and the whole world has changed, especially since Russia's aggression against Ukraine," said Jozef Sikela, the EU commissioner who led preparation of the strategy. "The Arctic is warming up to four times faster than the global average and has become the object of genuine geopolitical rivalry."

The plan envisages strengthening the EU icebreaker fleet, protecting critical infrastructure, and a new Centre for Maritime Security to track Russia's shadow fleet beyond the Mediterranean and Baltic into the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean. It also proposes a sovereign Greenland-Europe subsea cable — part of a wider pan-Arctic link involving Canada, Japan and South Korea — and IRIS2 satellite connectivity designed to rival Starlink. Some measures appear aimed at reducing dependence on Washington.

On drilling, the EU position remains firm. "Oil, coal and gas must stay in the ground," said Energy Commissioner Dan Jorgensen — a stance that diverges sharply from the Trump administration, which has loosened environmental rules to maximise extraction in the US Arctic.

## What to watch

The most consequential elements of the Sino-Russian partnership are also the least dramatic: port automation, digital shipyards, shared technical standards and joint research. As Asia Times noted, "whoever controls the software, data, equipment and logistics architecture of ports can shape trade without ever firing a shot."

The Arctic is unlikely to displace Suez soon. But the direction is set. China supplies Russia with capital, goods and technology; Russia offers China strategic geography. The metric to watch is whether the two can turn these strengths into a coherent parallel maritime system — and whether the West's icebreaker gap and fragmented Arctic posture can close fast enough to matter.
