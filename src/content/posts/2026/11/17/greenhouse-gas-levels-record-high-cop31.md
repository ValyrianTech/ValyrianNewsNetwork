# Greenhouse Gas Levels Hit Record High as COP31 Seeks Action

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases reached unprecedented highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) has reported, locking in decades of additional warming even as negotiators at the COP31 climate summit in Türkiye work to translate pledges into concrete action.

According to a [WMO press release](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high) issued on 27 October 2026, the globally averaged surface concentration of carbon dioxide (CO₂) reached 426.8 parts per million (ppm) in 2025—153% of the pre-industrial baseline of 1750. Methane and nitrous oxide, the second and third most important long-lived greenhouse gases tied to human activity, also climbed to new records.

The finding carries particular weight because the WMO Greenhouse Gas Bulletin reports the *accumulated stock* of gases in the atmosphere rather than new annual emissions. About half of the CO₂ emitted each year remains in the atmosphere, while the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans. Because CO₂ persists for centuries, even sharp cuts to emissions cannot quickly lower concentrations—they can only slow the rate of accumulation.

## A Record That Keeps Being Broken

"Another year, another new record. Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue. CO₂ has a very long life-time and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries."

The scale of the shift is stark when set against the bulletin's own history. When the WMO first published the report in 2004, the annual average CO₂ level measured by its Global Atmosphere Watch network was 377.5 ppm—roughly 49 ppm below today's figure.

Concentrations of methane reached 1,948 parts per billion (ppb) in 2025, or 267% of the 1750 level, while nitrous oxide hit 339.1 ppb, 126% above pre-industrial times. Methane accounts for about 16% of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases, with roughly 65% of emissions coming from human activities such as cattle, rice farming, fossil fuel exploitation and landfills.

Per the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's Annual Greenhouse Gas Index, radiative forcing—the warming effect on the climate—from long-lived greenhouse gases rose 56% between 1990 and 2025, with CO₂ responsible for about 81% of that increase.

## Growth Rate Triples Since the 1960s

The pace of accumulation has itself accelerated. CO₂ growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, rising from an average of 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year over the 2011–2020 decade. From 2024 to 2025, concentrations rose by 2.8 ppm—above that decadal average but slightly below the exceptional 2023/2024 surge.

WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed the more modest 2025 increase to natural variability. "The more modest growth rate from 2024 to 2025 reflects recovery of the land carbon sinks and reduced fire carbon emissions in 2025 after the strong 2023/2024 El Nino, which reduced the efficiency of the biosphere through more drought, drier vegetation and more wildfires," she said.

She cautioned that the trend is unlikely to ease. "However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning. We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026 because of fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño which will impact land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024."

The bulletin also flagged two less abundant but potent trace gases tied to the clean-energy transition itself. Sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆), used mainly as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, has an atmospheric lifetime exceeding 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO₂—and its increase in 2025 was a record high. Molecular hydrogen (H₂), increasingly used as an energy storage medium, rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024; although not itself a greenhouse gas, its leakage influences atmospheric chemistry and has an indirect warming effect.

## COP31 Puts Fossil Fuels on the Table

The WMO released the bulletin explicitly to inform negotiators at COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, running from 9 to 20 November 2026. As the summit entered its second week, Australian Climate Change Minister Chris Bowen, serving as president of negotiations, released an 18-page draft cover decision based on submissions from 160 countries and 144 provisions.

According to [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-17/chris-bowen-launches-bold-climate-agenda-at-cop31/107393394), the draft calls for "accelerated action to phase out fossil fuels, including through the immediate transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems," and backs the "35-by-35" pledge to make 35% of the world's energy use electric by 2035. It also urges developed nations to reach net zero by 2030 and net-negative emissions by 2040, and seeks to triple adaptation finance to at least US$120 billion by 2035.

The text expresses "deep concern that global warming is expected to exceed 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels"—an implicit acknowledgment that the Paris Agreement's most ambitious target may be missed.

Reactions split along familiar lines. Greenpeace Australia Pacific COP31 lead Simon Bradshaw called it "some of the strongest language we've seen in a draft COP decision," while Andreas Sieber, head of political strategy at 350.org, said the wording "stopped short of establishing a clear road map to end fossil fuels." More than 60 civil society groups signed an open letter urging Bowen and Australia—the world's third-largest fossil fuel exporter—to "lift ambition."

## The Gap Between Pledges and the Atmosphere

The juxtaposition of the two stories is the point. Even as renewables expand and electrification pledges advance, atmospheric concentrations keep climbing because the cumulative stock of greenhouse gases is enormous and fossil fuel use persists. This annual record is not a surprise—it is a structural feature of the climate problem: the atmosphere responds to accumulated emissions, not to the promises made about future ones.

## What to Watch

Three variables will shape the months ahead. First, whether COP31's final text preserves the draft's language on fossil fuel phase-out or waters it down during the second week of negotiation. Second, how much a developing El Niño pushes concentrations higher in 2026, potentially producing another record jump. And third, whether the energy transition's own emissions—from SF₆ and hydrogen leakage—are managed carefully enough to avoid undermining its climate benefits.

The WMO's finding is unambiguous: the planet's heat-trapping gas stock is still rising, and the decisions made at COP31 will determine only how fast that accumulation might eventually slow.
