# Hoax Bomb Threats Disrupt 27 European Flights

At least 27 passenger flights across Europe were hit by hoax bomb threats on Thursday, forcing diversions, emergency landings and the scrambling of fighter jets in Italy, France and Bulgaria, according to [RTE News](https://www.rte.ie/news/europe/2026/1105/1598073-europe-flight-threats/). Among the affected services was a Polish LOT flight from Warsaw to Brussels, which landed safely at Brussels Airport in Zaventem before Belgium's federal police concluded that no additional measures were required.

The disruptions spanned the continent from Ireland to Bulgaria in the largest of three consecutive waves that week. No explosives were found on any aircraft.

## A Wave of Disruption

The threats were received via anonymous emails sent to publicly available addresses such as customer service inboxes, according to airport operators. In Belgium, a Ryanair flight from Charleroi to Pescara found a note bearing the word "bom" in the aircraft toilets; the alert was declared false and the flight departed for Italy. The earlier LOT Warsaw–Brussels threat prompted a risk analysis by Belgian federal police, who said no further measures were needed but were investigating a possible link to earlier incidents, [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/) reported.

The most dramatic episode involved an Aer Lingus Airbus A320 flying from Dublin to Rome. The flight departed at 06:24 and was diverted as a security precaution to the Evreux-Fauville military air base about 100 km west of Paris, escorted by French fighter jets. According to the office of French Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu, the jets were dispatched after the pilot did not cooperate with authorities as expected — an account Aer Lingus disputed, insisting its pilots "fully co-operated." The pilot and co-pilot were questioned by a French regional prosecutor. All 151 passengers and six crew disembarked safely and a bomb unit attended the scene.

In Italy, Italian air force jets escorted a Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome to a landing at Fiumicino, where bomb experts found nothing unusual. Aegean Airlines and Ryanair flights also received threats and landed in Milan, while a Wizz Air service from Milan to Warsaw was diverted to Vienna.

In Bulgaria, three Ryanair flights were targeted. Two from Copenhagen and Madrid landed as planned in Sofia, while a flight from London Stansted was diverted to Plovdiv. Bulgaria's air force escorted all three. In Spain, the Guardia Civil received three emailed bomb threats in under 20 minutes, involving Air Europa departures from Madrid to Paris and four inbound Iberia flights; several aircraft in Madrid were halted and passengers disembarked while planes were inspected.

Four Prague-bound flights operated by Ryanair, easyJet and Sky Express received threats, as did two aircraft at Copenhagen Airport and a LOT flight from Warsaw to Paris. Two additional aircraft received bomb threats in Bucharest, including a KLM flight to Tel Aviv with a stopover in the Romanian capital.

## Cautious EU Response

EU Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas said it was "too early to draw conclusions on whether the reported incidents are linked," while stressing that "any threats against civilians are unacceptable," [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/05/airbus-plane-from-dublin-to-rome-diverted-to-france-after-bomb-alert) reported. No authority has formally attributed the wave to any actor or state.

Yet the incidents unfolded amid a broader surge of suspected hybrid attacks against Europe — a pattern of cheap, easily deniable tactics ranging from sabotage and cyber attacks to drone incursions and espionage that fall below the threshold of armed response. Denmark, Poland and Romania, all affected by the Thursday disruptions, have previously accused Moscow directly, and Germany blamed Russia for an attack on Leipzig airport in August. Russia and Iran, also suspected in some plots, deny involvement.

Eric Schouten, head of the aviation security group Dyami, framed the events through that lens. "From a geopolitical view, you always have to think about the scenario of hybrid warfare, where a country in this way — today we are looking at Russia — is attacking us on multiple fronts, from railways to supermarkets to blood banks," he said.

## A Wider Campaign?

The danger signs extend beyond aviation. The same week saw unidentified hackers for a second time shut down supermarket refrigeration systems across several European countries, including Ireland, and disrupt French blood banks — disruptions that analysts say impose real economic costs and erode public confidence in essential services.

Analysts also note a targeting pattern: low-cost carriers such as Ryanair, Wizz Air and easyJet were disproportionately hit, as were flights to and from Central and Eastern Europe and Italy — regions that have featured prominently in reports of Russian-linked hybrid activity. Ryanair alone was targeted by at least seven threats that week.

The method is troubling precisely because it is so cheap. Anonymous emails sent to public inboxes can force diversions, ground aircraft and scramble fighter jets, straining response protocols and imposing outsized operational costs.

## What to Watch

For travellers, authorities have stressed that operations are proceeding normally. Graeme McQueen of the Dublin and Cork airport operator daa said the threats were "very regrettable and very disconcerting" but that it remained "business as usual," noting that all threats are fully investigated.

French officials have warned of "the massing of hybrid attacks in all their diversity" taking shape across Europe, a signal that aviation may remain a target. The key questions are whether the campaign continues to escalate, whether it diversifies further beyond transport and civilian infrastructure, and whether investigators can move from suspicion to attribution. For now, the EU's message is one of caution: the dots are visible, but the line connecting them remains unproven.
