# 5-Year Suspended Sentence Sought in Leuven Pride Plot

Belgian federal prosecutors on Tuesday asked the criminal court in Leuven to impose a five-year suspended prison sentence on a man accused of plotting a jihadist attack on the city's first-ever Pride march, an attack that authorities say was averted when the suspect unknowingly tried to buy weapons from an undercover agent. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/17/proces-leuven/), the prosecution is seeking a two-year suspended sentence for a second defendant accused of being the ideological instigator of the plan.

The man, who was 18 years old at the time of the alleged offences in the summer of 2024, addressed the court at the start of the hearing. "I was not myself, I lost control," he said. "I want to say that I am deeply ashamed." He added that he accepted full responsibility for his actions.

## A weapons search that ran into a State Security agent

The case began to take shape when the teenager, who had recently reached adulthood, came onto the radar of Belgian intelligence services. He was searching for firearms via the encrypted messaging app Telegram. Unknown to him, the person on the other end was an undercover agent from the State Security Service (Staatsveiligheid). After discussions about weapons, the two agreed to meet.

According to the federal prosecutor's office, the suspect met the supposed "arms dealer" on the evening of 22 August 2024 and laid out his plan in detail. He intended to attack Leuven Pride that October, having first considered the Brussels Pride but dismissing it as too far away and too long a wait. Hours later, the federal prosecutor's office moved in, and the man was jailed the same night.

Months later, a prison guard heard him say that "unbelievers deserve to die," a statement that prosecutors cite as evidence of his radicalisation.

## Plans broader than first known

Investigators found that the plans, while never executed, were concrete. Beyond the Prides in Leuven and Brussels, the pair is said to have targeted the Jewish community, and they used the AI chatbot ChatGPT to look up targeted information, according to VRT NWS.

The federal prosecutor's office described the primary defendant as "the linchpin" of the plot, justifying the five-year suspended request. The two defendants have known each other since the first year of secondary school and are said to have been radicalised together. Since 2022, they exchanged Islamic State videos, and one of them reportedly tried to travel to IS territory in the autumn of 2023. By the spring of 2024, the prosecutor said, their radicalisation had evolved into concrete plans.

## Defence paints a different picture

The lawyer for the main defendant offered a contrasting portrait, describing a young man "with a harsh and unsafe home situation who had lost control of himself." According to the defence, the man's most frequent answer to questions about why he engaged in the alleged conduct was "no idea." Now, the lawyer argued, he has gained insight and feels "much shame and guilt" and realises he caused fear among groups in society. "The insight is no excuse, but hopefully an answer to the how and the why."

The second defendant maintains that he was unaware of the plans, a claim the prosecutor disputes, portraying him as the "ideological, extremist instigator" who "knew, agreed and contributed ideologically and morally" to the primary defendant's plans. His lawyer described the picture of his client as "very diffuse and vague."

Several minors are also implicated in the investigation and have been referred to the juvenile court.

## What happens next

The trial was reported as a two-day hearing, with the court's ruling expected to follow. The case has drawn attention to the security of LGBTQ+ events across Europe, coming amid a broader pattern of threats targeting Pride marches, and to Belgium's use of undercover operations on encrypted channels to interdict plots before they can be executed. It also raises questions about how courts weigh remorse, youth and the fact that no violence ultimately occurred when sentencing individuals convicted of preparing terrorist offences.
