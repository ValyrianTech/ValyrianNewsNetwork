# EU targets Montenegro accession treaty by summer 2027

The European Commission expects to sign an accession treaty with Montenegro by June 2027, setting the stage for the Balkan country to become the European Union's 28th member state in 2028 — the first new entrant to the bloc in more than a decade. The target, outlined by Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos and confirmed by [SeeNews](https://seenews.com/news/montenegro-could-sign-eu-accession-treaty-in-june-2027-kos-1304344), anchors a broader reassessment of the EU's enlargement agenda, in which Ukraine, Serbia, Albania and Moldova are advancing at markedly different speeds.

Speaking to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/europese-unie-wil-in-juni-volgend-jaar-toetredingsverdrag-ondert/), Kos said Montenegro was on course to become the bloc's next member and that the accession treaty was already being prepared intensively. Negotiations could be concluded by the end of 2026, with a further year of ratification by national parliaments before membership takes effect in 2028.

## A long road, nearly complete

Montenegro's candidacy is the most advanced in the enlargement queue by some distance. The country applied for membership in 2008, was granted candidate status in 2010 and opened negotiations in 2012. Since then it has worked through the EU's 33 negotiating chapters one by one — an arduous process that has stalled for most other candidates.

Montenegro's progress is now visible in the numbers. At an intergovernmental conference on 6 November, it provisionally closed two more chapters, bringing its total to 23 of 33. A further three — covering transport, energy and regional policy — were due to be closed at a conference on 19 November, according to [European Western Balkans](https://europeanwesternbalkans.com/2026/11/16/montenegro-to-close-three-more-chapters-in-eu-accession-talks/).

Kos has framed Montenegro's small size as an asset rather than an obstacle. "Montenegro is a small country with roughly as many inhabitants as Antwerp," she said. "I am sure we can bring the population on board."

## Croatia's leverage and the veto risk

The main uncertainty hanging over Montenegro's timetable is not Brussels but Zagreb. Because enlargement decisions require unanimity among all 27 member states, Croatia has been able to slow Podgorica's path over a series of bilateral disputes stemming from the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s.

Croatia has pressed eight demands, including compensation for former detainees at the Morinj camp, the search for 14 missing Croatian citizens, war-crimes prosecution, property rights, maritime border demarcation and ownership of the naval training ship *Jadran*. As [Serbian Monitor](https://www.serbianmonitor.com/en/kos-montenegro-could-sign-eu-accession-treaty-by-summer-2027/) reported, Montenegro recently moved to defuse the row — renaming a Kotor swimming pool that had honoured a former Morinj guard and retroactively approving a memorial plaque at the camp site.

Kos has stressed that Croatia's grievances are not formally part of the accession negotiations, but that Zagreb's consent remains essential. The recent de-escalation has encouraged Brussels, though the remaining demands leave room for last-minute friction.

## Four front-runners, very different speeds

Beyond Montenegro, Kos has identified Albania, Moldova and Ukraine as the EU's four front-runners — a grouping that underscores how uneven the enlargement landscape remains.

**Albania** aims to complete all negotiating chapters by the end of 2027. Kos praised Tirana's reform drive over the past year but flagged uncertainty over the political impact of street protests against the government, including environmental and democracy-focused demonstrations.

**Moldova**, a candidate only since 2022, has moved unusually fast, opening 25 of its 33 chapters this year. But its bid carries additional risk: Brussels is watching how Russia responds, and Moldovan society itself is divided between pro-European and pro-Russian camps.

**Ukraine** presents the most complex case. Kos hopes all negotiating chapters will be opened with Kyiv by the end of 2026. "Ukraine is currently fighting to survive, but we set the bar just as high for them as for the other candidate states," she said. "And that is not easy." Asked whether membership within five years was realistic, Kos replied: "It is possible that Ukraine will be a member of the EU within five years."

That timeline has been echoed — and sharpened — by Lithuania, which takes over the rotating EU Council presidency on 1 January 2027. Foreign Minister Kęstutis Budrys said Ukraine could join by 2030 under the existing accession methodology. "If they negotiate as we did in two and a half years, they can finish in 2028," he said, according to [European Western Balkans](https://europeanwesternbalkans.com/2026/11/17/ukraine-could-join-eu-by-2030-serbia-must-clarify-its-geopolitical-alignment/). "It took us an additional two years for the accession treaty to be ratified. So 2030 is doable."

## Serbia's opening — and its geopolitical test

The most consequential variable in the enlargement picture may be Serbia, where a student-led opposition defeated long-time leader Aleksandar Vučić in parliamentary elections in late October. [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/serbia-election-vucic-concedes-defeat-to-student-led-movement/a-79753482) reported that Vučić conceded defeat to the Students' List movement, ending more than a decade of rule by his Serbian Progressive Party, on turnout of nearly 70%.

Kos said Serbia was once second only to Montenegro in the queue, "until under Vučić's government steps backward were taken in recent years." But she suggested the new political landscape could transform the country's prospects. "If they are serious and really want to carry out big reforms, then with Serbia it can suddenly go very fast."

Not everyone is so sure the question is purely about reform. Budrys insisted that Serbia must first clarify where it belongs geopolitically, pointing to its alignment with Russia and China. "When we see Chinese missiles there, when we see alignment with Russia and very low levels of alignment with us, this is not about percentages," he said. "It is about geopolitical belonging."

## A geopolitical enlargement

Kos has placed the renewed enlargement push squarely in a security framework, arguing that bringing candidate countries into the bloc is a form of self-protection in an era of renewed great-power competition. "This is something we have not seen since the big bang of 2004, when 10 new countries joined the EU," she said. "More and more European leaders realise it is important to bring all these countries in, if we want to protect ourselves in these geopolitical times."

That framing marks a shift from the "enlargement fatigue" that gripped Brussels after Croatia joined in 2013. The 2004 "big bang" — which admitted ten countries at once — remains the reference point for the scale of ambition now being invoked, even if the current approach is deliberately more incremental.

## What to watch

The immediate milestones are concrete. Montenegro is expected to close three more chapters on 19 November and hopes to conclude negotiations by the end of 2026, with the accession treaty signature targeted for June 2027. Ukraine hopes to have all chapters opened by year's end. Lithuania's Council presidency, beginning in January, is likely to push the enlargement file higher up the agenda.

Yet each of these targets depends on factors Brussels cannot fully control: Croatia's willingness to settle its bilateral claims, the duration of Russia's war in Ukraine, the stability of Albania's political environment, and whether Serbia's new government chooses a decisive westward course. The accession treaty with Montenegro is, for now, the closest thing to a certainty — but even that remains a Commission expectation rather than a guarantee, contingent on reforms, unanimity and the resolution of disputes that have unsettled the region for three decades.
