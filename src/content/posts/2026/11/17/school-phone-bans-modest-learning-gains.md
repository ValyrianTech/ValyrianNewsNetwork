# Do School Phone Bans Help Learning? Gains So Far Are 'Modest'

The largest national study of school cellphone bans to date finds that strict, bell-to-bell restrictions produce small but steady academic gains — roughly an extra week or two of learning per school year — and that the benefits appear to compound over time. The study, led by Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research and released Tuesday, analyzed test scores of 3.3 million students at 4,600 schools, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/school-cellphone-bans-test-scores-79e11f36a1321815d43517b5243fbc34).

But the findings land amid contested evidence: a separate federal study published in May concluded strict bans had "close to zero" effect on test scores. Together, the research paints a picture of a popular policy delivering real but limited results — and raising hard questions about enforcement and the role of phones outside school.

## A modest but measurable boost

The Harvard study compared schools that lock students' phones in pouches for the full school day with similar schools that do not, using math and reading data for grades four through 10 over the past five academic years. It estimates the academic gains were equivalent to about an additional week or two of learning over a school year — roughly two additional minutes per hour of class time.

That boost equals about half the annual decline in test scores recorded since social-media-enabled smartphones became mainstream around 2013, the study said. Student achievement has been [falling globally since 2013](https://apnews.com/article/pisa-test-scores-china-singapore-usa-2c1c7b338e3d5b38031ef0eecef9ce5d), a trend researchers have sought to link to the rise of the smartphone.

"Our answer is yes, phone bans do have a positive effect on growth. But it's not a panacea," said Harvard professor Thomas Kane, one of the study's authors. Learning loss was gradual over a decade, he noted, and it is not surprising that gains would also be gradual.

"While schools should not expect large boosts in achievement immediately, storing students' phones seems to be a cost-effective way to add 1-2 weeks of instruction each year, which could add up over time, just as the initial declines did," the study says.

## A policy patchwork

More than 40 U.S. states have passed laws restricting cellphones in schools, according to AP News. But those laws vary widely and often let individual schools design their own policies, resulting in an inconsistent patchwork of rules and enforcement across the country.

That variability is central to the debate. Just because a school or state has a ban does not mean it is being enforced, said Jean Twenge, a psychologist at San Diego State University and author of "10 Rules for Raising Kids in a High-Tech World."

"It happens pretty often that there's a policy of no phones during the school day, bell-to-bell, and you go into the school and phones are everywhere," Twenge said. Teens devise creative workarounds — stowing burner phones or even phone-shaped rocks in their locked pouches — and adherence is spotty at schools that rely on students to keep phones in backpacks.

Even without phones, Twenge asked, "Do they have laptops at school? Are they just doing social media on laptops instead?"

## Competing evidence and unresolved questions

A [different study](https://apnews.com/article/schools-cell-phone-bans-study-findings-aa47a0f655d922be9069dc9e1191683e) published in May by the National Bureau of Economic Research concluded that strict school phone bans had "close to zero" effect on test scores. It compared ban schools with tens of thousands of others, using a different set of standardized tests, plus GPS phone activity, teacher surveys and data from Yondr, the leading provider of lockable phone pouches.

That study did find "modest positive effects" in high school test scores over three years, particularly in math, but its authors were hesitant to conclude broad learning benefits.

"The early results from school phone bans are largely disappointing and raise unresolved questions," said Stanford education economist Thomas Dee, an author of the NBER study. Noting that the Harvard study showed gains may accumulate, Dee added: "I hope they're right. However, I'm not comfortable making that extrapolation from estimates that are small, sensitive and based on just a few years of phone bans."

The reasons for the disappointing results, Dee said, could be that students are still distracted by other devices at school — or "it may be that we see these disappointing results because out-of-school phone use is harming child development."

## The big question: phones outside school

Kane agrees the effect of cellphone use outside school is "the big question." But researchers have been unable to investigate parents' and educators' suspicions that late-night binges on YouTube and other platforms are costly for students.

"I find it astonishing that social media companies know how much time kids spend on their platforms, but that data has never been linked to student outcomes," Kane said. "We can't provide answers until the social media companies share data."

Twenge echoed the concern. "If they have that phone available when they're studying, how much are they really taking in?" she said.

## What to watch

For now, the clearest takeaway is that phone bans are not a quick fix. The Harvard researchers argue the small annual gains could compound over time the same way the initial declines did, while skeptics caution that the estimates are based on just a few years of data and remain sensitive to how bans are designed and enforced.

What happens next depends on several unknowns: whether enforcement tightens in the patchwork of state and school policies; whether schools address other distraction sources such as laptops; and whether social media companies share the usage data researchers say is essential to understanding the full picture. Until then, the debate over just how much phones cost students — and how much banning them can recover — is likely to continue.
