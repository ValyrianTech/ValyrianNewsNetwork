# Quinault Tribe Races King Tides to Move Homes Uphill

On Washington State's Olympic Peninsula, the Quinault Indian Nation is racing the calendar — and the sea — to move its most flood-prone families to higher ground. In October, after receiving federal approval to install four modular homes, the tribe celebrated the first of 59 planned residences in a new upland neighborhood roughly a mile from its oceanfront village of Taholah, according to [KUOW](https://www.kuow.org/environment/2026-11-13/quinault-tribe-races-against-king-tides-to-relocate-coastal-homes). Now the arrival of the year's highest tides is threatening to outpace the construction.

Taholah, home to about 800 people, sits where the Quinault River empties into the Pacific, shielded by a rock seawall that floods each winter when high tides and waves overwhelm it. King tides are expected to strike the Washington coast around Thanksgiving, then again in late December and late January — and one of the strongest El Niños on record could push them to record heights.

## A Race Against the Tide

The highest tide of 2026 is predicted to reach Taholah just before noon on Christmas Eve. That timing weighs heavily on the tribe's relocation manager, Alyssa Johnston, whose move-in estimate has already slipped from Christmas to January.

"That stresses me out," Johnston told KUOW. "That's what makes me not sleep at night."

Another 32 homes are slated for construction in 2027. Residents of four homes along First Avenue, Taholah's most flood-prone street, are being given new houses at no cost; their current dwellings, moldy and propped on cinder blocks, are to be condemned.

For James de la Cruz, a Quinault elder and Army veteran who has lived on First Avenue in the shadow of the seawall for 35 years, the move is bittersweet. "I wholeheartedly don't want to move, but I understand the need for it," he said at the October groundbreaking. During high tides, he added, "there's three feet of water in my yard, so I had to have a boat tied to my back porch if I wanted to escape."

Lia Frenchman, another First Avenue resident, described the physical force of the winter water. "When we have the full-sized tides that come with the winter king tides, our street, our house, is just shaking for hours," she said. "There's just so much power behind that water."

## A Familiar Climate Threat

The shifting baseline is not lost on residents. "The fact is, it did not used to flood every year like it does right now, and that's definitely climate change," Frenchman said.

Heat-trapping pollution has raised the world's oceans about seven inches over the past century, according to the Washington Department of Ecology, and El Niño conditions this winter could temporarily boost the Pacific another six to 10 inches. Widespread erosion is expected along the outer coasts of Oregon and Washington, which could damage Taholah's seawall.

The Quinault effort is among the largest and most advanced examples in the country of what experts call "managed retreat." A [U.S. Climate Resilience Toolkit case study](https://toolkit.climate.gov/case-study/quinault-indian-nation-plans-village-relocation) documents how the tribe has planned for years to move Taholah out of reach of tsunamis and rising seas. The new neighborhood sits about 130 feet above sea level, with the tribe already having built a health clinic (2007) and a senior and daycare center (2021) on higher ground; a new K-12 school is under construction.

Quinault Indian Nation Chief Operating Officer M'liss DeWald said the tribe's leaders recognized the threat long before government agencies did. "Our leaders had the foresight and understanding to recognize the impacts that climate change would have on our villages long before the federal or state government recognized climate change as an issue affecting residential communities," she said.

## The Cost of Moving a Village

The project's scale carries a steep price. The tribe has spent more than $40 million in federal, state, and tribal money to start the new neighborhood; completing it will require hundreds of millions more. As [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2024/02/19/1228727075/how-a-northwest-tribe-is-escaping-a-rising-ocean) reported, the full effort is expected to cost at least $450 million. In 2020, the Bureau of Indian Affairs estimated that U.S. tribes would need $5 billion over 50 years to relocate or protect infrastructure from climate change.

Affordability compounds the challenge. Per-capita income on the Quinault Reservation was half the national average in 2024, according to the U.S. Census Bureau, and one in four residents lives in poverty. The tribal government provided the first four homes at no cost and is still deciding how much to subsidize the 2027 homes.

Housing scarcity weighs on families as well. Councilmember Kaylah Mail, whose home has been in her family for four generations, said the decision to move is painful but necessary. "That's going to be a little bit difficult to let go. But I have my 12-year-old to think about, too," she said. "That's my worst fear, is to be in a situation where I can't keep him safe."

## A Difficult History, a Difficult Choice

For the Quinault, leaving the water is an ironic turn. Living year-round on the ocean was originally forced upon the tribe by the U.S. government, which confined it to a single permanent village. As Frenchman noted, just a couple of generations ago her great-great-grandparents still moved seasonally up and down the Quinault River.

That history makes the relocation a test of culture as much as safety. "That sense of place is so ingrained in all of us now, being in the lower village, the proximity to the ocean," Frenchman said. "It's just a huge bittersweet feeling."

Yet the case for moving is gaining ground. "This relocation is one of the most important things our nation will ever do," said Zavier Davis, a college student and Quinault Youth Councilmember. "Relocation isn't something for someday. It's how we protect our people now."

## What to Watch

The coming months will test whether the homes up the hill arrive before the water reaches the First Avenue doorsteps. Residents are bracing for another winter of flooding even as they look ahead to the move — and the tribe is still searching for the hundreds of millions of dollars needed to finish a neighborhood that could become a national model for communities facing a warmer, wetter world.
