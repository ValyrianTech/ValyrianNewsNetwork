# Myria Flags Abuse Risks at Belgium's 127 bis Centre

Belgium's Federal Migration Centre Myria has raised the alarm over conditions at the closed detention centre 127 bis in Steenokkerzeel, warning of near-automatic solitary confinement, improper strip searches and serious gaps in mental-health care. The findings, published on 17 November 2026, stem from the first preventive visit ever carried out at a Belgian closed centre under the country's national mechanism against ill-treatment, and come with around 40 recommendations to the authorities. The Immigration Office says each will be examined.

## A first-of-its-kind inspection

The visit took place from 20 to 23 October 2025 and was conducted under the National Mechanism for the Prevention of Ill-Treatment in Detention (MNP), a body created by a 2024 Belgian law in line with the UN's Optional Protocol to the Convention against Torture. As [Myria](https://www.myria.be/fr/publications/rapport-visite-npm-centre-rapatriement-127bis) explains, 127 bis is one of six closed centres managed by the Immigration Office where foreign nationals are held administratively—not as a criminal penalty—pending their removal from Belgium. It sits beside Brussels-National Airport.

According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/isolement-fouilles-a-nu-acces-aux-soins-myria-alerte-sur-les-pratiques-au-centre-ferme-127-bis-11816686), the inspection unfolded in a tense climate, several days after a suicide at the facility and amid protest movements. "These are relatively opaque places. There is little transparency from the authorities about what goes on inside," said Antoinette Dutilleux, Myria's detention specialist and lead author of the report.

## Isolation applied "in a standardised way"

Myria's sharpest concerns focus on the procedures applied before special repatriation flights. During the visit, about ten people were awaiting departure on such a flight. Several had been placed in isolation cells for 24 to 48 hours, with major restrictions: no visits, sometimes no access to mobile phones, and strip searches.

"We realised that these procedures are applied in a standardised way," Dutilleux said. The detainees "did not appear to have an attitude that could justify these measures"—they "intended to leave" and were not considered dangerous or violent. Myria argues this raises questions of necessity and proportionality, since restrictive measures were applied automatically rather than according to each individual's situation.

The report also flags the physical conditions of isolation. In the relevant rooms, lights stay on day and night—a practice Myria wants stopped immediately. Strip searches take place in a room whose windows are not fully blacked out, and during the visit the glass door was left open.

## Gaps in mental-health care and suicide prevention

The centre's infrastructure still bears a prison-like character, with barred windows, barbed wire and isolation cells—features Myria says are poorly suited to administrative detention. International standards, the report notes, call for facilities closer to ordinary living spaces.

But the most acute concerns are medical. Detainees present significant mental-health needs, yet access to psychological care is very limited and psychiatric consultations are rare. Two people took their own lives at the centre in the two years preceding the visit. "The authorities are required to assume their duty of care towards everyone," Dutilleux said, but "the centre does not have the means to respond adequately to these needs."

At the time of the visit, the centre had no specific suicide-prevention procedure, and screening and follow-up of at-risk individuals were insufficient. Myria also points to structural understaffing and high absenteeism, particularly among security agents, which reduces the activities available to detainees.

## Long detentions and weak oversight

Oversight of detention itself is another source of concern. Detention is not automatically reviewed by a judge, and the calculation of the maximum detention period can be restarted in certain situations, extending confinement. During the visit, three people had been held for more than six months—one for thirteen consecutive months.

Myria did note several positive practices, including the professionalism of staff, daily follow-up by a care worker and multidisciplinary meetings, while stressing that personnel work in difficult conditions with insufficient numbers.

## What happens next

The report was sent to the Immigration Office and to the office of the Minister of Asylum and Migration. In its response to RTBF, the Immigration Office said each recommendation "will be analysed and taken into consideration where possible and relevant." Both Myria and the administration stressed the constructive nature of exchanges during this first visit.

The roughly 40 recommendations range from improving material detention conditions and guaranteeing adequate mental-health care to better regulating and limiting isolation, creating an independent complaints mechanism, revising the rules on maximum detention duration, and enshrining centre operating rules in law. Myria also wants medical staff moved from the Immigration Office to the SPF Public Health to strengthen their independence.

Similar visits are already planned at Belgium's five other closed centres. "A whole process of dialogue with the authorities will begin to achieve concrete changes in practice," Dutilleux said. The test now will be whether that dialogue produces binding reforms—and whether Belgium's administrative detention regime can be brought into line with the human-rights standards it has committed to uphold.
