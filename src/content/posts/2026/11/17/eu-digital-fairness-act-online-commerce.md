# EU Moves to End Abuses in Online Commerce

The European Commission on 11 November adopted its Digital Fairness Act, a sweeping proposal to stamp out the manipulative design tricks, trap subscriptions and misleading influencer marketing that have come to define online shopping. Presented as part of a broader "Consumer Package" alongside a Simplification Directive and a revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation, the plan has been billed by Brussels as the most significant upgrade to EU consumer law in more than two decades — a move to protect shoppers from the darker side of digital commerce, as [L'Echo](https://www.lecho.be/economie-politique/europe/economie/achats-forces-abonnements-pieges-influenceurs-l-europe-veut-mettre-fin-aux-derives-du-commerce-en-ligne/10699394.html) reported when the proposal was tabled.

## Why now

The reform follows the Commission's 2024 Digital Fairness Fitness Check, which concluded that the bloc's three core consumer directives — the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, the Consumer Rights Directive and the Unfair Contract Terms Directive — remain relevant but no longer fully fit a digital environment that has changed dramatically since they were written. The evidence was hard to ignore: according to the 2025 Consumer Conditions Scoreboard, around 60% of EU consumers shop online, yet online shoppers are more than 60% more likely to run into problems than those buying offline.

The financial stakes are large. The Commission's own impact assessment estimates consumers would be spared €88.4 billion in losses between 2025 and 2035, with annual benefits including time saved put at €13.7 billion — set against recurring business costs of about €678 million a year once overlaps between measures are accounted for. The Commission describes the measures as saving EU consumers "up to €13.7 billion a year." The European Consumer Organisation, BEUC, has long argued that EU law failed to adequately address dark patterns, addictive design and unfair personalisation, which it says cost consumers an estimated €7.9 billion a year.

The Digital Fairness Act supplements, rather than replaces, the existing EU digital rulebook — the Digital Services Act, the Digital Markets Act and the AI Act — but crucially it applies to all traders engaging in business-to-consumer practices, not only the largest online platforms. It also sits alongside the proposed EU KIDS Act on minors' online safety.

## What the law would ban

At the heart of the proposal is a blacklist of manipulative practices outlawed in all circumstances, so regulators no longer have to prove harm case by case. Drawing on reporting of a leaked draft, these include making cancellation disproportionately harder than sign-up; burying prices or auto-renewal terms; falsely suggesting no alternative exists; unqualified "only one left!" scarcity claims and repeated scarcity warnings through checkout; pre-ticked boxes that add extra products; framing a refusal as the wrong choice; nagging consumers to reverse a decision; confusing double negatives; and fake notifications implying someone has contacted the user. The Commission would be empowered to add new tricks to the list without returning to lawmakers.

The package also takes aim at pricing opacity. It would tackle drip pricing — hidden fees added at checkout — along with dynamic pricing and misleading price comparisons. In virtual queues, traders must show the current price in real time and the maximum possible price, and event-ticket resellers must disclose the original ticket price, type, row and seat.

Separately, the act introduces "fairness by design" rules. Consumers would be able to switch off addictive features such as autoplay, unrelated notifications, streak-style rewards and relationship-simulating chatbots. Virtual in-app currencies must display their real-money value, and for minors they would be off by default. Gambling-like mechanics — loot boxes, pay-to-win and pay-to-progress features — would be banned for minors altogether.

## Influencers and personalisation

Influencer marketing comes under explicit scrutiny. Creators would have to clearly disclose paid promotions, would be barred from presenting sponsored content as personal advice, and could not promote illegal or dangerous products. Brands and agencies would share responsibility for ensuring compliance — a provision aimed squarely at protecting minors who tend to trust the influencers they follow.

On personalisation, the Digital Services Act's bans on targeting minors with personalised ads and on using sensitive data such as health or ethnicity information would be extended to all websites and apps, not just major platforms. Higher personalised prices aimed at minors or vulnerable consumers would be prohibited.

## Simpler subscriptions — and rules for AI agents

The proposal also rewrites the rules on subscriptions and contracts. It would require a simple, unambiguous cancellation option, reminders before auto-renewal, active confirmation before a free trial converts to a paid plan, and — a first for EU consumer law — rules governing AI shopping agents. Users would choose which purchases must be approved, and agents would have to disclose sponsored suggestions.

Enforcement would be strengthened too. Under the revised Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation, the Commission gains direct powers to investigate large-scale breaches and issue fines of up to 6% of a company's worldwide annual turnover. The trigger: a breach likely to affect consumers in at least two-thirds of member states representing two-thirds of the EU population, involving a non-SME company. Once Brussels opens proceedings, national authorities must halt parallel investigations. The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive would become a directly applicable regulation across all 27 member states, reducing fragmentation, and non-EU companies would have to appoint an EU legal representative.

## Political headwinds

The path ahead is not guaranteed. Germany has signaled resistance: in a non-paper dated 14 October and reported by Agence Europe, Berlin argued that existing consumer directives should be amended rather than replaced by a new horizontal law, and said the case for converting the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive into a regulation "has not yet been sufficiently demonstrated." The proposals must now pass through the European Parliament and Council under the ordinary legislative procedure, with no fixed date for adoption. Once in force, the regulation would apply 12 months later.

## What to watch

Commissioner Michael McGrath, who steers the file, framed the reform as "both a pro-consumer and pro-business initiative," stressing that shopping "must never come at the expense of fairness" and that strong consumer protection is "the foundation of competitiveness." Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen said the reform would make everyday life "simpler, fairer and safer for consumers, while giving businesses the clarity and confidence they need to thrive."

The key test will be whether that balancing act survives negotiations. Consumer groups are broadly supportive; business will press for light-touch rules; and Germany's objections could slow or dilute the horizontal approach. For now, the direction is clear: Brussels wants the online marketplace to work as fairly as the physical one — and is prepared to legislate to make it so. The details of the final text, and the speed of its passage, will determine how much of that promise becomes reality.
