# In San Diego, Free Groceries Fill a SNAP-Sized Gap

It should have been her day off. Instead, early on a Saturday morning, Rosa Rodarte was back at the social service office where she works, pushing carts of food and loading them into people's cars.

Rodarte's official job is to help residents apply for programs like food assistance. Lately, she has been simply helping them get food. "We have the same goal," she says. "We want to make sure everybody is being helped and not judged."

The scene in San Diego's Kearny Mesa neighborhood captures a quiet but far-reaching consequence of the 2025 "One Big Beautiful Bill." Since the sweeping tax-and-spending law took effect, roughly five million people nationwide have lost federal food benefits — the fastest decline in decades and one that has far outpaced the federal government's [own projections](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2026-01/51312-2026-02-snap.pdf). In response, one of California's largest counties is doing something unusual: handing out free groceries at the very offices meant to administer aid.

## A steep and rapid decline

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, known as CalFresh in California, has seen participation plummet since H.R. 1 was signed into law. The legislation carved $187 billion from SNAP, tightened eligibility, imposed new work requirements, and barred food aid for most asylum seekers and refugees, [KPBS reported](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/06/25/san-diego-county-supervisors-backfill-federal-food-assistance-cuts).

The new rules require recipients aged 18 to 64 to work 20 hours per week unless they qualify for an exemption, such as a disability or caring for a young child. The requirements were extended to groups previously exempt: homeless people, former foster youth, veterans, parents with a child older than 14, and able-bodied adults aged 55 to 64. In California, the work requirements took effect June 1.

The results have been dramatic. Across the country, the decline in enrollment has exceeded even the Congressional Budget Office's forecasts, according to the [Center on Budget and Policy Priorities](https://www.cbpp.org/research/food-assistance/snap-tracker-people-are-losing-food-assistance-as-the-republican-megabill), which tracks the fallout state by state. In San Diego County alone, there were 36,000 fewer people on SNAP this September than in July 2025. Supervisor Monica Montgomery Steppe warned that the new criteria could leave more than 93,000 county CalFresh recipients without access.

"It's a deliberate policy choice, and a harmful one," she said. "Taking food away from struggling families doesn't build stronger communities — it creates instability, hardship and a greater risk of homelessness."

Nearly 370,000 county residents — including more than 113,000 children — rely on CalFresh, according to county data.

## From frustration to food lines

The idea to hold food distributions outside the county's social service offices came from a restless night. Montgomery Steppe, vice chair of the County Board of Supervisors, was shaken by how many of her neighbors were projected to lose benefits. Having relied on the assistance herself when she was younger, the issue felt personal.

"So one night, ideas just started coming to me, so I took my phone and just started writing them in a Google Doc," she says. "And I gave them to my staff and said, 'We have to do something.'"

That impulse became policy. In June, county supervisors unanimously approved a $9.16 billion budget that created 122 new Health and Human Services positions and earmarked $15.8 million to backfill CalFresh administrative costs, drawing on one-time reserve funds. Starting Oct. 1, H.R. 1 shifted 25% of those administrative expenses from the federal government to local governments.

"We had a choice to accept those cuts and let our neighbors go hungry, or fight back," Supervisor Paloma Aguirre said.

Not everyone agreed. Supervisor Jim Desmond opposed tapping reserve funds and dismissed concerns about the work requirements as "politically-generated mania and hysteria." Supporters of the policy, including U.S. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins, have argued that work requirements restore "the dignity of work" and move people off what they describe as a "lifelong trap of dependency."

## The burden behind the numbers

For the workers administering aid, the changes have meant more paperwork and heavier emotional weight. San Diego processed over 830,000 more mailed documents between January and October than in the same period the prior year — and mailed documents account for only about a third of all incoming paperwork.

"Many of them already work," Nina Olivas, who oversees San Diego's Access Customer Service Call Center, says of SNAP recipients. "So really what it is, is just that extra burden of having to really quantify how many hours they're working."

The toll is also human. "The emotionally draining part is where you see someone that's telling you on the phone that they need your help and they need it today, and when you can't do something," Olivas says. Still, she reminds her staff of their purpose: "We are not ending the call with nothing."

The food distributions reflect that commitment. The county partnered with Feeding San Diego and the San Diego Food Bank to hand out groceries at social service offices. Between August and September, nearly 70,000 pounds of food were distributed to more than 5,200 people, according to the county. Earlier this month, the line began forming about 90 minutes before the event opened — young families, seniors, and some wearing hats that read "veteran."

Allison Glader of Feeding San Diego said the organization is now serving 40,000 additional households, with the work requirements hitting seniors and veterans hardest. Amy Eilts, director of programs at the San Diego Food Bank, pushed back on the notion that recipients are simply dependent. "It's working families, it's seniors on fixed incomes," she said. "It really is a supplement to help make sure that families can purchase healthy food."

For Ambar Santiago, the help arrived at a critical moment. Her benefits hadn't come through that month because of a paperwork snag. After loading free vegetables, canned goods and diapers into her car, she sat with a worker who listened carefully and then reassured her: "We can do that."

Back home, Santiago prepared her mother's carne guisada — a Puerto Rican beef stew that stretches for days — with the ingredients she had just received. She had experienced homelessness not long ago, and she knew such help was not the norm.

"I feel like the assistance that I would've gotten in the hood or in the Southeast or anywhere else is completely different from my ZIP code that I give them now," she says. "It's just a blessing."

## A patch, not a fix

San Diego's effort is part of a broader scramble by state and local governments to soften the blow. Illinois sent a one-time $400 benefit to people dropped from SNAP; Phoenix offered up to $600 toward utility bills for those who lost federal food aid; and Maine created a state-funded food assistance program for certain noncitizens.

Elsewhere, the fallout has been severe. Texas' SNAP application backlog swelled from 3,700 pending cases in January to 175,000 in September, and more than 700,000 Texans have lost food aid since July 2025, according to CBPP. Arizona's rolls shrank by nearly half — the steepest decline in the nation — which state officials attributed to staffing shortages and error-reduction scrutiny, [KJZZ reported](https://www.kjzz.org/politics/2026-04-09/study-arizona-snap-recipients-dropped-47-since-big-beautiful-bill-more-than-any-other-state). In Louisiana, enrollment fell by 154,000, with three-quarters of removals stemming from procedural issues such as missing forms or missed interviews, according to an analysis by Invest in Louisiana.

Those procedural losses point to a central tension in the policy debate: whether the declines reflect genuine ineligibility or administrative friction that strips benefits from people who still qualify.

County officials are candid that groceries alone cannot replace a federal program. The distributions are "not a long-term fix," as one put it — a stopgap funded by reserves that will eventually run dry. And more pressure is coming: H.R. 1 also created new work requirements for Medi-Cal, California's Medicaid program, set to take effect in January, which the county says could require additional staffing.

For now, the San Diego model offers a revealing snapshot of how the country is absorbing a historic retreat in food aid — one cart of groceries, and one difficult phone call, at a time. Whether other localities follow suit, and whether reserve-funded stopgaps can outlast the need, are questions that will define the months ahead.

What is clear is the scale of the shift. A program long billed as a nutrition safety net is now shrinking faster than almost anyone predicted, and the frontline workers tasked with explaining the change are, increasingly, the same people handing out the food.
