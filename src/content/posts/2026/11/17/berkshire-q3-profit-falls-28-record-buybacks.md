# Berkshire Q3 Profit Falls 28% as Buybacks Hit Record

Berkshire Hathaway's third-quarter net profit fell roughly 28% year-on-year to $22.11 billion, driven by a sharp drop in investment gains, even as the conglomerate ramped up share buybacks to a quarterly record and let its cash pile grow further. The results, released on the evening of November 7 in Beijing time, offer a window into how Warren Buffett's company is positioning itself amid volatile markets and its first full year under a new chief executive.

## A Profit Drop Driven by Investments, Not Operations

Revenue rose 11.24% to $105.647 billion, according to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34429766), the Chinese outlet that covered the earnings. But net profit attributable to shareholders slipped from $30.796 billion a year earlier, and the culprit was investment performance rather than the underlying businesses.

Berkshire's investment gains fell about 45.7% to $9.407 billion, down from $17.311 billion. That swing alone explains most of the headline decline. Berkshire has long cautioned that such portfolio swings can distort its reported results. As [TipRanks](https://www.tipranks.com/news/berkshire-hathaway-brk-b-q3-earnings-revenue-beats-but-profits-fall-28) reported, the company's revenue of $105.65 billion actually beat Wall Street's forecast of $100.96 billion — the weakness was in earnings, not sales.

The company reiterated its familiar warning in the report: any single quarter's investment gains or losses are usually meaningless, and the resulting net profit figures may greatly mislead investors with limited understanding of accounting rules.

## Operating Profit Tells a Steadier Story

Operating profit — the metric Buffett has historically called a more reliable gauge of true performance because it excludes short-term portfolio swings — came in at $12.705 billion, down 5.8% from $13.485 billion a year earlier.

Results across Berkshire's many businesses were mixed. The BNSF railroad posted after-tax profit of $1.729 billion, up about 19%. Manufacturing, service, and retail operating profit rose roughly 12% to $4.042 billion, while Berkshire Hathaway Energy earned $1.612 billion, up about 8%.

The soft spot was insurance. Underwriting profit fell sharply — down 45% at GEICO alone, per TipRanks — as higher claims costs and expenses weighed on results, and insurance investment income also declined. That weakness in a key source of cash is the main point of concern for investors poring over the report.

## Buybacks, Cash, and a Cautious Stance

Berkshire repurchased roughly $9.6 billion of its own stock during the quarter, a single-quarter record, up from just $2.35 billion in the first quarter and about $4.5 billion in the second. The acceleration sends a clear signal: with few high-quality acquisition targets meeting the company's standards, Berkshire itself may be the most worthwhile asset to invest in right now.

At the same time, the company reverted to being a net seller of stocks, offloading about $426 million in holdings during the quarter — a reversal from the $11.625 billion it bought on a net basis in the first half of the year. Cash and equivalents climbed to $377.4 billion as of September 30, up from $365.51 billion in the prior quarter.

## The Google Bet and Its Drag

Berkshire's equity portfolio remains highly concentrated. As of September 30, five holdings — Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America, and Coca-Cola — accounted for 65% of the total fair value of its equity investments.

The Alphabet position, built up since the third quarter of 2025, has been a notable drag. Berkshire invested $10 billion in Alphabet's $80 billion equity raise in June 2026, split between Class A shares at $351.81 and Class C shares at $348.20. Both share classes have fallen nearly 25% since then, likely a key factor behind the quarter's weaker investment gains.

## Why Berkshire Outperformed

Despite the profit decline, Berkshire shares rose 5% over the week, outperforming the S&P 500, which fell 2.68% amid turbulence in AI-related trades. Analysts read the resilience as a sign that defensive capital is finding a haven in Berkshire — and that market anxiety over Buffett's departure from the CEO role is fading. Greg Abel took over as chief executive on January 1, 2026, with the 95-year-old Buffett remaining chairman.

## What to Watch

The next quarter will test whether Berkshire's non-insurance businesses can keep offsetting insurance weakness, particularly if lower interest rates continue to pressure investment income. For now, the message from the numbers is one of patience and caution: a company sitting on a record cash pile, buying back its own shares at an unprecedented pace, and content to wait for the right opportunities to deploy capital.
