# The Scholar Racing to Save China's Fading Manchu Language

At 71, Zhao Aping still spends her days in a research room at Heilongjiang University, reviewing frame after frame of audio and video recordings: elderly villagers in a remote northeastern village reciting family histories, folk tales and Manchu vocabulary in a language few people on Earth can still speak.

Zhao has devoted 40 years to documenting and studying Manchu, once the "national language" of China's Qing dynasty (1644–1912) and today classified as critically endangered. In an in-depth interview with [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34490375) published on November 17, the professor described a race against time to preserve a language whose remaining fluent speakers are dwindling with each passing year.

"Only one or two dozen elderly people" in a handful of villages in Heilongjiang province can still hold a conversation in Manchu, Zhao said, and there is essentially no environment in which the language is used at home or in daily social life.

## A 'Living Fossil' Village

The focal point of Zhao's fieldwork is Sanjiazi Village in Fuyu County, Heilongjiang, widely described as a "living fossil" of the Manchu language. Zhao first traveled there in 1986 as a young researcher, wading through muddy roads to sit with villagers and record their oral histories.

"I have followed the surveys to this day, and many of the elderly people who could speak Manchu have already passed away," she said. "Users over 50 keep dwindling."

That sense of loss has driven her career. Zhao graduated from Harbin Normal University's history department in 1982 and was steered toward Manchu by a professor who told her that serious Qing-dynasty scholarship required the language. She began studying Manchu in 1986, joined the Heilongjiang Provincial Manchu Language Research Institute in 1988, and later served as its director and as editor-in-chief of the journal *Manchu Studies*.

In 1999 she drove the institute's full-scale relocation into Heilongjiang University, where it became — according to the article — the only institution in China, and in the world, offering a complete undergraduate-through-doctoral training system in Manchu. Over four decades the program has produced more than 400 undergraduates and 100-plus master's and doctoral students.

## Archives Vast as the Sea

The stakes extend far beyond linguistics. Manchu archives, Zhao said, are staggeringly large: roughly 5 million volumes and documents worldwide, about 3 million of them in China. Yet only "the tip of the iceberg" has been catalogued or translated.

"Manchu archival materials are vast as the sea; what we've organized so far is just a drop in the bucket," she said.

Many of those documents are not just historical curiosities. The original Treaty of Nerchinsk with Russia, which settled the two empires' border in the late 17th century, existed only in Manchu and Russian. Zhao argued that such records remain directly relevant to contemporary diplomacy and territorial questions. Other Manchu materials document historical earthquakes and floods in Heilongjiang, offering data that modern researchers can use to study environmental change.

Machine translation, she cautioned, can handle only about 60 to 70 percent of standardized archives. Handwritten and cursive documents, along with culturally specific terms, demand trained specialists. "Translation work can't be done with two or three years of study; it takes a decade as a baseline," she said.

## Why Manchu Faded

Zhao places the language's decline in a broader historical frame. For more than two centuries the Qing court promoted Manchu as the "national language" (国语清文). Yet the Manchu elite actively absorbed Han Chinese language and culture and integrated into the wider Chinese nation — a dynamic Zhao describes without lament, as part of the formation of a multi-ethnic Chinese state.

Critically endangered languages may have "a day when they truly go extinct," she acknowledged. "Our generation happens to be around while Manchu hasn't yet disappeared; if we don't research, preserve and save it, that is a dereliction of duty."

The urgency is echoed in a [People's Daily](http://paper.people.com.cn/rmrb/pc/content/202604/28/content_30153610.html) feature from April 2026, which put the number of fluent conversationalists at fewer than 20 worldwide, concentrated in a few Heilongjiang villages. The slight discrepancy between "fewer than 20" and "one or two dozen" reflects the inherent uncertainty of counting speakers for a language on the brink.

## A Talent Bottleneck

The interview arrives amid a national push to support "rare and endangered disciplines" (冷门绝学) — little-studied fields such as ancient scripts, oracle-bone studies and bamboo-slip research that underpin China's cultural and historical continuity. The University of the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences recently established a dedicated School of Rare and Endangered Disciplines, and a related alliance was launched to coordinate institutions.

As [New Beijing News](https://m.bjnews.com.cn/detail/1759209325168005.html) noted, the policy framing casts these fields as the bedrock of "an independent knowledge system" and a long-term civilizational investment, backed by the National Social Science Fund.

But Zhao's most pointed warning is about talent, not money. Of the hundreds of students her program has trained, fewer than 100 actually work in the field. The Manchu department of the First Historical Archives of China has about 20 staff, roughly a third of them her graduates — but specialized job openings are so scarce that it may hire only one or two people every few years.

"Funding is not the big problem," she said. "My biggest worry is that money won't produce results." She also worries that fewer young researchers are willing to endure the hardship of fieldwork, which once meant living with villagers, drinking with them and recording through unreliable power supplies.

Zhao leads a major National Social Science Fund project, launched in 2019, to build a multimodal corpus database covering five Manchu-Tungusic languages: Manchu, Hezhen, Oroqen, Evenki and Xibe. The goal, she said, is to leave future generations an objective record of how these languages actually sounded — "even if the language ultimately disappears."

## What's Next

Zhao is candid about the limits of her work. "We can only do our best to delay the time of Manchu's extinction," she said. "If we do well what should be done now and leave future generations a precious ethnic-cultural heritage and research foundation, then we will have no regrets."

Whether the new national push translates into durable careers — and deeper scholarship rather than credentialed fast work — remains an open question. For now, the burden rests on a small cohort of aging scholars and a younger generation Zhao hopes will pick up the thread, one whispered recording at a time.
