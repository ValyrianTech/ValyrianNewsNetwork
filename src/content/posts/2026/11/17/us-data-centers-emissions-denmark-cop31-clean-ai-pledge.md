# US Data Centers Emit as Much CO2 as Denmark, Study Finds

The 403 largest data centers in the United States emitted roughly 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide between May 2024 and April 2025—about as much as the entire nation of Denmark—according to a peer-reviewed [Harvard University study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-026-01985-7) published in *Nature Sustainability* on 16 November 2026. The findings landed the same day that the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, unveiled a voluntary [clean-AI pledge](https://apnews.com/article/cop31-climate-data-centers-ai-pollution-a1b338e7f781976f89260d172a7c65e2) that has so far drawn only nine national backers, AP News reported.

## A booming industry's hidden footprint

The study, led by Gianluca Guidi with Harvard biostatistics professor Francesca Dominici as senior author, analyzed electricity use and emissions at the hyperscale facilities—the massive campuses that power cloud computing and artificial intelligence workloads. The centers consumed about 82 terawatt-hours of electricity, enough to power nearly 7 million homes for a year. More than half of that power, roughly 54%, came from fossil fuels.

"It is the equivalent to the CO2 emission of an entire state like Michigan or a small European country," Dominici said. The estimate is two to three times higher than earlier assessments, and the researchers describe their figure as a lower bound—the study period captured only the early phase of the AI buildout, with thousands more facilities planned.

"Since 2025 there have been many, many more datacenters so we need to think of this level of emissions as a lower bound," Dominici said. "We are going backwards toward the goal of controlling CO2 emissions."

The geographic concentration of that demand is striking: energy-hungry clusters have clustered in states including Virginia, Oregon, Ohio and Iowa, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/16/datacenter-emissions-study). A separate analysis by the advocacy group Climate Action Against Disinformation found that electricity consumption by major tech companies rose 240% between 2022 and 2025, AP noted.

## A pledge with limited reach

The "Antalya Artificial Intelligence Pledge," launched by COP31 host Turkey and the International Telecommunication Union, calls on signatories to power data centers with clean energy, improve efficiency, and disclose their power, water and emissions footprints. It is voluntary and sets no binding targets.

As of 17 November, only nine countries had endorsed it: Andorra, Brazil, Colombia, Luxembourg, Sierra Leone, Somalia, South Korea, Turkey and Uganda, [New Scientist reported](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2599357-just-nine-countries-back-pledge-to-tackle-ais-environmental-footprint/).

"We cannot ask people to accept higher bills, more planet heating pollution and deeper inequality today in exchange for promises of a better tomorrow," UN climate chief Simon Stiell said at the launch. "Claims about future emissions savings cannot excuse rising pollution now, making climate disasters even worse."

Independent analyst Ketan Joshi questioned the pledge's ambition. "I'm surprised that more countries haven't signed up, given the pledge's limited demands. It's not a pledge that really strikes at the heart of the problem," he told New Scientist, warning of "a return to greenwashing, a return to sort of empty promises."

## Dueling narratives on AI and climate

The study sharpens a debate over whether artificial intelligence is net climate villain or savior. Former US Vice President Al Gore told AP he is optimistic that AI's computing power can find new efficiencies, citing British economist Nicholas Stern's projection that AI could drive a 6 to 9% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030.

Caleb Max, president of the National Artificial Intelligence Association, argued the "fears may be overblown" and that AI will help solve the energy challenges data centers face. But Climate Action Against Disinformation spokesman Philip Newell countered: "We have hope in one hand and then harms in the other. They're selling us hope while hiding the harms from us."

Outside experts said the study's reliance on modeling was reasonable given limited industry disclosure. "We cannot manage environmental impacts effectively without reliable measurements and disclosure," said Cornell University energy systems scientist Fengqi You, who was not involved in the research. "Ultimately, the environmental footprint of AI is not fixed. It depends on where we build data centers, how efficiently they operate, and how we supply their electricity."

The study's methodology, detailed in [New Scientist's coverage of the findings](https://www.newscientist.com/article/2599078-massive-datacentres-use-as-much-electricity-as-an-entire-us-state/), estimated emissions for facilities whose operators do not publicly report their energy use, using available data and modeling. Critics including data scientist Alex de Vries-Gao noted such figures are difficult to independently validate.

## Political pressure and what comes next

The findings arrive amid mounting political friction. US midterm elections in November 2026 amplified calls from some Democrats for data center pauses, while President Donald Trump has defended the buildout as essential to economic competitiveness. Local opposition has flared near projects in Tennessee, Utah and elsewhere over noise, water use and air pollution.

Mitigation is possible. Capturing waste heat from data centers for district heating is gaining attention; the International Energy Agency estimates such systems could heat around 10% of European homes by 2030. But for now, the gap between AI's rapid expansion and climate commitments appears to be widening—and with more than 2,000 additional data centers planned, the study's authors warn their already-alarming figure is likely just the floor.
