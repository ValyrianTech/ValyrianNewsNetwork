# Belgian Budget Talks in Crisis After Vandenbroucke Remarks

Belgium's federal budget negotiations have slid deeper into crisis after Deputy Prime Minister Frank Vandenbroucke publicly attacked his own coalition partners, prompting a leading political analyst to warn that the talks have been frozen even further shut. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/17/vandenbroucke-heeft-de-onderhandelingen-nog-wat-meer-vastgezet/), commentator Ivan De Vadder said the Vooruit minister, "in all his zeal to explain why he said no on Sunday, has jammed the negotiations a good deal further closed" — leaving Prime Minister Bart De Wever scrambling to piece the coalition back together.

The standoff caps weeks of tension inside the so-called "Arizona" coalition of N-VA, Vooruit, CD&V, MR and Les Engagés, which has been struggling to close a budget gap of roughly €10 billion to satisfy European fiscal rules.

## How the impasse unfolded

The immediate trigger came on the evening of 16 November, when Vandenbroucke appeared on VRT NWS's *Terzake* and on VTM after Vooruit had refused De Wever's latest budget proposals the previous day. Rather than confine his objections to the negotiating table, the health minister launched a wide-ranging critique — targeting Les Engagés, MR president Georges-Louis Bouchez and the prime minister himself.

The budget dispute has followed a familiar left-right fault line. Vooruit, Les Engagés and CD&V want the "strongest shoulders" to carry more of the burden, while N-VA and MR resist higher taxes. When talks collapsed at the start of November, De Wever offered his resignation to King Filip, who held it in deliberation rather than accept it — a decision [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/02/geen-begrotingsakkoord-politieke-crisis-overzicht/) amounted to an official "political crisis." Political scientist Carl Devos noted at the time that "nobody believes new elections would offer a solution."

## De Vadder: a 'communitarian sting'

Speaking on Radio 1's *De Ochtend*, De Vadder dissected how Vandenbroucke's remarks hardened positions. By framing the abolition of a roughly €250 million tax benefit regime for professional footballers as something close to a trophy demand, the minister made it clear who would "win" or "lose" the talks. As [Business AM detailed](https://businessam.be/vandenbroucke-verwijt-de-wever-mislukking-begrotingsoverleg-en-hekelt-invloed-van-bouchez/), Vandenbroucke dismissed a proposed €20 million adjustment as mere "cosmetic surgery" and accused powerful figures of dictating financial limits while vulnerable groups were forced to make sacrifices.

De Vadder also flagged a communitarian edge. Vandenbroucke attacked Les Engagés leader Maxime Prévot over a strengthened indexation mechanism that would affect teachers' and pensioners' wages, framing it as forcing Flemish teachers to pay more so that Francophone teachers lose less through the French Community budget. "Moreover, in this case there is also a communitarian sting to it, which I find very striking," De Vadder said.

The same edge appeared in Vandenbroucke's remark that more attention is paid to "an old-fashioned Belgicist who doesn't speak Dutch" — a clear reference to Bouchez — than to "a moderate social democrat with a Flemish-leaning reflex." De Vadder described this as a pointed criticism aimed squarely at the prime minister.

## Criticism from within Vooruit

The damage was compounded by veteran Vooruit figure Louis Tobback, who told *De Standaard* that De Wever had "completely betrayed" the socialists' trust, saying the premier wants to "become the man who drives the country apart." The [*De Standaard* interview](https://www.standaard.be/politiek/louis-tobback-na-de-bromance-met-de-n-va-volgde-het-grote-bedrog.-de-wever-heeft-ons-vertrouwen-volledig-beschaamd/164623062.html) underscored that the grievance extends beyond a single minister. "So it is not just Frank Vandenbroucke," De Vadder observed. "Apparently that feeling lives in several sections of the party."

## The trust problem facing De Wever

De Vadder's central concern is that the public recriminations have poisoned the atmosphere at precisely the moment a deal was within reach. Some 80 pages of worked-out proposals — detailed "notifications" — already sit on the table, meaning one more negotiating round would normally suffice to close the gap.

"But then that process has to be started, and that did not happen last Sunday," De Vadder said. He expects a return to one-on-one "bilaterals" as De Wever tries to find common ground with each party separately. "After last night, you do feel that a good deal of extra irritation has been added. Especially among the other parties, people keep thinking that Vooruit wants elections and is busy with campaign language."

His advice to the political class was unusually direct: "Speech is silver, silence is gold. That is the advice I would give to politics."

## No hard deadline

De Wever is due to report to King Filip on Monday 23 November, but that is not a binding deadline. Because the "provisional twelfths" mechanism takes effect in January — allowing the government to spend at prior-year levels without a passed budget — negotiators face no strict cutoff. That removes some urgency, even as De Vadder warned that "a sense of despondency is creeping in."

"How can you still speak of a well-functioning government?" he asked. "Even in the Netherlands, despite all the crises, they manage to move forward and create a new momentum. We unfortunately do not succeed in that."

The European Commission has warned that Belgium's budget is deteriorating sharply, with only France projected to be in a comparably poor position by 2028 — adding external pressure to a crisis that is, for now, being fought out largely on domestic and communitarian lines.

## What to watch

The coming days will test whether De Wever can repair relations with Vooruit after being publicly blamed for the deadlock. The key questions are whether the socialists genuinely want a deal or are positioning for elections, and whether the absence of a hard deadline breeds complacency or, eventually, compromise. A year ago, a near-identical impasse over €10 billion was resolved on 24 November 2025 with a €9.2 billion savings deal — proof that this coalition can still land an agreement. Whether it can do so again, after such open recriminations, is the exam De Vadder says will only be graded in hindsight.
