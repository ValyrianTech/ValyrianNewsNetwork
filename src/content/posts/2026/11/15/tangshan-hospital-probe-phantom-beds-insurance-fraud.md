# Tangshan Hospital Probed Over Phantom Beds, Insurance Fraud

A whistleblower complaint from medical staff has triggered a joint investigation into Tangshan Hongci Hospital in northern China's Hebei Province, after an undercover report alleged the facility systematically registered patients as inpatients while they were absent from their beds, and billed the state medical insurance fund for care that was not delivered. Tangshan's health commission confirmed it has formed a joint investigation team and launched a citywide rectification campaign, the [news outlet The Paper reported](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34346324).

The case, published on 14 November, has renewed scrutiny of guachuang (bed-hanging), a practice in which hospitals keep patients on the books to claim insurance reimbursements, and it has exposed an unresolved dispute between local and provincial authorities over how the conduct should be defined and punished.

## What the investigation found

The Paper's reporter, Pang Jingtao, entered Tangshan Hongci Hospital as a patient on 18 October after expressing concern about blood-streaked sputum. On admission, a nurse advised him that if he went home at night he should keep his phone reachable, because insurance inspectors conducting bed-checks would call and he should return in time.

That evening, a ward listed as holding four patients contained only the reporter. Another ward registered 40 inpatients against a capacity of 34 beds. The next morning, fewer than 20 people were visible in a corridor while the patient board listed 42. In the orthopedics department, room 901 listed three patients but held no personal belongings and no occupants.

Whistleblowers supplied what they said were head-nurse night-round logs covering 295 days between 2021 and 2023. According to The Paper, the records show not a single day on which the number of patients actually present matched the registered inpatient count. On 254 of those days, fewer than half of registered inpatients were present. Averaged across the period, the daily presence rate was about 40.52 percent, meaning nearly 60 percent of registered inpatients were absent overnight. The starkest single day came on 18 April 2023, when 721 patients were registered and only 221 were present, leaving 500 unaccounted for at night.

## The money trail

The financial stakes are considerable. Even for absent patients, the hospital continued to levy basic fees: a bed fee of 25 yuan per day, a diagnosis and treatment fee of 20 yuan, and a Grade-II nursing fee of 20 yuan, totaling 65 yuan per person per day. The Paper estimated that these basic charges alone exceeded 6 million yuan (roughly $840,000) across the 295 days.

Beyond base fees, the investigation pointed to over-treatment. The reporter's stay of less than 24 hours generated a bill of 1,463.43 yuan, of which 1,328.08 yuan fell within insurance-reimbursable scope. Examination and testing accounted for 79.61 percent of the cost, while bed, treatment and nursing fees made up just 4.44 percent. Antibiotics were prescribed before blood test results returned, and a doctor told the reporter that "the purpose of coming to hospital is to get anti-inflammatory IVs; patients rarely come without getting them."

Complainants also alleged a workers' compensation angle: that the orthopedics ward registered patients who were never physically present, with medications packaged and handed to patients on discharge, labeled with bed numbers and names. The Paper obtained recordings it said showed the hospital coaching patients ahead of inspections, for example telling a patient that "social insurance is coming to inspect tomorrow; come in this afternoon, bring toiletries, towel and basin, and top up the paperwork."

## Ownership and incentives

Tangshan Hongci Hospital is a Grade II-A general hospital with 530 approved beds and a predecessor dating to a 1957 steelworks hospital. It is designated to serve employee medical insurance, urban-rural resident insurance and workers' compensation. The facility belongs to New Journey Health Group, which controls the A-share listed company New Journey (ticker 002219). The group holds a 70 percent stake in the hospital through a subsidiary, with Tangshan Iron & Steel Group holding the remaining 30 percent, The Paper reported.

The case also touches on a structural incentive. The reporting's companion social-media tags reference a claim that admission-related performance bonuses can run about ten times those for outpatient work, a financial pull toward over-admission that fraud enforcers have long flagged as a root cause of phantom-hospitalization schemes.

## A definitional dispute

The outcome of the investigation may hinge less on the facts than on how bed-hanging is defined. A lawyer, Zheng Mingwei of Beijing Zhongyin (Shenzhen) Law Firm, told The Paper that the long-term pattern of nighttime absence amounts to "a typical systemic bed-hanging hospitalization," violating Article 15 of the national Regulations on the Supervision and Administration of the Use of the Medical Security Fund, which took effect in May 2021 and prohibits designated medical institutions from decomposing hospitalizations and engaging in bed-hanging hospitalization.

Tangshan's medical insurance bureau, however, applied a narrower standard. An official told The Paper on 11 November that "mere nighttime absence cannot be defined as bed-hanging," citing a 2024 provincial inspection guide that frames the violation as leaving the hospital for a long time during treatment or not actually receiving relevant care. Still, the official said the public would be reimbursed normally while the bureau would recover related costs from the hospital to protect the fund.

The same province has read the rules differently elsewhere. A July 2024 reply from Hengshui city authorities to a political advisory body proposal explicitly listed as bed-hanging the case of "patients whose condition is mild, receiving only daytime treatment and not staying overnight for observation," conduct subject to orders to return funds and fines of one to two times the loss.

## What happens next

On 12 November, Tangshan's health commission said it had formed a joint investigation team "at the first opportunity" to conduct a comprehensive inspection of the hospital's practice. It acknowledged that patients, especially nearby residents, completed daytime treatment and went home at night, reflecting "inadequate implementation of patient management systems" and "inadequate publicity and notification." On the more serious allegation that some patients were absent all day, the commission said: "We are investigating this thoroughly."

A citywide rectification campaign is now underway, and The Paper reported that authorities have recently contacted the complainants to gather information. Six complainants were placed on standby by the hospital on 14 September, on the stated grounds that the department's business had undergone major changes, a move that raises retaliation concerns.

The hospital's director and medical-affairs staff did not respond to repeated calls or a text message seeking comment, The Paper said.

Several questions remain open. It is unclear whether the joint investigation will produce a criminal referral for fraud or an administrative penalty involving cost recovery and fines. The total sum improperly billed across the 295 days of night-round records, and a broader set of 963 days covering 2024 through August 2026, has not been quantified. Nor is it known whether New Journey, as a listed company, will issue a disclosure, or whether the case will prompt audits of other hospitals in its network.

What is clear is that the Tangshan case has landed at the intersection of privatized state-enterprise hospital ownership, state insurance-fund integrity, and a definitional gap that lets the same conduct be read as a management lapse in one Hebei city and as fraud in another, a gap that the investigation may now be forced to close.
