# Liege police arrested 343 in October as 52 officers injured

The Liege police zone arrested 343 people during the wave of student-led unrest that swept the city in October, the force has disclosed, in a balance sheet that also records 52 injured police officers and nine complaints of alleged police violence. The figures, published in the run-up to a fresh national student mobilisation in Brussels, quantify for the first time the full scale of a month of turbulence that drew its inspiration from protests across France and Germany.

## What the figures show

According to the [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/politie-luik-heeft-in-oktober-343-mensen-gearresteerd-52-agente~1792507723130/) liveblog, the Liège police zone counted 343 arrests between 1 and 24 October. Of these, 280 were administrative detentions — preventive measures taken to restore public order and head off further rioting — while 63 were judicial arrests linked to suspected offences. About half of those detained were minors; [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/782764/article/2026-11-14/manifestations-du-mois-doctobre-liege-la-police-devoile-son-bilan) puts the precise figure at 178.

On the police side, the toll was equally heavy: 52 officers were injured, 42 of them from the Liège zone itself and 10 from other zones called in as reinforcements. One officer remained totally incapacitated from work more than a month after the events, Le Soir reported. The force said a retrospective analysis of its interventions and conduct during the protests is still ongoing.

## A dispute over police conduct

The balance sheet is not merely a statistical exercise. It arrives at the centre of a widening dispute over how the arrests were carried out. The Liège police zone acknowledged it had received nine complaints of alleged police violence, all of which, it said, are being investigated. "Toute plainte contre un policier fait l'objet d'une enquête et de vérifications" — every complaint against an officer is subject to investigation and verification — the force stated.

It invited anyone wishing to report incidents to file a complaint with its internal control services, the independent police oversight body Comité P, or the General Inspectorate of the federal and local police (AIG). The force defended the administrative arrests as justified by the "situation exceptionnelle vécue en octobre à Liège" — the exceptional situation the city experienced in October — and cited the legal basis allowing police to detain a person where there are reasonable grounds to believe they are preparing to commit an offence that gravely endangers public tranquillity or safety.

In a closing note, the zone struck a conciliatory tone, saying "l'heure est à l'apaisement et à la désescalade" — the time is for calming and de-escalation — while urging caution in the interpretation of certain testimonies or images taken out of context.

## How the city reached this point

October's unrest had two faces in Liège. At schools such as the Athénée Léonie de Waha, students staged what Le Soir described as "bon enfant" — good-natured — blockades, singing "Siamo tutti antifascisti" and borrowing barriers from the funfair opposite the school. Speaking to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/775022/article/2026-10-05/liege-la-colere-des-eleves-gagne-la-rue-va-rester-jusqua-ce-que-ca-change) in early October, 17-year-old pupil Lou explained the impulse behind the movement: "Ce qu'il se passe en France a créé une impulsion" — what is happening in France created an impulse. Students spoke of a "sick school" and of fighting for their future.

But the mobilisation also spilled into significant disorder in the city centre, prompting the administrative arrests and the injury toll. The movement is rooted in anger over education budget cuts in the French-speaking Community (Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles), against a backdrop of rising living costs that has mobilised young people across the continent — from France, where strikes and clashes erupted in Paris, Lyon and Marseille, to Germany, where the "Gen Z regelt" movement took to the streets, and the Netherlands, where protests gripped The Hague.

## What's next

The timing of the disclosure is pointed. It comes days before the Fédération des étudiants francophones (FEF) is due to lead a new national student mobilisation in Brussels on Tuesday 17 November, the International Students' Day. Whether that demonstration passes off peacefully — and whether the nine complaints and the retrospective analysis produce consequences for individual officers — will shape how the Liège balance is read in hindsight: as the sober accounting of an exceptional month, or as the opening of a reckoning over policing in Belgium.

For now, the force's own message is one of restraint. But with investigations pending and a fresh wave of protest on the horizon, the full story of October in Liège is far from closed.
