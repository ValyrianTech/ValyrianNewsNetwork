# China Opens Survey on Enforcement of Eight-Point Rules

China has launched a public online questionnaire to gauge how the Communist Party's flagship conduct regulations are being enforced, giving citizens until November 20 to grade officials on everything from their work style to their handling of corruption "around the people." The survey, announced on November 8, is run jointly by the Public Opinion Survey Center of the National Bureau of Statistics and the People's Daily's People.cn platform, according to the [official launch notice](http://app.people.cn/h5/detail/normal/7083982508524544).

It is the latest installment in an annual exercise tied to the Central Eight-Point Regulation, the 600-character set of rules adopted in December 2012 that became the defining symbol of Xi Jinping-era discipline. The regulation governs how officials conduct research, hold meetings, travel abroad, and manage news reporting and spending.

## A Carefully Designed Instrument

The [questionnaire](https://paike.people.com.cn/dw_survey2026/) contains 19 substantive questions and five demographic items covering respondents' province, age, education, political status and occupation. Most questions use a five-point satisfaction scale, while multi-select items ask people to identify the most visible achievements and the most stubborn problems still to be solved.

The survey is anonymous. "This survey does not record names; the content you fill in is protected by the Statistics Law, and we will keep it strictly confidential," reads the questionnaire's preamble, echoing the launch notice's appeal for "netizens to participate enthusiastically and offer valuable suggestions."

The slate of questions offers a revealing map of the leadership's 2026 priorities. Several items focus on the "correct view of political achievements" — a theme that ran through a nationwide party education campaign this year. Q10 asks respondents to flag biases such as the pursuit of quick results, "flip-flopping" policy, blind decision-making, and "image projects" and "political-achievement projects."

A substantial block on curbing formalism and easing the burden on grassroots officials culminates in Q16, which asks respondents to rate the effectiveness of the "Several Provisions on Rectifying Formalism to Reduce the Burden on the Grassroots" across 17 detailed items — an unusually granular evaluation of a single regulation. The provisions cover everything from cutting redundant documents and meetings to curbing "formalism at the fingertips," such as forced check-ins and likes on government apps.

## Tied to a Broader Campaign

The survey lands at a moment of sustained anti-corruption messaging. On October 27, the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection and National Supervisory Commission published its September data on Eight-Point violations — the 157th consecutive monthly release, People's Daily reported the following day. The January-to-September figures showed 800,000 cases filed, including 73 at the provincial and ministerial level.

The questionnaire also tracks a drive against "misconduct and corruption around the people." Q7 asks respondents to rate the effectiveness of a "concentrated rectification" of such problems, while Q8 offers a checklist of persistent ills: superficial research, illegal eating and drinking, illegal gifts and money, public-funded tourism disguised as study tours or party-building, and officials who are inactive or derelict in duty.

## What the Survey Can and Cannot Show

The survey is best understood as a managed instrument rather than a neutral poll. The scale channels answers into "very satisfied," "relatively satisfied," "not very satisfied," "not satisfied," or "unclear — no comment," with no stronger expression of disapproval. Respondents can flag only the problems the questionnaire pre-approves, though an "other" option lets them add their own.

Its results are not typically published, meaning its function is to feed information to leadership and signal responsiveness more than to produce transparent, verifiable data. Because it is an open online survey hosted on Party platforms, the respondent pool may skew toward party members and engaged citizens rather than a representative national sample. It sits alongside the monthly enforcement data, which is published, as a softer, public-facing complement to hard reporting.

The survey's near-identical structure to the 2025 edition, reported by the same correspondent, confirms its institutionalized, recurring nature. What has changed is the emphasis: the added granularity on formalism and grassroots burden reduction suggests where the leadership sees the most persistent complaints.

## What to Watch

The key question is whether any aggregate results emerge publicly — historically they have not. The timing, coming in the opening year of the 15th Five-Year Plan and after a year of discipline-focused messaging, suggests the survey is meant to reinforce that campaign rather than break new ground. For now, its clearest signal is the priority list it embeds: a leadership concerned less with grand gestures than with the daily behavior of officials and the paperwork burden on those at the bottom.
