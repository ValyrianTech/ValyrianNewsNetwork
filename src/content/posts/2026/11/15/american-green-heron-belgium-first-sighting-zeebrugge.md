# American Green Heron Spotted in Belgium for First Time

A green heron native to North and Central America has been recorded in Belgium for the first time, drawing crowds of birdwatchers and photographers to an industrial zone near the port of Zeebrugge. The bird was logged on Tuesday 10 November at 12:31 by observer Arne Depoorter on the citizen-science platform [Waarnemingen.be](https://waarnemingen.be/observation/427317510/), which classifies the species as "very rare" in the region.

The sighting, in the Dudzele industrial area near a small waterway between Bruges and Zeebrugge, still requires official review before it can be formally recognised as a national first. But for Belgium's birding community, the identification has already caused a stir.

## What was found

The green heron (*Butorides virescens*), sometimes called the little green heron, is markedly different from any heron native to Europe. At roughly 45 centimetres, it is less than half the size of a fully grown grey heron, which can reach about a metre tall. Its glossy green-black back, reddish-brown neck, white throat and yellow legs make it distinctive.

According to [Het Laatste Nieuws](https://www.hln.be/brugge/zeldzame-groene-reiger-uit-amerika-duikt-op-in-brugge-vogelspotters-reppen-zich-voor-eerste-belgische-waarneming-ooit~a8698e57/), the bird is unringed and has undamaged plumage. Dozens of birdwatchers from across Belgium lined a cycle path in the industrial zone to see it, keeping their distance to avoid startling the animal.

![Green heron (Butorides virescens) at the Dudzele industrial zone near Zeebrugge](https://waarnemingen.be/media/photo/174888645.jpg)

## How it got to Belgium

The bird should by now have been in Central America for the winter. Its arrival in Western Europe is unexplained, and two competing theories have emerged.

For Christine Delrue, a volunteer of 20 years at a bird rehabilitation centre, the most plausible explanation is that it travelled as a stowaway. "It got lost, perhaps over the Atlantic. It found a spot to land, so as not to fall into the water from exhaustion. It found a boat. Then it arrived here, travelling for free on a boat, in the port of Zeebrugge," she told [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/une-premiere-en-belgique-un-heron-vert-originaire-d-amerique-observe-pres-de-zeebruges-11815690), which reported on the sighting based on field reporting by Eric Boever.

Others favour a storm-driven crossing. "I imagine the storms pushed it off its normal migration route and it ended up here," one observer told RTBF. The true mechanism remains unresolved.

## A bird with no way home

Ornithologist and wildlife photographer Vincent Legrand described the visitor as a young bird that is entirely disoriented. More significantly, he said the bird cannot realistically return to the Americas and will not survive a Belgian winter, given the cold and insufficient food.

"There is no longer any chance [of reaching] South America," he told RTBF. "But it can imagine continuing its migration south, because it won't be able to spend the winter here. There will probably be nothing to eat. And it's a bird that lives in warmer regions. So it needs to get to the equivalent of Latin America, that is, the Mediterranean."

In the meantime, the bird has been seen calmly perched on a branch and fishing for fish and insects in the ditch, apparently untroubled by the attention — though one spotter suggested it may be weakened.

## Why it matters

Green herons are rare visitors to Europe at all. According to species background compiled on [Dutch Wikipedia](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groene_reiger), the bird has been recorded in the Azores, the Canary Islands, Iceland, the British Isles, France and the Netherlands, with unringed individuals logged near Amsterdam and Zaandam in 2006, 2008 and 2009. A Belgian record is a genuine novelty.

The episode fits a broader pattern of American vagrant birds turning up in Western Europe after storms or via ship-assisted crossings — a phenomenon that fascinates birders and occasionally fuels discussion of shifting weather and migration patterns. One onlooker who spoke to RTBF speculated that climate or weather played a role, though experts stopped short of confirming any such link.

## What to watch

Birdwatchers will be watching whether the green heron begins moving south toward the Mediterranean before the cold sets in. If it finds enough food and the weather stays mild, it could linger in the region for weeks or months — a possibility that HLN noted is not unusual for vagrant birds that stumble into suitable habitat.

For now, Belgium's first green heron remains a small, unringed and improbable guest in an industrial zone — and a reminder of how far a wrong turn can carry a migrating bird.
