# Antwerp doubles winter shelter beds as centres fill up

Antwerp began its seasonal winter shelter for homeless people on Sunday evening, doubling capacity from 115 to roughly 240 beds across three centres. Organisers say the extra places are welcome but that the need extends well beyond the cold months — part of the night shelter is already full every night of the summer, leaving an average of about five people without a bed.

The expansion is coordinated by the Centrum Algemeen Welzijnswerk (CAW) Antwerp under fixed agreements with the city. The three winter centres are De Biekorf in the Dambruggestraat and Victor 4 and Victor 5 in the former Erasmus hospital in Borgerhout, with Victor 5 reserved for people without residence papers, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/14/winteropvang-voor-dak-en-thuislozen-start-in-antwerpen/).

## A fixed calendar, a flexible winter

The timing drew attention because the preceding week had already been noticeably cold. Anthony Verhasselt of CAW Antwerp said the winter period is locked into arrangements with the city, but he hopes there is room to revisit it as the climate shifts.

"That period is fixed in agreements with the city of Antwerp," Verhasselt said. "If winter weather were to arrive sooner or be more extreme in the future, I hope there is room to discuss that with the city."

He stressed that the extra capacity generally allows everyone to be placed. "It doesn't happen that often that we have to refuse people or leave them on the street because there is no bed," he said, while cautioning that "how great the need will be this winter remains to be seen."

The doubling is nonetheless framed by organisers as necessary rather than generous. Even in summer, part of the night shelter fills every night — the seasonal expansion addresses a peak, not a baseline.

## Beyond the night bed

CAW's response stretches well past the night shelter itself. Mobile teams go out onto the streets to reach people where they are. Day centres offer soup, bread and warmth during daylight hours. A separate care team runs a sanitary site where people can shower, do their laundry and receive medical checks. The organisation has also appealed to the public for donations, used to buy scarves, hats and gloves for people living on the street in winter.

According to the most recent official count from 2024, roughly 3,500 people in Antwerp are homeless or without stable housing — a figure aid groups describe as already straining the 115 beds available outside the winter period.

## A contested selection rule

One of the most contentious dimensions of Antwerp's approach is how it handles people without legal residence papers. Writing in April 2025, the medical and humanitarian organisation Dokters van de Wereld (Doctors of the World) argued that Antwerp's system lets administrative status — rather than vulnerability — decide who gets a bed.

"In other major cities like Ghent and Brussels, the first consideration is how vulnerable, ill, old or young someone is," said Anneleen De Maeyer, the organisation's Antwerp coordinator. "In Antwerp, a person's administrative status determines whether or not they are assigned a bed."

[Dokters van de Wereld](https://doktersvandewereld.be/nieuws-publicaties/115-bedden-voor-bijna-3500-dak-en-thuislozen-in-antwerpen-streng-en-ontoereikend) reported that when the winter shelter closed at the end of March 2025 and capacity dropped from 240 back to 115, 90 people — including 15 women — were put out on the street. Within days, 19 people who had been expelled from the night shelter approached the organisation, each of them physically or mentally vulnerable, citing conditions such as diabetes, heart disease, broken limbs and autoimmune illness. The group has called on local authorities to drop exclusion based on administrative status, expand capacity significantly and abandon the seasonal logic altogether.

## Scale and a growing younger face

The Antwerp figure sits within a wider regional problem. Across Flanders, [reporting by De Standaard](https://www.standaard.be/politiek/vlaanderen-telt-bijna-20.000-dak-en-thuislozen/40710824.html) has placed the number of homeless people at nearly 20,000, with the majority not sleeping rough but staying temporarily with friends or family for lack of their own housing — a hidden group that official street counts tend to miss.

Antwerp has also recognised a rising number of homeless young people. In October 2026 the city launched a pilot project alongside CAW to rebuild the bond between homeless youth and their parents or other close relatives, identifying young people through the welfare centre. Nathalie van Baren, the city's social affairs alderman, said waiting lists in youth shelter "keep growing." As [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/02/antwerpen-pilootproject-sterker-netwerk-voor-dak-en-thuisloze-j/), the city is investing 138,800 euros in the project, which runs until the end of 2027 — and where abuse or physical violence is present, returning home is ruled out and other options are sought instead.

## What to watch

The immediate question is whether this winter's demand stays within the expanded 240 beds. Beyond that, three points are likely to shape the debate: whether the city reconsiders the fixed start date as extreme cold arrives earlier; whether the 115-bed warm-weather baseline is ever raised; and whether the administrative-status access rule is challenged politically or legally.

For now, the beds are opening — and the organisations running them say that is welcome, even as the underlying mismatch between need and capacity remains unresolved.
