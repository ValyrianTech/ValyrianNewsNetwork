# GPS Jamming Suspected After Dozens of Aircraft Disrupted

The GPS signals of dozens of passenger aircraft flying over Belgium, the Netherlands, eastern England and northwestern Germany were lost for roughly 20 minutes during an evening incident on Monday 9 November, according to an investigation by Dutch newspaper [NRC](https://www.nrc.nl/nieuws/2026/11/15/gps-signaal-van-tientallen-vliegtuigen-bij-schiphol-verstoord-mogelijk-vanaf-een-schip-op-de-noordzee-a4941397). Investigators and a Dutch-based data analyst have traced the probable source to a position on the North Sea, north of the Dutch island of Texel, most likely emanating from a ship — raising suspicions of deliberate electronic interference.

The episode is the second large-scale disruption of navigation signals over the Netherlands in four months and signals a growing pattern of electronic interference reaching Western European airspace.

## What happened

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/15/gps-signaal-van-tientallen-vliegtuigen-bij-schiphol-verstoord/), the disruption emerged around 18:00 and lasted approximately 20 minutes. Data experts estimate that some 80 aircraft across the four countries were affected, most of them flying at high altitude at the time.

Dutch air traffic control at Schiphol confirmed to NRC that several aircraft suffered disruption of the RNP (Required Navigation Performance) satellite-based navigation system. At least seven aircraft experienced problems during landing but landed safely with guidance from controllers.

When a GPS system fails or degrades, pilots cannot rely on it to determine their altitude and position — a risk that becomes acute during descent and landing. Aircraft and airports maintain back-up systems, and aviation authorities stress that jamming does not automatically compromise safety. Still, [Het Parool](https://www.parool.nl/nederland/gps-signaal-van-tientallen-vliegtuigen-bij-schiphol-verstoord~bce26d91/) noted that interference has previously contributed to accidents, citing the crash of a medical aircraft in New Mexico in August after GPS disruptions caused by US military jamming.

## The Belgian dimension

Online flight-tracking data indicated that aircraft over Belgium also experienced navigation problems at the same moment. But Belgian air traffic control provider Skeyes told VRT NWS that it received no reports of GPS jamming or spoofing that Monday.

"We checked the various reports from last Monday," said Skeyes spokesperson Kurt Verwilligen. "No reports of GPS jamming or spoofing were registered in them." The affected aircraft flying over Belgium at that moment did not need to land there, which helps explain the absence of formal reports — highlighting a gap between crowdsourced tracking data and official air traffic control reporting.

## Jamming versus spoofing

The incident has focused attention on two distinct forms of electronic interference. In jamming, a GPS signal is deliberately overpowered with noise, blocking it entirely. In spoofing, a false signal is broadcast, misleading aircraft with incorrect position or altitude data.

Dutch air traffic control considers jamming the likely cause. US data analyst John Wiseman localized the source of the disruption on the North Sea, north of Texel, and his analysis suggests the signal came from sea level — probably from a ship.

## Russian vessels in the area

At the time of the disruption, six Russian-flagged ships were in the suspect area, according to [NH Nieuws](https://www.nhnieuws.nl/nieuws/365469/gps-signaal-van-tientallen-vliegtuigen-bij-schiphol-verstoord). Three of them, per maritime shipping news sites, formed a convoy for Russian weapons transport and were escorted by the Russian navy. The three ships and their owner are on the EU sanctions list over arms transfers between North Korea, Iran and Russia.

The coincidence is central to the geopolitical framing of the incident, but no official body has confirmed that the vessels were responsible. Attribution to a specific ship or state remains an inference based on location, not proven fact.

## A widening pattern

For years, GPS jamming and spoofing have been routine in conflict zones and along NATO's eastern flank — the Baltic states, the Black Sea region and Ukraine. Interference reaching the airspace of the Netherlands, Belgium, the UK and Germany is considered unusual and significant.

Jamming is regularly used as a military tool to interfere with military aircraft and drones. The suspicion here is that civil aviation is collateral damage from a state-linked electronic warfare operation.

The July incident, in airspace between the UK and the Netherlands, affected dozens of aircraft in a similar fashion. Whether the same source or method was involved has not been established.

At Skeyes, controllers report that more and more aircraft are arriving unable to perform satellite-based approaches because their navigation systems were disrupted earlier in their route. "In such cases the aircraft must switch to an alternative approach procedure, such as an ILS approach, using ground-based navigation signals," Verwilligen said.

## What to watch

Investigators have not publicly attributed the 9 November disruption to a specific actor, and Dutch and Belgian authorities have yet to open a formal joint inquiry. The key questions are whether the source can be positively identified, whether the July and November incidents share a common origin, and how NATO and the EU respond to electronic interference creeping into Western European civil airspace. For now, the incident stands as a reminder that the electromagnetic spectrum has become a front line — one that passenger jets cross every day.
