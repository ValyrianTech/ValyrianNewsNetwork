# FBI Investigates Bomb Found at Illinois Ethanol Plant

Federal investigators are leading the hunt for whoever planted a powerful explosive device at a riverfront ethanol facility in Peoria, Illinois, an attempted bombing that forced thousands of residents to flee a one-mile evacuation zone and kept the region on edge for roughly 18 hours. No arrests had been announced as of Sunday, and the FBI abruptly postponed a planned media briefing, leaving central Illinois waiting for answers about who was responsible and why.

The device was discovered at BioUrja Renewables, a sprawling fuel and alcohol production plant at 1 Edmund Street in South Peoria, after the company reported a bomb threat to police at 1:08 p.m. Friday, according to [The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/nation/2026/11/14/bomb-found-illinois-ethanol-plant-thousands-evacuated-fbi-investigates/). Police located a suspicious package about an hour later and, by 6:45 p.m., deemed it a credible threat. Bomb technicians rendered the package safe at 9:12 p.m.

## A Threat Officials Took Seriously

From the outset, law enforcement stressed that this was no ordinary scare. Bomb threats arrive routinely and almost always prove false, but officials said the package at BioUrja was different.

"Nationally when a bomb threat comes in, typically it's not legitimate. This is not the case here," Peoria Police Chief Brad Dixon said at an overnight news conference. "We found a credible package that we believe to be a threat. We're not considering this a hoax."

Mayor Rita Ali went further. "A device has been identified. We know it's a credible threat. We know it's a bomb," she said, warning that authorities could not initially rule out that more than one device had been planted. "It is real — this is a real thing."

The FBI later confirmed the package was an actual explosive device — the first formal law-enforcement acknowledgment that a bomb, not a hoax, had been found.

## The Evacuation

Shortly after 7 p.m. Friday, police issued a mandatory evacuation order covering a one-mile radius around the plant. The zone swept across parts of Downtown Peoria, the Warehouse District and the Southside, crossed the Illinois River into East Peoria, and included several Caterpillar buildings. Residents were told to leave the blast radius while bomb squads worked.

Shelters opened at Manual High School in Peoria and the EastSide Centre in East Peoria. About 75 people took refuge at the high school at the height of the response, Dixon said. The city's Civic Center, Riverfront Museum and libraries closed, and weekend events — including a hockey game, the Saturday portion of a Comic Con and a Jon Pardi concert — were postponed or canceled.

The evacuation order was lifted around 1 p.m. Saturday after an exhaustive overnight search of the roughly 44-acre facility found no additional devices. Street barricades came down and residents were allowed to return home.

## Federal Investigation Takes Over

The FBI's Springfield field office, led by Special Agent in Charge Ryan Presley, has taken the lead in the investigation, working alongside the ATF, Illinois State Police, the Peoria County Sheriff's Office and local first responders. Presley said agents would pursue whoever was responsible "tirelessly" and "bring them to justice."

Yet the investigation's public face has been notably quiet. A planned FBI media briefing in Springfield, set for 10:30 a.m. Sunday, was abruptly postponed with no new date announced, a sign that investigators are keeping their findings close as they pursue leads. Authorities have declined to describe the device's type or yield, and no suspect or motive has been disclosed.

## Why an Ethanol Plant

The choice of target has drawn particular scrutiny. BioUrja produces ethanol for fuel and industrial alcohol — highly combustible materials that officials cited as a central reason for the unusually large evacuation radius.

"Ethanol and highly combustible types of chemicals … that's what alarms us," Ali said. The plant, which dates to a 1930s whiskey distillery and was formerly an Archer-Daniels-Midland facility, handles chemicals that could magnify the consequences of any detonation.

Whether the facility was targeted specifically because of those hazards — or as a broader statement — remains unconfirmed. Investigators have offered no indication of the intended blast yield, and the "one-mile blast radius" framing has drawn public skepticism, with some observers suggesting the radius reflects a precautionary evacuation model rather than the confirmed reach of a single device.

## A Region That Knows Bombings

While bombings are rare in central Illinois, they are not unprecedented. Peoria's Planned Parenthood clinic was firebombed in 2023, causing more than $1 million in damage and leading to a 10-year prison sentence for a Chillicothe man. Two churches in Danville and Oakwood were bombed over a six-month span in 1997 and 1998, killing one person and injuring 34; the suspected bomber died in an explosion as police closed in.

The discovery also came on a chaotic national day, unfolding as an FAA-ordered ground stop halted more than 11,000 U.S. flights for about four hours and a tanker-cruise ship collision in New Orleans injured more than 30 people.

## What's Next

The central questions remain unanswered: Who built and planted the device, and what did they intend? The FBI has urged the public to remain vigilant and to report any information by calling 911, 1-800-CALL-FBI, or submitting tips online at tips.fbi.gov, where tipsters may remain anonymous.

With the evacuation lifted and the plant secured, attention now turns to the federal investigation — and to whether a postponed briefing will soon deliver the details a shaken community is waiting for.
