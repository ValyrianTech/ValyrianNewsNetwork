# Greenhouse Gases Hit Record Highs, WMO Warns

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases reached new record highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization said on 27 October, warning that the accumulated heat will shape the planet's climate "not just for years but for decades and even centuries." The finding, published in the WMO's annual Greenhouse Gas Bulletin, landed the same day as a UN report showing that national climate pledges would cut emissions by only a fraction of what scientists say is needed, setting a stark backdrop for the COP31 summit that opens in Antalya, Türkiye, in November.

According to the [WMO press release](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high), the globally averaged surface concentration of carbon dioxide reached 426.8 parts per million in 2025, or 153% of the 1750 pre-industrial baseline. Methane climbed to 1,948 parts per billion (267% of the pre-industrial level) and nitrous oxide to 339.1 ppb (126%), both also records.

## Concentrations, Not Emissions

The bulletin measures what has *accumulated* in the atmosphere rather than the volume of fresh emissions released in a given year—a distinction the WMO was careful to stress. About half of all CO2 emitted annually stays in the atmosphere, with the remainder absorbed by land ecosystems and the oceans. Because the gas persists for centuries, today's concentrations lock in future warming regardless of how quickly emissions are later reduced.

"Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett in a [statement accompanying the report](https://wmo.int/secretariat/ko-barrett/record-greenhouse-gases-trap-more-heat-future). "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies. The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue."

Barrett added that "every additional part per million matters because it traps additional heat. In effect, we are supercharging the ocean and the atmosphere. That means that heatwaves, tropical cyclones, and rainstorms are intensifying faster than our historical baselines prepared us for."

## A Tripling Growth Rate

The long view is perhaps the most sobering. When the bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 level measured by WMO's Global Atmosphere Watch network was 377.5 ppm—roughly 49 ppm below today's figure. Growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from about 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year on average between 2011 and 2020.

From 2024 to 2025, CO2 rose by 2.8 ppm—above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024, which coincided with a strong El Niño that undermined the land's capacity to absorb carbon through drought, drier vegetation and wildfires. "The more modest growth rate from 2024 to 2025 reflects recovery of the land carbon sinks and reduced fire carbon emissions in 2025," said WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova.

That reprieve is unlikely to last. The WMO projects that an "exceptionally strong El Niño in 2026 sets the stage for another surge in CO2 levels," with warming oceans also reducing how much CO2 the seas can absorb.

The [UN News service](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/10/1168677) reported that the bulletin also flags two less abundant but potent gases tied to the energy transition: sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), used in electricity distribution, which has an atmospheric lifetime of up to 3,200 years and a global warming potential more than 24,000 times that of CO2; and molecular hydrogen, whose leakage from production and storage infrastructure poses a growing but indirect climate concern.

## Pledges Fall Far Short

The atmospheric data arrived alongside a parallel UN Climate Change assessment of nations' nationally determined contributions (NDCs). As [RTÉ News](https://www.rte.ie/news/2026/1027/1596904-climate-emissions-un/) reported, the synthesis of 154 countries' pledges—covering 71% of global emissions—found they would cut emissions by just 6% below 2019 levels by 2035, or 9% if conditional targets are met. Scientists estimate roughly a 60% reduction is needed to keep warming to 1.5°C.

The shortfall is a marked deterioration from last year's projection of a 10% cut, a gap attributed in part to the United States' withdrawal from the Paris Agreement under President Donald Trump and to weak new pledges. UN climate chief Simon Stiell said the COP31 meeting, with its focus on "practical measures to support greater implementation," could help "address some of the key gaps."

## The Feedback Risk

Scientists warn that the two trends—rising concentrations and lagging policy—reinforce each other through feedback loops. Warming oceans and stressed land ecosystems absorb less CO2, leaving more of each year's emissions in the atmosphere. Alex Vermeulen, director of the ICOS Carbon Portal and a member of the bulletin's editorial team, framed the stakes in generational terms.

"Despite all the promises made by countries that signed the Paris Agreement in 2015, we still see no decline in the atmospheric growth rates of CO2," Vermeulen [said](https://www.icos-cp.eu/news-and-events/news/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high). "When I was born, CO2 concentrations were at 320 ppm, and now they are at 426 ppm. If we continue on this path, the goal of limiting global warming to 2°C above pre-industrial levels will soon be out of reach. That is not a good prospect."

## What to Watch

The WMO's findings will form part of the scientific backdrop as negotiators gather in Antalya from 9 to 20 November. The central question is whether the summit can translate the Paris Agreement's architecture into faster real-world reductions, given that the atmospheric record continues to climb even as the policy response stalls.

The coming year offers an early test: if 2026 delivers the CO2 surge the WMO anticipates, it will underscore how natural cycles can amplify—or mask—the underlying human-driven trend. What the bulletin makes plain is that concentration is a cumulative ledger. Every year of delay adds to a balance that will not be quickly written down.
