# Russia and China Build a Polar Silk Road in the Arctic

Russia and China are quietly assembling a shared Arctic logistics system — a "Polar Silk Road" anchored in Russian ports, maritime research and technology transfers — in a rapprochement that is alarming Western capitals and pushing the European Union to overhaul its northern strategy. The analysis, syndicated through the European public-broadcaster network "Vu d'Europe / A European Perspective" and published in French by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719), frames the Far North as one of the world's defining geopolitical flashpoints — reshaped by climate change, resource competition and the fallout from Russia's war in Ukraine.

## A Warming Region, a Sharpening Rivalry

The structural backdrop is climate change. As sea ice retreats, previously inaccessible routes are opening to commercial navigation, while the Arctic seabed sharpens resource appetites. According to the U.S. Geological Survey, cited by RTBF, the Arctic is estimated to hold nearly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and 13% of its undiscovered oil.

At the center of the diplomatic push is a recent visit to China by Nikolai Patrushev, an adviser to President Vladimir Putin. According to [Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/), the October talks covered linking China's Maritime Silk Road with Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor, developing Russian ports, exchanging technology for automated container terminals and conducting joint maritime research. Russia wants Chinese companies involved in the Northern Sea Route, direct container links between Shanghai's Yangshan port and Russian harbors, and Chinese expertise in port automation, shipbuilding and maritime training.

Cooperation has widened over the past decade. In 2023 the two countries signed a key Arctic cooperation agreement. Chinese actors now co-finance major extraction projects such as Yamal LNG and are significantly expanding their own nuclear icebreaker fleet. Trade underpins the relationship: China is Russia's largest trading partner, and according to Chinese government data, bilateral trade exceeded US$200 billion in 2025 for the third consecutive year.

## The Northern Sea Route's Commercial Surge

Maritime transport is becoming a strategic extension of the Moscow-Beijing relationship rather than merely a means of moving cargo. The Northern Sea Route (NSR) cuts distances sharply — the trip from Shanghai to Hamburg takes about 18 days via the Arctic, versus 30 to 35 days through the Suez Canal.

Commercial interest is accelerating. In 2026, Russian oil exports via the NSR rose nearly 50% year-on-year, with about 20 million barrels delivered to Asia between July and mid-October, according to [Modern Diplomacy](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/10/19/russia-ramps-up-arctic-oil-exports-as-global-shipping-crisis-disrupts-trade-routes/), citing Reuters. Exports could rise by another 5 to 10 million barrels before the navigation season ends, and Gazprom Neft plans to increase deliveries by about 35% this year. Putin has said cargo volume could double to 70 million tonnes a year by the end of the decade, helped by Rosneft's giant Vostok Oil project.

In August, Chinese shipping firm Sea Legend launched the first regular weekly container service across the Arctic, realizing Beijing's long-sought Polar Silk Road, the [South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/week-asia/economics/article/3364859/india-joins-china-and-south-korea-push-arctic-shipping-routes) reported. South Korea and India are also exploring the icy shortcut to Europe.

## A Chokepoint Hedge

The timing reflects disruption elsewhere. With the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea unsettled and tanker rates at record levels, Asian refiners are seeking alternatives. For Moscow, the NSR offers a safe channel to export its "black gold" while circumventing Western sanctions imposed over the war in Ukraine. For Beijing, the polar corridor is a hedge against its dependence on the Strait of Malacca — and a way to access Russian Arctic resources and infrastructure.

The route's limits, however, are real. Asia Times notes that Suez flows remain far larger than Arctic traffic, which is constrained by ice, high insurance premiums, sparse infrastructure and a short operating season. A companion RTBF explainer, citing credit insurer Coface, estimated that only about 3.5% of trade between East Asia, Northern Europe and North America could realistically shift to Arctic routes, with commercial potential through 2030 concentrated on raw materials rather than containerized trade.

## Western Alarm and the EU Response

The strategic implications are being watched closely in Washington and Brussels. Asia Times observed that "for Moscow, Chinese capital, shipping demand and technology could help turn an ambitious Arctic strategy into a functioning logistics system." Vlastislav Bříza, a security expert at Charles University in Prague, put it plainly: "China aspires to the status of a global superpower. One of its attributes is dominance, or at least a presence, in geographically important regions of the world — and the Arctic is unquestionably one of them."

The [East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/21/china-adapts-to-a-colder-arctic-reception/) noted that China's Arctic ambitions are meeting growing opposition from Western Arctic states, with proposed investments blocked and scientific activities scrutinized — though China's military capabilities in the region will remain limited in the near term. Western countries accuse Beijing of using research stations for military purposes, a charge China denies; in 2025 the U.S. Coast Guard reported five China-linked research vessels operating in or near the U.S. Arctic.

Russia's own militarization is a deeper concern. Moscow controls roughly 53% of the Arctic coastline, with Northern Fleet bases clustered around Murmansk and Soviet-era installations reactivated under Putin. According to [Defence24](https://defence24.com/geopolitics/cold-war-at-the-top-of-the-world-the-militarisation-of-the-arctic-and-the-sino-russian-alliance-on-the-northern-sea-route), Russia has deployed Bastion coastal-defense systems, Arctic-adapted S-400 air defenses and hypersonic-missile-armed fighters. EU foreign-policy chief Kaja Kallas warned: "Russia is expanding its military presence in the North. Moscow has reopened Soviet bases and is testing new weapons in the region... Drone incursions, GPS jamming and sabotage of undersea cables undermine Arctic security."

Defence24 also highlighted the West's capability gap: the United States has only two aging operational icebreakers, while Russia has more than forty, including Arktika-class nuclear vessels. NATO's problem, it argued, "is not geography, but the lack of specialized operational platforms." The Alliance launched its Arctic Sentry initiative in February 2026 to unify patrols, intelligence-sharing and exercises.

## Europe's New Arctic Strategy

The EU unveiled its own Arctic strategy in October, built on four priorities: security and resilience, climate action, and prosperity for Arctic communities. "The Arctic is warming up to four times faster than the global average and has become the subject of genuine geopolitical rivalry," said Commissioner Jozef Síkela, who prepared the strategy.

The plan would strengthen the European icebreaker fleet — currently only about ten vessels — protect critical infrastructure and expand surveillance of Russia's "shadow fleet" through a new Centre for Maritime Security extending into the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean. Per [Le Monde](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2026/10/20/eu-unveils-new-arctic-strategy-in-face-of-us-russia-threats_6760261_4.html), some initiatives aim to reduce reliance on Washington, including a "sovereign and secure" submarine cable linking Greenland to Europe and the IRIS2 satellite constellation to compete with Starlink. On drilling, Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen was blunt: "oil, coal and gas must stay in the ground" — a stance that diverges sharply from President Donald Trump's push to maximize U.S. Arctic extraction.

## What to Watch

The Russo-Chinese Arctic partnership is real but uneven: Russia supplies geography and icebreaker capacity, China supplies capital, demand and technology. Whether it matures into a parallel maritime network will depend on how far the NSR scales beyond bulk commodities, whether the EU's strategy translates into funding and ships, and how quickly Washington closes its icebreaker gap. For now, the Far North is less a new Suez than a new front — one where commercial logistics and geopolitical rivalry are becoming impossible to separate.
