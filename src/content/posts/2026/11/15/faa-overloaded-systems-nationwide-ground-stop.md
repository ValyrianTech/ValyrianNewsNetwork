# FAA Blames Overloaded Systems for Nationwide Ground Stop

A routine maintenance upgrade to the nation's air traffic control network triggered the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years, federal officials said Saturday, as airlines worked to recover from a disruption that stranded travelers, sports teams, and cargo across the country.

The Federal Aviation Administration halted most U.S. flight departures for more than four hours on Friday, Nov. 13, before lifting the order that evening, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/flights-halted-airports-faa-f81b0904331dafad6133f291e5f531a8). By Saturday, officials had traced the failure to a contractor replacing old copper wire with fiber at a remote FAA facility — work that somehow overloaded the system and caused intermittent communications and radar gaps at air traffic centers nationwide.

"Over 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds... gave us issues with our communication with airplanes, and it also caused some disruption with our radar feeds," Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said at a news conference, adding that the contractor, L3Harris Technologies, "owns this network" and "we're going to hold people accountable."

## Context: A System Under Strain

The disruption struck an air traffic control system long flagged as aging. The FAA manages 22 regional centers overseeing high-altitude flights, working alongside hundreds of airport towers and approach facilities. Controllers hand aircraft between these centers sequentially, relying on remote radio sites connected through a vast telecommunications network that is slowly being migrated from copper to fiber.

Congress approved $12.5 billion last year for an overhaul, and the FAA has already swapped thousands of copper wires for fiber optic lines and upgraded hundreds of radio systems. But roughly 30% of the old copper remains, and the administration is pushing for $30 billion more to finish the job.

"We have aging infrastructure throughout the airspace," Duffy said, while stressing that flying remains safe. "This is all the old legacy equipment. All of the new equipment that's been deployed did not go down."

## The Failure and the Fallout

Friday's outage began when L3Harris performed maintenance at a remote facility and the system began to overload, producing unstable frequencies at as many as 17 of the 22 regional centers. None went dark entirely, but the instability was enough to prompt the ground stop.

FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford described the moment officials grasped the scale of the problem. "If that had been a single facility, we would have just worked through it," he said. "But when we saw it go to two, three, 10, 17 facilities, that's when we started to realize there was something much larger going on here."

At the time, more than 3,500 planes were in the air. More than 3,000 landed safely using established procedures, and at least 827 flights were diverted — 670 sent back to their origin airports and 157 redirected elsewhere, according to aviation analytics firm Cirium.

Officials moved quickly to tamp down security fears. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there were "no indications of an attack against the nation" and no "footprints" pointing to anything nefarious. Bedford added: "We see zero evidence of anything nefarious, and zero evidence of anything AI-related." Officials said the FAA's new AI-powered prediction system was not connected to the failed equipment.

## Human and Economic Cost

The human toll rippled across the country. Some 11,000 flights carrying more than 1.5 million passengers had been scheduled to depart after 4 p.m. EST on Friday. Airlines canceled more than 6,000 flights on Saturday, and 1,500 more were scrubbed by 10 a.m. Sunday, according to [CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/14/politics/faa-ground-stop-what-we-know), citing FlightAware data.

At Chicago O'Hare, traveler Amber McQuilkin, 27, learned of the ground stop only by glancing at departure screens. "There's not really any information being passed to me," she said, weighing whether to abandon her trip.

Professional sports were disrupted as NBA, NHL, and college teams were left stranded. The No. 19 Ole Miss football game at Oklahoma was pushed to Sunday. Cargo operations were delayed too, with FedEx activating contingency plans. U.S. airlines move about 55,340 metric tonnes of goods daily, the industry group Airlines for America said.

Experts said the nationwide scale set this incident apart. "The thing that was unique about this is that it was a type of failure that wasn't local because it was part of the communication system," said R. John Hansman, an aeronautics professor at MIT. "It propagated around the country." Aviation consultant Andrew Charlton offered a blunter analogy: "What you're watching here is trying to change the tires of your car when the car's going at 80 miles an hour down the expressway, because nothing stops in aviation."

## A Political Flashpoint

Duffy and other administration officials used the episode to press Congress for further investment, framing the outage as evidence of chronic underfunding even as the secretary acknowledged that the upgrade work itself had overloaded the system.

"By doing nothing, you inject way more risk into the system," Duffy told lawmakers, warning that "you will have more of these" if funding lags. Outgoing House Speaker Mike Johnson, speaking on Fox News, said: "We don't really need any investigation to understand we need further investment."

Not everyone shared that framing. The Professional Aviation Safety Specialists union, which represents FAA technicians, told [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/ground-stop-air-traffic-control-0a71f6d3b52f9087105ebea7c527bdd7) that the FAA normally consults its own technicians to assess the risks of upgrades before proceeding, "but that didn't happen in this case."

The FAA and FBI are on site at an L3Harris laboratory examining a router that may have caused the failure, officials said, investigating whether contractor error or a faulty device is to blame. L3Harris said in a statement that it is "supporting the FAA in its investigation" and takes the disruption "seriously."

## What's Next

Bedford said the "worst is behind us" and projected a return to normal operations by late Sunday, ahead of the Thanksgiving travel rush. Airlines are still repositioning aircraft and crews, a process that typically takes days.

The incident is the first nationwide ground stop since January 2023, when a key FAA computer system that sends notices to pilots malfunctioned, and the second since the Sept. 11, 2001, attacks. It follows a regional air traffic communication outage in September that snarled Northeast flights after a construction crew severed a fiber optic line.

Safety expert Steve Arroyo, a former United Airlines pilot, said grounding aircraft is the correct call when controllers cannot reliably communicate. "If you don't have that ability... you've got to stop airplanes on the ground and not put them in the air, where you're going to risk not being able to communicate with them," he said.

With the modernization effort roughly two-thirds complete and billions more in funding at stake, the question now is whether Friday's failure accelerates the work — or becomes a cautionary tale about how it is carried out.
