# Belgium's Coalition Returns to the Table as Deadline Looms

Belgium's inner cabinet reconvened on Sunday afternoon at 2 pm for what is being framed as a last-ditch round of budget talks, with Prime Minister Bart De Wever racing to deliver a "positive message" to King Filip on Monday. The meeting follows a dramatic fortnight in which the prime minister offered his government's resignation, the King refused it, and the coalition was ordered back to the negotiating table to close a budget gap of roughly €10 billion by 2029.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/top-van-regering-komt-vandaag-opnieuw-bijeen-vanaf-14-uur~1793247532380/), De Wever summoned his deputy prime ministers for a final consultation after a weekend of technical working groups calculating concrete measures. A full budget accord is unlikely by Monday; one participant told the broadcaster he hoped the premier might at least be able to present the "big framework" to the King.

## A Coalition on the Brink

Belgium is governed by the so-called "Arizona" coalition, a five-party arrangement named after the colours of the state flag, bringing together Flemish and Francophone parties across the political spectrum. Its partners are De Wever's Flemish nationalist N-VA, the Francophone liberal MR led by Georges-Louis Bouchez, the Flemish socialists of Vooruit, the centrist Les Engagés, and the Christian democrats of CD&V.

The coalition's structure has been a recurring source of strain. Only two of its five party leaders actually sit in the government — De Wever and Deputy Prime Minister Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés. The others, notably Bouchez of MR and Conner Rousseau of Vooruit, wield significant blocking power from outside the cabinet, a hybrid arrangement that political scientists have described as a strange and fragile construct.

## Why the Talks Are So Difficult

The negotiation centres on a roughly €10 billion adjustment to the federal budget by 2029, aimed at stabilising public finances threatened by a structural deficit and rising debt-servicing costs. The deepest fault line runs between MR, which insists that no more than €1.1 billion of the effort come from new revenues as agreed in the coalition pact, and Vooruit, which wants a larger contribution from "the strongest shoulders."

The talks grew so fraught that De Wever offered his government's resignation to King Filip earlier in the month. The King held the resignation in deliberation before refusing it and asking the coalition parties to confirm their willingness to make concessions and continue negotiating within the government, setting Monday 16 November as the date for the premier to report back.

De Wever has since pursued a strategy of bilateral talks with each deputy prime minister individually, a process that saw MR's Bouchez and Deputy Prime Minister David Clarinval meet the premier for two hours last week. The premier even skipped a European summit in Dublin to concentrate on the budget. At Armistice Day commemorations in Brussels, he quipped that a ceasefire in the government was something "we're still waiting for."

## Measures Under Discussion

While the headline figure is €10 billion, the concrete measures have shifted repeatedly over the weekend. Among the ideas on the table have been scrapping a public holiday for civil servants and removing a private-sector holiday that falls on a weekend. Proposals to tighten healthcare reimbursement — initially expected to raise €750 million — were reportedly scaled back to around €100 million, and Les Engagés has pushed to extend a transition scheme for caregivers facing the loss of unemployment benefits.

On Friday evening, the first full inner cabinet meeting since the resignation offer began with a dinner at 19:00 and ran into the early hours, [De Standaard reported](https://www.standaard.be/politiek/premier-de-wever-roept-zondagnamiddag-kernkabinet-opnieuw-samen-in-de-hoop-op-positieve-boodschap-voor-de-koning/35089272.html). Sources close to the talks indicated that four of the five parties were prepared to work with the table on offer, with Vooruit the principal holdout.

## What's at Stake

Beyond the arithmetic lies the survival of the coalition itself. As VRT NWS political journalist Benedikte Coussement has outlined, three scenarios loom for Monday: no deal, in which case the government falls; further extensions granted by the King; or a deal — full or partial — that De Wever can take to the palace.

Even a €10 billion agreement would not fully repair Belgium's finances, with analysts warning the budget picture remains dire and that the country will likely enter 2027 operating under "provisional twelfths" because the 2026 budget is already late. The debate also carries a tangible social dimension: student groups have called protests for 17 November against education cuts and the rising cost of living.

Within the coalition, the pressure is mounting. N-VA's Chamber faction leader Axel Ronse has said it "would be criminal not to reach an agreement," while CD&V chair Sammy Mahdi warned that parties must realise they would otherwise be responsible for "political chaos with a big economic impact." Opposition Vlaams Belang, by contrast, argues there is "no other alternative than new elections."

## What to Watch

The decisive variable remains Vooruit, whose willingness to accept the current package could tip the talks toward a deal. Monday's audience at the Royal Palace will reveal whether Belgium's leaders can present a framework for agreement — or whether the coalition's long budget crisis deepens further. De Wever's stated ambition, for now, is modest and hopeful: to walk into the palace with good news.
