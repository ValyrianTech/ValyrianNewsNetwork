The European Union is preparing to conclude Montenegro's accession negotiations as early as the end of 2026, setting the stage for an accession treaty to be signed in June 2027 and full membership in 2028 — which would make the small Adriatic nation the bloc's 28th member and its first new entrant since Croatia joined in 2013.

According to [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/eu-says-montenegro-on-track-to-become-blocs-next-member-state-by-2028/a-79855011), the European Commission said in its annual enlargement report that Montenegro is the "frontrunner" among the nine countries currently seeking membership, and that negotiations could be wrapped up by the end of the year. The treaty would then need ratification by all 27 member-state parliaments, a process expected to take roughly a year.

In an interview with [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/europese-unie-wil-in-juni-volgend-jaar-toetredingsverdrag-ondert/), EU Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos was emphatic. "Montenegro is on its way to becoming the next new member of the EU," she said. "The accession treaty is already being prepared."

## A small country with a big symbolic role

Montenegro's size is precisely part of its appeal as a test case. "Actually, Montenegro is a small country with roughly as many inhabitants as Antwerp," Kos told VRT NWS, referring to a population of about 630,000. "I am confident we can bring the population on board."

A candidate since 2010, Montenegro uses the euro unilaterally, is a NATO member and has largely aligned with EU foreign policy — factors that make it a low-risk "proof of concept" that enlargement can still work. The Commission nonetheless says Podgorica must still implement reforms, particularly on fundamental rights and financial services.

The most serious threat to the 2028 timeline may be political rather than technical. Croatia has sought to slow Montenegro's accession over unresolved bilateral disputes, prompting Germany to publicly pressure Zagreb to settle the issues rather than hold the process hostage, [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/16/germany-pressures-croatia-to-resolve-bilateral-disputes-with-montenegro-over-eu-accession) reported.

## Four frontrunners

Beyond Montenegro, Kos identified three other candidates making real progress: Albania, Moldova and Ukraine.

**Albania** aims to complete all negotiation chapters by the end of 2027 — roughly a year behind Montenegro. "Over the past year, Albania has really sprung into action with all kinds of reforms," Kos said. "The question now is what impact the street protests against the government will have on their progress."

**Moldova**, a candidate only since 2022, has surprised observers by opening 25 of its 33 negotiation chapters this year. Its challenges are distinct: deep domestic division, with a portion of the population remaining strongly pro-Russian, and the prospect of Russian interference as it accelerates its pro-EU course.

**Ukraine** is the most geopolitically charged case. Fighting a full-scale Russian invasion, Kyiv must still meet the same standards as every other candidate. "Ukraine is currently fighting for its survival, but we are setting the bar just as high for them as for the other candidate countries. And that is not easy," Kos said. She hopes all negotiation chapters will be opened with Ukraine by the end of 2026 and believes membership within five years is possible — "although that will also depend on how long the war continues."

That optimism sits in tension with the Commission's more cautious official roadmap, which sets no membership date for Ukraine and only limited 2027 milestones in seven policy areas.

## Serbia's potential wildcard

The most consequential development may be in Serbia. Long the laggard of the Western Balkans after years of democratic backsliding under Aleksandar Vučić, Serbia was shaken in October 2026 when a student-led movement — born from nearly two years of mass street protests — defeated his party at the polls.

"After Montenegro, they were actually the furthest along, until steps were taken backwards under Vučić's government in recent years," Kos said. "I have already received them here in Brussels. If they are serious and really want to carry out major reforms, then things could move very quickly for Serbia."

## The bigger picture

Commission President Ursula von der Leyen framed the moment as a turning point. "The significant progress achieved by some candidates over the last year allows us to turn the promise of enlargement into reality," she said. "Step by step, we are building a more complete, stronger Union."

Kos went further, likening the current push to 2004, when ten countries joined the EU at once. "We have not seen this since the 'big bang' of 2004," she said. "I have the feeling that more and more European leaders realize it is important to bring all those countries on board, if we want to protect ourselves in these geopolitical times."

That framing — enlargement as security strategy rather than mere economic integration — is central to the bloc's revived ambition. After more than a decade of stagnation, Brussels is using its enlargement report as a political tool to lock in reforms and reward progress.

## What to watch

The 2028 target hinges on factors largely outside Montenegro's control. Whether Croatia drops its objections, whether Albania can contain its street protests, whether Serbia's new government delivers on reform, and whether the war in Ukraine allows Kyiv to keep pace all remain open questions. But for the first time in years, the direction of travel is clear: the EU is expanding again.
