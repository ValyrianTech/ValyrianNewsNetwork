# Older Belgians Demand Local Facilities to Stay Independent

More than 2,300 older people across Flanders and Brussels have told the Flemish Council for Older People (Vlaamse Ouderenraad) that they need basic amenities closer to home — shops, doctors, buses, cash machines and public toilets — if they are to keep living independently. The council distilled their needs into a framework and issued 30 concrete recommendations to federal, Flemish and local authorities, as well as to transport operators, banks, supermarkets and the hospitality sector, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/15/bevraging-vlaamse-ouderenraad/).

## A simple question, a massive response

The council, which represents more than 30 organisations working with older people, runs a large-scale survey every two years to gauge what would raise seniors' quality of life and self-reliance. This year it posed a single open question: *"How can I help you?"* The response, gathered through a digital questionnaire and around 50 in-person "thinking sessions", was, in the council's words, massive. Details of the findings were [published by De Zondag](https://www.dezondag.be/actua/ouderen-komen-met-duidelijke-boodschap-roep-om-meer-basisvoorzieningen-in-de-buurt/) in a feature by Laurens Kindt.

"The response was enormous. We reached more than 2,300 older people," said Magda De Meyer, chair of the Flemish Council for Older People. "Many older people feel they want to keep the reins of their lives in their own hands for as long as possible. That requires services close by — and those services have been steadily scaled back in recent years."

## The "5 B's"

The needs were summarised as a set of "B's". The first four: **boodschappen** (being able to do groceries independently), **basisgezondheidszorg** (basic healthcare — a doctor, a pharmacist), **bussen** (buses and other public transport) and **bankautomaten** (cash machines). The fifth, the council stresses, is just as fundamental: public toilets.

"Being able to do groceries: very important. People want to do that themselves and not have to rely on others," De Meyer said. She noted that basic healthcare and transport are closely linked. "Often people have to make huge journeys for that, or they hit a patient stop [doctors no longer taking new patients]. Buses and other forms of public transport also ensure that people stay mobile and can maintain their social network."

Cash machines matter too. "Quite a lot of older people like to keep cash at home, or use it to pay. Having cash gives a sense of security."

## Isolation, and a policy "upside down"

De Meyer argues that the erosion of these services directly drives isolation. "Especially with the cutbacks to bus lines, there are many people who are cut off from basic services because they simply can no longer reach them," she said. "We also collected testimonies from people who say they can no longer get to the hospital, or to visit their partner in the care home. By scaling back these basic services, people become increasingly isolated."

The survey lands against a backdrop of significant cuts to Flemish public transport. From 1 July 2026, more than 40 bus lines were scrapped across Flanders as a result of budget savings, with rural areas hit hardest — a move that passenger group TreinTramBus warned would leave some municipalities without regular daytime service, as [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/04/23/de-lijn-oost-vlaanderen-treintrambus-schrappen-buslijnen/) at the time.

De Meyer directs sharp criticism at the logic behind transport planning. "For public transport, people used to start from the user's demand ... Now that policy vision has been turned upside down and one looks at the supply: where is public transport used most? And that's where extra investment goes. That's the world upside down." She pointed to the Flemish government's Loneliness Plan as a patch for a problem she says transport and service policy itself creates.

## Local solutions as models

The council highlights a series of local initiatives as examples of tailoring services to seniors. Among them is De Buurtkar in Bornem, a mobile grocery van that has toured a different sub-municipality each weekday for about a decade and also acts as a satellite for municipal services.

"For older people, De Buurtkar is a real added value," said Tom Van Ranst, the alderman responsible for the scheme. "Often these people are less mobile and send their children to the supermarket. But then they lose a degree of independence." Beyond groceries — including fresh local products such as asparagus — the van lets residents renew an ID card, and staff are trained to spot social problems such as loneliness. All revenue is reinvested in social projects.

Other models cited include a dedicated seniors' police officer ("seniorenflik") in Leuven and Eeklo, a mobility scheme in Bruges, accessibility screening for hospitality businesses in Hasselt, dedicated seniors' desks in municipal offices, and additional public toilets secured by local advisory councils in Lummen and Poperinge.

## What's next

The council's 30 recommendations — covering government, De Lijn, NMBS, banking federation Febelfin, supermarkets and the hospitality sector — frame convenience spending as a cost-saving investment. Investing in local transport and medical posts, De Meyer argues, eases pressure on expensive hospitals and care homes.

"I would advise every local authority to sit down with the Council for Older People," she said. "Often we can arrive at creative solutions that way. Because what you do for a 70-year-old is also good for a 7-year-old."

With Flanders now counting more than 1.5 million older people, how quickly authorities respond — and whether the recommendations surface in the implementation of the Flemish Older People's Policy Plan 2026–2030 — will determine whether these calls translate into concrete change.
