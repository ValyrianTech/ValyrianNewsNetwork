# EU Moves to Ban Online Sales Traps and Dark Patterns

The European Commission on Wednesday presented its long-awaited **Digital Fairness Act (DFA)**, a sweeping proposal to end manipulative practices in online commerce—from bogus "only two left!" warnings and pre-ticked add-ons to subscription traps and undisclosed influencer marketing. According to [the European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/making-your-online-experience-fairer-2026-11-11_en), around 60% of Europeans now shop online, yet online shoppers are more than 60% likelier to run into problems than those buying in physical stores.

The proposal would replace the EU's 2005 Unfair Commercial Practices Directive with a regulation that applies directly and uniformly across all 27 member states. It must still be negotiated and agreed by the European Parliament and EU governments before it can become law, and would apply 12 months after entering into force.

"Shopping has never been more convenient, but convenience must never come at the expense of fairness," said Michael McGrath, Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection. "Whether people shop online or in a physical store, they deserve the same high level of protection."

## A Blacklist for Dark Patterns

At the heart of the text is a new list of ten manipulative design tricks—known as "dark patterns"—deemed unfair in all circumstances, meaning authorities would no longer have to prove consumer harm case by case. As [Euronews reported](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/09/eu-to-blacklist-ten-manipulative-online-design-traps-leaked-draft-shows) from a draft ahead of the launch, the list covers making cancellation disproportionately harder than sign-up, burying prices or auto-renewal terms, falsely suggesting there is no alternative, and false or unqualified claims about a product's availability or popularity.

It also targets scarcity warnings repeated throughout checkout, pre-selected options that add extra products or quantities, framing a refusal as the wrong choice, repeatedly nagging consumers to change a decision already made, confusing double negatives, and fake notifications implying someone has contacted the user. The Commission could add new tricks to the list without returning to lawmakers.

Beyond the blacklist, the DFA bans "drip pricing," where unavoidable fees appear only at checkout. It also prohibits misleading "was/now" discounts calculated against a manufacturer's recommended price rather than a competitor's real price, and addresses dynamic pricing for cultural and sports events. In virtual queues, companies would have to show the current real-time price and the highest possible price.

## Subscription Traps and the Right to a Human

The proposal takes direct aim at so-called subscription traps, a recurring cross-border complaint that the Commission says costs consumers billions of euros yearly. Free trials could no longer roll silently into paid subscriptions unless the consumer actively consents, and companies would have to remind customers before automatic renewals. Subscribers would also get a "terminate the contract here" button and clearer rights to cancel digital media subscriptions within the withdrawal period.

Consumers would additionally gain the right to speak to a human in customer service—a provision aimed at the automated mazes many shoppers encounter when trying to resolve a dispute.

In a first for EU consumer law, the proposal also sets rules for **AI shopping agents**. Users would choose which purchases require their approval, and the agents would have to disclose sponsored suggestions.

## Protecting Children and Tackling Addictive Design

The DFA places particular emphasis on minors. Paid loot boxes and pay-to-win or pay-to-progress mechanics would be banned for children in video games. In-game currencies would have to show their real-money value and be switched off by default for minors unless a parent approves. Advertising based on profiling minors would be prohibited for all traders, not only the large platforms already covered by the Digital Services Act.

Under a "fairness by design" principle, all users would gain the right to switch off autoplay, notifications unrelated to their own activity, streak-style rewards, and chatbot features that simulate relationships to keep people engaged. Influencers, meanwhile, would have to be transparent about commercial content.

## Carrots, Sticks and a Six Percent Fine

The rules come with real enforcement teeth. A parallel revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation Regulation would empower the Commission to investigate cross-border cases and impose fines of up to 6% of a company's global annual turnover, [Reuters reported](https://www.yahoo.com/news/world/articles/eu-threatens-significant-fines-unfair-112205092.html).

"Our consumer protection rules will help ensure that businesses compete on the merits of their products and services, not on their ability to exploit consumers or their ability to evade the rules," McGrath said. "We are not making it harder to do business, we are making it harder to do business unfairly."

The Commission estimates the rules would spare consumers €88.4 billion in financial losses between 2025 and 2035—an annual benefit of €13.7 billion including time saved—against recurring costs to business of €678 million a year once overlaps are accounted for.

## Business Pushback and a German Objection

Not everyone is convinced. BusinessEurope, the Brussels-based industry lobby, warned that the law risks layering new obligations onto an already crowded rulebook. "The Digital Fairness Act should make the existing framework more coherent, predictable and effective," Director General Markus J. Beyrer said. "Instead, it risks introducing unnecessary obligations and overlapping requirements that undermine legal certainty and run counter to the EU's simplification agenda."

The proposal also faces institutional resistance from Berlin. In a non-paper dated 14 October, the German government argued that existing consumer directives should be amended rather than replaced by a new horizontal regulation, and said the case for converting the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive into a regulation "has not yet been sufficiently demonstrated."

## What to Watch

The Digital Fairness Act is arguably the most comprehensive overhaul of EU consumer law in two decades, shifting enforcement from reactive, case-by-case action toward a prescriptive, design-based regime. Its most novel frontiers—rules for AI shopping agents and blanket restrictions on profiling minors—venture into genuinely new legal territory rather than merely updating existing rules.

Whether that ambition survives the legislative process is the open question. The proposal now passes to the European Parliament and member states, where Germany's preference for amending directives rather than creating a regulation, and industry warnings about regulatory overlap, will shape the final text. Consumers, for their part, will be watching for one thing above all: whether the loopholes that made "only two left!" so effective finally close.
