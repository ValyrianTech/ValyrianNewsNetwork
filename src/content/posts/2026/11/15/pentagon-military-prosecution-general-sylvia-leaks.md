# Pentagon Weighs Its Own Case Against Leak-Accused General

The Trump administration has directed the U.S. military to explore bringing a criminal case against Army Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia, the three-star general whom Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's team accused of leaking sensitive information to journalists, [The Washington Post reported](https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/11/15/pentagon-prepares-its-own-prosecution-lt-gen-brett-sylvia/) on Sunday. The move marks an escalation of the administration's anti-leak campaign and means Sylvia now faces potential legal jeopardy on two fronts: the previously announced referral to the Justice Department and a parallel track inside the military justice system.

According to people familiar with the matter, the matter was delegated "for appropriate disposition" to Adam Telle, the acting Army secretary. To date, neither the military nor federal law enforcement has charged Sylvia with a crime. He has not spoken publicly, and attempts to reach him have been unsuccessful.

## Background of the Case

Sylvia, 54, a highly regarded combat veteran who has earned a Purple Heart and six Bronze Stars, served as director of Strategy, Plans and Policy for the Joint Staff — effectively the Pentagon's chief military planner — reporting to Gen. Dan Caine, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff. Hegseth abruptly fired him in late October 2026 without offering an initial explanation, as [The Washington Post reported](https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/10/29/hegseths-team-forces-out-lt-gen-brett-sylvia-officials-say/). The removal was part of a broader campaign by Hegseth against leakers, which has included roughly two dozen senior officer ousters and retirements.

The allegations against Sylvia surfaced quickly. A statement attributed to an unidentified "War Department official" alleged that he leaked classified information about the Iran war, the Russia-Ukraine war "and more," and met journalists outside the Pentagon to circumvent press restrictions. Pentagon chief spokesman Sean Parnell said the general failed three polygraph tests and that an "extensive" internal investigation had been conducted. "The Department of War has zero tolerance for the intentional, unauthorized disclosure of classified information," Parnell said, according to [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/29/us/hegseth-army-general-sylvia-trump.html).

A U.S. official alleged that Sylvia acknowledged to military investigators taking notes from material classified as secret and providing information from it to a reporter, and that he communicated with a reporter via the Signal messaging app and then deleted the chat. Others said Sylvia acknowledged relationships with reporters but denied disclosing classified information. Axios, citing an internal military report, [reported](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/29/fired-general-leaks-iran-ukraine) that the general admitted leaking to journalists from two media outlets. The Post has not independently verified these claims, and the Pentagon declined to share its internal investigation.

At the time of his removal, the matter was referred only to the Justice Department. Pentagon officials said then that the ouster was necessary "to protect sensitive information," as [USA Today reported](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/10/29/pentagon-refers-general-for-criminal-prosecution/92504400007/). The Justice Department has not confirmed that it is investigating the Pentagon's specific claims, saying only that it has "ongoing investigations related to possible leaks of classified information" and works closely with "government partners," including the Defense Department.

## A Second Track in Military Justice

The new development places the case at the intersection of two contentious issues: Hegseth's aggressive pursuit of leakers and longstanding questions about whether the military justice system can fairly try a general whose prosecution is being pushed by the civilian leadership above him.

The Associated Press noted at the time of the referral that [it was not clear](https://apnews.com/article/hegseth-general-firing-iran-war-strikes-6726895f0c7fe93c7cbc94cae0ff7621) why the Pentagon appeared to be pursuing charges in civilian courts instead of the military justice system. The Nov. 15 reporting answers that question: both tracks are now active.

If the case proceeds inside the Defense Department, it would place extraordinary pressure on the military justice system. A court-martial jury would be composed of eight of Sylvia's peers — by regulation, officers of equal or higher rank, meaning four-star generals, senior three-star officers or their retired equivalents.

## Legal Hurdles and Political Questions

Whether Sylvia can be convicted is far from certain. It is unclear whether he generalized information to avoid disclosing classified material, as officers routinely do in briefings, and deleting a Signal chat proves nothing by itself. Polygraph results are inadmissible in court and, if publicized, could taint the jury pool.

Franklin Rosenblatt, a retired Army officer, lawyer and associate professor at Mississippi Christian University School of Law, said the emphasis on polygraphs is "problematic" because the machines cannot substitute for human judgment. "The jury is charged with determining who is credible and who is lying, not machines," he said.

Rosenblatt also suggested that the case may be more about sending a message than securing a conviction. "In many recent national security controversies in the second Trump administration, the process is the punishment, and cases are brought without a realistic view of whether the government can take them to trial and secure a conviction," he said. He added that Sylvia's defense team will "almost certainly" raise unlawful command influence — the "mortal enemy of military justice" — given the direct role of Hegseth's team in pushing the case.

Eugene R. Fidell, a visiting lecturer at Yale Law School with decades of military justice experience, said an Article 32 hearing is a "significant early step to decide whether there is a basis for a trial." Such hearings are typically open to the public, with partial exceptions for classified material.

The political backdrop has added to the scrutiny. President Donald Trump, when asked about the firing, misgendered the general and said, "She's gonna have big problems... We're very much looking for leakers." Parnell, the Pentagon spokesman, wrote on X on Oct. 29: "Our commitment was not an empty promise. We vowed to find you, and we meant it." The Joint Staff initially issued a statement thanking Sylvia for 32 years of "distinguished service," then retracted it, saying upon review it "did not appropriately reflect the full circumstances" — an unusual reversal that underscored friction between the political leadership and the uniformed military.

The case has also drawn attention to the Pentagon's broader anti-leak apparatus. Under Hegseth, the department has administered mass polygraph tests to senior leaders, including Joint Staff members, tightened nondisclosure agreements, restricted media access on Pentagon grounds and set up a Justice Department-Pentagon leak task force. The New York Times noted that mass polygraph tests at the top ranks of the U.S. military are "without parallel" in modern history.

Skeptics point to precedent. In 2025, three Pentagon officials were fired over an unauthorized disclosure but never referred for criminal prosecution; one, Dan Caldwell, was later rehired in the administration, undercutting the credibility of earlier leak allegations. The Post also noted that details of the allegations against Sylvia have surfaced only through "selective leaks authorized by the Pentagon," raising transparency and fairness concerns.

## What to Watch

The central questions remain open. Neither the military nor the Justice Department has brought charges, and it is unclear whether either will. Legal experts suggest the case may test whether the military justice system can withstand political pressure from above, and a defense motion on unlawful command influence could prove decisive if a court-martial moves forward.

The escalation also lands at a charged political moment — amid the eight-month Iran war and just after the 2026 midterms — raising questions about whether the pursuit of Sylvia is driven by accountability, deterrence or politics. For now, the general remains uncharged, and the two-track approach signals that the administration intends to press the case on whatever legal terrain it can.
