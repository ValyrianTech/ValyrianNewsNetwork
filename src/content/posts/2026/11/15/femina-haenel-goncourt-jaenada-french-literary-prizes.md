# Femina to Haenel, Goncourt to Jaenada in French Prize Season

France's most prestigious literary prizes were awarded in Paris in early November, with the Femina going to Yannick Haenel for a novel about the solitude of teachers and the Goncourt to Philippe Jaenada for a true-crime investigation into a long-forgotten murder. The back-to-back announcements capped a "rentrée littéraire" that was shadowed throughout by a controversy over artificial intelligence in publishing.

The all-female Femina jury awarded its main prize on Wednesday 4 November to Haenel's *La solitude des professeurs est infinie* (Gallimard), a partly autobiographical novel drawn from his years teaching French in the Paris suburbs, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576). The Goncourt had been handed the previous day to Jaenada's *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel* (Flammarion), announced from the steps of the Drouant restaurant in the traditional manner by Académie president Philippe Claudel.

## A novel about teachers, timed to a protest movement

Haenel's win arrived at a pointedly contemporary moment. The author dedicated the prize "to teachers, to high-school and middle-school pupils" as a student protest movement continued in the streets of France, and he has said he felt the need to write about teachers' solitude after the murders of Samuel Paty in 2020 and Dominique Bernard in 2023.

The book follows Jean Deichel, a 22-year-old trainee French teacher posted to a collège in Mantes-la-Jolie, a working-class Paris suburb. It depicts low pay — he is lodged in a tennis club — three hours of commuter train each day, the struggle to assert authority, and the moral wear of a profession whose protagonist feels "abandoned and scorned by society." Writing in *Charlie Hebdo* after Paty's murder, Haenel had put it plainly: "Teachers are much more than civil servants; they are flame-bearers."

[RFI noted](https://www.rfi.fr/fr/culture/20261104-yannick-haenel-prix-femina-2026-proclame-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie) that the novel reads as both a tribute to educators and a reflection on the (im)possibility of passing on knowledge in a fragile institution. The 59-year-old Haenel, born in Rennes, taught in the 1990s before turning to writing; he had previously won the Prix Décembre, the Prix Interallié and the Prix Médicis, and in 2014 launched the *Aventures* review and collection at Gallimard.

The Femina's other laurels went to the American writer Laura Kasischke for *Baignade surveillée* (foreign novel) and to Jennifer Tamas for *Barbe Bleue contre-enquête* (essay). Haenel saw off fellow finalists including Emma Marsantes and Lucie Rico, as well as David Djaïz and Élise Lespade, according to [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/culture/livres-bd/2026/11/04/prix-femina-2026-yannick-haenel-distingue-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-23DQ7VULJRHWJNFYUBXGSWNS7A/), which reported that the jury met in the Musée Carnavalet–Histoire de Paris.

## Jaenada gives a murder victim back her name

At the Goncourt, the emphasis was on restoration rather than education. Jaenada's 528-page *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel* reconstructs the life of Louise Cansot, a young Breton-born artists' model found strangled on the quai de Javel in September 1949 — a case the police filed away within six months.

"He makes visible a woman who had been made invisible, a victim, a young victim the police did not much care about," Claudel said as he announced the winner, [RFI reported](https://www.rfi.fr/fr/culture/20261103-littérature-philippe-jaenada-remporte-le-prix-goncourt-2026-pour-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel). "I feel joyful," Jaenada said simply on arriving at Drouant. The Académie's nine voting members gave him six votes to three for Haenel.

Rather than solving the murder, Jaenada — who read all 75 of Georges Simenon's Maigret novels during his research — seeks to restore the victim's memory, tracing her childhood in Tréguier and the narrowing of a life cut short. It continues the archive-driven true-crime method that has become his signature, seen in *Sulak*, *La Petite Femelle* and *La Serpe*, the last of which won him the Femina in 2017. The same day, the Renaudot went to François-Henri Désérable for *Le Palais* (Gallimard).

Jaenada succeeds Laurent Mauvignier, who won the 2025 Goncourt with *La Maison vide*.

## An AI scandal, and questions of authorship

The 2026 season was defined as much by what was removed as by what was crowned. Thélyson Orélien's *C'était ça ou mourir*, long tipped as a favourite, was withdrawn from the Goncourt and other prizes over suspicions that it had been produced with artificial intelligence — a use his publisher later acknowledged, according to [RFI's earlier coverage](https://www.rfi.fr/fr/culture/20261021-affaire-thélyson-orélien-l-éditeur-boréal-annonce-que-l-écrivain-a-utilisé-l-ia-pour-c-était-ça-ou-mourir).

The affair has pushed the Académie Goncourt toward formalising a rule that the prize "can only crown a work written by a human being." How such a standard would be defined — and enforced — remains one of the season's open questions. It also complicates the celebration of the winners themselves: Haenel's Femina book has been the subject of a plagiarism controversy raised by the outlet ActuaLitté, which compared it to a manuscript by Fabrice Sanchez; Haenel denies ever having seen it, echoing a 2007 plagiarism accusation over *Cercle* that was rejected in court.

## What the prizes mean — and what to watch

Beyond the ceremony, France's autumn prizes are commercial engines: a Goncourt or Femina win reliably drives sales and readership, which is precisely why Jaenada has described a prize as "one of the important ways to make a book known." Both winning houses, Gallimard and Flammarion, are now positioned for a strong season.

The prizes also remain a lightning rod for debate about who gets recognised. Days after the awards, more than 200 women authors published an open tribune denouncing what they called the "sexism" of the French literary-prize system — an irony not lost on observers, given that the Femina itself was founded in 1904 by women writers to counter the perceived misogyny of the Goncourt.

For now, the season's two headline books sit at the centre of the conversation: one a quiet, angry account of what it costs to teach, the other a patient act of remembrance for a woman history forgot. What to watch next is whether the Goncourt makes its "human authorship" rule real, whether the questions around Haenel's novel gain traction, and how the push against prize-culture sexism reshapes the institutions that hand out France's most coveted literary crowns.
