# Bomb Hoaxes Hit 27 European Flights, Including Brussels

A wave of hoax bomb threats disrupted passenger aviation across Europe on Thursday 5 November, targeting at least 27 flights and forcing emergency diversions, military fighter-jet escorts and temporary groundings from Helsinki to Bucharest. Among the affected aircraft was a LOT Polish Airlines flight bound for Brussels, which landed safely at Brussels Airport in Zaventem, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/). No explosives were found on any of the flights.

It was the second such wave in a week. Three days earlier, on 3 November, at least seven flights received similar threats, and no explosives were ever located. Investigators are now examining whether the incidents are linked, and who is behind them.

## The Brussels-bound flight

The LOT service from Warsaw to Brussels was the most directly relevant to Belgian authorities. After landing at Zaventem, the federal police carried out a risk assessment. "After investigation by the federal police, no additional measures proved necessary," the airport's press office said, as reported by VRT NWS. No suspicious items were found aboard, and police are investigating a possible connection to earlier incidents.

Belgium was struck a second time the same day. A Ryanair flight from Charleroi to Pescara, Italy, was disrupted after a note bearing the word "bom" ("bomb") was found in the aircraft toilets at 13:13 local time. The alert was declared a false alarm and the flight later departed for Italy.

## A continent-wide disruption

The threats rippled across borders throughout the day. A second LOT flight, bound for Paris, was stopped during taxiing and searched, but no suspicious package was found. An Airbus A320 operated by Aer Lingus en route from Dublin to Rome was diverted to a French military air base at Evreux, roughly 100 kilometres west of Paris, where French air force fighter jets scrambled to intercept and escort it. All 157 occupants disembarked safely, and Aer Lingus described the move as "a security precaution."

In Italy, a Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome was escorted by Italian air force fighter jets into Rome's Fiumicino airport, where nothing unusual was found. Flights from Thessaloniki and Porto received threats and landed at Milan, while a Wizz Air service from Milan to Warsaw was diverted to Vienna.

Romania's intelligence agency reported two additional planes received threats, including a KLM flight to Tel Aviv with a stopover in the Romanian capital; earlier, a Wizz Air flight from Milan to Bucharest landed safely. In Bulgaria, three Ryanair flights received threats, two from Copenhagen and Madrid landed at Sofia as a precaution and a third from London diverted to Plovdiv, Bulgaria's transport ministry confirmed. Czech police confirmed four aircraft en route to Prague received false threats, and in Austria a flight to Warsaw landed in Vienna after a letter to Polish authorities contained an abstract threat.

According to the [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cmpqg08nz3pqo), flights to Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, France, Italy, Poland and Romania were affected, and the UK's Heathrow and Birmingham airports also received threats assessed as not credible.

## The Male State claim

An anti-feminist group calling itself "The Male State", or "Fighters of the Male State", claimed responsibility for some of the threats, emailing bomb warnings to a logistics company working with Rome Fiumicino Airport and naming two Rome-bound flights, one from Helsinki and one from Dublin. An airport spokesperson confirmed the authenticity of a screenshot of the email.

The message, in Italian, read: "You're all complicit in a criminal plot, we want to annihilate you for this... We, the fighters of the Male State, with our actions will leave a mark on history. Your feminist regime will never forget it." It also threatened to "turn the skies red."

According to the BBC, the group name likely refers to the Russian organisation Muzhskoi Legion ("Male Legion"), an ultra-right group so extreme it was banned by a Moscow court in 2021, yet it retained tens of thousands of online followers and cheered on Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Italian authorities had not commented on the email at the time of reporting.

Crucially, the Male State claim covers only a subset of the threats. No authority has confirmed that a single actor is behind the entire wave, and the Brussels-bound LOT flight has not been publicly attributed to any specific group.

## Official response and the hybrid-threat question

EU Transport Commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas said it was "too early to draw conclusions on whether the reported incidents are linked," adding that national investigations remain ongoing. He said an EU-level information-sharing mechanism on aviation security had been activated, but that "we don't have information yet whether this is a cyberattack." UK Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander described the incidents as "malicious hoax threats" and said the government was taking them seriously.

Aviation security experts urged caution about drawing firm conclusions. Philip Baum, a professor of aviation security at the University of Coventry, told the BBC that the industry faces bomb threats daily but that he had been surprised by this week's incidents, noting a flight is diverted "only when the threat is credible." Marco Chan of Kingston University said the threats were "most likely a hoax, but the system is right to treat it as real," and that some experts view them as "possibly part of a pattern of cheap, deniable disruption," though he cautioned that "three days of incidents is too small a sample to separate coordination from contagion."

## What's next

The hoaxes fit a pattern of heightened hybrid-threat activity against Europe, alongside cyber incidents and drone incursions that authorities have warned about in recent months. The central unanswered questions are who is orchestrating the campaign, whether the 3 November and 5 November waves share a common source, and whether the Russia-linked Male State claim is genuine, a component of a broader effort, or a false flag.

For now, the practical toll is clear: hundreds of passengers delayed, military escorts deployed, airports briefly disrupted and a fresh test of European aviation-security coordination. As the EU's information-sharing mechanism stays active, the coming days are likely to bring either clarification or more threats.
