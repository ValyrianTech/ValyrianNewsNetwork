# SpaceX Catches Starship From Orbit in Historic First

SpaceX achieved a landmark in reusable rocket technology on Saturday, catching its Starship upper-stage spacecraft in midair with the giant mechanical arms of its launch tower after the vehicle returned from a more than 22-hour trip in orbit. It was the first time a Starship — or any spacecraft of its class — was recovered this way after flying back from space, though the triumph was partly shadowed by the explosion of the rocket's first-stage booster.

The 407-foot (124-meter) Starship, the world's largest and most powerful rocket, lifted off late Friday afternoon from SpaceX's Starbase facility in Texas, near the U.S.-Mexican border, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/spacex-elon-musk-starship-rocket-df394c1b5a0f85e11194f9ff7db9ee6b). The upper stage separated cleanly and soared into orbit, releasing 26 advanced Starlink V3 satellites, while the Super Heavy booster malfunctioned and exploded over the Gulf of Mexico.

## A 22-Hour Journey Comes Full Circle

Starship circled the globe at an altitude of 165 miles (265 kilometers), completing 14 laps of Earth — far exceeding the one to two orbits of its previous mission. It was SpaceX's 15th flight of a full-scale Starship since 2023 and only its second trip to orbit. On Saturday, the stainless-steel, bullet-shaped spacecraft descended and lowered itself upright into the grasp of the tower's "chopstick" arms above Launch Pad 2.

"Can you believe it?" SpaceX commentator Dan Huot shouted as the craft was caught. "Look at her! Baby, she's back!"

Calling it an "epic day," CEO Elon Musk later ascended the launch tower to take up-close video, and even hugged the spacecraft. "Looks great," he reported via X. SpaceX said the catch demonstrates Starship's full reusability, which is crucial for the rapid and low-cost recycling of the vehicle. The company reported that everything on the spacecraft, including its vital heat shield, appeared to work well.

## The Booster Misses Its Mark

The mission had been envisioned as a historic "double catch," in which both stages of the rocket would return intact to the launch site. Those ambitions were dashed Friday when the booster veered off course minutes after takeoff and self-destructed over the Gulf, as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/nov/14/spacex-starship-double-catch) reported. Wreckage remained in the Gulf, cordoned off by buoys.

SpaceX spokesperson Kate Tice said the booster's engines stopped firing too soon on the way down, and its guidance system sensed it was out of position and automatically initiated the watery detour and self-destruction. Engineers are still investigating the cause. "It was doing what it was supposed to do," Huot said. "Basically, it detected that the booster was not in the place it was supposed to be to proceed with a catch." Musk noted the booster was also "slightly low on fuel." Despite the setback, SpaceX had already made three previous successful booster catches, validating the technique.

## Why It Matters

The success carries weight beyond SpaceX's own ambitions. NASA is paying billions of dollars for Starship to land Artemis astronauts on the Moon later this decade and help build a permanent lunar base. "It was almost magic," NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said via X. "Making more believers by the day … one step closer to" — followed by emojis of the Moon and the U.S. flag.

Musk's goals are grander still: he wants to launch a fleet of Starships to build a city on Mars. Reusability is central to that vision. Musk has said he wants Starship to land and take off much like a commercial airplane, dramatically cutting the cost of space travel, and he has outlined plans to ramp test flights up to a weekly, then twice-weekly cadence.

## What's Next

SpaceX expects to begin launching Starships from Florida's Kennedy Space Center within the next few months — the starting point for NASA's next Artemis mission. Artemis III will skip the Moon and remain in orbit around Earth for lunar lander practice, with four astronauts attempting to dock their capsule with both SpaceX's Starship and Blue Origin's Blue Moon. The two companies will take turns delivering crews to the lunar surface. NASA is targeting as soon as 2028 for Artemis IV, which would be humanity's first Moon landing since the Apollo era more than half a century ago.

For now, the catch stands as a proof point: the upper stage of the world's biggest rocket can fly to orbit, deploy a payload, and come home to be caught by the arms that sent it on its way. Whether that becomes routine — at a weekly cadence, with both halves of the rocket returning intact — is the next test.
