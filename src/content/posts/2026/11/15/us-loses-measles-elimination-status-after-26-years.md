# US Loses Measles Elimination Status After 26 Years

The United States has lost its measles elimination status after holding the distinction for a quarter century, following a determination by an independent expert commission that reports to the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) that the virus is now continuously transmitting within the country, [CNN reported](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/13/health/us-measles-elimination-status). The decision, widely anticipated after years of declining vaccination rates and record case counts, was first reported by The Wall Street Journal.

Measles had been considered "eliminated" in the US since 2000, meaning there was no continuous spread of the disease within the country and new cases appeared only when someone contracted it abroad and returned home. The committee determined that the US no longer meets that standard.

## What Elimination Status Means

Elimination is not the same as eradication. A country is considered to have eliminated measles when there has been interrupted endemic transmission for 12 months or more, in the presence of high-quality surveillance. Because measles still circulates globally, occasional imported outbreaks are permitted, provided they fizzle out within a year through herd immunity.

The international committee that monitors elimination status classifies an infection as endemic, or regularly occurring, when infections caused by the same strain are passed from person to person for at least a year within the same country. To make that judgment, panel members examine the genetic code of viruses sampled from patients.

The decisive evidence came from Utah, where scientists at the state's public health laboratory published a preprint study showing the same measles strain had circulated for more than a year, accounting for more than 95% of cases there. It traveled from the southwestern part of the state to central Utah, causing more than 600 cases between June 2025 and June 2026. Sustained outbreaks also occurred in South Carolina, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and New York, among other states.

Dr. Angela Rassmussen, a virologist at the University of Saskatchewan and editor-in-chief of the journal Vaccine, told CNN that the Utah data alone would have been enough to make the case.

## A Record Surge in Cases

The determination caps two years of escalating measles activity. More than 5,400 measles cases have been reported in the US this year, the highest number since 1991, according to [the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/measles/data-research/index.html). Most are linked to the 43 new outbreaks reported this year, while more than 1,000 cases are tied to outbreaks that began last year.

Nearly all of this year's cases have been in people who had not received the measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) vaccine or whose vaccination status is unknown. People have been hospitalized in roughly 1 in 9 reported cases, and at least 10 people have died after catching measles, according to state reports. The CDC's official dashboard currently lists six deaths for 2026 and three for 2025, but notes that the figure is subject to change and that the agency is working with states to standardize how measles deaths are counted.

"Measles elimination is a vital sign for our public health system. It shows cracks in our foundation," said Dr. Demetre Daskalakis, former director of the National Center for Immunization and Respiratory Diseases at the CDC. "Losing elimination status means that we are less prepared as a nation to address infectious and noninfectious disease challenges."

## 'Measles Is Back'

The declaration is a humbling moment for a nation that played a leading role in ridding the world of smallpox and worked for decades to drive measles offshore. Public health experts framed the loss as the reversal of a generation of progress.

"This is just so devastating and so sad that we are where we are today," said Dr. Nuwan Gunawardhana, an infectious disease epidemiologist at Columbia University Irvine Medical Center. "This just kind of points towards the direction of really reversing a generation of public health progress."

Dr. Peter Hotez, co-director of the Center for Vaccine Development at Texas Children's Hospital, called the loss "incredibly embarrassing" but stressed a more fundamental concern. "Basically, measles is back," he said. "Whether or not we've officially lost our elimination status to me is much less important than, basically, measles is here. It's pervasive, and vaccination rates are down across the country, and I don't see us getting back to normal anytime soon."

The MMR vaccine remains highly effective. Two doses cut an exposed person's chances of getting sick by about 97% compared with someone who is unvaccinated, and herd immunity requires coverage of at least 95%. But national two-dose MMR coverage among kindergartners fell from 95.2% in the 2019-20 school year to 92.4% in 2025-26, according to the CDC, leaving roughly 280,000 kindergartners at risk. Only a handful of states report coverage at or above the 95% target.

Measles is among the most contagious viruses known to science. A single infected person typically spreads it to 12 to 18 others, and the virus is airborne and can linger in a room for hours. Beyond fever and rash, measles can cause "immune amnesia," leaving patients vulnerable to secondary infections, and in rare cases a progressive, fatal neurological condition called subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, or SSPE. Pregnant women who catch measles can pass it to their babies in the womb.

## Could the US Regain Its Status?

Regaining elimination status is possible but difficult. Current outbreaks must end, and future outbreaks must stop spreading within a year. With outbreaks expanding in several states, that moment is not on the horizon.

"That's not easy, particularly in the current environment with diminished public trust in vaccines, the increasing nonmedical exemptions that we're seeing in schools, and declines in measles, mumps and rubella vaccination among our kindergarten students, and the undermining of vaccine policy," said Dr. William Moss, a measles expert at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health. "So it's not going to be a trivial task for the United States to regain its measles elimination status."

The US is not alone in backsliding. In 2016, PAHO and the World Health Organization declared the 35 countries of the Americas free of ongoing measles transmission. That regional designation was lost last year when Canada's elimination status ended after a lengthy outbreak spanning several provinces. Mexico, which has had several large outbreaks of its own, is also in danger of losing its status.

The loss also carries a practical cost. Beyond the human toll of hospitalizations and deaths, the past two years of outbreaks have imposed an estimated $125 million in medical care, public health response and productivity losses, according to a Johns Hopkins University estimate. Public health experts warn that a future with endemic measles means recurring pressure on already strained health systems and difficult decisions about closing schools and day-care centers.

For now, the milestone stands as a stark marker: a disease the US once pushed to the margins is, by the technical definition, endemic again.
