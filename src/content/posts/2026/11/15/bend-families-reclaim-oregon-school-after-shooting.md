# Bend Families Reclaim Oregon School After Four Killed

BEND, Oregon — For 27 days, the doors of Silver Rail Elementary stood shuttered. When they finally reopened on November 12, the families of the children who once filled its halls had made their choice clear: they would not let the campus become a monument to violence. According to [CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/15/us/bend-oregon-silver-rail-elementary-return), more than 86% of Silver Rail families asked to return to the building where a 16-year-old gunman shot through the glass and killed three special-education students and a teacher on October 15.

On the first full day back, 431 of the school's 447 students walked through the doors. Sixteen did not come back — thirteen families chose to leave, and three children were killed.

## A Choice Between Rebuilding and Reclaiming

After mass shootings at schools, American communities have typically faced the same wrenching question. In Newtown, Connecticut, and Parkland, Florida, officials chose to tear down the buildings where children died and start anew. Bend took the other path.

District leadership was genuinely uncertain how families would respond, CNN reported. But the survey results were decisive.

"The resolve that came through in the survey was palpable," said Steven Cook, superintendent of Bend-La Pine Schools. "Meaningful."

As CNN put it, the families "would not allow the school grounds to become an ugly stain of gun violence on Bend. They would reclaim the school, on their own terms." Students were greeted by the school's motto on the marquee: "Kindness matters."

## The Day Everything Changed

On October 15 — a picture day — the shooter, a former special-education student at Silver Rail, forced his way in by shooting locked doors and entered the special-education classroom just left of the entrance. He fired as many as 15 rounds. The dead included Rhoam Summer, age 5 — the youngest person ever killed in a deliberate mass attack at a U.S. school — along with an 8-year-old, an 11-year-old and 25-year-old teacher Megan Lambert. A 7-year-old girl and a 54-year-old teacher were wounded.

A teacher threw her cellphone at the attacker and tackled him; a bystander father, Kaylub Giamanco, followed the suspect into the school and fatally shot him. The entire attack lasted roughly three minutes. It was the 36th K-12 school shooting in the United States in 2026, according to CNN's analysis of gun-violence databases.

## Tough Conversations and Heavy Grief

Returning was not simple, even for families who wanted to. Parents wrestled with how to explain violence and death to children too young to grasp it.

Cearrow Young's kindergartener and third grader were excited to go back, but as the first day neared, she grew anxious. After Rhoam — the same age as her daughter — was killed, she knew she had to talk to her children about what happened.

"I don't know that my 5-year-old was even aware that a 5-year-old could pass away," Young said. After the first day back, her daughter told her something she could not shake: "She said she didn't want to be dead when she was older."

Nicole Ybarrondo had been undecided about sending her two children back. Her 9-year-old wanted to return; her 6-year-old son at first refused, telling her the school had "too many fire drills. And kids die at fire drills." By the end of the first week, he was the happier one to be back.

"It's really heavy knowing that we get to go back, and other people don't get to walk through those doors again," Ybarrondo said. She has since started a nonprofit for Silver Rail children, working with programs like Healing Reins, an equine-therapy center that welcomed students after the tragedy.

## A Mural Where a Classroom Stood

Inside the school, the special-education classroom where four lives were taken has been blocked off. Its facade was extended to blend into the hallway and replaced with a mural by a local artist depicting the Three Sisters, the volcanic peaks that rise over Central Oregon.

Cook said the art is an important reminder "that it is a different day. And that things have changed."

The reopening followed repairs and safety-code compliance, with staff returning days earlier to prepare classrooms, [OPB reported](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/11/02/silver-rail-elementary-school-shooting-oregon-bend-crime/).

## Security and Funding Questions Linger

Returning families did not set aside their concerns. They asked how the district would keep their children safe, how they would access mental health services, and what was being done to fortify the campus.

The shooter had bypassed multiple security measures — secure vestibules, ID checks, cameras and upgraded locks — by shooting through locked doors. Debates over armed officers at every school, metal detectors and other hardening measures are ongoing. Cook has said nothing is off the table but that decisions rest with the school board.

The attack has also thrust special-education funding into the spotlight. Oregon caps special-education funding at 11% of the total student population even when more students qualify, and Bend-La Pine faces roughly a $9 million gap to support its highest-needs students, [OPB reported](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/10/27/silver-rail-shooting-survey-86-percent-families-want-to-return/). The shooting targeted a classroom serving children with intensive behavioral and social-skills challenges — the same program the shooter had once attended.

## What Comes Next

For Cook, who has led the district for six years, the reopening marked only the first step in a long recovery. He noted the district's obligation to the youngest students, who have more than a decade of schooling ahead.

"We're going to ride this with you," he said.

Bend's decision to reopen rather than rebuild makes it a notable case study in how communities grieve and recover. What remains unresolved is how the district will fund the mental health supports families say they need, how it will close the security gaps the attack exposed, and whether children like Callie Young — who now understand that a 5-year-old can die — will find the stability their parents say has become so elusive.

"It feels like nothing is predictable these days," Ybarrondo said.
