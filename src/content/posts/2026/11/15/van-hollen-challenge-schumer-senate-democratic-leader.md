# Van Hollen to Challenge Schumer for Senate Democratic Leader

Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland announced Thursday that he will run for Senate majority leader, directly challenging Sen. Chuck Schumer of New York for control of the Democratic caucus — and, with it, the direction of the party just days after it swept the 2026 midterm elections, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/van-hollen-schumer-senate-democratic-leader-6d74c189cb6c065c9a810a1cf1a53436). The contest will be settled by secret ballot at a closed-door caucus meeting on Dec. 1, when Democrats formally take the majority.

Van Hollen framed his bid not as a personal rebuke of Schumer but as a response to the mandate voters delivered. "The American people called for change," he said in a statement. "It's now on us to meet this moment, and to do that we need new leadership. This is not about any one person — it's about answering the call of the American people. That is why I'm running for Senate Majority Leader."

## A Majority in Hand, a Fight Over Its Use

Democrats captured their largest Senate and House majorities in years in the Nov. 4 midterms, with 55 Senate seats and a possible 56th depending on the final count in Alaska, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/12/nx-s1-6039357/chris-van-hollen-chuck-schumer-senate-majority-leader). That triumph has become the central argument in the leadership fight: Schumer's allies say the results vindicate his stewardship, while Van Hollen and progressive groups argue the wave belonged to voters, not to the incumbent leader.

The race exposes divisions that have been building since early 2025, when Schumer advanced a Republican-backed government funding measure rather than force a shutdown. The compromise infuriated House Democrats who had rejected the deal and progressive organizations that concluded he was no longer fit to lead. A second wave of calls for his resignation followed later that year, when dissident Senate Democrats ended a record-long shutdown without winning Republican concessions on healthcare — a deal widely believed to have had Schumer's backing, [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/13/democrats-midterms-senate-leader).

As Schumer's standing with the base eroded, Van Hollen's rose. The 67-year-old, elected to the Senate in 2016 after more than a decade in the House, drew national attention in 2025 for lobbying for the release of Kilmar Abrego Garcia after he was wrongfully deported to an El Salvador prison. He has also become one of the party's most assertive critics of Israel policy, voting against arms transfers and co-releasing a report accusing Israel of planning to ethnically cleanse Gaza.

## The Whip Count

Schumer has moved quickly to project confidence. "I welcome any discussion about the future of our caucus and the important work ahead," he wrote on X. "I'm proud of what we've accomplished together—55 seats and counting is an amazing result—and I have the votes to continue serving as Democratic Leader."

[Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/13/schumer-van-hollen-senate-majority-leader) noted that the nearly three-week window before the Dec. 1 vote gives grassroots activists time to pressure senators — a dynamic that could shift a secret ballot that public endorsements may not reflect. Van Hollen, who called himself the "underdog," claimed support from "more than 10" members who will vote, though a whip count reported by [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/13/democrats-schumer-van-hollen-senate-leader) showed Schumer ahead with public backing from 14 senators, including himself, to Van Hollen's 5.

Among those backing Van Hollen are independent Sen. Bernie Sanders, who caucuses with Democrats, and incoming senators Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota. El-Sayed told NPR that while he "deeply appreciate[s] Senator Schumer," a return to business as usual risks squandering the party's mandate. Others — including Michigan's Elissa Slotkin, who said only "I am seeking change" — want a new leader but have not named a preference, leaving the race genuinely uncertain.

## Analysis: A Referendum on How to Confront Trump

The contest is less a verdict on Schumer's resume than a debate over the party's post-Trump identity. Schumer's defenders credit him with shepherding major legislation through a razor-thin caucus and point to the midterms as proof of effectiveness. "Why would you fire the manager when you just won the World Series?" asked Maine independent Sen. Angus King, who endorsed Schumer.

Van Hollen counters that the sweep was voters-driven and that closing what he calls the "trust deficit" requires fresh leadership rather than a "return to the status quo." Progressive groups echo that framing. "Our members are tired of politicians content with incrementalism," said Joel Payne, executive director of MoveOn. "They want bold, creative leadership ... without delay or compromise."

The outcome will also preview how the new majority wields power — whether it confronts Trump aggressively or governs pragmatically, and how it handles the party's internal fault line over Israel aid. Because the vote is secret, declared endorsements may not predict the result, keeping the race tighter than Schumer's public lead suggests.

## What to Watch

The decisive moment arrives Dec. 1, when Senate Democrats cast their secret ballots. The undecided senators — Slotkin, Adam Hamilton of Kansas and Juliana Stratton of Illinois — say they want change but have not committed, and their choices could tip the balance. Van Hollen has said he is not interested in a 2028 presidential run, but the leadership result will shape the coalition and the message the party carries into the next Congress and beyond.
