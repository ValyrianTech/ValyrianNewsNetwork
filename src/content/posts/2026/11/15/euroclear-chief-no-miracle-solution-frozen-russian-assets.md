# Euroclear Chief: No Miracle Solution on Russian Assets

Nearly five years after the EU froze roughly €210 billion in Russian central bank reserves, the head of the Brussels clearing house holding most of that money has said the central question remains unanswered: how to use the assets without creating new risks for Europe's financial system. In an interview with the Belgian daily [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/782489/article/2026-11-13/la-patronne-deuroclear-au-soir-sur-les-actifs-russes-il-ny-toujours-pas-de) published on 13 November, Valérie Urbain, chief executive of Euroclear, reiterated that there is still no "miracle solution" for the roughly €185 billion sitting on her institution's balance sheet — and that any scheme to transfer or mobilise those funds would eliminate none of the dangers her company, the eurozone and Belgium face.

"The best use of the Russian assets is to integrate them into peace negotiations," Urbain said, framing the frozen reserves less as a ready source of cash for Kyiv than as leverage in the search for an end to the war.

## A Clearing House at the Centre of a Geopolitical Storm

Euroclear is not an ordinary bank. As Europe's largest central securities depository, it settles more than the entire global gross domestic product each month and holds about €42.5 trillion in assets under custody, according to figures cited by economist Olena Havrylchyk in a [Kyiv Independent](https://kyivindependent.com/europes-plan-to-fund-ukraine-is-being-blocked-by-one-company-euroclear/) opinion piece. Euroclear Holding accounts for roughly 49% of all European securities in custody and occupies a dominant position in France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Sweden, Finland, Ireland and the United Kingdom.

That systemic role is precisely why the frozen assets have become such a delicate issue. When Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the EU immobilised about €210 billion in Russian state reserves, and the bulk of it — around €185 billion — landed at Euroclear in Brussels. Ever since, European capitals have wrestled with whether, and how, to turn that money into support for Ukraine without destabilising a financial utility on which the continent's markets depend.

## The Long, Unfinished Debate

The search for a workable mechanism has produced a string of partial solutions and stalled proposals. In 2024, the G7 agreed a $50 billion "Extraordinary Revenue Acceleration" (ERA) loan, serviced by windfall revenues generated by the immobilised assets — most of which have now been disbursed to Ukraine, as analysts Vladyslav Rashkovan and Gregory P. Wilson recount in a [Carnegie Europe](https://carnegieendowment.org/europe/posts/2026/10/turning-russian-assets-into-financing-for-ukraine-now) commentary.

The most ambitious push came in late 2025, when the EU advanced a so-called "reparations loan" of up to about €140 billion backed by the frozen assets. It collapsed in December 2025, with Belgium — where Euroclear is headquartered and where the legal and financial exposure would concentrate — leading the resistance. Reporting at the time captured the depth of that opposition: Prime Minister Bart De Wever warned that "the reparation loan is sailing in uncharted waters" and insisted that if Europe were to take the risk, "we jump all together," a position [reported](https://www.euractiv.com/news/belgium-still-opposed-to-e210-billion-ukraine-loan-says-de-wever/) as leaving the EU's flagship plan torpedoed.

What emerged instead was a compromise: a €90 billion "Ukraine Support Loan" (USL), under which EU leaders also agreed to immobilise the Russian assets indefinitely, removing the six-month unanimity renewal that had left the freeze vulnerable to a single member's veto. The first USL tranche reached Kyiv in June 2026, and about €15.7 billion had been disbursed by early October, according to the Carnegie analysis.

## Why Urbain Says the Risks Remain

The Euroclear chief's argument is not that the assets should be returned to Moscow. It is that relocating or repurposing them — whether by shifting them to the EU or into a new legal vehicle — would not make the underlying problems disappear. Transferring the funds would eliminate neither the bankruptcy risk for Euroclear, nor the risk of damaging the eurozone's reputation, nor the risk of destabilising Belgium and its taxpayers, she told Le Soir.

Her warning echoes a letter she sent to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President António Costa in late 2025, in which she argued that a forced scheme would raise borrowing costs across the bloc. Forcing Euroclear to invest in a "zero-interest" instrument, she cautioned, would be seen by Russia as confiscation and invite retaliation and legal challenges for which the company should be covered.

Critics argue those fears are overstated. Havrylchyk, a professor of economics at the University of Paris 1 Panthéon-Sorbonne, contends that the European Commission's proposal included explicit state guarantees and that Euroclear's systemic importance means European authorities would never allow it to fail. She points to a precedent that she says undercuts Belgium's position: in March 2025, Euroclear obtained authorisation from Belgian authorities to use €3 billion of Russian assets to compensate clients whose holdings had been seized in Russia — a step taken, she notes, without provoking alarm about financial stability.

## Pressure Beyond the Negotiating Table

The debate has also unfolded against a backdrop of intimidation. As [Novaya Gazeta Europe](https://novayagazeta.eu/en/articles/2025/12/18/russian-intelligence-targets-belgian-politicians-and-finance-bosses-over-potential-use-of-frozen-assets-en-news) reported, European intelligence agencies believe Russian military intelligence has targeted Belgian politicians and senior Euroclear executives — including Urbain — in a campaign aimed at dissuading the EU from using the assets. Euroclear has said any potential threats are treated with the utmost priority and investigated deeply, often with the support of authorities. The pressure has been physical as well as political: since 2024, the company's top management has been under personal security.

## The Search for a Way Forward

With the reparations loan stalled, attention has turned to mechanisms that could raise money for Ukraine without formally touching the Euroclear accounts. Rashkovan and Wilson propose two: an "ERA 2.0" loan mobilising €30–50 billion through the existing revenue-sharing infrastructure, and a "USL 2.0" that would raise the Ukraine Support Loan ceiling to €135–150 billion, unlocking a further €45–60 billion. Both are designed so that repayment hinges on future Russian reparations rather than on European taxpayers.

Other ideas circulate as well, including quarantining the Russian assets in a "bad bank" or transferring them to a legal entity outside Belgium — options that proponents say could insulate Euroclear while still putting the money to use. Each faces the same obstacle that has bedevilled the file from the start: the absence of a consensus on whether mobilising sovereign reserves crosses the line into confiscation under international law.

## What to Watch

Urbain's intervention is a reminder that the technical workarounds have not dissolved the political and legal deadlock at the heart of the issue. With Ukraine's financing needs pressing into 2027 and European budgets under strain, the pressure to find a solution will only grow. But as the Euroclear chief's "bargaining chip" framing suggests, the choice may ultimately be less about engineering than about strategy: whether Europe treats the frozen billions as a fund to be spent now, or as leverage to be held until peace talks put them on the table.

For now, the answer to the question that has hung over Brussels for nearly five years remains the same. There is still no miracle solution — only a set of hard trade-offs that Europe has yet to resolve.
