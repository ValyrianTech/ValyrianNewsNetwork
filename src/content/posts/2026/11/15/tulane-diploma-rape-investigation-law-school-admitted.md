# Tulane Let Him Graduate Mid-Rape Inquiry; Law School Got Him

Tulane University assured a student that the classmate she accused of rape would not be allowed to graduate while the school investigated. Months later, he had a diploma — and, eventually, a seat at a Virginia law school. That sequence, detailed in a new [Washington Post investigation](https://www.washingtonpost.com/investigations/2026/11/15/tulane-university-gave-tate-weston-diploma-while-investigating-him-rape/), has renewed scrutiny of how colleges handle sexual misconduct cases and whether findings of responsibility travel with students between institutions.

The accused student, Tate Weston, 25, of Austin, Texas, pleaded guilty Thursday to second-degree battery, a felony, and misdemeanor sexual battery under a deal with New Orleans prosecutors. He was sentenced to three years' probation and will not be required to register as a sex offender. The accuser, Cameron Lewis, now 26 and a graduate student at Tulane, agreed to be named in the Post's account.

## How the case unfolded

Lewis told the Post that Weston pinned her to her bed, choked her and raped her at her off-campus New Orleans residence in September 2021. She called 911 within minutes of his departure and underwent a sexual assault forensic exam, during which a nurse documented small red marks on her neck.

Because the alleged conduct occurred off campus, Tulane handled the matter not as a Title IX case but as a "major matters investigation." In April 2022, at Weston's request, the university deferred its inquiry to a parallel New Orleans Police Department investigation. He was barred from campus and placed on interim suspension, but the Post reported that Tulane told Lewis he "won't be able to graduate" while that suspension stood.

That assurance did not hold. That summer, Weston earned credits at Texas State University near Austin and transferred them back to Tulane, which granted him a diploma. In October 2022, a Tulane conduct official called Lewis to tell her he had graduated.

## A finding of responsibility — after the degree

Only in April 2023 — roughly 18 months after the alleged assault — did Tulane investigator Jacqueline Barber conclude that Lewis's allegations were "credible enough by far" under the preponderance-of-evidence standard. Weston was found responsible and sanctioned with expulsion, the most severe penalty available. Barber noted that she lacked the power to revoke a degree obtained through what the Post described as "procedural gamesmanship." Weston's appeal was denied, and Tulane formally separated him from the university in May 2023, with the expulsion noted on his transcript.

Even so, Washington and Lee University's law school admitted Weston in April 2024. A school spokesman, Peter Jetton, told the Post that admissions staff were aware of the Tulane finding at the time but conducted no "additional inquiry." In November 2024, Lewis emailed the law school's dean and Title IX coordinator, warning that Weston "may pose a potential threat to the safety of other students" and attaching Tulane's findings. An associate dean met with Weston, but the school took no apparent action at the time.

Weston's attorneys, Sarah Chervinsky and Amelia Schmidt, said he disclosed the Tulane matter in detail in his application, was found responsible and expelled, and "promptly and repeatedly updated law school leadership" as the criminal case developed. They said he "has always adamantly maintained that he is innocent of rape" and that the plea shows he "is not, and was never, the violent rapist portrayed in reports."

## The criminal case, resolved after years

Progress in the criminal matter was slow. Lewis's rape kit was not tested until early 2025, when a DNA warrant matched Weston. He was arrested in December 2025 and later indicted on second-degree rape charges before pleading guilty to the lesser counts this week.

In an impact statement in court, Lewis described the toll: "After you raped me, I wished at times to forgo my education, to forgo my career and to end my life." She said the institutions she turned to — including the police and Tulane — "possessed the power to help me escape what you did. Instead, they left me trapped in it." After the hearing, she said she hoped Tulane's administrators "can find accountability, or that accountability will find them."

## A pattern beyond one campus

The Post frames Weston's path through three universities in three states as emblematic of wider gaps. The case echoes a separate controversy this fall involving Cornell University, where a student expelled over sexual assault allegations later transferred to a South Florida school. As the Post [reported in October](https://www.washingtonpost.com/nation/2026/10/15/one-cornell-students-accused-rape-transferred-florida-school/), officials at Nova Southeastern University said they were unaware of the allegations at the time of admission, raising the question of whether disciplinary information is reliably shared between institutions.

Experts say the problem lies in the discretion schools reserve for themselves. "Schools are trying to make everyone happy and not actually just do the right thing," Laura Dunn, a New York attorney who specializes in sexual violence cases, told the Post. "That discretion is almost always where the vast majority of horrible errors are made." Aubrey Harris, Lewis's former attorney, put it more simply: "It is heartbreaking when the institutions people turn to for help fail to show up for them."

## What's next

Tulane spokesman Michael Strecker declined to explain why the university awarded Weston a diploma, citing confidentiality laws, but said the school now prohibits students from graduating while under an investigation that could result in expulsion. Washington and Lee placed Weston on leave and hired a law firm to review its admissions and "character and fitness" practices after the Post began asking questions; its president and board of trustees were not told of his misconduct finding or arrest until late October.

The case leaves open several questions: whether Tulane's new graduation policy will apply retroactively or prompt a review of its degree-revocation rules, whether Weston's felony conviction affects his eligibility for bar admission, and whether Congress will revisit legislation — first proposed a decade ago — that would require colleges to disclose findings of responsibility to schools where students seek to enroll. For Lewis, the resolution arrived years after the assurances she was given, and only after the institutions that investigated the case had, in her words, left her to carry it alone.
