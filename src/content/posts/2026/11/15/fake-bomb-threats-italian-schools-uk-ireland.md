# Fake Bomb Threats Hit 6,300 Italian Schools, UK and Ireland

About 6,300 of Italy's 7,476 state schools received an identical anonymous email on Wednesday warning that a bomb had been planted on their premises, triggering evacuations across the country and prompting similar hoax alerts at hundreds of schools in Scotland, Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland. No explosive devices were found in any school, and no country has formally attributed the threats to a specific group or state.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/11/bommeldingen-scholen-europa/), the threats were sent just before 10:00 and were initially reported as affecting "more than 300" schools, all of which were evacuated. The education ministry later confirmed that roughly 6,300 schools had in fact received the email, though not all were cleared. In some schools, pupils did not even realise a bomb alert had been issued—they assumed the bell had simply signalled an early break.

## A Threat Signed by a Fictitious Figure

The email was signed by "Mikhail Andropov, leader of the Vilayat of Misoginistan"—the same fictitious Russian name used days earlier in bomb threats against European airlines, airports and media organisations. According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2026/nov/11/bomb-threats-hoax-schools-scotland-northern-ireland), the message included the words "we have come to annihilate you" and reportedly threatened to place a bomb in the engine of Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's car.

The composite persona appears designed to confuse rather than clarify. As Italian analysis noted, "Vilayat-i faqih" echoes the Shia political theory underpinning the Islamic Republic of Iran, while "Misoginistan"—"land of misogynists"—seems to gesture toward the Russian ultranationalist "Male State" group that signed earlier airline threats. The ambiguity may be deliberate: it complicates attribution and invites states to suspect one another.

## Scotland, Northern Ireland and Ireland Also Targeted

The same day, about 1,000 schools in Scotland—from Dumfries in the south to Aberdeenshire and Orkney in the north—received hoax warnings, and at least 300 schools were affected in Northern Ireland. A "generic communication" was also sent to schools in the Republic of Ireland.

Police moved quickly to reassure the public. "Our inquiries have established that there is nothing to suggest that they represent a credible threat or that there is any wider threat to the public," a Police Scotland spokesperson said, adding that there was "no requirement to evacuate schools or keep children away from education based solely on the content of the emails." Anthony McNally, an assistant chief constable at the Police Service of Northern Ireland, said the force took such incidents "very seriously" but considered "the risk to the public as low." Ireland's Garda Síochána assessed the threat as "not credible" and did not call for evacuations.

## A Second Day—and a Deliberate Change of Posture

On Thursday, about 3,778 Italian schools, from Milan to Sicily, reported similar threats. But the government had learned a lesson overnight. [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cr894w453e90o) reported that authorities advised schools that "in the absence of concrete evidence beyond the simple receipt of a mass email indicating a danger, buildings must not be evacuated." The result was stark: only 77 schools evacuated on Thursday, down from the hundreds the previous day. Schools reopened as usual across the country.

Prime Minister Meloni, in a video posted on social media, struck a defiant tone. "To date there is no element indicating a concrete danger," she said. "These messages do not serve to strike, they serve to frighten. Whoever writes them above all wants our fear, the lost days, suspicion, chaos." She added: "We will not allow an anonymous email to decide our children's school calendar."

## Tracing the Origin—and Its Traps

Some Italian officials said Wednesday's email originated from a compromised account at Daye University in Changhua County, Taiwan, reportedly a former student's account. Thursday's email was reportedly sent from the hacked account of a teacher in the Philippines. Investigators have not concluded whether the trails are genuine leads or deliberate redirections.

The speed and breadth of the attack point to a structural vulnerability. Schools in the autonomous regions of Trentino-Alto Adige and Valle d'Aosta were spared—because they do not use the national @istruzione.it email domain. The attack, in other words, exploited a centralised mass-distribution list rather than targeting individual schools.

## A Wider Pattern of Hybrid Warfare

The school hoaxes are the latest front in a broader 2026 campaign of what analysts call "hybrid," "grey-zone" or "sub-threshold" warfare—actions that cause disruption but remain anonymous and deniable, deliberately falling short of triggering NATO's Article 5. Earlier in November, bomb threats disrupted at least 21 flights and several European airports; Irish broadcaster RTÉ and other media were evacuated. Cyberattacks on supermarket refrigeration systems followed, and France blamed Iran for cold-chain attacks. Italy's Deputy Prime Minister Antonio Tajani has blamed pro-Russian hackers for related data leaks, linking them to Italy's support for Ukraine.

Yet attribution remains unresolved. Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi said it was "premature to speak of a common hand" behind the incidents. Defence Minister Guido Crosetto cautioned that the culprits were not necessarily hostile states: "It is not always the work of other countries or hostile entities. There are also attention-seekers and madmen." The Russian "Male State" group, meanwhile, denied any involvement, mocking the suggestion to the BBC Russian Service.

## Political Fallout and a "Democratic Shield"

Meloni convened her intelligence and cybersecurity chiefs on Wednesday and then summoned opposition leaders to Palazzo Chigi the following day, appealing for national unity. According to [ANSA English](https://www.ansa.it/english/news/politics/2026/11/12/meloni-appeals-to-opposition-for-unity-after-false-school-bomb-threats_049b1bd6-9f5d-4d6d-abd7-7c3f981a553b.html), the meeting produced a fragile consensus: opposition parties offered cooperation but refused to write "blank cheques." Democratic Party leader Elly Schlein called for more investment in cybersecurity, while 5-Star leader Giuseppe Conte warned that "the country came to a standstill because of an email. We cannot afford that." Former premier Matteo Renzi was blunter: "Yesterday's alert was something that could have been resolved with a good antivirus program."

The episode has also carried a financial cost—Italian press estimates put the disruption at around €3 million—and stirred public anger. In Rome, parents protested against a headmaster who chose not to evacuate after spotting typos in the threat and judging it fake.

## What to Watch

Italy is reportedly working on technical email "filters" to screen out mass false alerts, alongside clearer evacuation protocols. Scotland's cyber and fraud unit is liaising with UK and overseas partners, and multiple investigations remain open across Italy, the UK and Ireland. But cross-border attribution and prosecution of anonymous email hoaxers remain extraordinarily difficult.

The central unresolved question is who is actually behind the campaign—and whether it is a state-directed operation, an extremist network hiding behind state-like branding, or tech-savvy copycats exploiting a known vulnerability. Officials have warned that more fake threats are likely. "We will see more and more of them," Crosetto said, "because, unfortunately, the mother of fools is always pregnant." For European governments, the challenge is to build resilience without handing the attackers the chaos they seek.
