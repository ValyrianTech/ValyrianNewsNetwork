# Belgium Taps Sewer Heat to Warm Homes as Riothermy Expands

Every day, hot water from showers, washing machines and kitchen sinks disappears down the drain — carrying energy that would otherwise be lost. In Belgium, a growing number of projects is now recapturing that heat from the sewers and pumping it back into buildings. The practice, known as riothermy, has just reached a milestone with the commissioning of the Keerdonk district in Mechelen, the first project in the country to heat a residential complex at scale, [RTBF reports](https://www.rtbf.be/article/recuperer-la-chaleur-des-egouts-pour-chauffer-les-batiments-la-riothermie-se-developpe-en-belgique-11814347).

## What Is Riothermy?

The term derives from the Dutch *riool*, meaning sewer. Riothermy recovers waste heat from wastewater flowing through the sewer network and re-injects it into buildings for heating and domestic hot water. The same system can deliver cooling in summer, when the wastewater is warmer than the building. Because it reuses infrastructure that already exists, it is both a renewable and a remarkably low-disruption energy source.

"It's easy to install because we use infrastructure that already exists," said Maarten Raemdonck, Innovation Manager at Aquafin, Flanders' main wastewater treatment operator, in comments to RTBF.

## How the Keerdonk System Works

A few metres beneath the surface of the Keerdonk district, a heat exchanger has been laid on the floor of a sewer pipe. It takes the form of a long metal tray stretching about 140 metres. Through simple contact with the metal, the wastewater — whose temperature stays between 15 and 20 °C year-round — transfers its heat to a transfer fluid, glycol, contained behind the metal wall. The two liquids never mix.

The warmed fluid rises to the surface to a heat pump, which raises its temperature from around 20 °C to 55 °C for sanitary hot water before distributing it to the apartments above. "Here you see the fluid coming up from the sewers at 20°," explained Menno Janssens, CEO and co-founder of Noven, the company behind the installation, during a tour of a residential building's technical room. "Then the heat pumps do their job, raising the heat from 20° to 55° for sanitary hot water, and then the water goes into the tanks before being distributed to all the homes above us."

Up to 350 apartments and offices will eventually be supplied by the network. When riothermy proves insufficient — during a cold snap, for example — it is topped up by natural underground heat drawn from geothermal (BEO) wells drilled about 150 metres deep beneath the district's main square. Aquafin describes the combination of sewer heat and geothermal at this scale as unique in Europe, with installed capacity of 670 kW.

## A National Resource, Concentrated in Cities

For its backers, Keerdonk demonstrates that Belgium can heat buildings using entirely local, fossil-free energy. "It's the future, in fact," Janssens said. "We must use the energy available on site, for example in the ground and in the sewers, resources that are available everywhere in Belgium, and stop importing energy that comes from elsewhere."

The potential is real but uneven. Riothermy needs a significant flow of wastewater to be profitable, which makes it a natural fit for large cities and collective buildings rather than individual homes. According to [Aquafin's official documentation](https://aquafin.be/nl/wat-we-doen/hergebruik-van-water-en-warmte/warmte-uit-afvalwater-riothermie), feasibility depends on the flow rate and temperature of the wastewater, the location of the sewer, and local heat demand.

Aquafin has nonetheless catalogued the sewers in Flanders that carry enough wastewater to qualify, and has made the map available to cities and communities. "It is renewable energy that, otherwise, is lost," Raemdonck said.

The scale threshold matters. As [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2022/04/01/wat-is-riothermie/) when Aquafin first presented the technology at its Aartselaar headquarters in 2022, the operator looks at scenarios with a heat demand of at least 50 kW — a swimming pool, offices, apartments, or a cluster of around 15 homes. A single family, drawing roughly 3 to 10 kW, is generally not suitable. At Aquafin's own campus, an 80-metre exchanger with 270 kW capacity now serves more than 400 employees and saves 308 tonnes of CO₂ a year.

## Costs, Subsidies and Environmental Gains

Riothermy's economics are comparable to geothermal energy. According to Aquafin, investment costs run between €700 and €1,000 per kW, with annual inspection and maintenance of €5,000 to €10,000. Operating costs are much lower than those of an air-to-air heat pump, because less energy is required to extract heat from wastewater than from cold outside air.

The environmental case is strong: the technology is 100% renewable and emits more than 75% less CO₂ than fossil fuels. Where the exchanger pump and heat pump run on solar power, the installation can be fully CO₂-neutral. The Flemish Energy and Climate Agency (VEKA) recognises riothermy as a green heat source and offers subsidies of up to 65% for eligible projects.

Space is another advantage. No additional footprint is needed on the property itself; the exchanger sits in the existing sewer beneath the road, and only supply and return pipes to the building's heat pump must be added.

## From Pilot to Rollout

Keerdonk is the culmination of more than a decade of development. Aquafin ran two trial projects before presenting its first full-scale installation at its renovated headquarters in Aartselaar in April 2022. Since then, projects have multiplied across the country. In Brussels, the water utility VIVAQUA has installed riothermy at Uccle's municipal administrative centre, where exchangers supply up to 25% of the building's heating and cooling needs, and has equipped the Brucity administrative centre for cooling via chilled ceilings fed by sewer water.

In Flanders, Aquafin acts as a neutral facilitator. Through its Aquamarkt platform, it maps technical heat potential, publishes open calls so that public and private players can compete fairly, and allocates potential to the project with the greatest societal value. Once a project is awarded, Aquafin carries out the works in the public domain — the exchanger and pipes to the property boundary — and handles maintenance over the installation's lifetime. The operator's stated ambition is for riothermy to be possible in every Flemish city and municipality.

## Limits and What to Watch

The technology is not a universal fix. Only 2 to 3 °C of heat are extracted per pass, and over-extraction risks harming the biological wastewater treatment downstream. "We must ensure we don't extract too much heat from the wastewater, otherwise the biological water treatment at the end of the chain is endangered," Raemdonck warned in 2022. Deployment is also most advanced in Flanders; Brussels, by RTBF's earlier reporting, has only small sewer portions currently suitable, and major projects there require heavy investment and renovation works.

Riothermy is nonetheless part of a broader shift toward recovering waste heat — alongside data-centre heat, metro-tunnel heat and waste-incinerator heat — as Belgium seeks to curb a heating sector that still runs largely on fossil fuels. For cities weighing how to decarbonise, the question now is how quickly the potential maps translate into awarded tenders and, ultimately, warmer homes powered by what residents once simply flushed away.
