# China Adds 51 Novel Drugs to Insurance in Access Push

China has added 107 medicines to its national medical insurance catalogue, including a record 51 "Class-one" new drugs and — for the first time — a CAR-T cancer therapy that previously cost more than 1 million yuan per treatment. The 2026 catalogue, released at a conference in Suzhou on November 14, takes effect on January 1, 2027, and marks the largest single expansion of patient access to cutting-edge medicines to date, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261114/58248a648683482782ea7008e2a5e865/c.html).

A further 12 drugs were added to a parallel commercial insurance innovative drug catalogue, part of a "dual-catalogue" system now in its second year that aims to widen payment pathways for high-priced therapies.

## What Is in the New Catalogue

The 107 additions span major disease categories: 31 oncology drugs, 24 for chronic diseases, 18 for rare diseases, 11 traditional Chinese patent medicines, 7 anti-infectives and 5 psychiatric drugs.

Several entries fill long-standing gaps in coverage. The catalogue adds a long-acting assisted-reproduction drug, a chronic migraine treatment (fremanezumab), and three non-factor hemophilia therapies that fill a gap in routine adult replacement treatment. Also included are mazdutide injection for adult type 2 diabetes and an edaravone dexborneol sublingual tablet for acute ischemic stroke.

The headline inclusion is rujicabtagene autoleucel injection, the first CAR-T therapy to enter basic medical insurance. It addresses a clinical gap for relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma and follicular lymphoma — conditions for which such treatments previously cost over 1 million yuan, putting them out of reach for many families.

"This adjustment focuses on including innovative drugs with clear clinical benefit that can fill treatment gaps, while also covering urgently needed varieties for major diseases, pediatric use and geriatric chronic disease use," said Huang Xinyu, director of the Medical Service Management Department at the National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA), according to Xinhua.

The commercial catalogue, meanwhile, added a set of global firsts: tofersen injection, the world's first disease-modifying therapy for ALS, and surugacabtagene autoleucel, the world's first CAR-T drug for solid tumors.

## Innovation Pipeline Accelerates

The catalogue expansion reflects a rapid maturing of China's domestic drug pipeline. Yang Ting, director of the Drug Registration Management Department at the National Medical Products Administration, said China has approved 69 Class-one innovative drugs so far in 2026, of which 15 are first-in-class with new mechanisms or targets and two were globally synchronized filings launched first in China.

"Class-one" is China's most innovative registration category — a drug not yet marketed anywhere in the world, with a new structure or mechanism and proven clinical value. The 51 added this round surpass last year's record of 50.

The government frames the spending as "strategic value purchasing." NHSA data show negotiated drug fund expenditure under agreement periods exceeded 120 billion yuan in the first 10 months of 2026 — an average of more than 10 billion yuan per month.

"The strategic value purchase of medical insurance lets people use good drugs sooner, gives companies reasonable returns on R&D investment, and provides stable, sustained feedback to source innovation," said Yu Dechao, founder of Innovent Biologics, per Xinhua.

## The "Last Mile" Problem

Catalogue inclusion is only the first hurdle. Even when innovative drugs are reimbursed, they are often not stocked in hospitals — a bottleneck widely described in China as the "last mile." [CCTV News](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20261115A01ZLI00) reported that innovative drugs reach only about 14% coverage in tertiary hospitals and roughly 38% in key hospitals.

Barriers include slow pharmacy-committee reviews, the "one drug, two specifications" limit that caps how many brands of a given chemical a hospital may stock, and performance metrics that penalize hospitals for high drug costs.

Just days before the catalogue release, the National Health Commission and three other ministries issued a notice requiring hospitals to convene pharmacy committees promptly and add innovative drugs to their formularies. It also removes the two-specification limit for Class-one new drugs in both catalogues and excludes them from certain public-hospital performance monitoring. Beijing went further, allowing negotiated and Class-one drugs to be procured directly upon launch without a selection meeting.

"The dynamic adjustment of the medical insurance drug catalogue achieves a three-way win: safeguarding livelihood, incentivizing R&D, and industrial upgrading," said Wu Jing, party secretary of the School of Pharmaceutical Science and Technology at Tianjin University, according to Xinhua.

## Who Pays?

The deeper structural challenge is payment. According to NHSA figures cited by CCTV, basic medical insurance bears 44% of China's innovative drug sales costs, commercial insurance only 7%, and the remaining 49% falls to patients out of pocket or through other channels.

That gap underlies the push to develop commercial insurance as a supplementary payer through the "commercial insurance explores, basic insurance takes over" model, in which drugs can first enter the commercial catalogue, build real-world evidence, and later apply for basic coverage.

The commercial pathway, however, faces headwinds. [National Business Daily](https://m.nbd.com.cn/articles/2026-06-12/4425342.html) reported that applications to the commercial innovative drug catalogue fell more than 50% year-on-year in 2026, from 141 to 61, after the first edition underperformed expectations.

"For some drugmakers, whether entering the commercial catalogue actually translates into market sales and patient access remains highly uncertain," Zhu Junsheng, a professor at Peking University, told the outlet, noting that payment, hospital admission and risk-sharing arrangements are still being worked out.

The 12 drugs added this round therefore represent a smaller, more selective bet on a mechanism still finding its footing.

## What to Watch

The catalogue's real-world impact will hinge on details not yet public — notably the negotiated price reductions for the 51 Class-one drugs, which determine actual affordability. Also critical is whether the four-ministry notice and the "three exclusions" that shield commercial-catalogue drugs from certain cost controls succeed in overcoming the "catalogue without prescription" problem.

China's innovative drug market surpassed 100 billion yuan in 2024, and the government targets annual growth above 20% during the 15th Five-Year Plan period. With outbound licensing deals exceeding $120 billion as of September 2026, the country is positioning itself as both a producer and a fast-adopter market for frontier medicine.

But as insiders note, the challenge has shifted from "can we make it" to "who pays for it." The 2026 catalogue answers that question for 107 more medicines on paper. Whether patients can fill those prescriptions is a test that begins January 1.
