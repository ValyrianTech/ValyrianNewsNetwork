# UN Talks End With No Ban on Killer Robots

After five days of negotiations in Geneva, a United Nations conference on "killer robots" ended early Saturday without an international ban or a legally binding treaty. Instead, participating states agreed to a watered-down mandate to keep talking for at least two more years, leaving lethal autonomous weapons systems essentially unregulated internationally for now. Nearly 100 countries had pushed for negotiations toward a binding ban, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/21/vn-bereiken-overeenstemming-tijdens-conferentie-over-killerrobot/).

## A Weakened Mandate

The Seventh Review Conference of the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), held at the Palais des Nations, ran through all-night talks before concluding just before 5 a.m. on Saturday, November 21, 2026. The 128 states party to the convention agreed to "make a start on the elaboration of an international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons systems," with work to be completed by 2028, according to conference chair Julien Thöni.

The final text asks countries "to elaborate by consensus" an instrument that — as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/21/international-talks-control-ai-autonomous-weapons-collapse) reported — may not carry any legal obligations. In the years ahead, negotiators must determine which autonomous weapons could be prohibited under international humanitarian law and which might remain permitted but subject to rules.

Autonomous weapons — the "killer robots" of the campaigners' shorthand — use sensors, algorithms and artificial intelligence to select and engage targets without a human giving the final order. The issue has grown urgent as AI-assisted weapons have already appeared in conflicts from Ukraine to Gaza and Iran. The Pentagon, according to the Guardian, has reportedly aimed for as many as 1,000 AI-assisted targeting decisions per hour, and the United States and Israel have used AI-enabled targeting systems to conduct tens of thousands of strikes in Gaza and Iran.

## Consensus Paralysis

The outcome was dictated by the CCW's rules. Decisions require the unanimous agreement of all 128 parties, meaning a single country can block any accord. That consensus rule has prevented the convention from passing any new law regulating weapons in 30 years.

Russia led the objections, staging what diplomats described as obstructive stalling tactics and filibustering. The obstruction began on the opening day, when Russia blocked adoption of the conference agenda over the participation of observers, forcing the session to be suspended before informal consultations resumed the following day, as [Geneva Solutions](https://genevasolutions.news/peace-humanitarian/russia-s-delay-tactics-derail-un-talks-on-autonomous-weapons-in-geneva) documented. On the final day, the exclusion of several non-member countries prompted a rare protest in the chamber, with the Egyptian delegation shouting at the conference chair.

Other military powers invested in AI warfare were also reluctant to sign up to binding rules. The United States, while denying it was stalling, had proposed guidelines lasting another five years rather than a treaty. A working paper published in September, joined by Russia and Israel among others, signaled that Washington would block formal negotiations. South Korea, Japan, Turkey, the UK, France and Germany — defence-tech powers exploring AI-enabled weapons — likewise resisted being bound by new law, favouring a non-binding agreement, the Guardian reported.

## A Missed Opportunity

A bloc of seven countries — Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama — had led the push for a binding treaty to be negotiated within a year. Many European and NATO states, including Ukraine, as well as China and Iran, expressed support or willingness for a new protocol.

Diplomats and advocates voiced sharp disappointment. Brazil's ambassador Frederico Meyer, a proponent of stricter rules, pointed to the gap between the speed of technology and the pace of diplomacy. "Technology evolves faster than diplomacy. Diplomats are very slow," he said, according to VRT NWS. Speaking on the floor earlier in the week, Brazil's delegate put the stakes plainly: "These are not weapons of the future being discussed here, these are already in use and being further developed."

Beatrice Fihn, founder of Lex International and former head of the International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons, blamed the process itself. "Some states are weaponising consensus, on both procedural and substantive issues, to ensure that there will be no outcome this week," she told Geneva Solutions. Anna Hehir, head of military AI governance at the Future of Life Institute, called it "another case of consensus paralysis."

The nongovernmental group Stop Killer Robots described the result as a "watered-down mandate" that does not genuinely clear the path to a legally binding agreement, while Human Rights Watch called the outcome "disappointing." Max Tegmark, chair of the Future of Life Institute, said: "Slaughterbots are already here. Yet states have agreed it is sufficient to just talk about this for the 14th year." One European diplomat, speaking to the Guardian, was blunter: "There was no political will to really regulate these weapons. No will commensurate to the urgency and need."

## What's Next

The failure extends a years-long stalemate. The previous CCW review in 2021 also ended without agreement on autonomous weapons, with a handful of states blocking progress.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres had hoped that formal negotiations would begin in 2026 — a goal that has now been missed. In August 2026, he and ICRC President Mirjana Spoljaric issued a rare joint warning that autonomous weapons were "dangerously close to crossing a moral red line."

With the CCW route exhausted, attention is turning to alternatives beyond Geneva. Countries and campaigners are weighing whether to take the issue to the UN General Assembly, invoke a CCW provision allowing a group of states to ask the Secretary-General to negotiate a new protocol, or pursue a host-country-led process — the model that produced the 2008 cluster munitions ban after similar deadlock. That path is clouded by uncertainty: Guterres' term is ending and no successor has been named.

For now, the practical effect is a two-year extension of the status quo. Those who want binding rules argue that the historical precedents — landmines, cluster munitions, nuclear weapons — show treaties can be negotiated far faster than the CCW has managed. Whether the political will exists to bypass the consensus-bound forum remains the open question, and one that the next two years of consultations are unlikely to answer on their own.
