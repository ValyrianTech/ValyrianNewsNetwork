# MAHA Influencers Cash In on Dubious Wellness Products

A sweeping Washington Post investigation has found that the most prominent figures of the Make America Healthy Again movement have built lucrative wellness businesses around products with questionable safety records, minimal regulatory oversight, and—in some cases—claims that experts say could endanger the public.

The [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/interactive/2026/11/22/coffee-enemas-drinkable-silver-booming-industry-maha-influencers/) examined more than 37,000 social media posts, podcasts and newsletters since January 2024, focusing on 18 influencers whose Instagram followings grew by 50 percent or more between August 2024 and June 2026. It found that all but one of them promoted products, with their total number of product or brand promotions ballooning from 257 in the first half of 2024 to 795 over the same period in 2026—a roughly threefold increase.

## From Movement to Merchandise

The central finding is that these influencers—unlike past wellness entrepreneurs—built their platforms under the mantle of a federal political agenda, tying commerce to a government-sanctioned movement. Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the movement's leader, was sworn in as Secretary of Health and Human Services in January 2025, giving MAHA unprecedented influence in Washington.

Among those analyzed were Del Bigtree, a communications director for Kennedy's 2024 presidential campaign; Nicole Shanahan, his vice presidential running mate; and Casey Means, whom President Donald Trump selected as his second surgeon general nominee before withdrawing her nomination this spring. A previous Post investigation found that Means was paid more than half a million dollars for partnerships and sponsorships with companies selling "herbal remedies and wellness products" and "teas, supplements, and elixirs."

Others have acted as informal MAHA surrogates, using viral content to promote the movement while monetizing their audiences. Alex Clark, the "Culture Apothecary" podcaster for Turning Point USA, posted 352 promotions in the first half of 2026—nearly three times her count from the same period in 2024. Courtney Swan, creator of the podcast "Realfoodology," went from 25 promotions in the first half of 2024 to 137 this year.

The Department of Health and Human Services pushed back against any suggestion that Kennedy endorses the commercial activity. "MAHA's mission is to improve Americans' health through better nutrition, prevention, and rigorous science," spokeswoman Emily Hilliard said, adding that Kennedy's leadership "should not be interpreted as a blanket endorsement of every influencer, commercial partnership, or product associated with the movement."

## Concrete Health Risks

The investigation identified specific, documented hazards. At least 14 of the brands promoted by the movement's fastest-growing influencers offer products that environmental watchdogs say contain lead at levels high enough to warrant special labeling under California's Proposition 65—a stricter standard than federal rules. Under that law, disclosure is triggered above 0.5 micrograms of lead exposure.

"You see lead contamination in the supplements, that should raise some alarm bells," said Thomas Galligan, a scientist at the Center for Science in the Public Interest. There is no safe level of lead exposure, and even low levels can damage organs and disrupt brain development, according to the [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/about/index.html).

In one striking example, physician-influencer Joel "Dr. Gator" Warsh promoted Sovereign Silver—a colloidal silver product—for children's immune systems. The [National Institutes of Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/colloidal-silver-what-you-need-to-know) warns that colloidal silver is not safe or effective for any health condition and can cause kidney, liver, or nervous system problems, and can permanently turn skin bluish-gray. Warsh, who runs a $34.97-a-month Substack with more than 150,000 subscribers and offers $499-a-year "Vaccine Coaching" sessions, told followers the product was "part of our family's wellness routine."

The Post also found that at least 15 products promoted by these influencers would likely score as a "no-go" on the Defense Department's [Operation Supplement Safety scorecard](https://www.opss.org/opss-scorecard), a tool designed to flag supplements not recommended for troops. Separately, the Federal Trade Commission fined TruHeight—a company promoted by a MAHA advocate—$4 million in April 2026 for falsely claiming its products could make children taller, a judgment later reduced to $750,000.

## False Hope in Cancer Care

The investigation flagged one influencer's promotion of alternative cancer treatments as especially troubling. Carly Brown, formerly known as Carly Shankman, promoted coffee enemas, juice cleanses and an alternative treatment center in Mexico called Hope4Cancer, which faced since-settled lawsuits from the estates of two patients who died months after beginning treatment there. Brown said she could not speak to the clinic's legal matters and that she doesn't identify as a MAHA influencer.

"I can only speak to my own experience as a patient. I'm just a mom sharing what's helped me," she said.

Joshua Sharfstein, a former principal deputy commissioner of the Food and Drug Administration, warned of the inherent danger in offering cancer patients false hope. "Even a harmless product that keeps someone from using a needed treatment can be quite dangerous," he said.

## Echoes of Vaccine Skepticism

Several influencers leveraged vaccine skepticism into sales. Swan promoted an "Ultimate Spike Detox" costing about $89.99 a month, marketed to "detox" from COVID vaccine "spike proteins"—a product category for which former federal vaccine officials said there is no large-scale human trial evidence. Public health experts warn that such messaging could further erode already declining U.S. vaccination rates.

The commercial activity is drawing sharp criticism from medical ethicists. Arthur Caplan, a professor emeritus at NYU Grossman School of Medicine, described MAHA influencers who sell science-unsupported supplements as engaged in "completely dishonest, unethical ripoff profiteering," noting the industry is regulated less than a local restaurant. "If it sounds too good to be true, it almost certainly is," said David Gorski, a professor of surgery and oncology at Wayne State University.

## A Movement Losing Its Sway

The Post's analysis lands at a moment of acute political stress for MAHA. Some movement allies have grown disenchanted with the Trump administration's failure to deliver sweeping reforms on food, pesticides and vaccines. The investigation found that the influencers now invoke the movement less than half as often as they did in early 2025—even as they lean harder on their commercial identities.

That gap between political rhetoric and retail ambition may be the investigation's most revealing insight. By building a sales and marketing infrastructure, some of the movement's biggest names have cemented their online staying power. The influencers may have capitalized on a political moment, but the businesses they built—and the customers they cultivated—could well outlast it.
