# A Small-Business Loan That Became a 7-Year Nightmare

Vahe Shahinian thought he had found the financing that would carry his concert-promotion business to its next stage: a $750,000 line of credit at an annual interest rate just under 9%. Instead, according to an [NPR investigation](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/22/nx-s1-5752261/small-business-loan-merchant-cash-advance) published November 22, he was pulled into a seven-year ordeal built on merchant cash advances — a fast-growing, lightly regulated form of business financing that can carry annualized costs far above what a traditional loan would allow. His lawsuit against his funder, EIN Cap, could become one of the first such fraud cases to reach trial.

Sitting at a desk in his teenage bedroom — a room he never expected to occupy again in middle age — the 51-year-old Shahinian rereads the emails that divided his life into before and after. "From that day," he told NPR, "it's been one long day, one long nightmare."

## A business in need

Before what he calls "the incident," Shahinian had spent 15 years turning a side project into a livelihood. Educated as a computer scientist and formerly an aerospace engineer programming satellites, he built a ticketing and seating-chart platform called It's My Seat in 2004, then grew it from selling tickets to local galas into producing his own concerts and tours — from a 400-seat flamenco show to a 3,000-seat Engelbert Humperdinck performance and, by 2015, national touring.

By 2018, he says, ticket sales were climbing toward $5 million a year and profits toward $250,000. He self-funded largely through savings, credit cards and American Express merchant financing — instant short-term loans at roughly 6% — and says he had never missed a payment.

Then, while booking his biggest tour yet, Amex declined his fifth round of merchant funding; it was phasing out the program. He had about two weeks to raise roughly $200,000. He first lined up two merchant cash advances totaling about $250,000, owing back nearly $400,000 — enough to save the tour but consuming all of its profit.

## The fateful offer

That is when a broker contacted him with the pitch that changed his life: a line of credit worth $750,000 at just under 9%. The catch was that he would first accept a third of the sum and begin repaying immediately in daily installments of $3,600. After 30 days, he could access the rest.

Shahinian says the broker framed the arrangement as a routine "test" of dependability before a preapproved line of credit. He had no lawyer or adviser to consult. He looked up the finance firm — Wall Street-based EIN Cap — which advertised itself as a "provider of working capital to local businesses."

"I was assured that 'We've done this for years. We don't care about a 30-day program — our goal is long term,'" Shahinian said. "The whole setup was to make someone like me feel assured, feel safe — and I felt safe to go in it."

He received $228,000 rather than $250,000, with the difference withheld as an initial fee — a common feature of merchant cash advances. But the line of credit never materialized. After 30 days the broker blamed "technical" delays; Shahinian's emails grew increasingly urgent. "I don't think you understand the urgency of this situation," one read. "I'm basically out of time." After 40 days of daily payments, his bank account hit zero.

## A spiral of debt

What happened to Shahinian has a name in the trade. His lawyer, Shane Heskin, calls it "carroting" — a carrot dangled in front of a business owner in the form of an attractive low-interest loan, only to be swapped for an unsolicited merchant cash advance. "I see two or three a week," Heskin told NPR. "They're exploding." Common targets, he said, are first-time business owners, first-time borrowers and especially immigrants.

Days before his biggest tour, with accounts empty and venues half-paid, Shahinian did what many struggling owners do: he took out more cash advances. About $250,000 "evaporated into expenses in half a day." He then tapped credit and home equity — his own, his parents' and his best friend's — exceeding $1 million in debt to friends and family within weeks.

"From here," he said, "the avalanche of money over money, over money, over money started."

He canceled concerts at six-figure losses, defaulted on debts, and watched some clients receive lien letters redirecting their payments to his funders. Eventually he lost his Glendale, California, home of 16 years to foreclosure and moved back into his childhood bedroom at his elderly parents' house.

## An industry outside the rules

Shahinian's story illustrates the mechanics of a sector the U.S. Treasury Department has documented as the fastest-growing source of funding for small U.S. businesses — and one that largely escapes lending law. Because merchant cash advances are structured as a "purchase of future sales" rather than loans, funders are generally not required to be licensed and face no legal limit on what they charge. Effective costs, translated into annual rates, run from roughly 25% to 200% or more, according to [Treasury figures](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/136/Financing-Small-Business-Landscape-and-Recommendations.pdf) cited by NPR. MCA firms argue the high costs reflect the high risk they assume.

The void these products fill is real: Treasury data show big banks reject about 20% of business loan applications and respond to roughly a quarter with only partial funding, while a [Federal Reserve survey](https://www.fedsmallbusiness.org/reports/survey/2026/2026-report-on-employer-firms) found even lower odds. The Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City reports businesses increasingly use MCAs to cover basic expenses like payroll, and the advances are a frequent feature in small-business bankruptcies, which have been rising. This reporting is part of a broader [NPR series](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/22/nx-s1-6048657/the-booming-cash-advance-industry-trapping-americas-small-businesses) on the industry.

## A rare fight

Shahinian sued EIN Cap and the brokers in 2019 in U.S. District Court in Los Angeles. About three years later he settled with the brokers to focus on EIN, whose case accuses the funder of fraud. The trial date has been delayed three times, most recently by nine more months.

EIN Cap has argued in court that it never promised any loan or line of credit — only a merchant cash advance, which it delivered — and that the brokers arranged the deal and made the promises. Much of its testimony on its broker ties is sealed. "EIN Cap denies Mr. Shahinian's claims, and its position is set out in its public court filings," Levi Silver, counsel for EIN, said in a statement to NPR, declining further comment on the pending case.

Shahinian says the broker who introduced himself as "Bryan Stein" used different names. The Brooklyn District Attorney's Office told NPR the man, Boris Shteyngart, pleaded guilty in 2012 to grand larceny and served prison time. In the past decade, EIN has faced lawsuits in at least nine states, records show, most settled or dismissed. In one Washington state case, EIN was ordered to return daily fees it kept withdrawing from an already-insolvent business. In Illinois, a federal judge ruled that EIN "propped up" and "prolonged" a Ponzi-like scheme through its 2019 funding and ordered it to return $184,854 in profits.

## What's next

Shahinian hopes to recoup more than $5 million — his estimate of the debt he has accrued since the deal, including some $3 million in interest and fees. His lawyer says the best case is a multimillion-dollar judgment with punitive damages, "and then it's good luck collecting on it."

A handful of states, including California, now require MCA companies to disclose fees more clearly in writing. California recently passed AB 2116, which, [starting January 1, 2028](https://calmatters.digitaldemocracy.org/bills/ca_202520260ab2116), will require commercial financing providers and brokers to be licensed and undergo background checks and inspections. It is too late for Shahinian, whose business is back to basics — local ticketing and software coding.

He still believes a win would matter. "Hopefully it would be a case for the industry to self-heal, self-correct," he said. "And it'll be hope for others who are stuck — give them motivation. Don't give up, you know? Don't be on path of least resistance. Go fighting."
