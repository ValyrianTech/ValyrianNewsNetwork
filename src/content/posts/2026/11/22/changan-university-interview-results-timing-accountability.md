# Chang'an University Disciplines Officials Over Hiring Error

Chang'an University, a national key university in Xi'an, Shaanxi Province, has held its human resources director and other officials accountable after candidates noticed an apparent impossibility in a counselor recruitment process: the interview results appeared to be dated November 13, a day before the interviews themselves were actually conducted on November 14.

The case, which was reported by Xinhua and republished by [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34530115), has reignited public debate over fairness in hiring at Chinese public institutions, where competition for stable university posts is intense and suspicion of insider favoritism runs high.

## How the discrepancy surfaced

Candidates in the counselor (辅导员) recruitment examination raised questions after noticing that the results page carried a date preceding the interviews themselves, according to an [earlier report by The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34512026). The logical impossibility — results on November 13 for interviews held on November 14 — prompted candidates to publicly challenge the fairness of the process.

The university subsequently issued a formal response and applied internal accountability measures against its HR director (人事处处长) and other relevant personnel. According to the reporting, these measures are said to include suspension pending inspection, transfer, and formal admonishment.

## A technical error or something deeper?

The university has framed the matter as an administrative or technical error. Yet the severity of the disciplinary response — reaching the HR director — and the fact that a results page apparently carried a formal, incorrect date have left many observers unconvinced that this was a simple clerical slip.

Critics noted that pre-building a results page ahead of an examination could be a routine preparatory step, but a wrong formal date tied to a high-stakes recruitment is harder to dismiss. The disciplinary action, rather than settling the matter, became its own point of contention: some commenters argued the punishment was too light to reflect a genuine problem, while others contended that if no misconduct occurred, the sanctions were disproportionately harsh.

## Public trust at stake

University counselor posts are widely viewed in China as stable, civil-service-adjacent positions — a modern "iron rice bowl" — which raises the stakes of any perceived unfairness. Anomalies in public-sector recruitment routinely draw allegations of "萝卜招聘" (pre-arranged hiring), a term netizens use to describe roles seemingly tailored for specific insider candidates.

Public reaction to the Chang'an University case reflected that deep skepticism. Commenters on The Paper's coverage asked whether personal connections had "penetrated to this extent," dismissed the administrative measures as not constituting real punishment, and argued that the university should open up its hiring process to close loopholes rather than simply announce internal discipline.

## What's next

The university's handling — an internal, rather than independent, investigation combined with administrative measures widely viewed as light — has left public trust largely unrepaired. Several questions remain unresolved: whether an independent review took place, the precise sanctions imposed and the discretion behind them, whether the recruitment outcome was re-examined, and whether affected candidates received any remedy.

For Chang'an University and other public institutions facing similar scrutiny, the episode underscores a broader challenge: in high-salience hiring contests, even minor procedural errors can rapidly escalate into allegations of systemic corruption. Rebuilding confidence, analysts suggest, will likely require transparency about the process itself — not just accountability for those who mishandled it.
