# Brussels Samusocial Turns Away 9 in 10 Families

Brussels' principal emergency shelter service is now forced to refuse nine out of ten families who ask for a roof, leaving parents and children to sleep on the streets of the Belgian capital, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/short/bruxelles-le-samusocial-doit-refuser-9-familles-sur-10-7355). The Samusocial, which runs the capital's emergency reception centres, says more than four in five families were already being turned away in July — a figure it described as a "strong and continuous" rise in demand it can no longer absorb.

## What the numbers show

RTBF's late-November report frames the situation starkly: nine families in ten are now rejected. The most detailed official figures, published in August and confirmed on the [Samusocial's own website](https://samusocial.be/suppression-de-1000-places-daccueil-le-secteur-appelle-a-la-mobilisation/), give a concrete picture. In July, 299 families requested accommodation and 244 were refused — an 83.5% increase in refusals from the previous month.

Among those families were 238 children, including 65 babies under two and 15 infants under six months. One person in four sheltered by the Samusocial is a child. The organisation houses roughly 2,000 people each day across its network, including three centres reserved for families.

"There are children currently on the street in Brussels," Samusocial director general Sarah de Liamchine said. "Despite the additional places opened in the sector for the winter, we are forced to refuse requests."

## A system squeezed from several sides

Frontline staff describe a daily grind of impossible choices. "We have to refuse families every day," social worker Loren Gilson said. "If we extend one family's shelter in our centre, we also prolong the street stay of another family, perhaps even more vulnerable."

When no bed is available, families are sometimes offered tents — with dangerous consequences. "Unfortunately, we have had families given tents who were attacked," social assistant Thomas said, noting that staff advise avoiding risky areas such as near Gare du Midi.

The pressure comes from several directions at once. Rising Brussels rents slow the turnover of residents moving from emergency shelters into permanent housing, creating a bottleneck. Under the so-called Dublin procedure, asylum seekers who first applied in another EU country lose the right to material aid in Belgium, pushing them toward the Samusocial and the Red Cross. And street teams report a steady flow of families arriving from conflict zones in Eastern Europe.

## The "Brussels Deal" cuts

The most direct driver, however, is political. Under the "Brussels Deal" — a cooperation agreement in which the federal government funds emergency places for asylum seekers without a Fedasil place — Federal Minister for Asylum and Migration Anneleen Van Bossuyt (N-VA) announced in July the removal of 1,000 of the 2,000 places, with closures planned before winter.

The consequences have already materialised. As [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/bruxelles-perd-130-places-d-accueil-avec-la-fermeture-du-centre-wtc-du-samusocial-11792811), the Samusocial's WTC reception centre closed on 30 September, eliminating 130 emergency places and cutting the organisation's capacity for isolated men to 240. Twenty-five of the centre's 33 staff lost their contracts. BelRefugees closed its Amran centre on 1 August, shedding places for 66 unaccompanied minors and young adults along with roughly 20 jobs.

The figures for single men illustrate the scale of unmet demand. From the WTC's opening on 2 February until its closure, the Samusocial accommodated 5,143 isolated men — 3,813 of them at the WTC — while refusing 18,624 out of 39,202 requests, a refusal rate of roughly 47%.

De Liamchine has stressed that not all announced cuts have taken effect: "The 700 places whose removal was announced are still open to this day," she said in September, suggesting a partial and contested implementation.

## Analysis: a political choice meets a humanitarian reality

The refusal of nine families in ten crystallises a wider collision in the heart of the EU capital: federal budget decisions running headlong into rising housing costs and restrictive migration policy. The sector has repeatedly warned that cutting places before winter risks reviving tent camps, with the associated health problems and social tensions.

More than 1,500 people were already on the BelRefugees waiting list for emergency accommodation in August, and more than 1,000 men remained on it by late September. The Samusocial and partner federations have argued that these are not abstract statistics but families — often single mothers fleeing domestic violence — for whom the shelter is "the last safety net before the street."

## What to watch

The immediate question is whether the remaining 700 places will be scrapped before winter sets in, and whether extreme-weather capacity will be reopened in time if temperatures plunge. The federal government has so far shown no sign of pausing the Brussels Deal cuts despite the mobilisation and the worsening figures.

Beyond the emergency, the deeper challenge is structural: breaking the bottleneck between emergency shelter and permanent housing in a capital whose rental market keeps pricing out the most vulnerable. Until that gap closes, the Samusocial's staff expect to keep answering the same question at their door — and, nine times out of ten, giving a family the same answer. "These families should not end up here," de Liamchine said.
