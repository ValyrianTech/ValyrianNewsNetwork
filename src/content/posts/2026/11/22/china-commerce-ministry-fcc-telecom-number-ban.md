# China Slams FCC Ban on Telecom Number Access

China's Ministry of Commerce on Sunday sharply condemned a US regulatory decision barring major Chinese telecom operators from directly obtaining American internet telephone number resources, warning that Beijing would take "necessary measures" to defend its interests.

The statement, carried by [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20261122/851d80b000224df0bed28c95c524e15a/c.html) and sourced to the ministry's website, came two days after the US Federal Communications Commission voted to adopt rules targeting China Telecom, China Unicom, China Mobile and other carriers placed on Washington's "Covered List" of national-security risks.

## What the FCC Decision Does

The FCC voted on Nov. 19 to block those operators and other Internet Protocol-enabled service providers from directly accessing US numbering resources. Under the prior system, providers offering Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) services could obtain US phone numbers directly from numbering authorities rather than routing through intermediate carriers, lowering costs and enabling advanced calling features.

As [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/politics/fcc-bans-chinese-firms-direct-access-us-phone-numbers-bid-curb-robocalls) reported, the ban forces such traffic through intermediaries and forms part of a wider national-security crackdown aimed at curbing scam robocalls and alleged Chinese cyber espionage. FCC Chair Brendan Carr framed the vote as a defensive measure: "With this decision, the FCC will be strengthening our first line of defense against bad actors and illegal robocallers."

The vote is the latest step in a multi-year US effort to sever Chinese state-linked telecom firms from American communications infrastructure. The commission has already barred China Mobile, China Telecom and China Unicom from providing international telecom services to the United States, restricted internet-based providers relying on Huawei and ZTE equipment, and moved to ban Chinese laboratories from testing US electronics starting in December 2028.

## Beijing's Response

The Commerce Ministry did not mince words. Its spokesperson accused the FCC of "abusing its regulatory power," of "over-generalizing so-called national security risks," and of acting "in the absence of factual basis" while violating "the principles of non-discrimination and fair competition."

Beijing further charged that the agency "has recently continued to push for decoupling and severing of supply chains" between the two countries across telecom, communications, and testing and certification. Such practices, the ministry said, "violate the important consensus reached by the two heads of state" and "undermine the stable situation of China-US economic and trade relations."

The statement demanded that Washington "immediately revoke the relevant measures and return to the correct path of resolving issues through consultation," adding that China "will take necessary measures to firmly safeguard its own interests."

## Context and Escalation

The phone-number restriction is one link in a longer regulatory chain. In late 2024, the US accused a Chinese state-sponsored hacking group dubbed "Salt Typhoon" of infiltrating American telecom firms and stealing call records, a threat the FBI said this year remains "still very much ongoing." In July, the FCC added California-based Digitalsystem Technology to its national security risk list over its ties to Chinese telecom firms. The following month, Hong Kong carrier HKT, a subsidiary of PCCW in which China Unicom holds a stake, agreed under a consent decree to halt its US telecom operations by September 2027.

The measures fit a broader US-China "de-risking" push that now spans numbering resources, network equipment, testing laboratories and data infrastructure. Beijing has consistently denied US espionage allegations and characterized the restrictions as discriminatory and politically motivated.

## What to Watch

Several questions loom. Chinese officials have not specified what "necessary measures" might entail, though their language mirrors earlier calibrated warnings rather than an immediate rupture. Washington may also proceed to revoke existing direct-number authorizations already held by Chinese firms, and the agency is separately weighing barring those companies from connecting to US networks or operating US data centers, steps that, if adopted, would extend restrictions from numbering into core data infrastructure.

The explicit invocation of the "heads of state consensus" raises the diplomatic stakes, suggesting the telecom standoff could complicate broader efforts to stabilize bilateral ties. For now, the two sides remain locked in a familiar pattern: Washington tightens its regulatory perimeter, and Beijing promises to respond.
