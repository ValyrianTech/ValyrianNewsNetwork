# Flemish checks on social tenants' foreign property plunge

Investigations into whether social-housing tenants in Flanders secretly own property abroad have fallen sharply over three years, dropping from 506 in 2022 to just 89 in 2025, according to figures from N-VA Flemish MP Gijs Degrande and the official Housing Annual Report. In 2025 those 89 investigations led to 39 tenants being caught, and more than half of the region's housing companies carried out no checks at all. "Whoever wants to tackle social fraud must also be willing to check," Degrande said, [according to VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/20/n-va-controles-buitenlands-verblijf-sociale-huurders/).

## A rule with real consequences

In Flanders, the Dutch-speaking region that sets housing policy in Belgium, tenants of a social home are not permitted to own a house or land abroad. Those caught must leave the home and repay the social rent discounts they received. The checks began [in 2021](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2020/12/10/vlaamse-regering-trekt-5-miljoen-uit-voor-vermogensonderzoek-van/), after the Flemish government earmarked roughly five million euros for asset investigations under then-Housing Minister Matthias Diependaele of N-VA. The scheme reflects a simple premise: social housing is a scarce, subsidised good that should reach those who genuinely need it.

That premise carries political weight in a region where demand vastly outstrips supply. According to official Vlaanderen housing figures, nearly 188,000 candidates were on the waiting list at the end of 2024 — roughly one in three in Antwerp province — with average waits stretching to several years. Long queues make any suggestion of eligibility fraud highly sensitive.

## Wide gaps between housing companies

The most striking figure in the report is not the overall decline but the inconsistency across the region's 41 social-housing companies, known as woonmaatschappijen. Of those, only 18 carried out any foreign-property investigations in 2025; the other 23 conducted none. "It cannot be that more than half of the housing companies do not check at all," Degrande said. "The rules must be applied equally everywhere. Every housing company must take its responsibility and actively check when there are indications of foreign property ownership."

The decline may owe less to a single policy decision than to a relaxation of effort at the local level. The figures suggest a governance gap rather than a deliberate dismantling of the anti-fraud apparatus. The research also notes that the high hit rate — 39 tenants caught out of 89 checked, around 44 percent — partly reflects a selection effect: because checks are suspicion-based rather than random, a smaller number of targeted investigations can still produce a high share of positive findings, even as fewer fraudsters are detected in absolute terms.

## New rules and sharper tools

The Flemish Parliament has recently approved a decree that will make it mandatory for housing companies to actively cooperate with checks and to disclose how they use private and public partners to trace foreign property. The regulator, meanwhile, gains additional powers — including the ability to use data on the volume of household waste collected as an indicator of possible undeclared occupancy or ownership, a proxy that can flag homes where more people may be living than are officially registered.

Degrande is now pressing Housing Minister Melissa Depraetere of the socialist Vooruit party to push the number of investigations back up. "More checks mean more fairness for the families who are rightly waiting for a social home," he said. "That is why we want the number of investigations to rise again and for all housing companies to take part. Social homes must go to people who genuinely have a right to them."

## What to watch

The decree and the regulator's expanded powers set the stage for check volumes to rebound in 2026 and 2027 — but only if the 23 housing companies that stopped investigating choose to restart, and only if they receive the budget and staffing to do so. Several questions remain open: why so many companies halted checks entirely, and whether the new framework contract that began in 2024 changed the incentives or costs involved. For now, the nonpartisan anchor is the official Housing Annual Report itself, which records both the steep decline and the uneven application of a rule designed to protect a heavily oversubscribed public resource. Whether Flanders closes that enforcement gap will determine whether the trend reverses — or whether the checks continue to fade.
