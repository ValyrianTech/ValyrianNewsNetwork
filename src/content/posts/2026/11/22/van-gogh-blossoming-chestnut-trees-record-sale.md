# Van Gogh Masterpiece Sells for Record 173 Million Euros

Vincent van Gogh's late masterpiece *Blossoming Chestnut Trees* sold at Sotheby's in New York on Wednesday, 18 November, for a hammer price of $165 million — roughly $190.4 million including buyer's fees, or about €173 million. The result set a new auction record for the artist, smashing his previous mark of roughly $117 million, and capped a dramatic week in which the sale nearly collapsed over a Nazi-era restitution dispute that was settled only hours before the gavel fell.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/19/schilderij-van-vincent-van-gogh-bloeiende-kastanjebomen-geve/), the Belgian public broadcaster, the painting had never fetched so much: the work went under the hammer for more than €173 million, surpassing pre-sale expectations of around €163 million.

## A final masterpiece, hidden for decades

Painted in May 1890 in Auvers-sur-Oise, France, just months before the artist's death that July, *Blossoming Chestnut Trees* (*Châtaigniers en fleurs*) depicts three women in colourful dress seen from behind, walking in the shade of the trees, while a man in blue work clothes descends the street in the opposite direction. It had been out of public view for roughly 60 years, held in the private Blaquier-Arrieta collection in Argentina.

The painting headlined the *Blaquier-Arrieta Collection* sale, part of an evening that saw two back-to-back auctions at Sotheby's. As [The Art Newspaper](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2026/11/19/van-gogh-painting-sells-for-record-7778m-after-nazi-loot-claims) reported, the combined sales brought in $663.26 million ($777.8 million with fees). The Van Gogh sale made it the fourth most valuable painting ever sold at auction and the second most valuable object ever sold at Sotheby's, behind Gustav Klimt's *Portrait of Elisabeth Lederer*, which fetched $236.4 million last November.

The winning bidder was William Acquavella, head of Acquavella Galleries in New York, who prevailed after nearly five minutes of bidding.

## A record, but not a runaway

Despite the headline figure, the result carried signals of a cooling high-end market. The painting hammered below its pre-sale estimate of around $180 million, and the broader Blaquier-Arrieta collection brought in $358 million ($412 million with fees) against an estimate of roughly $450 million. Six of the collection's 12 lots hammered below their low estimates, and only one work exceeded expectations. The evening nonetheless produced new records for Mary Cassatt and the Harlem Renaissance artist Aaron Douglas, whose *Harriet Tubman* (1931) fetched $14.48 million with fees.

## The restitution claim that nearly stopped the sale

The sale might never have happened. On 12 November, the Berlin-based restitution researcher Gunnar Schnabel wrote to Sotheby's on behalf of the heirs of the painting's former Jewish owners, Samuel and Hedwig Fischer, questioning the circumstances under which the work left the family. The Fischers, a Berlin publishing family, had acquired the painting in 1909; their son-in-law deposited it with an Amsterdam gallery in the early 1930s, and it was bought by Paris collectors Henriette and Marcel Kapferer in 1936.

Schnabel argued that the 1936 sale took place just three months after the family fled Berlin for Vienna, and asked Sotheby's to prove the work had sold at market value and not under duress, requesting that the auction be postponed.

On 17 November, Sotheby's announced that an agreement had been reached between the Blaquier-Arrieta family and all legal descendants of the Fischers, clearing the sale to proceed. "In recognition of the painting's complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust, the two families have reached a mutually satisfactory resolution," the auction house said in a statement reported by [The Art Newspaper](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2026/11/17/sothebys-makes-last-minute-deal-with-jewish-heirs-to-sell-180m-van-gogh-painting).

The terms were not disclosed, and it remains unknown whether the Fischer family received financial compensation. In their statement, the heirs said they were grateful for "the trusting and fair negotiation and collaboration with Sotheby's and the Blaquier-Arrieta family, which make it possible to acknowledge the suffering of the Fischer family during the Nazi era."

## A growing pattern of out-of-court settlements

The case reflects a wider resurgence in restitution claims for artworks that Jewish collectors were forced to relinquish during the Nazi period. As VRT noted, such claims have increased again in recent years, and many are now resolved outside the courts through mutual agreements rather than litigation — precisely the path taken here.

The episode also highlighted a tension at the heart of the restitution debate: Sotheby's maintained throughout that its own provenance research found the work had not been sold under Nazi duress, even as it brokered a settlement that acknowledged the family's suffering. The compromise resolved the claim without any public finding on the underlying allegations.

## What to watch

With the painting now in the hands of a prominent dealer, questions linger over whether it will remain in private hands or return to the market, and over the long-term effect of the dispute on its value. What is certain is that *Blossoming Chestnut Trees* has written its own chapter in the history of both the art market and the unfinished reckoning with Nazi-era looting — a record sale inseparable from the story of the family that once owned it.
