# European Commission Confirms Major AI-Assisted Cyberattack

The European Commission has confirmed it was the target of a major cyberattack that exploited a publicly available artificial intelligence tool, disrupting internal administrative systems — including the platform managing staff salaries, leave and timesheets — for more than a week. The intrusion, first reported by the US outlet Politico, raises urgent questions about the exposure of sensitive personal data and the identity of the attackers.

According to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/783618/article/2026-11-19/la-commission-europeenne-victime-dune-importante-cyberattaque), the incident could have compromised a large quantity of personal data and particularly affected the processing of employees' leave. Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier confirmed the attack, telling reporters that "the European Commission has taken the necessary steps in response to a detected cyber campaign," adding that "individual systems were targeted and, in line with our standard practice, we have informed those concerned."

## How the Attack Unfolded

[Politico's Brussels Playbook](https://www.politico.eu/newsletter/brussels-playbook/cyberattack-hits-the-commission/), which broke the story, reported that the incident left staff unable to input salary and leave information and prompted senior officials to discuss the crisis at their weekly meeting. The disruption lasted over ten days, with the Commission's own cybersecurity watchdog, CERT-EU, detecting that international hackers had used ARTEX — a publicly available, AI-powered tool designed to identify vulnerabilities in IT systems.

"The Commission believes it has contained most of the attack, but the full extent of any damage remains unclear," an EU official, granted anonymity because of the sensitivity of the issue, told Politico. "The systems may be coming back online. The answers are taking a little longer."

The primary target was Sysper, the internal human resources platform where officials check salary information, request holidays and sick leave, submit timesheets and sign up for training. The Joint Sickness Insurance Scheme (JSIS), the medical reimbursement system, was also affected. As a precaution, several other internal applications were temporarily taken offline to prevent the attack from spreading, most of which have since been restored.

## No Ransomware Demand, No Confirmed Data Breach

According to [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/19/eu-commission-hit-by-large-scale-cyberattack-with-data-leakage-unknown), Regnier stated that the attack was based on a publicly available AI tool, that it was first detected on 22 October, and that the Commission received no ransomware request. On the day of the public disclosure, Sysper was working again while JSIS remained unavailable, two EU officials told the outlet.

Separately, [RTL Info](https://www.rtl.be/actu/monde/europe/la-commission-europeenne-ciblee-par-une-cyberattaque-utilisant-lintelligence/2026-11-19/article/807080), citing Belga, reported that the intrusion was detected on 22 October before being formally confirmed on 26 October, following a preliminary investigation.

"The investigation is still ongoing. We are taking any necessary steps based on the findings as they come," Regnier said. "We have not detected any evidence of a data breach affecting Commission staff as a whole."

Yet the key question is whether the attacker — who has not been identified — gained access to the data held in Sysper, JSIS and other HR systems. Those platforms hold sensitive personal details, including officials' names, pay grades, home addresses and contact details. Such a database, Euronews noted, could fuel far more sophisticated campaigns targeting individual staff.

## The AI Tool at the Heart of the Attack

[Le Monde](https://www.lemonde.fr/pixels/article/2026/11/19/une-cyberattaque-assistee-par-l-ia-a-cible-la-commission-europeenne-en-octobre_6813314_4408996.html) reported that the hackers used ARTEX, a Chinese AI-based cybersecurity tool, according to a European official. Designed to help organisations strengthen their defences through AI-powered security testing to detect weak points, the tool had already been used in a series of hacks targeting Japanese companies, according to the conclusions of the cybersecurity branch of a major Tokyo-based internet group.

The Commission framed the incident as part of a global pattern of AI-assisted intrusions. "If AI can of course help detect vulnerabilities and prevent cyberattacks, it can obviously be diverted from its original use to conduct cyber operations," Regnier said, as quoted by RTL Info.

Critically, the naming of the Chinese tool does not amount to attributing the attack to the Chinese state. Officials explicitly declined to attribute the intrusion to a specific actor, and investigators are still trying to establish who is behind it. The tool is a publicly accessible, dual-use vulnerability scanner — legitimate in the right hands, dangerous in the wrong ones.

## A Deliberate Silence Under Scrutiny

The Commission's decision not to publicise the incident until Politico broke the story has drawn sharp criticism. The spokesperson defended the approach, invoking standard procedures and arguing that making public the countermeasures deployed to counter the attack would have risked rendering them ineffective and further exposing the institution.

That reasoning has not satisfied everyone. "The problem I have with the European Commission is that they keep the information about it internally and don't disclose it to the public," Dutch MEP Bart Groothuis of the Renew group told Euronews. "Each time they don't bring information to the public about intrusions that hit EU institutions, bodies and agencies, they miss an opportunity to explain and sell the economic security policy we set out as the EU against our adversaries."

Internally, the response was swift. The Commission convened its highest-level internal crisis-management body, the Corporate Management Board, for an urgent meeting, and Tech Commissioner Henna Virkkunen briefed fellow commissioners on the immediate response. CERT-EU and the European Data Protection Supervisor were both informed.

## A Pattern of Mounting Cyber Pressure

The attack is not an isolated incident but part of a wider surge of cyberattacks in Europe in late 2026, several of them increasingly AI-enabled. Earlier in 2026, the Commission's cloud infrastructure hosting its Europa.eu websites was compromised in March, resulting in the theft of around 91.7 GB of data, and in January an attack targeted its central mobile-device infrastructure, potentially exposing staff names and phone numbers.

On the same day the Commission confirmed the breach, the European Central Bank called in a report for stronger cooperation against cyberattacks, warning that "agentic" AI systems let hackers operate with growing efficiency as advanced models become cheap and accessible. Just weeks earlier, roughly ten European countries were hit by cyberattacks targeting supermarket refrigerators in food retail, and Belgium's own FPS Finance tax agency was targeted on 28 October 2026, disrupting digital services and filing deadlines.

That context amplifies the significance of the Commission incident: it is both a warning about the vulnerabilities of Europe's core institutions and a live test of whether the EU's economic-security messaging can survive its own instinct for secrecy.

## What to Watch

Four questions remain open. Was any personal data actually exfiltrated from Sysper or JSIS, and if so, whose? Who is responsible — a state-linked actor or a criminal group? Has the attacker been fully evicted, or is the campaign still active? And will the Commission revise its disclosure policy in the face of mounting criticism from lawmakers?

For now, the answers are taking a little longer. But as the AI tools that power both defence and attack grow cheaper and more accessible, the pressure on Europe's institutions to be both more secure and more transparent is only likely to intensify.
