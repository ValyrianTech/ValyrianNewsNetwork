# China, Vietnam Conclude 41st Beibu Gulf Joint Naval Patrol

The Chinese People's Liberation Army Navy and the Vietnam People's Navy completed their 41st joint patrol in the Beibu Gulf on 21 November, closing a two-day operation that covered 351 nautical miles and 32 hours of sailing, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261122/3f0d555b3fd84bf2a4471862e26405e9/c.html). The patrol, held along the settled China-Vietnam maritime demarcation line, is the latest iteration of a bilateral mechanism that has now run for two decades.

Each side deployed two naval vessels. The Chinese task force comprised the PLAN Wuzhou and the PLAN Enshi, which cruised in a double-column formation along the demarcation line alongside their Vietnamese counterparts.

## A Twenty-Year Mechanism

The patrol is a routine, treaty-based activity rather than a response to any specific incident. China and Vietnam signed the Agreement on Joint Patrols by the Navies of China and Vietnam in the Beibu Gulf in 2005, and the first patrol took place in 2006. This year's edition marks 20 years of the arrangement, which Xinhua described as having progressed “from exploratory running-in to institutionalization and regularization.”

The Beibu Gulf — known in the West as the Gulf of Tonkin — is a semi-enclosed body of water bordered by China's Guangxi and Guangdong provinces to the north and Vietnam to the west. In 2000, the two countries signed a maritime boundary delimitation agreement for the gulf, the first maritime boundary China ever resolved through bilateral negotiation. The 2005 naval patrol agreement built on that settled line, creating a framework for the two navies to jointly patrol and cooperate on safety at sea.

## Drills Test Coordination

This iteration combined the patrol with a series of drills designed to test operational coordination. On the night of 20 November, the two formations conducted a communications exercise, using light signals and VHF radio to exchange friendly messages. On 21 November, they carried out a joint search-and-rescue drill built around a simulated scenario in which each country had a fishing boat in distress with crew overboard. Both formations adjusted course and speed, formulated search plans, located the targets, and completed the rescue and transfer of personnel.

The ships also practiced formation maneuvering, transitioning in sequence among single-column, single-line-abreast, and double-column formations under agreed alternating-command rules. Throughout the patrol, the two sides periodically transferred command, exchanging course, speed, and other navigational data to maintain real-time awareness of the maritime situation.

The arrangement had been announced in advance by China's Ministry of National Defense, which said the patrol would include joint search-and-rescue, communications, and formation maneuvering drills and would “further enhance the two militaries' joint patrol coordination and their ability to jointly safeguard the security of the relevant sea areas,” according to [China Military Online](http://www.81.cn/yw_208727/16500787.html).

## A Showcase of Managed Maritime Relations

The operational scale of the patrol is modest — two ships per side, roughly 350 nautical miles over two days — but its significance lies in continuity and signaling. The Beibu Gulf is a zone where the two neighbors have resolved their boundary and cooperate routinely, in contrast to periodic friction farther south in the South China Sea, where the two countries continue to disagree over the Paracel and Spratly island groups. The gulf mechanism is therefore frequently cited in official discourse as evidence that China-Vietnam differences can be managed through bilateral channels.

A Chinese joint patrol formation commander framed the drills in those terms, saying that setting exercise subjects on top of the patrol “not only improves the operational coordination capability of Chinese and Vietnamese vessels” and deepens practical cooperation, “but also fully demonstrates the two sides' firm determination to work together to safeguard regional peace and stability.”

The mechanism has also broadened over time. During the 40th patrol in March 2026, held alongside the 10th China-Vietnam Border Defense Friendship Exchange, the two navies conducted their first-ever joint maritime training, including escorting simulated merchant vessels and engaging simulated “pirate” targets, [Global Times](https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202603/1357322.shtml) reported. That iteration covered 247 nautical miles over 28 hours, according to Vietnamese reports. The 41st patrol reverted to a patrol-plus-search-and-rescue format, suggesting the two sides may deliberately keep routine patrols and higher-end joint training on separate tracks.

## What's Next

Attention now turns to a separate but parallel mechanism: a Vietnam-China coast guard joint patrol, the second of 2026, scheduled for 24-26 November in waters adjacent to the Beibu Gulf demarcation line, [Vietnam.vn](https://www.vietnam.vn/canh-sat-bien-viet-nam-va-trung-quoc-tuan-tra-chung-tren-vung-bien-vinh-bac-bo) reported. The upcoming law-enforcement patrol operates under a different agreement from the navy arrangement and underscores how the two countries have layered cooperative mechanisms across their shared maritime frontier.

Whether either side will publicly link the Beibu Gulf model to proposals for managing disputes in the South China Sea proper remains an open question. For now, the 41st patrol stands as a reminder that even as the two neighbors navigate deeper disagreements to the south, their most settled maritime boundary continues to be patrolled jointly, on schedule, two decades on.
