# Belgium's Groen accepts nuclear as bridge to renewables

Belgium's Flemish green party Groen has formally abandoned its long-standing opposition to nuclear power, with members voting to accept atomic energy as a possible bridge towards a fully renewable supply. At a party congress in Antwerp on Saturday, 70 percent of voting members backed the shift, a decision that reverses one of the defining positions of the Belgian green movement.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/21/kernenergie-groen/), 394 of the 566 members present at the 'Over Hoop' ("About Hope") congress approved the proposal framing nuclear energy as a transition technology. The party insists its ultimate goal is unchanged: an energy system running entirely on renewable sources.

## A reversal rooted in the party's history

The vote marks a striking turn for a party founded as Agalev out of the environmental movement, in which resistance to nuclear plants held a central place. The nuclear phase-out, or *kernuitstap*, was one of Groen's most important campaigns: when Agalev joined the Verhofstadt I government in July 1999, phase-out agreements were written into the coalition accord.

The issue remained sensitive as recently as the 2022 energy crisis, when extending the lifespan of Belgian reactors proved especially difficult for Groen—then holding the federal energy portfolio through minister Tinne Van der Straeten. Under its 2024 election programme, the party accepted extending two reactors, Doel 4 and Tihange 3, but considered prolonging the other five technically and economically unfeasible.

## What the proposal says

The approved text, published on [Groen's own congress pages](https://www.groen.be/congresvoorstellen), is carefully conditional. It states that "the priority of Groen is a fossil-free and climate-neutral energy supply," with renewable energy as "the most important building block." Nuclear energy, it adds, "can, under certain conditions, play a role as a transition technology" to reach that goal faster.

The accompanying rationale offers the party's reasoning in its own words: while renewable energy remains the cheapest and fastest route to cutting emissions, "the world has also changed." It cites "anti-democratic forces" turning Europe's dependence on gas into "a geopolitical weapon," alongside sharply rising expected electricity demand. The nuclear phase-out, the text concedes, "started from justified concerns," but "insufficient preparation by successive governments without greens, and a changed context, mean we are adjusting our course."

Nuclear can be part of the transition only "if it is available quickly, safely, affordably, and with limited environmental impact," and its role is explicitly framed as temporary. For small modular reactors, the party sets strict conditions: rapid and cost-effective delivery, no costs or liabilities shifted onto government or society, full passive safety, no high-level long-lived radioactive waste, and no risk of nuclear weapons proliferation.

## 'Damn proud'

Party chair Aimen Horch framed the decision as part of a wider renewal. "I am damn proud of my party," he said, according to VRT NWS. "We had difficult conversations today, with great respect for all perspectives. I see a distinctly progressive, green party that dares to change."

Horch, elected to shape the party's future, added: "With an open mind, without taboos. And this is only the beginning."

The nuclear vote was one of nine proposals tabled at the congress. Members also approved measures to make electricity more affordable through a basic household consumption package—with the first consumption tier priced below higher tiers—and to make data centres contribute fairly through taxation and permitting conditions.

## Analysis: a calculated political bet

The shift reflects both changing geopolitics and domestic calculation. Nuclear energy has long been a fault line in Belgian politics, with N-VA advocating a renewables-and-nuclear mix and the Flemish regional government signalling a renewed nuclear push in March 2026. By softening its stance, Groen removes a longstanding point of attack while keeping its environmental identity intact—insisting nuclear is a means, not an end.

The conditions attached are the party's safeguard. By requiring that nuclear be fast, safe, affordable and low-impact—and by rejecting any transfer of costs or liabilities to the public—Groen leaves itself room to oppose specific projects it deems reckless while accepting the technology in principle. The party has already criticised what it called a "reckless" nationalisation of nuclear plants, showing that doctrinal acceptance does not mean blanket endorsement.

## What to watch

The decision will be tested as Belgium's energy debate moves from principle to practice: which reactors, at what cost, and under whose liability. Groen's conditions on cost-sharing and waste give it clear grounds to contest concrete plans. For a party whose green identity was built partly on opposing nuclear power, the coming months will show whether this bridge holds—or whether the base that approved it by 70 percent will accept the traffic it carries.
