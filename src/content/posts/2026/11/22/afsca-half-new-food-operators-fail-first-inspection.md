# Half of new Belgian food businesses fail first inspection

More than half of the food businesses that opened in Belgium this year failed their first food-safety inspection, according to a targeted enforcement and coaching campaign run by the country's federal food agency in October. Of 1,674 new operators visited, 52.9% received an unfavourable result on their first control, the [Federal Agency for the Safety of the Food Chain (Afsca)](https://favv-afsca.be/fr/publications/communiques-presse/l-afsca-controle-et-accompagne-les-nouveaux-operateurs) said in a press release on Sunday 22 November.

The agency was quick to stress that most shortcomings were minor and did not pose a serious danger to food safety. Sixteen establishments, however, were temporarily closed because of a serious risk to food safety.

## A campaign built around coaching, not just enforcement

The October operation was a planned, coordinated drive aimed specifically at businesses that had opened during 2026 and had never before been inspected. Throughout the month, inspectors focused almost exclusively on these newcomers. It was the first time Afsca had organised a nationally coordinated action with this orientation, and it had been announced in advance, with guidance published on the agency's website.

The target list was broad, covering establishments in direct contact with consumers: restaurants and other horeca businesses, collective canteens, shops, snack bars, bakeries, butchers, fishmongers and other food retailers, [RTL Info reported](https://www.rtl.be/actu/belgique/societe/sur-1674-controles-de-lafsca-plus-de-la-moitie-ne-respectaient-pas-les-regles-de/2026-11-22/article/807352).

Afsca framed the drive as much as an exercise in prevention as in enforcement. "This action has clearly demonstrated its usefulness: nearly half of new establishments are already on the right track, while for the others we see that additional support is needed," spokesperson Aline Van den Broeck said in the release. "By supplementing inspections with coaching and a broad offer of free (online) training, Afsca helps to instil good habits."

That emphasis was echoed in the field. During an earlier visit to a sandwich shop in Quaregnon, inspector Elisa Maton told [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/l-afsca-controle-les-nouveaux-commerces-alimentaires-en-ce-mois-d-octobre-objectif-inculquer-les-bonnes-pratiques-des-le-debut-11805023) that the agency was "also here to do some prevention". "Afsca always has a bad image, but we are here for food safety and for the consumer," she said.

## Where new businesses slip up

The shortcomings identified clustered around a handful of recurring problems. The most common concerned the cleanliness of surfaces that do not directly contact food, such as windows, doors, ceilings and floors. Protection of foodstuffs against contamination, including through closed packaging and adequate storage, was also a recurring weak point. In addition, inspectors found insufficient knowledge of self-monitoring, or HACCP, obligations.

Allergen information was a particular concern. Of 1,501 allergen-management checks, 39% were unfavourable, meaning consumers were not correctly informed about allergens. As Van den Broeck put it in October, the "common weak point we often find is really the cleanliness of infrastructure and good practices for handling and storing foodstuffs".

## What happened to failing businesses

Enforcement followed a graduated scale. Of the operators with unfavourable results, 46.2% received a formal warning, while an official report (procès-verbal) was drawn up for 6.6%. Businesses that failed are re-inspected to verify that shortcomings have been corrected.

For context, among all Afsca controls carried out in 2025, about 57% were favourable and 1% led to a temporary closure, with the rest receiving a warning and a deadline to comply. That figure covers the entire control population, however, and is not directly comparable to a first inspection of brand-new businesses, which are more likely to make early-stage errors.

## A ministry endorsement — and a nuance worth noting

The campaign drew political backing. "Starting out in the food sector requires knowledge, rigour, but also support," said David Clarinval, deputy prime minister and agriculture minister, in the release. "This first coordinated action combines inspection and support. It protects the consumer while giving new entrepreneurs the means to establish themselves sustainably."

Afsca itself presented the headline figure in a markedly different light from the "half fail" framing. Its press release was titled "Afsca inspects and supports new food-sector operators: half succeed the first inspection", framing the glass as half full. The release also noted that for 48% of new establishments the basic control proved unfavourable, a figure slightly below the 52.9% overall unfavourable rate — a reminder that the topline number aggregates several types of checks.

## What to watch

Afsca, which has monitored the food chain from producer to consumer for 25 years, carries out more than 103,800 inspections a year with about 1,400 staff and analyses some 62,000 product samples. All businesses that failed in October now face follow-up inspections. The coming months will show whether the combination of checks and coaching translates into better compliance among this cohort — and whether a first-of-its-kind campaign becomes a standing feature of how Belgium brings new food entrepreneurs up to standard.
