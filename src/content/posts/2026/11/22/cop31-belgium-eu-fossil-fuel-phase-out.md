# COP31: Belgium and EU fight to keep fossil fuel phase-out

Belgium and roughly twenty other countries have joined forces at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, to reinstate an explicit commitment to phase out oil, gas and coal in the final agreement—a reference that vanished from the latest draft text. The European Union formally rejected the draft accord overnight, and the conference has now become the longest climate summit in history as consensus collapsed.

According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/cop31-a-antalya-la-belgique-et-une-vingtaine-de-pays-haussent-le-ton-pour-reinscrire-la-sortie-du-petrole-et-du-gaz-dans-le-projet-d-accord-11818457), the fight over fossil fuels has become the central sticking point of this year's talks. The Turkish presidency's draft made no explicit mention of phasing out oil, gas and coal—three years after countries agreed in Dubai to transition away from these fuels, which are the primary driver of global warming.

## A step backward

The EU's rejection was announced on behalf of the bloc by Irish Climate Minister Darragh O'Brien, whose country currently holds the EU presidency. "We will not support an agreement that does not even mention fossil fuels, the main cause of climate change," O'Brien said. "We will not accept a step back in time. And we will not accept a climate conference that greenwashes fossil fuels."

Belgian federal Climate Minister Jean-Luc Crucke went further, denouncing the text as a retreat from science. "The text submitted to us last night is unacceptable. It is not simply a text lacking ambition: it is a step backwards," he said, as reported by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/rejet-de-l-accord-final-de-la-cop31-par-l-ue-jean-luc-crucke-denonce-un-retour-en-arriere-et-un-texte-qui-tourne-le-dos-a-la-science-11819351). "It turns its back on science, ignores the main cause of global warming and would lock us durably into our dependence on fossil fuels."

## Two coalitions, one text

The dispute has crystallised into two camps. On one side sit the EU and a coalition of about twenty countries, including Belgium, which have drawn up or committed to a roadmap to concretise the transition away from fossil fuels. Pressure also comes from a second group—France, Panama and the Solomon Islands—which published an open letter demanding the final decision mention a "clear transition away from fossil fuels" and "clean and immediate electrification."

On the other side are oil-producing nations, notably Saudi Arabia and Russia, which have resisted any explicit reference to fossil fuels. Saudi Arabia has positioned itself as a voice for developing countries, criticising the absence of a firm figure for climate adaptation finance and defending carbon capture technology against the EU's "greenwashing" charge.

Caught in the middle are developing countries demanding climate finance. Negotiators from poorer nations had sought a commitment of $120 billion by 2035 for adaptation, a figure that does not appear in the draft.

## Carbon capture at the heart of the fight

A key point of contention is carbon capture and storage (CCS), which oil-producing nations succeeded in writing into the text. Critics see the technology as a stopgap that lets countries keep burning fossil fuels. Crucke sought to draw a line: "Carbon capture is necessary for certain hard-to-decarbonise industries. The IPCC and Europe recognise this. But it cannot become an alibi to prolong our dependence on oil, gas and coal."

EU Climate Commissioner Wopke Hoekstra framed the rejection around the credibility of the UN process itself. "It is essential for the credibility of these processes that we do not let everything go and that we do not accept that things we agreed before are simply deleted," he said.

## A record overtime, and a diminished room

By 14:00 Turkish time on Sunday, COP31 had become the longest climate conference ever, surpassing the COP25 in Madrid in 2019, which ran 44 hours past its scheduled end. COP President Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister, opened the closing plenary by acknowledging the impasse. "We recognise that there is no consensus on this document. As a consequence, these decisions are not adopted," he said.

The drawn-out talks have taken a toll on the negotiating room. As delegations from poorer countries depart—unable to afford extra nights in Antalya—their voice in the final outcome diminishes, raising concerns about quorum and the shrinking influence of the Global South. Environmental groups have been scathing. Mohamed Adow of Power Shift Africa called COP31 "without doubt one of the worst COPs ever."

## Analysis: testing the credibility of the COP process

The confrontation cuts to a deeper question: whether the UN climate process can still deliver on the commitments it makes. COP31 was meant to build on the COP28 Dubai pledge—the first COP text to name all fossil fuels. Instead, the latest draft substitutes an explicit phase-out with a mere reference to the earlier Dubai decision, a retreat that the EU says undermines trust in the entire negotiation cycle. This is a repeat of the dynamic at COP30 in Belém last year, where the EU threatened a veto over a missing phase-out roadmap but ultimately accepted an agreement without one.

A summit ending without agreement would be historic—only COP15 in Copenhagen (2009) and COP6 in The Hague (2000) failed to reach consensus. With the EU and small island states like the Marshall Islands and Panama now openly rejecting the text—the Marshall Islands' representative warned that "accepting this deal would be a betrayal of the people of the Marshall Islands"—the no-deal scenario is closer than at any recent summit.

Belgium's role is notable for a small federal state that has no direct vote in the plenary, where only the EU speaks. Yet it has shaped the European position and helped lead the coalition of twenty. Crucke announced he was leaving Antalya, declaring "the game is over, as far as we are concerned," though his negotiating team remains on site. He has insisted the fight is not finished: "The COP is not over. We remain mobilised should an alternative text be submitted to us."

## What's next

The one substantive achievement still in play is the so-called 35x35 electrification goal—raising electricity's share of total final energy consumption worldwide to 35 percent by 2035. But its downgrade to a voluntary option, without a guarantee that the electricity is generated cleanly, has drawn criticism from environmental groups and from Belgium, which wants the target anchored in renewables.

Negotiations continue in the hope that a compromise or alternative text may yet emerge. The open question is whether the EU will follow through on its veto threat—or fold, as it did in Belém. For now, the Antalya summit stands as a test of whether the world's climate machinery can still hold the line it drew in Dubai.
