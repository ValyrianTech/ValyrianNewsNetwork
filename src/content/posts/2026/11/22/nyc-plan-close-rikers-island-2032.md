# NYC's Bold Plan to Close Rikers by 2032

New York City officials on Friday unveiled an ambitious plan to close the troubled Rikers Island jail complex and relocate its detainees to four smaller, more humane borough-based jails by the end of 2032, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/20/nx-s1-5981356/new-york-city-rikers-jail-plans-germany-prison-model). The proposal, released by Mayor Zohran Mamdani, draws inspiration from Germany's prison system and seeks to dramatically reduce the city's detainee population—while formally pushing back a closure deadline the City Council set seven years ago.

## A decades-old promise, finally on a path

The effort to shut Rikers is not new. The island jail complex, which sits in the East River between Queens and the Bronx just 200 feet from a LaGuardia Airport runway, opened in 1932 and has long been a symbol of violence, neglect, and overcrowding. Campaigners have pushed for its closure for more than a decade, and in 2019 the City Council voted to close Rikers, mandating a December 31, 2026 deadline that was later extended to 2027.

While federal court oversight now governs the jail's operations—a judge last year stripped the city of its authority over Rikers—the new plan marks the most concrete roadmap yet to finish the job. "It is long past time to close what's been a moral stain on our city," Mamdani told NPR. "We cannot simply accept the old approaches to public safety just because they've always been done that way."

## What the plan entails

According to the [Mayor's Office](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/11/mayor-mamdani-releases-city-s-first-comprehensive-plan-to-close-), the plan commits to opening four borough-based jails by the end of 2032, beginning with Brooklyn in 2029. Each facility will be closer to courts, public transit, and the communities where incarcerated New Yorkers live, making it easier for people to maintain family relationships and meet with attorneys—factors the city says improve outcomes after release.

The new jails are designed to be safer and easier to supervise, with fewer beds per housing unit, individual cells for greater privacy, dedicated programming space, and access to outdoor areas. Each site will also include at least 20,000 square feet of community space open to neighborhood residents, and the city will centralize women in custody at the Queens facility.

Beyond construction, the plan invests in people. The administration will direct $146 million next fiscal year toward mental health care, substance use treatment, education, and job training—both inside jails and in communities—alongside building hundreds of therapeutic beds for New Yorkers with serious mental illness or complex medical needs. The city will also restore its online bail payment portal, which has been unavailable since 2023, making it easier for families to post bail without unnecessary delays.

## Inspired by Germany

The plan's emphasis on human dignity reflects lessons drawn from abroad. The remediation manager overseeing Rikers, Nicholas Deml, and Correction Commissioner Stanley Richards traveled to Germany earlier this year on a trip organized by the Vera Institute of Justice, observing prisons regarded as an international model.

"While German correctional systems are not directly transferable to New York, they reflect a fundamentally different orientation—one that places human dignity at the center of incarceration," Deml wrote in his plan, according to NPR. At Berlin's Moabit jail, visitors noted quiet hallways and cells with windows to the outside, some connected to adjacent cells by an interior door so detained people can talk to a neighbor.

## The stakes and the skepticism

The plan's success hinges on shrinking the jail population enough to fit the smaller facilities. NPR reported that the new jails will hold about 2,000 fewer people than the roughly 6,600 Rikers holds today, and the city is investing more than $250 million over the coming years in diversion programs, electronic monitoring, and alternatives to incarceration. Yet critics warn the city has no backup plan if it fails to reduce the population.

"It's really hard to have the guts to forecast lower incarceration numbers. Because if you are wrong, then you have crowded jails and you're in trouble," Vincent Schiraldi, a former city correction commissioner, told NPR. Others worry that moving the same system into new buildings could simply move the problem of violence along with it. The city says it will address that through more staff training, tighter oversight, and expanded programming.

Some also note the political sensitivity of the delay. Mamdani, as a candidate, once protested the construction of new borough jails; he now commits to building them, and the proposal to push the legal deadline to 2032 could invite legal and political challenges.

## What's next

Mamdani has already taken early steps, including permanently closing one Rikers facility in June 2026 and appointing Richards—the first formerly incarcerated person to lead the Department of Correction. The City Council is expected to introduce legislation updating the legal timeline to match construction. For supporters, the plan represents a long-awaited chance to turn the page. "If New York City can do this at scale, which it will, it is encouragement for places like LA, like Chicago, like Philadelphia, like Houston to do the same," Insha Rahman of the Vera Institute of Justice told NPR.

What remains to be seen is whether the city can reduce its jail population quickly enough—and whether the culture of the island can be left behind as the buildings are torn down.
