# Trump, Xi and Putin Meet, but No Nuclear Breakthrough

The leaders of the world's three largest nuclear powers sat down together for the first time on Thursday, but their unprecedented three-way summit in the southern Chinese city of Shenzhen produced little beyond symbolism and a modest joint declaration. President Donald Trump, China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin met for roughly 70 minutes behind closed doors at the Kylin Villa on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, with the wars in Ukraine and Iran and the dangers of nuclear arsenals on the agenda, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-xi-putin-nuclear-ukraine-iran-a04dc76ed8e786b087e2c1ea9598783a).

## A summit billed as historic

The meeting was cast by its participants as a historic moment in great-power diplomacy. "We are really making history," Trump said at the start of the session. "The United States, Russia and China are uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on Earth and also to lessen the risk of those weapons being used."

Before the trio convened, Trump held separate bilateral meetings with Xi — their third substantial encounter this year — and with Putin, their first since a failed summit in Alaska last year. He repeatedly referred to both leaders as friends. Xi greeted Putin at the APEC summit as "my old friend," and the Russian leader called Xi his "dear friend," a warmth that analysts noted exceeded the reception Trump received during his September state visit to Washington.

The joint statement that followed, released through Chinese state media and reported by [CBC News](https://www.cbc.ca/news/world/trump-xi-putin-apec-meeting-9.7427718), contained four broad commitments. The three countries pledged to reform the United Nations so it can "respond more effectively to global challenges"; to increase the production and supply of refined petroleum products while reaffirming stable energy flows; to affirm that "the International Criminal Court and other similar bodies must not abuse their jurisdiction"; and to "conduct a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons," restating that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought."

## Substance lagged behind the rhetoric

Despite the lofty framing, the summit's deliverables were narrow. As [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/live/cmlye926e2eet) reported, its North America correspondent concluded that the joint communique was "mostly a vague endorsement" of strategic stability, with pledges of further "dialogue" providing "minimal substance beyond the rhetoric." Trump left the villa and boarded Air Force One without speaking to reporters on-site, later calling the visit "Highly Consequential" on social media and promising a Friday press conference at the White House.

The nuclear pledge, in particular, amounted to a statement of intent rather than an agreement. The last remaining U.S.-Russia arms control treaty, New START, expired in February this year, leaving the world's two largest arsenals uncapped for the first time in more than half a century. Trump has said he wants a "new, improved and modernized" deal that includes China, but Beijing has little incentive to accept limits: its arsenal of more than 600 warheads is far smaller than the 5,000-plus believed to be held by both Washington and Moscow.

"We need more than diplomatic theater and photo opportunities between Xi, Putin and Trump," said Daryl Kimball, executive director of the Arms Control Association. "And one meeting will not likely produce dramatic breakthroughs."

## Stalemate on Ukraine and Iran

Neither of the wars that were nominally central to the agenda yielded any agreed path forward. Trump told U.S. envoys and more than 40 European leaders gathered in Berlin — after dialing into their meeting on Ukraine — that he had discussed a peaceful end to the war with Xi and Putin. Yet as Putin spoke of peace in China, Russian drones were striking Kyiv, in what the BBC's eastern Europe correspondent described as some of the most intense daytime attacks in months.

The joint declaration did not commit the three powers to any measure to end either conflict, and no concrete agreement on the wars emerged from the talks. Ahead of the summit, the White House had signaled that the two conflicts would be handled on different tracks: a pre-summit preview in [The Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/international/article-914839) reported that Trump intended to raise the war in Iran with Xi and Putin, while Ukraine would be pursued separately on "a whole nother track" in which, a senior official said, "China is not really a part of." Both wars were nonetheless on the stated agenda going in, and both emerged from Shenzhen without a negotiating pathway.

The energy commitment in the declaration appeared aimed at addressing fallout from Trump's decision to wage war on Iran with Israel in February, which disrupted commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and pushed up global oil prices. Trump said he and Xi shared an interest in keeping oil flowing freely through the waterway, but no concrete measures accompanied the pledge.

## Putin emerges as the chief beneficiary

Of the three leaders, Putin appeared to gain the most. Wanted by the International Criminal Court over the war in Ukraine and largely isolated from Western capitals, he stood shoulder to shoulder with the American and Chinese presidents — an image that analysts said conferred legitimacy. "We need more than diplomatic theater," Kimball said, but for the Russian leader the theater itself carried value.

"The pushback against international norms and courts is the most interesting aspect," said Jonathan Eyal, international director at the Royal United Services Institute in London. "The big powers dictate, the small powers suffer." Eyal said Putin "gained credibility and escape from pariah status," while Trump "gained a photo opportunity he didn't need."

The three powers' shared assault on the ICC's jurisdiction represented the summit's most concrete — and, for European and NATO capitals, most unsettling — outcome. Putin faces an arrest warrant from the court, and the Trump administration has imposed sanctions on ICC officials as part of what it describes as a campaign to dismantle the tribunal.

The reaction abroad was swift and pointed. Poland's Prime Minister Donald Tusk wrote on X: "God, protect us from such friends." U.S. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said Xi and Putin were "playing Donald Trump like a cheap violin." Japan's Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, who held a 30-minute bilateral with Putin, insisted that "Japan's interests will not be compromised." Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, addressing Putin directly at APEC, said simply: "End your war, end it now."

## Optics over outcomes

Several analysts questioned whether the meeting would leave any lasting mark beyond its imagery, which some had billed as a modern "Yalta 2.0." Writing in [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-19/trump-xi-putin-meeting-live-blog-apec-summit/107394202), the broadcaster's Americas editor noted that Trump went into the talks with "little on the agenda and no clear asks — and it appears that he left with little in return," with the visuals "the only lasting impression left by the summit."

James Laurenceson, director of the Australia-China Relations Institute at the University of Technology Sydney, put it bluntly: "I can't help but think the biggest thing that's going to come out of it is a photo. And for Donald Trump, that's probably what he's most interested in." Melissa Conley Tyler of the University of Melbourne's Asia Institute saw more at stake — "the fact that you've got the right people in the room" to potentially end wars in Ukraine, Iran and the unresolved standoff over Taiwan, she said, "makes it fascinating," even if it probably won't.

For Beijing, the summit served a different purpose. Alicja Bachulska of the European Council on Foreign Relations said China wanted to be seen as "a convener of the major powers, without necessarily committing to, or being seen as endorsing, either Moscow's or Washington's key interests."

## What's next

Attention now shifts to the G20 summit at Trump National Doral in Miami on Dec. 14–15, where Trump and Xi are expected to meet again. Putin said he would consider attending but needs to weigh the situation in Russia — an invitation that would likely unsettle U.S. allies. The near-term test will be whether this first three-way encounter becomes a recurring format capable of producing real arms control progress, or whether it remains, as critics charge, a well-staged photograph. For now, the world's biggest nuclear risks remain as unresolved as before the leaders gathered.
