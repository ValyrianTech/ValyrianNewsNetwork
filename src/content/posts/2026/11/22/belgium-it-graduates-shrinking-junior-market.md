# Belgium's IT Graduates Face a Shrinking Junior Market

Young IT graduates in Belgium are struggling to secure their first jobs, even as the developer profession remains officially listed among Wallonia's "shortage occupations." Junior positions are growing scarcer while the number of graduates rises, creating intense competition in a market increasingly reshaped by artificial intelligence, according to an [RTBF investigation](https://www.rtbf.be/article/plus-besoin-de-juniors-les-jeunes-diplomes-en-informatique-peinent-a-trouver-un-premier-emploi-11815000) published on 22 November.

## Fewer offers, more candidates

The numbers tell a stark story. Wallonia's public employment service, Le Forem, recorded 2,128 IT-sector job offers since the start of the year — a 34% decline compared with 2025. In Brussels, Actiris observes a "marked decline in application-developer job offers since 2024," while "the number of jobseekers in this profession is rising sharply among the under-30s," according to its spokesperson Romain Adam.

For individual graduates, the squeeze is personal. "It's clear there are far fewer offers than in 2020," says Baptiste, who holds a master's degree in computer science specialising in data sciences from Namur. "We were told we'd have no trouble finding a job." Tonesko, who graduated with an IT master's from UMons in September, echoes the sentiment: "Junior positions aren't very numerous — and those asking for less than a year of experience are even rarer."

The result is fierce competition. "We quickly reach a hundred applicants for the same offer," Baptiste says.

## An experience paradox

Employers are simultaneously raising the bar. Of 128 offers for IT analysts, only 27 are accessible without experience. For IT support roles, positions requiring no experience account for just one-third of offers. Actiris confirms "a more frequent demand for several years of experience."

Gregory Seront, an IT professor and internship coordinator at Haute Ecole Léonard de Vinci, captures the absurdity: "Some companies ask for juniors with ten years of experience." Beyond experience, graduates face a skills mismatch. "The skills demanded for a junior profile are often different from what we learned at university," Baptiste notes. Tonesko adds that language can be another barrier: "I don't speak Dutch, and some positions require that skill."

## When AI does the junior's job

The central puzzle — a shortage occupation with no room for newcomers — points to a structural shift. "The tasks young graduates do at the very start are very technical and standardised," explains Frédéric Verschueren, a macroeconomist at IWEPS, the Walloon Institute for Evaluation, Forecasting and Statistics. "And these are precisely the tasks that are easily automated by artificial intelligence."

Verschueren cites a [US study](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/18723/generative-ai-and-the-redefinition-of-entry-level-software-work) showing a 14–15% relative decline in job offers for junior developers versus seniors since the arrival of ChatGPT — a trend Actiris has observed since 2024. The research, published as IZA Discussion Paper No. 18723 in June 2026, used near-universe US online vacancy data and found that rising experience requirements were driven by employers demanding more experience within the same job titles, not by a shift in the mix of roles.

AI boosts individual productivity, reducing the need for intermediaries. Baptiste experiences this first-hand: while waiting for a contract, he designed an online Advent-calendar platform entirely alone. "I can launch this kind of project from A to Z, completely alone, in a few days and on a reduced budget, whereas a few years ago it would have taken far more time and people."

## The junior profile in mutation

"The IT profession will not disappear," Verschueren cautions — but developers' skills are evolving. Faster and cheaper AI tools generate quality code, shifting human value toward oversight. "AI writes the code, the developer verifies it," Seront summarises. So-called soft skills — client contact, needs analysis, communication — are increasingly appearing in job listings, and employers also expect juniors to handle supervision, testing and debugging.

Cybersecurity is another growth demand. "Using AI at national scale can pose a problem," Seront warns over security flaws; Belgium has recorded 588 cyberincidents since the start of the year, up from 2025, as the [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/cyberattaques-une-seule-faille-peut-suffire-alerte-le-patron-de-la-force-cyber-belge-11807980).

## Education adapts, slowly

Higher education is trying to keep pace. At UCLouvain, AI is not banned from lecture halls. "Nowadays, when producing code, we do it in collaboration with AI," says Pierre Dupont, a professor at the Ecole Polytechnique de Louvain. Its policy: students may use any tool but must cite it, or risk being accused of plagiarism — a rule Dupont admits is "easily circumvented." Oral exams are used to test real understanding without AI assistance.

Colleges face practical constraints. "Some students are already well advanced in their bachelor's; we cannot change everything at once," Seront says. For his part, Tonesko watched his own curriculum evolve: "At the start of my studies, AI wasn't yet deployed as it is today; we mostly studied theoretical concepts. Toward the end, we began to see concrete AI applications in software development."

## A structural, not cyclical, shift

A federal report reinforces the trend. In February 2026, the Superior Employment Council (CSE) published its analysis of AI and the Belgian labour market, finding that nearly 35% of Belgian companies used AI in 2025 — up 20 percentage points in two years — and that one in three workers used generative AI professionally. Yet institutional adoption remains uneven, concentrated in large firms and sectors such as ICT, scientific and technical activities.

Crucially, the [CSE report](https://emploi.belgique.be/fr/actualites/rapport-du-conseil-superieur-de-lemploi-lintelligence-artificielle-et-le-marche-du), released by the SPF Emploi, Travail et Concertation sociale, warns that "opportunities for young workers in professions highly exposed to AI appear more limited, complicating the early stages of their career paths." While there is no general collapse in employment yet, the effects are unequal — and the young bear a disproportionate share.

The report also flags a skills bottleneck: 39% of workers say they need more AI knowledge, but only 14% have received training, and Belgian youth digital skills sit below the European average.

## What to watch

The squeeze risks a "junior hiring trap": if companies stop hiring entry-level talent, the future supply of experienced developers may dry up. For now, graduates like Baptiste and Tonesko keep adapting — building projects, learning the tools and staying informed. "Employers are looking for candidates who can adapt to these new technologies," Tonesko says, determined to "stay competitive in the market." The question is whether employers and education systems can move as fast as the technology reshaping what it means to be a junior.
