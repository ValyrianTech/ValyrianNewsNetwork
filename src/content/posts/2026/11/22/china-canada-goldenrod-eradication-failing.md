# China's 10-Year War on an Invasive Flower Is Failing

Driving toward the Honggutan district of Nanchang, capital of Jiangxi province, yellow blooms blanket the wasteland along the highway, impossible to miss. The plant is Canada goldenrod (*Solidago canadensis*), one of China's most notorious invasive species—and after nearly a decade of dedicated eradication campaigns, it is still spreading, according to [an in-depth investigation by The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34491597).

The findings, published Sunday, expose a widening gap between policy on paper and enforcement on the ground: chronic underfunding, fragmented responsibility across government departments, and removal methods too shallow to kill a resilient perennial that can regenerate from its roots.

## An official reassurance, and a contradictory reality

On Nov. 17, an official from the Nanchang Agriculture and Rural Affairs Bureau told The Paper that the city had surveyed more than 8,000 mu (roughly 533 hectares) of Canada goldenrod infestation this year and cleared over 4,000 mu (about 267 hectares) as of two weeks prior. The recent weeks had not yet been tallied, the official said, describing the situation as "basically alleviated."

Yet a county-level agricultural worker offered a starkly different picture. Last year, the county cleared more than 500 mu; this year's survey found more than 900 mu—a near doubling of detected infestation. "We've handled 300-plus mu this year and are still working," the official said.

A villager in Louxia Xincun, in the Honggutan district, said the flowers appeared four or five years ago and have proved impossible to root out. "This plant has a big impact; with it here, nothing you cultivate will grow," the villager told The Paper. Villagers clear the flowers on their own farmland, but nobody manages roadsides, house surroundings, or wasteland—which then re-infects the fields.

## A decade of expansion, not containment

The trajectory is well documented. According to Jiangxi Daily reporting cited by The Paper, in 2005 the plant was found in just four counties across three prefecture-level cities, covering a few hundred mu. By the first half of 2015, surveyed area had reached more than 6,000 mu—a tenfold expansion in a decade.

Large areas were detected in Nanchang as early as 2018, and organized clearing began then. From 2021 onward, the city has run near-annual special eradication campaigns, with limited success. The city's own 2023 data illustrates the shortfall: a survey covering 251 hectares in October–December 2023 resulted in just 4.58 hectares cleared—about 1.8 percent of the infested area.

A city resident told The Paper that the flowers "seem to be growing more and more, even approaching the urban area."

## Why the campaign keeps losing ground

The biology of Canada goldenrod makes campaign-style cleanup a poor match. A single plant can produce tens of thousands of seeds that survive in the soil for years, and it spreads via underground rhizomes. Cutting only the above-ground growth leaves the roots alive, so the plant regrows the following season.

National guidance acknowledges this. A technical directive issued by the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs' Ecological Station—[republished by the Jiangxi Provincial Department of Agriculture and Rural Affairs](https://nync.jiangxi.gov.cn/jxsnynct/hbny/content/content_1991431277512757248.html)—calls for deep mechanical tillage to remove rhizomes, followed by targeted herbicide, and replanting to prevent re-invasion. It stresses that because the plant grows mainly on abandoned village land and roadsides, government action alone cannot cover everything; mass public participation is essential.

But effective treatment requires synchronized, region-wide action—and funding does not allow it. A county agricultural official said the county's total annual budget for all invasive species is just 40,000 yuan (about US$5,600), which must also cover other invasives such as the golden apple snail. "Every cent has been allocated," the official said. Spraying alone costs several tens of yuan per mu.

A Nanchang Agriculture Bureau official conceded the plant is "very hard to eradicate" and that, limited by funding, "it's simply impossible to plow and clear synchronously and comprehensively."

## Fragmented authority creates blind spots

Responsibility for clearing is split across jurisdictions: urban parks fall to the city management and forestry departments; roadsides to transport departments and sub-district offices; homesteads and wasteland to village committees. This fragmentation creates what the guidance calls "blind spots" in prevention and control.

A [notice issued by the Jiangxi Provincial Department of Agriculture and Rural Affairs](https://nync.jiangxi.gov.cn/jxsnynct/tzgg/content/content_2116084303398502400.html) on Oct. 30, 2026, urged prefecture-level agriculture bureaus to strengthen coordination across agriculture, housing and urban management, forestry, ecology, water resources, transport, and railways—explicitly to "eliminate blind spots." The notice arrived as the plant entered its peak season, the key window for cutting flower heads before seeds mature.

## The broader stakes

Canada goldenrod was introduced to East China as an ornamental plant in the 1930s and later escaped into the wild, forming single-species dominant stands across eastern, central, and southwestern China. It was added to the *Key Management List of Invasive Alien Species* in 2022 by six ministries including MARA. It remains in a "rapid expansion phase," now spreading north into parts of North, Northwest, and Northeast China, and officials are monitoring newer, earlier-flowering diploid variants detected in the north and northwest.

The plant degrades ecosystems, outcompetes native flora, reduces crop yields and quality, and its pollen can aggravate respiratory ailments in autumn and winter. The guidance also notes an auxiliary use: harvested goldenrod can feed Hu sheep, with protein content of 11.4–14.7 percent—comparable to peanut vine—though this is a supplementary measure, not eradication.

## What to watch

The case illustrates a recurring pattern in Chinese environmental governance: robust top-level policy exists, but local implementation is hamstrung by thin budgets, divided authority, and the mismatch between annual cleanup cycles and a perennial, wind-dispersed invader. It remains unclear what Nanchang's true total infested area is—officials admit it is "hard to count accurately." Whether the revised national guidance will be backed by dedicated, ring-fenced funding, or remain an unfunded mandate, will determine whether the next decade looks any different from the last.
