# Trump Backs 10% Credit Card Rate Cap With Democrats

President Donald Trump said Saturday that he would work with the incoming Democratic-controlled Congress to cap credit card interest rates at 10%, reviving a populist campaign promise he failed to deliver when Republicans last held the levers of power. "I would do it. These credit card companies have made a lot," Trump told Fox News Channel's "Saturday in America," according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/trump-credit-cards-interest-rates-warren-banks-098a69b520e520e535eebe595cd0c64a).

The pledge lands amid a dramatic shift in Washington's balance of power: Democrats won both the House and Senate in the midterms and take control in January 2027, clearing away Republican leaders who had blocked the idea. The proposal could reshape a consumer credit market in which Americans carry roughly $1.23 trillion in card debt and pay median interest rates near 22%.

## A Promise Revived

Trump first floated the 10% cap on the 2024 campaign trail, then formally proposed it in January as part of the White House's affordability push, calling for a one-year ceiling to take effect on January 20. That deadline passed without action. Banks and card issuers immediately and aggressively opposed the plan, arguing that a hard cap would force them to cancel cards for less-creditworthy customers or sharply cut the rewards they offer, AP reported.

Resistance also came from within Trump's own party, and the GOP-controlled Congress made little effort to advance the measure. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the cap was "not something I'm out there advocating for," while Sen. Thom Tillis of North Carolina said he was "totally against" it.

Now the political math has inverted. Sen. Elizabeth Warren, the Massachusetts Democrat expected to chair the Senate Banking Committee, has repeatedly said she wants to cap rates. In response to Trump's Fox News interview, she said: "Let's cap credit card interest rates and save Americans money." Warren had signaled her readiness earlier this month, telling CBS's "Face the Nation" on Nov. 8 that "President Trump himself said that he wants to reduce interest rates on credit cards to 10 percent. I got the bill. I'm ready to go."

The idea is not exclusively Democratic. Sen. Josh Hawley of Missouri, who has introduced his own rate-cap legislation, posted on X: "The President is right. Let's pass my bill to cap credit card interest fees." Rep. Maxine Waters of California has also endorsed rate caps.

## What a Cap Would Mean for Consumers

A 10% ceiling would sit far below prevailing market rates. The median credit card interest rate was roughly 22.2% in an August survey by the New York Federal Reserve. Researchers who studied Trump's pledge found that Americans would save roughly $100 billion in interest a year if rates were capped at 10%, and that while the industry would take a major hit, it would remain profitable—though rewards and perks might be scaled back.

About 195 million Americans had credit cards in 2024 and were assessed $160 billion in interest charges, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. With balances at record highs, affordability has become a central political theme, and the midterm results reflected voters' demand for economic relief.

## The Industry Pushes Back

The financial sector remains the chief obstacle. The American Bankers Association "continues to strongly oppose a government-imposed cap on credit card interest rates," warning in a statement that the "real-world result would be reduced access to safe, affordable credit for millions of Americans, particularly those with lower credit scores or limited credit histories." The Consumer Bankers Association has argued a 10% cap could restrict access to roughly 75% of current cardholders, and JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon has warned of a drastic contraction in the credit card business.

Even some consumer advocates counsel caution. Jennifer Doss, a credit card analyst at CardRatings.com, told AP that a 10% cap "sounds like a win for anyone carrying a balance at today's 20 percent+ average interest rates" and offers "immediate relief," but she cautioned about potential "hangover effects."

## The Road Ahead

Democratic control of Congress improves the odds but does not guarantee enactment. Trump would still need to sign whatever passes, Republican and industry opposition remains substantial, and the details of any bill—duration, scope, and whether a cap applies to existing balances—are unresolved. Trump's original proposal was a one-year cap; Hawley has pushed a five-year ceiling; Warren's bill specifics have not been detailed publicly.

For Democrats, the calculation is also political. A memo from Congressional Progressive Caucus chair Rep. Greg Casar urged members to send Trump populist bills like the credit card cap: "If he signs them, good. If he vetoes those bills, that will propel us to a continued Democratic majority."

For now, the unusual alignment between Trump and progressive lawmakers has put a once-longshot idea closer to reality than at any point in recent memory. What to watch: whether Warren's committee advances a bill early in the new session, how Trump's own proposal takes legislative shape, and whether banks succeed in narrowing the cap's scope before it reaches the president's desk.
