# Iran Widens Gulf Attacks, Striking US Base in Kuwait

Iran widened its aerial campaign across the Persian Gulf this week, striking a US air base in Kuwait and launching multiple attacks on Bahrain in retaliation for a US strike on Iran's Qeshm Island the previous day, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/iran-us-kuwait-gulf-attacks-bahrain-b1f0b530cffe951e6fcc1e27b2e1af94). The strikes mark a sharp escalation in a war that began with US-Israeli attacks on Iran in late February and have renewed fears that the conflict could engulf the broader region and global energy markets.

## A Tit-for-Tat Cycle

The latest exchange followed a familiar pattern. On Tuesday, Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) launched one-way attack drones at a US radar installation at Bahrain's Sheikh Isa Air Base, citing US drone incursions into Iranian airspace. US Central Command said the drones were intercepted with no damage or injuries. US fighter jets then struck an IRGC missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, which a US military official described as "self-defense" in response to aggression against US personnel and assets.

By Wednesday, Iran had widened its reach. Iranian military drones targeted Patriot air defense systems and drone and missile launch platforms at Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait, per Iran's state broadcaster IRIB. Kuwait said its air defenses intercepted the attacks, and Bahrain said its own defenses similarly intercepted a number of Iranian strikes. Neither country initially reported damage or casualties.

The pattern continued into the weekend. On Saturday, an Iranian attack hit a civilian industrial facility in Sitra, Bahrain, causing a fire, according to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/21/bahrain-says-iranian-attack-causes-fire-at-industrial-facility). Bahrain's Interior Ministry reported material damage but no injuries. The IRGC said it had targeted US military positions at the al-Riffa and Awali bases, claiming to have destroyed two US Patriot air defense radars in retaliation for US drone flights and an earlier strike on a radar on Iran's Hengam Island.

## The Cost of the Blockade

Behind the strikes lies a punishing economic squeeze. The US maintains a naval blockade on Iran, and Iran maintains a counter-blockade of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passed before the war. The US has been escorting some tankers through the strait while maintaining its own counter-blockade of Iranian ports.

A wave of new sanctions imposed in August as part of "Operation Economic Outcast," combined with the naval blockade and overland trade disruptions, is hurting Iran. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent claims the embargo will cause a 25 percent contraction of Iran's economy by the year ending March 2027. Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian has described a "silent economic war."

The strain on Iran's economy appears to be driving its calibrated escalation. "Even if neither side intends to enter a broader confrontation, the accumulation of military incidents across multiple fronts increases the risk of miscalculation and escalation beyond what either side originally intended," Hamidreza Azizi, a senior Iran analyst at the International Crisis Group, wrote on X.

Danny Citrinowicz, a researcher at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies and a former Iran specialist, described the pattern in blunter terms. "Iran appears to be pursuing a strategy of deliberate, calibrated escalation," he wrote. "The objective is not necessarily to trigger an all-out war, but to steadily increase the military, economic, and political costs of maintaining the blockade."

Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi framed the blockade as an act of war. "We consider the economic siege a hostile act, and we will take any necessary measures to break this economic siege," he told Iranian reporters.

## A Region on the Edge

The human and material toll is mounting. Ali Al Salem air base in Kuwait has been struck at least 30 times since the war began, damaging or destroying at least 15 aircraft hangars, numerous warehouses and 10 buildings near a residential area, according to an AP analysis. Kuwait has condemned the repeated Iranian attacks as "criminal," saying they threaten its security and stability, and its Foreign Ministry cited Article 51 of the UN Charter and UN Security Council Resolution 2817.

Bahrain hosts the US Navy's Fifth Fleet and has been attacked repeatedly, including three drone attacks in recent days after a lull of several weeks. In its statement, the IRGC claimed that US escort and aerial protection operations had failed and resulted in significant losses to equipment and vessels under US protection, warning that Iran's airspace is a "red line" that would meet "a harsh and decisive response."

The economic ripples are global. At least 25 tankers were hit in and around the Strait of Hormuz in the seven days before Nov 22, according to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/22/whats-behind-irans-escalation-of-attacks-in-the-gulf). Shipping traffic has fallen sharply: 263 ships passed through Hormuz between Nov 12 and 18, 15 percent fewer than the prior week and 20 percent fewer than the week before. Only 37 were oil tankers, down from 52 the prior week. Before the war, about 135 ships passed daily.

Insurance rates have risen from 0.10 to 0.25 percent before the war to 3.5 to 7.5 percent. Oil prices crossed $100 a barrel before settling around $83, still more than $15 above pre-war levels. The International Energy Agency reported cumulative losses of 2.8 billion barrels of oil and 60 billion cubic meters of gas supply from the Gulf.

## Diplomacy Behind the Scenes

Even as the strikes intensified, Gulf states have mounted intense behind-the-scenes diplomacy. Government and security officials from Gulf countries, Pakistan and Turkey have engaged in concerted diplomacy, including calls with senior US officials, in a bid to avert a renewed war, according to four regional officials briefed on the matter who spoke on condition of anonymity.

Qatar's Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani met Trump at the White House, and a Qatari delegation visited Tehran for two hours of talks. Oman and Qatar are pursuing mediation. Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the talks must be given a chance. "The ongoing talks in the region must be given a chance, and the blockade must be lifted rationally," he said. "As long as Iran's demands are not met, the discussion on the Strait of Hormuz and its opening is pointless."

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is pressing the Trump administration for a joint air campaign on Iran. Mossad chief Roman Gofman visited Washington, meeting CIA Director John Ratcliffe and then envoy Steve Witkoff in Miami. CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper met in Tel Aviv with Israel's military chief Eyal Zamir and Gofman.

Trump has sent mixed signals. He has said new strikes are "highly unlikely" but also that the military was "locked and loaded." He has said he wants a "permanent" deal preventing Iran from getting a nuclear weapon and that he was never very interested in regime change. "I want a deal that can be lasting," he told Fox News. "I'm going to finish it off — soon. One way or the other, we'll finish it off."

## What's Next

The Gulf Cooperation Council condemned the attacks and expressed "full solidarity" with Bahrain and Kuwait, calling on the UN Security Council to take decisive measures. "Any attack on the Kingdom constitutes an infringement on the security of all GCC states," Secretary-General Jassim Mohamed AlBudaiwi said. Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Jordan issued similar condemnations, with Jordan describing the strike on Bahrain as a "flagrant violation" and "dangerous escalation."

The conflict remains locked in a fragile standoff. Trita Parsi, executive vice president of the Quincy Institute, warned that continuing the naval blockade would risk further escalation. "If he continues the blockade, there will be escalation," Parsi said, arguing that Iran would not refrain from retaliating against continued economic pressure.

Rami Khouri, a public policy fellow at the American University of Beirut, said Iran aims to "spread the pain of the war widely to include the entire [Gulf region] and, indirectly, global economies via higher shipping costs and stressed supplies of essentials." The question now is whether Gulf and Qatari mediation can produce a negotiating framework before miscalculation triggers a wider war — and whether Washington will choose to press its economic advantage or step back from the brink.
