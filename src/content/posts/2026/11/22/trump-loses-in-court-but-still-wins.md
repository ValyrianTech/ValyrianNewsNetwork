# Trump Loses in Court, But Still Wins

President Donald Trump's second-term administration has been ruled against by federal judges at an unprecedented rate, yet it repeatedly achieves its policy objectives anyway. A new [Washington Post analysis](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/11/22/trump-keeps-losing-court-heres-how-he-gets-what-he-wants-anyway/) by Justin Jouvenal published Sunday documents how the administration converts courtroom losses into de facto victories, using the courts' slow pace and preference for narrow rulings to its advantage—a strategy with profound implications for the balance of power between the executive branch and the judiciary.

## A Playbook of Legal Jujitsu

The administration has employed what the Post describes as a kind of "legal jujitsu," exploiting the traditional presumption that the executive branch is acting in good faith. When courts rule against it, the administration often does not defy the orders outright. Instead, it outruns the clock, swaps legal rationales, and uses the language of compliance to obscure what critics call actual resistance.

The pattern is visible across some of the administration's highest-profile projects. The Supreme Court killed a lawsuit against Trump's White House ballroom on a technicality, though Chief Justice John G. Roberts Jr. wrote that the project was "likely unlawful." Breakneck construction—250 workers on the job 20 hours a day, seven days a week—ensured the project would be substantially complete before any court could halt it. By August, Solicitor General D. John Sauer told the justices the ballroom was 65 percent complete and "too far along to stop."

"There are projects that are just way more difficult to unwind once the bell has been rung," said Leah Litman, a University of Michigan law professor who has studied the administration's compliance with court orders. "The East Wing is gone. Is a court really going to say, like, 'Rebuild it'?"

## Outrunning and Remaking the Law

The same tactic has played out repeatedly. After the Supreme Court struck down Trump's sweeping tariffs in February, the president reimposed 10 to 12 percent levies on more than 60 countries just months later—using a different statutory authority, a 1974 law covering goods made with forced labor. Critics called the new rationale a pretext, noting the administration had previously cut $500 million for programs combating international forced labor. "If he was serious, he would not be applying the same tariff rate to China, one of the worst forced labor abusers in the world, as he does to countries like Australia," Rep. Linda Sánchez (D-California) wrote on social media.

In another case, government lawyers said they would notify a federal judge before starting work on a project to remake D.C.'s East Potomac Golf Links, then abruptly gave notice and bulldozed roughly 400 trees over a holiday weekend. "Rarely, if ever, has the United States Department of Justice urged a federal court not to believe the sitting President," U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes wrote in an opinion blocking further work. "And yet, here we are." She has opened an inquiry into whether the administration misled the court.

The administration has also leaned on national security as a post-hoc justification. Trump initially touted his proposed 250-foot triumphal arch as a tourist attraction, but later claimed it would house drones, snipers, and ammunition at the military's request. A judge halted the project and called that rationale "belated and unsupported." Rachel Heron, a former Justice Department attorney, warned that courts "are in danger of becoming the administration that cried national security."

## A Fraying Trust

At the heart of the analysis is the erosion of the "presumption of regularity"—the long-standing doctrine that courts assume the executive branch acts honestly and in good faith. According to [Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/155321/presumption-regularity-erosion-challenges-executive-action/), courts had by March 2026 identified 90 cases in which they expressed distrust of the federal government's factual representations. As one judge put it, "Trust that had been earned over generations has been lost in weeks."

The scale of the confrontation is staggering. As of Nov. 19, [Just Security reported](https://www.justsecurity.org/165130/1000-litigation-cases-trump-administration/) a milestone of more than 1,000 cases filed against Trump administration policies, with plaintiffs succeeding 66 percent of the time when courts have ruled. A concurrent [New York Times investigation](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/02/us/trump-courts-judges-orders.html) by Charlie Savage documented a widening breakdown in trust, including a Justice Department misconduct complaint filed against nearly every active district judge in Minnesota.

Scholars have coined a term for the administration's approach: "legalistic noncompliance." In a [University of Michigan paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5241936), professors Daniel Deacon and Leah Litman describe how government attorneys use the language of the law to claim compliance while actually resisting judicial oversight. "They are making specious arguments about why they are complying with lower-court orders when, in fact, they are not," Litman said. "It doesn't allow courts or public to say, 'Aha, they are defying court orders.'"

## What It Means

David Super, a Georgetown University law professor, said the administration's approach has knocked judges off-balance. "This administration doesn't respect some orders that are not legally binding and some orders that are," he said. "They just rush ahead. Judges who are accustomed to the Justice Department bending over backwards to be compliant are surprised and shocked."

The White House rejects the characterization. Spokesman Davis Ingle said the administration is abiding by court orders and going through proper legal reviews. "President Trump is moving at lightning speed because we don't have time to waste in our mission to make America greater than ever before," he said. The Justice Department did not respond to the Post's request for comment but has repeatedly accused lower courts of bias and points to its winning record before the Supreme Court as proof of legitimacy.

The analysis frames these tactics as a likely preview of how the administration may frustrate the oversight and investigations promised by the new Democratic congressional majority taking effect in January 2027. Skye Perryman, CEO of the advocacy group Democracy Forward, called the pattern "a highly troubling and unprecedented threat to the rule of law, far more than in the first Trump administration."

The deeper question, scholars say, is whether the damage to institutional trust will outlast Trump's tenure. Supreme Court Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson has described the administration's strategy as a "catch-me-if-you-can approach." Whether the nation's highest court ultimately reins in or blesses that approach—and whether future administrations can restore the presumption of good faith—remains an open question that will shape American governance for years to come.
