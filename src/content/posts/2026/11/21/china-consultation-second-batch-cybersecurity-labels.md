# China Opens Consultation on Second Batch of Cybersecurity Labels

China's Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) on 20 November opened a one-month public consultation on the second batch of its product catalog subject to network security labeling, extending a voluntary certification scheme to three consumer smart-device categories. According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261120/64773b42b7e645cbaf903e5fa9043923/c.html), the second batch covers consumer smart door locks, smart speakers and children's smart watches, with feedback due by 20 December 2026.

The consultation, published alongside three detailed draft implementation rules, marks the next step in Beijing's broader push to standardize, grade and visibly label the cybersecurity capabilities of internet-connected products sold domestically.

## Context: A Three-Agency Framework

The labeling program rests on the Network Security Label Management Measures, a framework jointly issued by the CAC, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) and the Ministry of Public Security (MPS). The scheme — whose English name is the China Cybersecurity Label — was first put to public consultation in November 2025, when the first batch proposed consumer networked cameras as the debut product category.

As [IT之家](https://m.ithome.com/html/899237.htm) reported at the time, products are graded from low to high as Basic, Enhanced and Leading, mapped respectively to one, two and three stars. The basic tier requires meeting relevant national standards — including no weak or universal default passwords, a vulnerability-management mechanism with dynamic patching, and maintained software updates. The enhanced tier requires domestic advanced capability; the leading tier requires international advanced capability plus penetration testing to verify resistance to high-grade attacks.

In June 2026, the three agencies formally issued the implementation rules for consumer networked cameras, making them the first products to be brought under the label, [CCTV reported via 21jingji](https://www.21jingji.com/article/20260619/herald/94992bdcd373e5465213a0aefd85cc92.html).

## What the Second Batch Adds

The full draft rules, attached to the [CAC's official notice](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2026-11/20/c_1796931132798351.htm), define each new category in detail:

- **Consumer smart door locks** are locks individuals or organizations can directly purchase and use, which rely on biometric features, electronic tags, near-field wireless communication, electronic passwords or remote-control commands as authentication, and which have internet connectivity. They are typically aimed at household and personal residential scenarios.
- **Smart speakers** are network-connected standalone devices with intelligent voice functions — speech recognition, voice interaction, semantic understanding and speech synthesis — manageable via a control unit, and which may add functions such as smart-device control, online content interaction and online payment.
- **Children's smart watches** are watches intended for children aged 3 up to 14, with information-processing functions such as calling, positioning and remote monitoring.

For all three, capability is assessed across five dimensions: physical and hardware security, system and software security, network and communication security, data security and personal-information protection, and security assurance. A product must meet all applicable individual requirements at a given level to earn that capability grade.

## Testing, Labeling and Enforcement

Under the draft rules, one-star and two-star products may be tested in a producer's own laboratory or by a qualified third-party body, while two-star testing in-house requires CNAS accreditation covering cybersecurity. Three-star products must additionally undergo third-party penetration testing.

The China Electronics Standardization Institute (CESI) acts as the filing agency, with registration and information publication handled through the platform at www.cncsl.cn. The label — a blue-background mark — carries the producer name, model, capability level, validity period, testing laboratory, the standard relied upon, and a scannable filing code linking to the test report and compliance statement. Labels remain valid for three years.

The rules carry teeth. Filing agencies must revoke registrations where materials are falsified, where a label is inconsistent with actual capability, or where a producer ends technical support. Producers that forge or misuse labels, or make false advertising claims, may face a one-year ban on filing; testing bodies that falsify results face a one-year non-recognition of their findings. Violations are punishable under the Cybersecurity Law of the PRC.

## Analysis: Voluntary but Market-Shaping

The scheme remains formally voluntary — producers are "encouraged" to label products and consumers "encouraged" to prefer labeled ones. But the deliberate focus on devices with high privacy and physical-security stakes is telling. Door locks guard the home's entry point, smart speakers place always-on microphones in living rooms, and children's watches track minors' location and communications. By targeting these categories, regulators align the program with stated goals of strengthening consumer rights protection and safeguarding public interest.

The parallel release of three technical practice guidelines by the National Cybersecurity Standardization Technical Committee — defining the concrete security requirements behind the labels — signals a maturing, systematized process: the catalog defines which products, the practice guidelines define what security means, and the implementation rules define how to test, file, label and enforce.

The three-star tier, with its penetration-testing requirement and international-advanced benchmark, also creates a competitive ladder that lets premium brands signal security quality — potentially turning the label into a market differentiator even without a hard mandate.

## What's Next

Public feedback runs until 20 December 2026, submitted by email to wajscy@cac.gov.cn or by post to the CAC's Network Security Coordination Bureau. How the final rules land — and which major door-lock, speaker and smart-watch makers are first to file, and at what star level — will reveal whether the label stays a light-touch standard or becomes a de facto requirement for the consumer IoT market. Questions also remain over how the scheme aligns with comparable IoT-labeling efforts in the US and EU, and whether it could affect cross-border trade for imported devices.
