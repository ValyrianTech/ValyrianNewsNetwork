# Trump, Xi and Putin Hold Rare Talks on Nuclear Risks

The leaders of the world's three largest nuclear powers met Thursday for the first time in a three-way gathering, but the roughly 70-minute session in Shenzhen produced symbolism rather than breakthrough. President Donald Trump, China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin agreed to a deliberately narrow agenda — reforming the United Nations, boosting fossil-fuel production, curbing the reach of the International Criminal Court and opening a "dialogue" on nuclear risks — without concrete commitments on the wars in Ukraine or Iran, [AP News reported](https://apnews.com/article/trump-xi-putin-nuclear-ukraine-iran-a04dc76ed8e786b087e2c1ea9598783a).

## A Historic Stage, a Modest Outcome

The meeting, held at the Kylin Villa on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, was billed in advance as a potentially game-changing diplomatic moment. Trump arrived having cast the encounter as proof that he could negotiate directly with two of America's toughest rivals, calling both leaders his friends.

"We are really making history," Trump said at the opening. "The United States, Russia and China are uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on Earth and also to lessen the risk of those weapons being used."

After the talks, Xi called the meeting positive, constructive and fruitful, while Putin described the discussions as open and candid, calling the format "useful and substantive." Yet Trump left the villa and boarded Air Force One without answering questions from reporters, later describing the trip as "Highly Consequential" on social media.

The joint "peace declaration" released after the meeting made no mention of either Russia's war in Ukraine or the U.S.-led conflict with Iran, [according to AP's review of the summit](https://apnews.com/article/trump-xi-putin-china-apec-25e17ec8d9c55fbff960d5631eaba71d). It affirmed only that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought" and that the three powers would pursue a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation — without numbers, frameworks or timelines.

## Putin's Symbolic Victory

For all the shared stagecraft, the summit's clearest winner may have been Putin. By standing shoulder-to-shoulder with the American and Chinese leaders — and being praised by Trump as "my friend" — the Russian president undercut the Western effort to isolate him after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. He secured the legitimacy of a great-power summit without offering any visible compromise on the war.

The contrast in relationships was striking. At the APEC gathering a day earlier, Xi greeted Putin as "my old friend," and Putin called Xi his "dear friend" — a bond rooted in China's economic support for Russia after Western sanctions. Trump's ties to both men appeared more transactional, built on personal rapport rather than shared interests.

Critics noted that Trump was negotiating with China and Russia without Ukraine or key U.S. allies — Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Japan and South Korea — at the table, a return to an era of great-power politics in which major nations decided the fate of smaller ones.

## Ukraine Handled Outside the Room

Tellingly, the Ukraine war was not resolved at the trilateral table. After the Shenzhen talks, Trump dialed into a meeting in Berlin between U.S. envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, alongside more than 40 European leaders backing Kyiv's war effort. Trump told the gathering that he had discussed a peaceful end to the war with Xi and Putin, Witkoff said.

The arrangement underscored a central tension of the summit: the most consequential diplomacy affecting Ukraine unfolded parallel to — and partly outside — the three-way talks, leaving Kyiv and its allies as bystanders to great-power conversations about their security.

## The Nuclear Arithmetic

The summit's nuclear dimension exposed a fundamental gap. The last U.S.-Russia arms pact, New START, expired in February, and Trump has said he wants a new, modernized deal that would include China. But Beijing has little incentive to accept caps: its arsenal of more than 600 warheads is far smaller than the 5,000-plus believed to be held by each of the United States and Russia.

A day after the summit, Trump sharpened that point at a White House news conference, warning that China is expanding its nuclear stockpile at a "hot and heavy" pace. "They'll catch us within 10 years," he said, [AP reported](https://apnews.com/article/trump-xi-china-nuclear-weapons-286391edaacc7e8544dc0dc023e253fb). The Pentagon has assessed that China's stockpile, in the low 600s through 2024, was on track to exceed 1,000 warheads by 2030.

Daryl Kimball, executive director of the Arms Control Association, cautioned against reading too much into a single meeting. "We need more than diplomatic theater and photo opportunities between Xi, Putin and Trump," he said. "And one meeting will not likely produce dramatic breakthroughs."

## A Subtle Message to Iran

The declaration's call for "stable energy flows" and non-proliferation carried an implicit rebuke to Iran, whose disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz and nuclear program have unsettled the region — though the statement did not name Tehran. Iran's leadership pushed back, framing the summit as a failure for Washington. "Trump entered China with nothing but empty promises, and left with empty hands and an unfavorable statement," said Mohammad Mokhber, an adviser to Iran's supreme leader, according to Iranian state media.

The language nonetheless revealed friction between Iran and its nominal partners, China and Russia, both of which have their own reasons to want Gulf oil flowing freely.

## A Shared Grievance Over the ICC

One point of genuine convergence was the International Criminal Court, which all three leaders accused of overstepping its jurisdiction and of failing to respect the immunity of state officials. Putin is wanted by the court over Ukraine, and the Trump administration has sanctioned ICC officials as part of a campaign to dismantle the tribunal over investigations into the United States and its allies. The shared language marked a notable alignment of interest among three leaders with exposure to international accountability.

## What to Watch

The Shenzhen summit's significance may lie less in what it produced than in what it normalized: direct, high-level engagement among the world's leading nuclear powers and the rehabilitation of Russia on the world stage. Whether the promised nuclear "dialogue" becomes a real negotiating process — with numbers, frameworks and timelines — remains the central unanswered question.

Several threads will shape what comes next. Trump and Xi are scheduled to meet again at the G20 summit in Florida in December, where trade, Taiwan and the two countries' nuclear trajectories are likely to resurface. The Strait of Hormuz and Iran's response to the summit's implicit pressure could test the region's fragile stability. And U.S. allies sidelined from the great-power table will be watching closely for signs that their security interests are being traded away.

For now, the historic photograph exists. The substance it promised does not — at least, not yet.
