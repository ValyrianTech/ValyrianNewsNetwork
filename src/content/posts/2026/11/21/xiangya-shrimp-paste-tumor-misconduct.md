# Xiangya Researchers Punished Over 'Shrimp Paste' Tumor

Central South University has confirmed serious academic misconduct in three research papers co-authored by scientists at its famed Xiangya hospital system, including a widely-mocked study whose mouse-tumor photographs online critics said resembled shrimp paste. The 19 November notice from the university's Office of Academic Integrity imposed demotions and multi-year funding bans on the senior authors involved.

The case has become a flashpoint for public frustration with China's research-integrity standards at its most prestigious medical institutions—and for a newer worry: the apparent use of AI-generated imagery to fabricate experimental results.

## What the Investigation Found

The university said it organized peer experts to review lab records, raw data and submission trails after images in the papers were questioned online, and issued its findings in an official "situation notice." According to the [Central South University notice](https://kxyjb.csu.edu.cn/info/1393/11790.htm), all three challenged papers contained "fabricated and tampered experimental data and figures," with two also involving improper authorship and, in one case, violations of research-ethics norms.

The three papers are:

- A 2025 study in the *Journal of Translational Medicine* on microplastics and nasopharyngeal carcinoma—first-authored by Deng (邓某某) at Xiangya Hospital's medical research center, with Zhao (赵某) of Xiangya Second Hospital's pathology department as corresponding author. This is the paper nicknamed the "shrimp paste paper."
- A 2026 paper in *Cell Death & Disease* on tumor-infiltrating B cells, first-authored by Cai (蔡某) and co-first-authored by Tan (谭某某), with Zhao as corresponding author.
- A 2026 paper in *Scientific Reports* on ovarian cancer and ferroptosis, with Deng as corresponding author.

## The Penalties

Under China's rules for handling research misconduct, Deng and Zhao received the heaviest sanctions: demotion in their professional-technical post grades, a five-year ban on promotions, talent-program applications, graduate admissions and awards, and a 10-year prohibition on undertaking government-funded science and technology activities. Cai and Tan received five-year bans on those same activities and privileges, while other co-authors were given formal academic admonishment talks. The university pledged "zero tolerance" for misconduct and said it would strengthen its monitoring and verification systems.

## How 'Shrimp Paste' Went Viral

The scandal earned its nickname on social media, where observers seized on a mouse-tumor image in the *Journal of Translational Medicine* paper that they said looked suspiciously like 虾滑 (shrimp paste), a common processed seafood product. The label spread after science outlets highlighted the resemblance.

The underlying allegations surfaced on [PubPeer](https://pubpeer.com/publications/13D64D1B10EF9F30570C8923ACE50A), the international post-publication review platform. In a thread of eight comments, one commenter wrote there was "a high suspicion that AI-generated images, including immunofluorescence images and tumor tissue photos, have been used," calling them "so blatantly fake that one can tell at a glance." Other commenters flagged a ruler beside the tumor whose tick marks did not match a standard centimeter scale, a pair of figures that appeared identical save for brightness, and internal contradictions between the paper's text and its dose-response charts. One commenter simply wrote: "Definitely AI."

The paper, published online in December 2025 as Volume 24, article 210 of the journal, has since logged roughly 32,000 accesses and 7 citations, according to its [publisher's record](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-025-07508-w).

## A Pattern of Concern at Xiangya

The episode is the latest in a series of integrity and governance problems at Xiangya, one of China's most storied medical brands. [Caixin](https://www.caixin.com/2026-11-20/102507011.html), which first reported the disciplinary notice in detail, noted that Xiangya's units secured 287 National Natural Science Foundation of China grants worth 127.2 million yuan in 2024 alone—illustrating the immense funding and output pressures on its researchers. The outlet's related coverage links the case to earlier controversies, including a 2.48 million yuan penalty for misuse of medical-insurance funds, the Liu Xiangfeng scandal at Xiangya Second Hospital, and the death of a resident trainee whose case raised questions about supervisor conduct.

## The Broader Stakes

Beyond a single embarrassing image, the case exposes two structural vulnerabilities. First, AI-generated figures evade the plagiarism-detection tools that universities and journals have relied on, leaving institutions scrambling for image-forensics capacity. Second, the "publish-or-perish" culture fueled by grant and promotion KPIs creates incentives that disciplinary actions alone may not reverse.

Public reaction captured that cynicism. Commenters on Caixin's report wrote of having "long suffered under academic KPIs," described universities as "commercialized" and paper publication as "KPI-ized," and coined a satirical fake paper title: "On the Replaceability of Mice in Research—Xiangya Shrimp Paste as a Case Study."

## What to Watch

The university's notice did not address whether the three journals—*Journal of Translational Medicine*, *Cell Death & Disease* and *Scientific Reports*—will retract the papers, a decision that now rests with their publishers. How forcefully those journals act, and whether Chinese institutions adopt tougher image-verification standards, will determine whether the "shrimp paste paper" becomes a turning point or merely the latest entry in a lengthening list.
