# China's Top Prosecutors Target Child Sexual Abuse

China's Supreme People's Procuratorate (SPP) has released five guiding cases on how prosecutors should handle sexual crimes against minors, a signal-setting move that instructs procuratorates nationwide to challenge acquittals and light sentences through the protest, or "kangsu" (抗诉), mechanism. The 64th batch of guiding cases—numbered Jianli No. 262 through 266—was approved by the SPP Procuratorial Committee on 12 June 2026 and formally issued on 9 October, before being published publicly on 21 November, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/legal/20261121/d2fae1b4d1444b8a886c0191b4394ef7/c.html).

## A Doctrine, Not Just a Crime Story

The significance of the release lies less in any single case than in its function as judicial doctrine. Guiding cases are not binding law in the way judicial interpretations are, but they shape how prosecutors across China charge, protest, and argue cases. This batch is dedicated to a single theme: how to protest rulings in sexual crimes against minors—challenging a court judgment the procuratorate believes is erroneous, either through a second-instance appeal or a retrial petition.

The SPP said the release "fully demonstrates the procuratorial organs' zero-tolerance attitude toward sexual crimes against minors and their firm stance of resolutely cracking down in accordance with law." It distills 12 case-handling rules addressing recurring points of dispute: evidentiary standards, admissibility of victim statements, determining whether an act was "against the will" of a minor, establishing a defendant's subjective knowledge of a victim's age, and applying aggravated sentencing.

Of the five cases, two resulted in acquittals being reversed to convictions, and three saw light sentences upgraded to heavier ones.

## The Five Cases

Jianli No. 262 concerned a 16-year-old girl with intellectual disability who was raped by four men in a village in 2015, including a retired teacher, Huang. Huang was initially convicted but was acquitted on appeal in 2017 after the appellate court held that her statement required corroboration and that his arm scars could not be proven to be bite marks. A first retrial protest was rejected in 2018; a second protest taken up by the SPP in 2020 led to expert analysis confirming that her account matched the cognitive pattern of someone with moderate intellectual disability, and that a dental mold matched Huang's arm scar. The Supreme People's Court ordered a retrial, and in 2023 the Gansu High People's Court convicted Huang and sentenced him to five and a half years.

Jianli No. 263 involved a 16-year-old employee, Wang, who was raped by her employer, a livestreamer named Xu, at a remote cattle farm in 2023. DNA was found, but the victim later changed her statement after accepting 90,000 yuan; a trial court acquitted Xu. Investigators found that Xu's relatives had paid her father 600,000 yuan under a compensation agreement and threatened the victim. The city intermediate court reversed the acquittal and sentenced Xu to four years, while several people who induced the false statement were prosecuted for obstruction of justice.

Jianli No. 264 dealt with a girl repeatedly raped and molested from age 10 to 14 by her uncle and her cousin, Sun Fei. The trial court convicted Sun Fei only of child molestation, rejecting the rape charge. On protest, the city intermediate court found that long-term abuse had created psychological coercion that persisted past age 14, convicting Sun Fei of rape and child molestation with a combined sentence of 14 years.

Jianli No. 265 addressed non-contact molestation. Li repeatedly exposed himself and performed obscene acts in front of eight primary-school girls near schools in 2020. A trial court convicted him of "picking quarrels and provoking trouble"—a public-order offense—rather than child molestation. After protests through three levels of procuratorates, the city intermediate court convicted Li of child molestation and sentenced him to five years.

Jianli No. 266 concerned Zhao, who used minors to lure and coerce a 12-year-old girl into intercourse in 2022. A trial court convicted him of rape but gave him four and a half years, citing a compensation payment and forgiveness letter. On protest, the city intermediate court raised the sentence to seven years, holding that multiple aggravating factors required a higher penalty and that compensation should not automatically mitigate serious crimes, especially where the defendant denies guilt.

## Victims at the Center

The release repeatedly invokes the "best interests of the minor" principle, requiring targeted victim assistance and special, priority protection. The SPP said the cases seek to achieve "the organic unity of punishing crime and protecting victims." In Jianli No. 264, the procuratorate arranged psychological counseling and judicial assistance for a victim who had developed severe depression.

The batch also sends a warning against witness tampering: in Jianli No. 263, adults who paid and pressured a victim to recant faced obstruction-of-justice charges. The SPP's guidance instructs prosecutors to investigate why a victim's statement changed and to treat a minor's lack of fierce resistance as anything but consent.

## Broader Context

The release builds on a stated enforcement priority. At a 2 March 2026 press conference, the SPP's Juvenile Procuratorial Department outlined a continuing push to strengthen the punishment and prevention of crimes against minors and to maintain its "zero-tolerance" stance toward sexual abuse and violence targeting children, according to [The Cover](https://www.thecover.cn/news/FjTGXrUdgyeH90qSdq8Jkw==). The 64th batch, issued later in the year, operationalizes that posture through concrete case guidance.

Several rules reflect child-victim-specific evidentiary realism: recognizing statements by minors with intellectual disabilities as reliable when consistent with their cognitive capacity; treating "non-firsthand-knowable details" as strong corroboration; and the requirement of psychological counseling and priority protection.

## What to Watch

The emphasis on upgrading light sentences and rejecting mitigation via compensation suggests a move toward stricter sentencing in sexual crimes against minors—a possible escalation of the zero-tolerance posture. How consistently the 12 rules are applied across provinces remains a known challenge, as does whether local budgets and specialized services can deliver psychological counseling and judicial assistance at scale. The release is guidance, not law; its impact will depend on how faithfully local procuratorates follow it.
