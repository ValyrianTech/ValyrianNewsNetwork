# FBI Probes Apparent Hack of Crime Database

The FBI is investigating an apparent hack of the National Crime Information Center (NCIC), the nationwide criminal justice database that local, state and federal law enforcement agencies rely on to identify fugitives, check warrants and run background checks, according to [the Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/fbi-database-ncic-hack-d066cff87a64a564db0a00172c124972).

The scope of the breach was not immediately clear, and the bureau declined to comment Saturday. The incident was first reported Friday evening by CNN, citing two people briefed on the investigation, and later confirmed to the AP by a person familiar with the matter who spoke on the condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to speak publicly.

## A Critical System at the Center of Policing

NCIC is among the most important data-sharing systems in American law enforcement, a computerized repository of criminal history records, fugitive and missing-person files, stolen property data and other information that agencies across the country query daily. A former FBI official who led investigations into cybercriminal groups told [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/20/politics/fbi-hacked-again-target-crime-database) that the risk extends beyond data theft.

"Every day, officers across the country rely on NCIC to identify wanted fugitives, check for outstanding warrants, and access information that can directly impact officer and public safety," the former official said. "My concern isn't simply whether hackers gained access to sensitive information, although that is an issue, but whether they could compromise the accuracy of the information that officers rely on."

That concern, that an intruder might alter records rather than merely read them, underscores why the incident is being treated with urgency even before investigators have established what, if anything, was taken.

## Political Reaction and a Second Breach

Asked about the breach Saturday during a Fox News interview, President Donald Trump said, "We just heard about it and I will say that it's not good." But he quickly pivoted to his long-standing grievances about the FBI, musing that the intrusion might reveal information about the Russia investigation and, without offering evidence, about "voter fraud."

"I always like to put things in a positive spin," Trump said. "I wonder who's doing it because wouldn't it be great to learn about Russia, Russia, Russia?"

The incident is the second apparent cyber breach to hit the bureau in recent months. In September, the FBI said it was investigating a hack of its jobs portal after a criminal group known as ShinyHunters claimed it had obtained sensitive data on thousands of agents and applicants, [the AP reported](https://apnews.com/article/fbi-criminal-hacking-group-jobs-website-beach-d627cbc6811344e7ad41e9f164cdf1c9). A Canadian cybersecurity researcher and author was later arrested and charged in connection with that breach. The FBI has also investigated "suspicious activities" on an internal surveillance system earlier in the year and suffered a 2023 intrusion into a New York field office system used in child exploitation cases.

The timing has drawn scrutiny. According to CNN, FBI Director Kash Patel appeared on Fox News on Thursday, after officials had already learned of the latest incident, to tout the bureau's cybersecurity work and a previously completed effort called "Operation Winter Shield."

"Other administrations could have made these updates, but they didn't," Patel said. "We are staying ahead of the curve and making up for lost time."

## A Wider Backdrop of Outages

The NCIC breach comes amid a series of high-profile digital disruptions that have affected banks, the National Security Agency and the Federal Aviation Administration's air traffic control system. Trump administration officials have attributed many of those incidents to routine upgrades and vulnerability patching rather than cyberattacks, and there was no immediate indication that the FBI incident was connected to them.

The administration's handling of those outages has itself become a political flashpoint. On Nov. 19, the White House replaced national cyber director Sean Cairncross, a move [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/white-house-national-cyber-director-cairncross-leaving-his-post-axios-reports-2026-11-19/) came after days of disruption to air travel, banking and national security systems. Trump confirmed the dismissal, saying he "didn't think that person was good enough." The outlet noted that the top U.S. intelligence official had said the disruptions were separate service outages tied to maintenance and technical changes, not a coordinated or state-sponsored attack.

## What Investigators Are Weighing

Key questions remain unanswered: who carried out the intrusion, whether data was exfiltrated or altered, how long any access lasted, and how the breach was detected. Investigators have not publicly linked the incident to ShinyHunters or any other group.

Security experts say the significance of an NCIC compromise would hinge on integrity as much as confidentiality. A database that police officers treat as authoritative is only as valuable as its trustworthiness; corrupt or falsified records could send officers toward the wrong person or away from a real threat.

The bureau's history as a frequent target for hackers, combined with a wave of disruptions across government and industry, has sharpened questions about the resilience of the systems that underpin everyday law enforcement. For now, the FBI's investigation is in its early stages, and officials have offered few details.

What to watch: whether the bureau confirms the breach publicly, whether any data was changed or removed, and whether the incident proves isolated or part of a broader campaign against federal systems.
