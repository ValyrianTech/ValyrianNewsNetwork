# Van Gogh Masterwork Sets Record at €173m After Last-Minute Restitution Deal

A luminous late landscape by Vincent van Gogh sold at Sotheby's in New York on Wednesday night for $190.4 million including fees — roughly €173 million — the highest price ever paid at auction for a work by the Dutch master, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/19/schilderij-van-vincent-van-gogh-bloeiende-kastanjebomen-geve/). The sale of *Blossoming Chestnut Trees* (*Châtaigniers en fleurs*), painted in May 1890 just weeks before the artist's death, very nearly did not happen: it was only cleared at the eleventh hour after a claim from the heirs of the painting's pre-war Jewish owner was settled the day before the auction.

The sale marks a 62% jump over Van Gogh's previous auction record of about $117 million, paid at Christie's in 2022 for *Orchard with Cypresses*. It also places the painting among the most expensive artworks ever sold at auction.

## A late masterpiece, unseen for decades

Van Gogh painted *Blossoming Chestnut Trees* in Auvers-sur-Oise, France, in 1890, shortly before his death on 29 July that year. The canvas depicts three women in colourful dress walking, seen from behind, in the shade of blossoming trees, while a man in blue work clothes descends the street in the opposite direction.

According to Sotheby's, the masterpiece had been on public display for the first time in 60 years ahead of the sale. It had been owned since 1975 by the Blaquier-Arrieta family of Argentina, from whose collection it was offered.

## A four-minute contest in the saleroom

[The Art Newspaper](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2026/11/20/deeper-look-van-goghs-record-breaking-blossoming-chestnut-trees-190m-sale-restitution-claim) reported that bidding opened at $135 million, drawing two telephone bidders before a third contender in the room won the contest in under four minutes at a hammer price of $165 million. The winner was the veteran New York dealer William Acquavella, head of Acquavella Galleries, who was presumably acting for an unidentified client.

The final hammer came in slightly below the roughly $180 million figure Sotheby's had suggested before the sale, though the with-fees total was described by the auction house as an astonishing result. In its coverage of the sale, [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/18/arts/design/van-gogh-painting-auction-sothebys.html) noted that the air is rare in the stratosphere, with few potential buyers placing a bid; auctioneer Oliver Barker spent more than three minutes trying to draw new bidders before Acquavella prevailed.

## The Nazi-era claim that nearly derailed the sale

The drama behind the gavel lay in the painting's provenance. Samuel Fischer, a Berlin-based publisher, bought the work in 1926; on his death in 1934 it passed to his daughter Brigitte. The Fischer family was Jewish and was persecuted under the Nazi regime. After the Anschluss in 1938 the family fled Austria, and the Gestapo confiscated their Vienna home, library and remaining art collection.

The painting had been consigned to the Cassirer gallery in Amsterdam in 1932–33 and was sold in June 1936 to the Paris-based collectors Henriette and Marcel Kapferer for $22,000.

Late in the process, Berlin-based looted-art researcher Gunnar Schnabel learned of the sale and wrote to Sotheby's in mid-November calling for it to be delayed, arguing that the Fischers had sold under duress created by the economic and political conditions the Nazis imposed. The heirs were supported by Anne Webber, co-chair of the Commission for Looted Art in Europe.

Sotheby's said it was aware of the persecution experienced by the Fischer family but argued that the circumstances around this transaction were different, noting that no claim had ever been made for the painting after the war and that recent research indicated the family had been free at all times to dispose of it. The Art Newspaper observed that the $22,000 paid in 1936 appears reasonable by period standards: *Orchard with Cypresses* — the very work that later set the previous record — had sold for $15,000 the year before.

## A confidential settlement, and a door left open

On the eve of the auction, Sotheby's announced that the current owners and all legal claimants of Samuel Fischer had reached a mutually satisfactory resolution. In a statement, the auction house said that in recognition of the painting's complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust, the two families had reached an agreement under which the work would be sold. The terms remain confidential, though analysts presume some form of financial compensation was paid.

Auctioneer Barker opened the sale by stating that the work was being offered pursuant to an agreement between the seller and the heirs of the Hedwig and Samuel Fischer family.

Schnabel told The Art Newspaper that the family was delighted that the painting, which hung in the Berlin home of Samuel and Hedwig Fischer for a quarter of a century, had now found a new owner. He expressed hope that the case may offer encouragement to other families who have faced persecution during the Nazi era, and that they too may find a path toward recognition of their rightful claims.

## Why it matters

The episode captures two forces shaping today's art market. The first is the seemingly insatiable demand at the very top of the market: even a landscape that specialists regard as fine but not among Van Gogh's very best — The Art Newspaper's Martin Bailey called the result astonishing — can now command nearly $200 million. The other 11 works in the Blaquier-Arrieta sale generally sold around or below their lower estimates, underlining how far the Van Gogh outpaced the rest.

The second is the renewed scrutiny of Nazi-era provenance. In recent years, restitution claims for art that Jewish collectors were forced to surrender during the Nazi period have again increased, and many such cases are now resolved out of court through negotiated settlements rather than litigation. The Fischer agreement is seen as opening the door for the heirs to pursue claims on other paintings once in their family should those works come to market.

## What to watch

Several questions remain open. The identity of the ultimate end-buyer is unknown: Acquavella bought in the saleroom, presumably on behalf of a client, and the painting's final resting place is yet to be revealed. The financial terms of the settlement have not been disclosed. And the precedent set by the Fischer case may reverberate through future auctions, as families and auction houses weigh how to handle works whose histories run through the darkest chapters of the twentieth century.

For now, a painting that Van Gogh made in the final weeks of his life — an artist who sold only one work during his lifetime — has become one of the most valuable objects ever to change hands at auction, and a symbol of how the art market's record heights are increasingly entangled with the unfinished business of history.
