# Honda, Toyota Recall 1.6M Cars Over Takata Airbag Risk

Honda and Toyota said Friday they are recalling about 1.6 million cars, trucks and SUVs worldwide over the risk that Takata airbag inflators could rupture and send metal shrapnel into the cabin, the latest chapter in the largest auto safety recall in U.S. history. Roughly 1.1 million of the affected vehicles are in the United States, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/autos/takata-airbag-recall-rcna610576).

Crucially, both automakers say there have been no inflator ruptures, crashes or injuries tied to the vehicles in these recalls. Unlike the inflators behind the roughly 30 deaths linked to Takata so far, the inflators in this recall contain a moisture-absorbing desiccant. Both companies describe the actions as proactive, prompted by long-term degradation analysis rather than any observed failure.

## What Is Being Recalled

Honda is recalling 810,592 older cars and SUVs in the United States, including certain 2013 Honda Accord, 2012–2013 Honda Civic, 2012–2013 Honda CR-V, 2013 Acura ILX and 2013 Acura RDX vehicles.

Toyota is recalling 790,000 vehicles worldwide, including about 314,000 in the United States, according to [Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/lifestyle/over-314k-older-toyota-lexus-vehicles-recalled-potentially-dangerous-airbag-issue). The Toyota recall covers the 2002–2010 Lexus SC430, 2011 Lexus IS250, 2017 Lexus GX460, 2003–2008 Toyota Corolla, 2005–2006 Tundra, 2005–2007 Sequoia and 2016–2017 4Runner.

## Why This Recall Is Different

The Takata crisis has unfolded for more than a decade. Takata's inflators used ammonium nitrate as a propellant, which over long-term exposure to heat, humidity and temperature cycling could degrade and burn too quickly, exploding the metal canister and hurling shrapnel into the cabin. The defect drove recalls of roughly 67 million inflators in the United States and about 100 million worldwide across two dozen automakers. Takata ultimately filed for bankruptcy.

This recall targets **desiccated** inflators — those built with a moisture-absorbing drying agent and once considered less prone to the catastrophic ruptures that killed occupants in non-desiccated inflators. Honda said its long-term, in-market monitoring found that even some desiccated inflators could experience propellant degradation near the end of their estimated safe service life in high-heat, high-humidity regions.

"Long-term monitoring and analysis of in-market desiccated inflators by Honda revealed potential propellent degradation in certain inflators within specific geographic regions, prompting this preventative action," American Honda said in a [statement](https://hondanews.com/en-US/honda-corporate/releases/release-b26ae02ff5dca50cb32a1f47831d8dd1-statement-by-american-honda-regarding-takata-desiccated-airbag-inflator-recall). "No inflator ruptures or associated injuries or fatalities have been reported related to the newly recalled inflators."

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration said analysis and degradation simulation models by independent testing groups allowed the automakers to act ahead of potential risks. "Honda and Toyota saw early evidence of degradation risk in certain of their vehicles and are currently conducting proactive recalls of vehicles," the agency said, adding that other automakers continue their own inflator testing — a signal that further recalls could follow.

## A Geographic Wrinkle

The Honda recall applies only to vehicles originally sold or ever registered in 14 states and territories known for high temperatures, high humidity and large temperature swings: Alabama, California, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina, Texas, Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, the Northern Mariana Islands (Saipan), Guam and American Samoa. Toyota did not specify which U.S. states are affected.

That geographic restriction is a practical nuance for owners: a vehicle previously considered "safe" because it carried the newer desiccated inflators may now require free replacement — but only if it falls within the covered regions.

## What Owners Should Do

Dealers will replace the affected front airbag inflators, or the entire airbag assembly, free of charge. Honda will begin mailing owner notifications in December 2026. Toyota expects replacement inflators to be ready by early 2027 and will mail notification letters between Dec. 28, 2026 and Jan. 11, 2027.

Owners do not need to wait for a letter to arrange repairs. They can check recall status through NHTSA's VIN lookup tool at nhtsa.gov/recalls, through Honda at recalls.honda.com or recalls.acura.com (or by calling 888-234-2138), or by contacting an authorized Toyota or Lexus dealer.

## What to Watch

The recalls underscore a broader reality of the Takata saga: the crisis is not fully closed. With NHTSA indicating that other automakers are still analyzing desiccated inflators, additional recalls across the industry remain possible. For now, the affected Honda and Toyota owners face a familiar but urgent task — confirming whether their vehicle is on the list, and scheduling a free fix before the inflators in their vehicles approach the end of their safe service life.
