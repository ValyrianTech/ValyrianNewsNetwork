# Hong Kong Adds Four Virtual Asset Licence Categories

Hong Kong published a bill on Friday that would extend its virtual asset licensing regime beyond trading platforms to dealers, advisers, asset managers and custodians, broadening oversight as the city seeks to develop the market while strengthening investor protection. The Virtual Asset Services Legislation (Amendment) Bill 2026 was gazetted on November 20 and is scheduled for its first reading in the Legislative Council on December 2, according to [Caixin](https://finance.caixin.com/2026-11-20/102507342.html).

## From one regulated activity to five

The bill's central change is to expand the licensing regime under the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance, known as the Anti-Money Laundering Ordinance, from a single regulated activity — operating a virtual asset trading platform (VATP) — to five. The four new regulated licence categories cover virtual asset trading service providers (dealers and over-the-counter venues), providers of advice on virtual assets, virtual asset management service providers, and virtual asset custodian service providers.

Hong Kong first introduced licensing for VATPs under its anti-money-laundering law in June 2023, creating a regime focused narrowly on trading. The amendment formalises what had until now been announced in stages: after signalling plans in late 2025 to add virtual asset dealing and custody licences, then proposing advisory and asset-management licences, the government has consolidated all four into a single legislative vehicle.

## A registration path for banks and payment firms

The bill also introduces a registration regime. Banks and licensed stored-value payment (SVF) facilities overseen by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority would be required to register with the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) if they provide any virtual asset services in Hong Kong.

That two-tier structure — full SFC licensing for pure-play virtual asset firms and a registration requirement for incumbent, HKMA-supervised financial institutions — appears designed to close potential regulatory gaps as traditional banks and payment operators move into digital assets, without subjecting them to the same licensing process as dedicated crypto businesses.

## Tokenized deposits kept outside the perimeter

The bill explicitly excludes tokenized deposits from the definition of a "virtual asset." The carve-out aligns with the HKMA's digital-money framework, under which tokenized deposits are treated as a form of private money. Keeping bank-issued tokenized deposits outside the SFC licensing perimeter lets lenders explore the technology within the existing banking rulebook rather than under a separate virtual asset regime.

The legislation also introduces penalties for misrepresentation. Making false claims or representations about a firm's virtual asset regulatory status would carry a maximum fine of HK$400,000 and two years' imprisonment, according to a [Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-11-21/hong-kong-proposes-broader-licensing-for-virtual-asset-businesses-102507373.html) digest of the story. The measure targets misleading marketing — a recurring problem in the crypto industry, where unlicensed operators have at times implied official approval.

## Building a regulated digital-asset hub

The bill is the latest step in Hong Kong's methodical effort, begun in 2023, to build a compliant digital-asset hub. The policy direction reflects the territory's "Digital Asset Policy Declaration 2.0" issued in June 2025, which aimed at a clearer, cross-departmental regulatory framework, and its parallel work on stablecoin licensing and tokenization.

Adoption has been gradual. As of October 31, 2026, the SFC had issued 13 licences and was processing five applications, Caixin reported. In the months since, Hong Kong has moved to deepen its tokenization market: licensed platforms were approved in April 2026 to conduct secondary-market trading of tokenized funds, the first retail tokenized funds were cleared to trade around the clock on secondary markets in late October, and in mid-November the SFC said the city's tokenization drive was shifting from experimental to commercial use.

## Why it matters

The shift from a trading-platform-centric regime to a full-value-chain regime — covering dealing, advice, management, custody and trading — is significant on two fronts. For market participants, it defines a clearer perimeter for who must be licensed and under what terms, reducing the ambiguity that has deterred some institutional entrants. For regulators, it establishes a single AML-based framework across the sector while coordinating with the HKMA on banks and payment firms.

The bill is also framed by officials as serving two goals at once: developing the market and protecting investors. That balancing act has defined Hong Kong's post-2022 pivot toward regulated crypto, as the territory positions itself to compete for digital-asset business with jurisdictions such as Singapore and Dubai while maintaining stricter investor safeguards than some rivals.

## What to watch

Several questions remain as the bill heads to LegCo. The precise scope and definitions of each of the four new licences will determine how fully over-the-counter dealers are captured and how advisors and managers are treated. Transitional arrangements for existing virtual asset businesses have yet to be detailed. And the legislative timetable beyond the December 2 first reading will shape how quickly the expanded regime takes effect.

For now, the gazetting of the bill signals that Hong Kong intends to press ahead with a comprehensive licensing architecture rather than retreat. Whether that architecture attracts the institutional capital officials are courting — while keeping bad actors out — is the test that will define the city's digital-asset ambitions.
