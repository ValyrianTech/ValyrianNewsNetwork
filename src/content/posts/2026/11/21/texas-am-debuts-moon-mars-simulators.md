# Texas A&M Debuts Giant Moon and Mars Simulators

HOUSTON — It's about as close as anyone can get to the Moon without boarding a rocket. On Friday, the Texas A&M University Space Institute opened a new facility next door to NASA's Johnson Space Center, anchored by a 117,000-square-foot indoor simulation of the lunar surface complete with craters and rugged terrain built from basalt rock, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/science/space/moon-mars-landscapes-texas-space-institute-nasa-rcna609897).

The institute — a $200 million, state-funded complex in Houston's Clear Lake area — features a pair of at-scale reproductions of the lunar and Martian surfaces, each covering roughly two and a half acres, about the size of Texas A&M's Kyle Field stadium. The landscapes are designed for astronaut training, spacesuit and rover testing, and research in support of future human missions to the Moon and Mars.

## A one-of-a-kind training ground

The facility is the first building inside NASA's Exploration Park, a 240-acre commercial property adjacent to Johnson Space Center, according to [Greater Clear Lake News](https://greaterclearlakenews.com/articles/texas-a-m-space-institute-sets-nov-20-opening-near-jsc-mw0qu3ty). Between the two terrains sit a series of 2,000-square-foot "garages" where researchers, private companies, and government agencies work side by side. Half of the garages open onto the lunar replica; the other half face the Mars replica.

Dr. Nancy Currie-Gregg, a former NASA astronaut who flew four spaceflights and now directs the institute, told [KXXV/KRHD](https://www.kxxv.com/news/local-news/in-your-neighborhood/brazos-county/texas-a-m-university/texas-a-m-builds-first-ever-life-size-simulation-of-the-moon-and-mars-surface) that the scale of the project is hard to overstate. "Kyle Field can fit in one of the scapes. So imagine two Kyle Fields with a building in between and a building in a building, if you will," she said.

She described the practical vision behind the design: "This fantastic facility is literally one of a kind where you can open up your garage door and roll your rover out onto the lunar surface and have somebody in a spacesuit. They're working, have habitats, test out the tools that are required as we go beyond low Earth orbit and start to explore beyond our own planet."

## Why physical terrain matters

Currie-Gregg, who also sits on the Texas Space Commission's Board of Directors, argued that no amount of virtual simulation fully replaces the real thing. "I know how I trained, I know what was good about the training, I know where some gaps were, and again, I'm really adamant of at-scale physical analogues," she said. "You know, there's no substitute for that."

Dr. Robert Ambrose, the institute's associate director, framed the immersive design as a leap beyond the university's existing space lab at its RELLIS Campus in Bryan. "Instead of opening the door and rolling out to Bryan-College Station, outside that door would be the lunar surface," he said. He noted that the analogue terrain lets engineers test hardware in exactly the kind of hazard it was built to overcome: "We wanted this ball initially to go to the bottom of a crater on the moon. Which is a really bad place for a person."

The Houston facility is a much larger successor to the RELLIS lab, where students and faculty already build and test robots for space missions, including thermal testing that ranges from minus 60 degrees Celsius to 120 degrees Celsius, according to researcher Emily Wilkinson.

## A state investment in the space economy

The project reflects a deliberate Texas strategy to capture growth in the space economy. The Texas Legislature created the Texas Space Commission in 2023 through House Bill 3447, setting aside $350 million for space programs, of which $200 million went to the Texas A&M University System for the institute. State Rep. Greg Bonnen, a Friendswood Republican, led the bill's drafting, Greater Clear Lake News reported.

The institute arrives amid NASA's Artemis era, following the crewed Artemis II lunar flyby, the first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17 in 1972. At-scale physical terrain analogues are increasingly valued for crew training and human-in-the-loop equipment testing that virtual simulations cannot fully replicate — a capability complementary to NASA's own CHAPEA habitat-isolation experiments at Johnson Space Center.

## What's next

Questions remain about how the facility will be used and who will have access to it. Public records do not reconcile Texas A&M's stated 400,000-square-foot building size with a state construction filing that lists approximately 440,000 square feet. Whether the Nov. 20 grand opening was open to the public was also not announced.

Meanwhile, Texas is competing to bring a new U.S. Space Academy to the state, a separate bid that could cost taxpayers up to $100 million. Johnson Space Center Director Vanessa Wyche has said development plans for the remaining roughly 200 acres of Exploration Park are "on a pause" pending that process — making the new institute the first piece of a corridor Texas hopes will anchor the next generation of space exploration.
