# Senate Democrats' Leadership Vote Tests New Majority

Democrats who swept into the Senate on promises of change have not even been sworn into office, yet the decision over who will lead their new majority is already testing that pledge. Senate Democrats will hold a rare contested leadership election on Dec. 1, when incumbent leader Chuck Schumer of New York faces a challenge from Chris Van Hollen of Maryland in a closed-door, secret-ballot vote, [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/schumer-vanhollen-majority-leader-progressives-new-senators-b7785d0b6aee5f4844f55b650a231f5d) reported.

![AP combination photo of 2026 Democratic Senate candidates who flipped seats](https://newsroom.ap.org/editorial-photos-videos/detail?mediatype=photo&utm_source=apnews&utm_campaign=gallery&itemid=78f0e1ba27254458818f8d5da9922585)

The contest, the first genuinely competitive race for the Senate Democratic leadership in decades, will shape the direction of the party as it prepares to take the majority in the 120th Congress. The last time a sitting Democratic leader faced a challenger was 1984.

## A Freshman Class Caught in the Middle

The 13 new Democratic senators form the largest incoming Senate class in decades, with several winning in traditionally Republican strongholds such as Texas, Mississippi, Iowa and South Carolina. Many campaigned as political outsiders, promising to shake up Washington and serve as independent voices. Now they are caught in a classic insider fight, forced to decide whether to stick with Schumer or back Van Hollen.

So far, most of the incoming senators from red-leaning states have avoided taking sides. South Carolina Sen.-elect Annie Andrews, a pediatrician who became the first Democrat in nearly three decades to win a Senate race in her state, has called for "generational change" and said she would not support Schumer—but has also rejected policies Van Hollen backs, such as "Medicare for All," and says she has made "no decision" on the race.

"The way I assess the situation as it is right now is that the generational change is represented by this incredibly dynamic incoming freshman class of senators," Andrews said.

Mississippi Sen.-elect Scott Colom said he wants to get to know both candidates better, describing himself as in "listening mode." Iowa Sen.-elect Josh Turek said his decision will hinge on which candidate best reflects the "common sense, prairie populist agenda" he campaigned on. Kansas Sen.-elect Adam Hamilton said he would not back Schumer but declined to endorse anyone, telling MSNow that in places like Kansas, "there's a lot of people who don't feel that in Chuck Schumer."

## The Cases for Each Candidate

Schumer's allies point to his success steering the party through the midterms. He helped recruit candidates such as Sherrod Brown of Ohio, Mary Peltola of Alaska and Roy Cooper of North Carolina, while raising money and directing spending in key races.

"When the manager takes you to the World Series, you give him another contract," said Arizona Sen. Ruben Gallego. Maine Sen. Angus King, an independent who caucuses with Democrats, put it similarly: "Why would you fire the manager when you just won the World Series?"

Van Hollen, 67, announced his challenge earlier this month, [arguing](https://apnews.com/article/van-hollen-schumer-senate-democratic-leader-6d74c189cb6c065c9a810a1cf1a53436) that "the American people called for change." A Maryland senator since 2017 and a former chair of the party's Senate campaign arm, he has positioned himself as a new face and has promised a more confrontational approach to President Donald Trump, an ambitious economic agenda and changes to how the party operates in primaries.

"It's now on us to meet this moment, and to do that we need new leadership," Van Hollen said. "This is not about any one person—it's about answering the call of the American people."

He has drawn backing from progressive standard-bearer Bernie Sanders and newly elected progressives including Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota. Large progressive organizations, including MoveOn and Indivisible, have endorsed him, while activists have protested at Schumer events, at one point shouting "Step down, Schumer!"

## A Rift Two Years in the Making

The leadership fight exposes a divide that [opened in the spring of 2025](https://apnews.com/article/trump-schumer-senate-democrat-book-tour-spending-vote-afbfed35d906f9ad6c8e6e9114f8a431), when Schumer was put on the defensive after siding with Republicans on a spending vote. Even as he has since led the caucus through a government shutdown and orchestrated major election wins, many in the party base are agitating for generational change.

The race also cuts across the party's ideological lines. Michigan Sen. Elissa Slotkin, a moderate who has criticized some of Van Hollen's more progressive policies, nonetheless said she would support him because she "wasn't supporting Schumer." Meanwhile, some prominent progressives, including Massachusetts Sen. Elizabeth Warren, have remained silent.

## What's at Stake

Schumer has said he has the votes, telling the AP in an interview, "I'm going to win it." He has been working with senators one-on-one to shape a first-100-days agenda that includes healthcare and tariff legislation and efforts to end the Iran war.

The secret ballot injects genuine uncertainty into a race that most reporting still tilts toward Schumer, given his long relationships and mastery of Senate machinery. But a large, restless freshman class—many of whom owe their seats to voters demanding change—could make the outcome closer than the establishment expects.

The vote is scheduled for Dec. 1. Whichever way it goes, the contest offers an early signal of how the new Democratic majority intends to govern—and whether the party's promise of change extends to its own leadership.
