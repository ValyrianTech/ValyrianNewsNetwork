# Belgian Victim Speaks Out After Homophobic Attack: 'I Am Not the Problem'

Bastien was boarding a tram alone at place Saint-Lambert in Liège when a stranger looked him up and down and threw a glass bottle that struck him full in the face. This week, the LGBTQIA+ activist decided to break his silence and tell his story, telling [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/victime-d-une-agression-homophobe-bastien-choisit-de-temoigner-ce-n-est-pas-moi-le-probleme-11818332) that the fault lies not with his identity but with his attacker. "I am not the problem," he said.

The decision to speak publicly was not automatic. As a committed activist, Bastien initially hesitated, because going public means becoming "even more visible." Silence, he reasoned, would only repeat the violence done to him. "I said to myself: but actually, why do I do it for others and not for myself?" he told the broadcaster.

## A 'Reflex' of Precaution, Then Violence

On an ordinary evening, Bastien had asked a friend to walk him home, as he often does — a precaution he described as having become second nature. When they reached place Saint-Lambert, the central transit hub of Wallonia's largest city, their paths split. He boarded a tram as a group of young men got off; one of them delivered the blow within seconds. "I don't think I quite understood what was happening at that moment," he said. The tram driver called police and an ambulance, and his friend, still nearby, returned to find blood running over Bastien's hands.

Despite the attack, Bastien refuses to retreat. "I refuse to go back into the closet," he said. "I decide to be myself, quite simply." He acknowledges the aftermath — hyper-vigilance, panic attacks at the tram stop, guilt over not having answered the insults — but insists the violence will not transform him. "It's not by insulting us or hitting us that we're going to stop existing," he said. "That diversity has always existed and will always exist."

## A Documented Rise in Anti-LGBTQIA+ Violence

Bastien's testimony is not an isolated case. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/fr/2026/05/13/l_an-dernier-unia-a-recu-311-signalements-de-violences-homophob/), Unia — Belgium's independent public anti-discrimination body — recorded 311 reports related to sexual orientation in 2025, or roughly one per day, a figure stable compared with 2024. In nearly half of those cases a file was opened, a rate roughly double the average for other discrimination criteria. On files closed in 2025, 40 percent involved hate acts such as assault and harassment, with gay and bisexual men the most frequent targets.

The picture at the European level is similar. The EU Fundamental Rights Agency's 2024 survey found that 54 percent of LGBTQIA+ respondents reported harassment in the preceding year, including 53 percent in Belgium, while 15 percent of Belgian respondents — above the EU average of 13 percent — had been physically attacked in the previous five years. The gender-equality institute IEFH separately tallied 454 reports of transphobic hateful behaviour in 2025.

## Youth Intolerance and the Role of Social Media

What most concerns experts is the direction of travel. A Flemish Jeugdonderzoeksplatform study published in April 2025 found that 16- to 18-year-old students are markedly less tolerant of lesbian and gay people than in 2018 or 2013, with [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/les-personnes-lgbtqia-confrontees-a-une-hausse-des-violences-en-belgique-11685716) reporting that one in five pupils now considers an attack against a homosexual person could be acceptable — up from fewer than one in ten in 2018. The same coverage notes that associations have documented a multiplication of ambushes organized through dating apps, attacks on LGBTQIA+ venues, and heightened harassment at work and online, while only 14 percent of victims report incidents to police.

Bastien himself points to "hyper-masculinity" in his attacker, an observation the experts confirm. Oliviero Aseglio, head of communication at Unia, described how hostility now travels through new channels: "New entry points for homophobic speech pass through social media, notably masculinist channels, through the American or Russian far right, through religious channels organized on the internet, which, through phones, can reach young Belgians."

## A Push for More Than Punishment

The violence is increasingly reaching the courts. Unia reported that over 2025 and the first quarter of 2026, ten homophobic violence cases in which it was a civil party came before judges, and the discriminatory motive was retained as an aggravating circumstance in nine out of ten. In some ambush cases, Unia notes, attackers' defenses were built on a supposed "pedophile hunt" conflating homosexuality with deviance — an argument no judge has accepted.

Yet Unia and the IEFH argue that the response cannot be purely repressive. They call for better police treatment of victims and measures that change mindsets, stressing prevention and education from an early age. "We're not going to solve the problem simply by locking people up," Aseglio said, adding that it is "primordial that LGBTQIA+ issues be addressed at school."

For Bastien, the message is simpler: a refusal to disappear. His testimony joins the statistics — and, he hopes, helps shift the debate from victimhood toward responsibility. What remains to be seen is whether Belgian institutions can translate their stated priorities — education, prevention, and tougher recognition of hate motives — into a measureable dent in the numbers his case now represents.
