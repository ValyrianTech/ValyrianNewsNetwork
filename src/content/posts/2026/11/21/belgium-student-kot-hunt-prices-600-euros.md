# Belgium's Student 'Kot' Hunt: Prices Cross €600

The annual hunt for an affordable student room in Belgium has become, in the words of one broadcaster, a *parcours du combattant* — an obstacle course. The average room, or *kot*, now costs €600 a month including charges, and finding one at a reasonable price has turned into a persistent struggle for students entering higher education. [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/parlons-solutions-avec-les-jeunes-pour-les-etudiants-la-quete-inlassable-d-un-kot-bon-marche-11818392) reports that Brussels is the most expensive region, rents keep climbing year after year, and the country faces an estimated shortfall of some 70,000 student rooms by 2030.

The figures come from the *Kotkompas 2026* study by Diggit StudentLife, which manages more than 3,000 student units, and the independent property expert Stadim. The study tracks the *cost of tenancy* (COT) — total rent with charges — across the ten largest student cities in Belgium.

## A Market Under Pressure

According to the study, the national average COT for a student room reached €600 a month this academic year, a rise of €10, or about 1.7%, compared with 2025. Bare rent climbed roughly 2%, from €485 to €495, while charges held steady at €105 a month. Since 2020, the average Belgian rent has risen by €125, from €475 to €600 — an increase of about 26%. The single largest jump, of €45, came at the start of the 2023 academic year, in the wake of the 2022 energy crisis.

The regional gap is stark. As [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/781952/article/2026-11-10/voici-ce-que-coute-en-moyenne-un-kot-par-mois-en-belgique), citing the Belga news agency, reported, a basic room without private sanitary facilities averages €480 a month in Wallonia, €510 in Flanders and €605 in Brussels. Rooms with private bathrooms cost €520 in Wallonia, €635 in Flanders and €720 in Brussels. Studios — the most expensive option — run to €640 in the south of the country, €725 in the north and €805 in Brussels. In all, Brussels averages €705 a month, roughly €160 more than Wallonia and €80 more than Flanders.

The scale of the difference is telling: a studio in Wallonia costs barely more than a simple room in Brussels, and the gap between a Brussels studio and a Walloon room approaches €3,900 a year.

## One City Bucks the Trend

Amid the across-the-board increases, the researchers flagged a rare exception: for the first time, rents fell in a Belgian student city, Kortrijk in West Flanders. They attribute the drop to increased supply. "This confirms the elementary economic principle that additional supply is reflected in the level of prices," Arne Hermans of Diggit StudentLife said, as quoted by Le Soir.

The finding underscores the central problem. The [*Kotkompas 2025* study](https://diggitstudentlife.eu/sites/default/files/2025-11/52025_0.pdf) estimated that Belgium will need about 70,000 new student rooms by 2030 to meet full demand. The student population is projected to peak at between 568,000 and 623,000 in the 2030–2031 academic year, and supply is struggling to keep pace. Across the ten main student cities, an average of 45% of students live in a kot, but the figure ranges widely — from about 20% in Antwerp to about 80% in Leuven. Pressure on the residential market is particularly acute in Ghent and Brussels.

## Universities as Landlords

For many students, the first door they knock on is their university. Both the Université libre de Bruxelles (ULB) and UCLouvain place particular emphasis on housing — the number of rooms, renovation of existing stock, construction of new units, rent caps and allocation criteria.

That route worked for Alexandre, a first-year master's student in medicine at UCLouvain, who secured a university room with shared living space, kitchen and shower at €425 a month, all charges included. "I really had the luck to be able to access that housing, because normally you have to be on a waiting list," he told RTBF. "It takes time, and sometimes you get a negative answer late, which makes last-minute searching complicated."

Comparing the private market, he added: "In the private sector, compared with the university kot, it was €550, €600 with a much smaller shared space. And it doesn't include charges — it quickly gets expensive. An amount I wasn't ready to pay."

UCLouvain says it owns "the largest stock of student housing in Europe" in its own right, with 6,000 beds to which it gives priority. That lets it cap rents at €390 a month all-inclusive and manage charges, including during energy crises. The university acknowledges that this does not meet all needs, but says the supply "helps limit rent increases in the private sector." In 2025 it built 130 new units, a 2.1% increase, and it plans to renovate 1,550 units by 2039 — about 100 a year, at an annual cost of €5 million.

The ULB, for its part, offers 1,140 beds against 2,500 to 4,000 applications each year. It has added 39% more rooms over the past three years. Both universities apply allocation criteria that favour first-time students, taking into account family income and the distance from the family home, and typically cap occupancy at two to three years to ensure rotation and access for newcomers.

## Living With the Landlord

Others find alternatives. Ulysse, a 19-year-old architecture student who moved from Nantes to study in Belgium, was looking for a low-budget room when he discovered "Un toit deux âges," an association that promotes intergenerational cohabitation. Since September 2025 he has lived with Denis, a retired widower, alongside another student, Gauthier.

Two rent options coexist: €450 a month all-inclusive, or €250 in exchange for around ten hours of work a month, such as doing the shopping. "The budget question was really very important — I came across this association directly and didn't even necessarily look for anything else in Brussels," Ulysse said. He stressed the value of the shared life: "Spending time with Denis, going to eat with him, doing the dishes — that's really very, very important for me and for him." It is, in effect, a win-win arrangement in which one person's small budget fills another's house with life.

## The Shared-Flat Route

Soraya, who studies tourism management at EPHEC, chose to stay in Brussels rather than commute. "I come from La Louvière, and doing an hour's journey in the morning and then in the evening, on top of everything else — the train delays and so on — that wasn't possible," she said. "And I have to combine that with a student job."

Watching prices rise, she reached a breaking point. "At one point it went to €700 and more, which was impossible for me." She left her previous home and, through word of mouth, found a shared flat with Bérénice, a recent graduate, and Estelle, another student, paying €575 a month all-inclusive. The search, she said, was long. "It was complicated for me to find something within my budget because I'm dependent on my parents. It's my father who takes care of all of it alone — helping me finance housing, groceries and all the equipment needed for my studies. And what I hear from my friends is that it's becoming more and more complicated to find a shared flat in Brussels at affordable prices, and often there are people who don't have the luck to have help from their family. It's starting to become impossible."

## What to Watch

The Belgian student-housing squeeze is part of a broader overheating of the rental market. Le Soir reported in November that some one-bedroom apartments attract up to a hundred applications, and that would-be tenants are increasingly falsifying payslips to win a lease.

The Kortrijk exception points to the lever that matters most: supply. Whether the construction pipelines and university expansions now under way can close a 70,000-unit gap before the student population peaks around 2030 remains the open question. So too does the future of charges, which the Kotkompas authors flag as a risk should energy prices rise again in 2027 and 2028.

For now, the message from students is consistent: without family support, a university place, or a lucky encounter with an association or a flat-share, the search for a cheap kot is less a choice than a test of endurance.
