# Anthropic Mathematician Claims AI Solved Riemann Hypothesis

A mathematician employed by artificial intelligence company Anthropic claims he used the firm's AI tool Claude to prove the Riemann Hypothesis, the 167-year-old problem widely regarded as the most important unsolved question in mathematics. Levent Alpöge, a member of Anthropic's technical staff, made the announcement Friday in a four-page note on his personal website, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/anthropic-claude-math-solution-ai-4fe8b69a891f160f9e7f0c85af8c55c8).

If confirmed, the result would rank among the most consequential breakthroughs in mathematics in more than a century — and a landmark demonstration of artificial intelligence applied to frontier research. Within a day, an independent community of mathematicians had certified the underlying proof code, even as the official prize body kept the problem listed as unsolved pending a lengthy formal review.

## A Question About Prime Numbers

First posed by the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in 1859, the hypothesis concerns the distribution of prime numbers — those divisible only by one and themselves — as they stretch toward infinity. It proposes that their seemingly random locations are in fact governed by a hidden and meaningful order, tied to the zeros of a mathematical function known as the Riemann zeta function. The problem underpins large areas of number theory with deep implications for fields including cryptography.

In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute named it one of seven Millennium Prize Problems, attaching a $1 million reward for a solution.

## A Proof Written as Code

Rather than a conventional paper, Alpöge released the claimed proof as roughly 17 million lines of Lean, a programming language that allows mathematicians to encode theorems so a computer can verify their logic line by line. His work with the AI reportedly produced more than 250,000 pages of material.

According to [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/21/anthropic-riemann-hypothesis-solved-claude-ai), Alpöge said Claude helped develop the solution over about six weeks. In his note, he anthropomorphized the model: "Claude and I have had a great time together with such breadth in recent months, but this time Claude far outpaced me near the finish line. No, Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line."

The format proved decisive for how fast the claim could be checked. "Usually when somebody gives you a paper, that's also kind of code for a proof and you have to actually read the paper, follow it yourself, figure out if you believe every single line in that paper," Alex Kontorovich, a mathematics professor and department chair at Rutgers University, told AP. "But the computer is extraordinarily picky, way pickier than I am and so if you can satisfy the computer, there's a very, very good chance you're going to satisfy me too."

## Timing Raises Eyebrows

The announcement landed one day after Anthropic's record Wall Street debut, in which the company raised $81 billion at a $1.5 trillion valuation — the most valuable initial public offering in history. Alpöge said he made the discovery late on the preceding Sunday night and messaged CEO Dario Amodei about his "ability to announce" the result as the company was about to grab headlines.

He acknowledged the awkward timing, writing that "the precise timing is horrible. If I could have, I'd have finished this all much earlier," the Guardian reported. Anthropic did not respond to an emailed request for comment from AP.

## Verification Moves Unusually Fast

Because the proof was submitted as machine-checkable code, verification advanced with striking speed. In a joint statement, the [Lean and Mathlib communities](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/) — endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative — certified that both the statement and the proof are correct, confirming that the library compiles cleanly and that it proves exactly the right theorem.

Still, the Clay Mathematics Institute has kept the problem officially unsolved. "This is an announcement of historic significance, which holds the promise of a dramatic transformation in the perception of what is possible in the struggle to extend the human understanding of mathematics," said institute president Martin Bridson. "But the journey from today's announcement to a place of real understanding will be long and difficult."

## A Dueling, All-Human Claim

Hours after Alpöge went public, a second, human-only claim surfaced. Alessandro Masullo, a Stanford-trained mathematician, circulated a roughly 940-page draft proof of the stronger Generalized Riemann Hypothesis that he said took nearly 12 years and did not rely on AI. [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/dueling-claims-of-riemann-hypothesis-proofs-emerge/) reported the competing drafts, noting that the human proof had not been independently verified and that problems with it were later found — partly with the help of AI models. That twist muddied any simple "human versus machine" narrative.

The episode is the latest in a year of rapid AI-assisted mathematical results. In September, rival OpenAI said its technology was key to a breakthrough on the Navier-Stokes equations governing fluid flow. Kontorovich described the cumulative effect as disorienting: "Navier-Stokes fell and now the Riemann Hypothesis. There's some kind of out-of-body experience where I'm looking at myself saying, 'Yes, the Riemann Hypothesis has been solved,' and kind of trying to comprehend what the hell these words are coming out of my mouth."

## What to Watch

The scientific question now shifts from whether the proof is valid to what it means and how it will be understood. Alpöge himself conceded he is "presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics" — the community will spend months unpacking the new ideas inside it.

For Anthropic, the claim burnishes its research credentials at a delicate moment, arriving alongside questions about the overlap between scientific breakthroughs and corporate storytelling. Whether a $1 million Millennium Prize ultimately changes hands will depend on the Clay Institute's deliberately slow review. For now, the computer has spoken; the mathematicians are still catching up.
