# Man Convicted of Starting Deadly Palisades Fire

A federal jury in Los Angeles on Friday found a 30-year-old man guilty of igniting the small New Year's Day brush fire that smoldered underground and reignited a week later into the Palisades Fire, the most destructive wildfire in the city's history. The verdict against Jonathan Rinderknecht followed a retrial, delivering a measure of accountability for a catastrophe that killed 12 people, destroyed 6,837 structures and caused an estimated $25 billion in damage.

The jury convicted Rinderknecht on both federal counts — destruction of property by means of fire, and arson affecting property used in interstate commerce — after deliberating for just over two days, according to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/pacific-palisades-fire-retrial-jonathan-rinderknecht-rcna610336). He remains in federal custody and is scheduled to be sentenced on March 17, 2027, facing a minimum of five years and a maximum of 40 years in prison.

"The question at the time was who was responsible. And now we have the answer to that question," First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli said after the verdict.

## A Fire That Refused to Die

Prosecutors alleged that Rinderknecht set what became known as the Lachman Fire at approximately 12:12 a.m. on Jan. 1, 2025, using a lighter to ignite dense brush near a street of the same name in the Santa Monica Mountains. The blaze burned roughly five to eight acres before firefighters extinguished it, [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/20/los-angeles-palisades-wildfire-trial-verdict) reported.

But the fire did not truly die. It continued to smolder as a "holdover fire" beneath the surface, and on Jan. 7, 2025, powerful Santa Ana winds fanned it back into an inferno. The revived Palisades Fire swept through the affluent Pacific Palisades enclave and spread into Topanga and Malibu, burning 23,448 acres. It took more than three weeks to contain and was not fully corralled until Jan. 31, 2025.

The scale of the destruction made the case one of the most closely watched arson prosecutions in California history. Survivors and victims' families packed the courtroom, and city leaders issued statements framing the verdict as a milestone in a long recovery.

"Our first thought remains with the families of those we lost and for everyone in our community whose lives were changed by this fire. No verdict, however important, could ever undo that loss," the Los Angeles Fire Department said in a statement.

Mayor Karen Bass struck a similar note, acknowledging that "no verdict can bring back the lives lost or erase the pain families have endured."

## A Second Chance for Prosecutors

The conviction marked a dramatic reversal from the case's first trial, which ended in a mistrial in June 2026 after jurors deadlocked 10-2 in favor of acquittal, according to [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/06/26/us/rinderknecht-mistrial-palisades-fire-los-angeles.html). That outcome hinged in part on doubts about the investigation and about the fire department's handling of the initial Lachman blaze.

At the retrial, which began with jury selection on Nov. 2, 2026, prosecutors sharpened their case. Key evidence included surveillance footage of Rinderknecht's car in Pacific Palisades before the fire, GPS location data, and a green Bic barbecue lighter whose DNA matched the defendant, prosecutor Mark Williams said. Prosecutors also told jurors that Rinderknecht had asked ChatGPT whether a person could be at fault if a wildfire was caused by their cigarette, and that he had recorded images of the fire.

Williams was blunt in his closing argument: "The only verdict you can reach is guilty."

The defense, led by attorney Steve Haney, argued there was no eyewitness and no camera footage of the ignition. They contended the Lachman Fire scene had been compromised after being open to the public for days before it was locked down — a flaw that retired Los Angeles County Sheriff's arson investigator Ed Nordskog, testifying for the defense, called "a case killer." The defense also offered an alternative theory that the initial blaze was sparked by fireworks at a private residence, which prosecutors dismissed as baseless.

Jurors ultimately found the prosecution's account persuasive. One juror, identified only as Cameron, told reporters he was initially the lone holdout for acquittal before changing his mind. "The strongest evidence was the defendant's own testimony," Cameron said, according to NBC News. "No one else was on the hill that day or night and happened to be smoking. Everything kind of started to point to one conclusion."

He added a note of ambiguity about Rinderknecht's intent: "His intent was to start a fire. What that fire did after his actions ... only he will know and only God will know."

The retrial jury had been explicitly instructed not to weigh the adequacy of the fire department's response to the Lachman and Palisades blazes — an issue that had featured prominently in the first trial's deliberations. The LAFD had faced criticism for failing to fully extinguish the Lachman Fire, a lapse that some first-trial jurors weighed heavily.

## What the Verdict Does — and Doesn't — Settle

The criminal conviction resolves the question of who started the fire, but it leaves a far broader legal reckoning unresolved. The city and county of Los Angeles continue to face consolidated civil lawsuits from fire survivors and victims' families, and those cases focus not on the cause but on the response.

"For the purpose of our civil case, the fire could have been started by lightning and would have had the same result," said Alex Robertson, an attorney representing fire victims, according to The Guardian. "It doesn't matter what started the fire. It's what happened — what didn't happen in terms of protecting the residents of the Palisades and Malibu after the fire started."

That distinction matters. The criminal verdict assigns individual blame; the civil litigation interrogates institutional preparedness — water systems, evacuation planning and emergency response in a region increasingly primed for catastrophic fire. The Palisades Fire became a political flashpoint as well: Mayor Bass was criticized for being out of the country when the blaze erupted and lost her re-election bid in early November 2026, weeks before the retrial verdict.

For the survivors, the verdict offers acknowledgment but little closure. "There is no amount of courtroom justice that will ever undo the harm that was caused," Los Angeles City Council member Traci Park, who represents the Palisades, said. "What happened was just so deeply personal and traumatic."

## What's Next

Rinderknecht's sentencing on March 17, 2027, will determine how long he spends in federal prison, with the range spanning from five to 40 years. Defense attorneys have signaled they may pursue an appeal, though no filing has been announced.

Beyond the courtroom, the region's longer-term challenges remain. Rebuilding in Pacific Palisades and Malibu continues to lag, and thousands of residents remain displaced nearly two years after the fire. The civil litigation is expected to grind on for years, testing how the city and county account for their response to a disaster that many residents believe was compounded by institutional failures.

For now, the verdict closes one chapter: the question of who lit the match that, a week later, consumed a community. The questions of how the region prepares for the next fire — and whether accountability extends beyond a single individual — remain open.
