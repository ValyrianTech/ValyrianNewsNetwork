# China Launches 30th Batch of Satellite Internet Satellites

China successfully launched the 30th batch of satellites for its low-orbit satellite internet constellation on the evening of 20 November, sending the spacecraft into their planned orbits aboard a Long March 8A rocket, according to state media. The mission marks another step in Beijing's push to build a large-scale broadband network in low Earth orbit, a project that has now moved well beyond its initial deployment phase.

The launch took place at 19:59 Beijing time from the Hainan Commercial Spacecraft Launch Site near Wenchang, [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/20261120/da600aa6b4e94c90a93b2dd59f9081f3/c.html) reported. "The satellites smoothly entered their planned orbits and the launch mission was a complete success," the agency wrote, crediting reporters at the scene. [CCTV News](https://news.southcn.com/node_47a8059d6c/20ead1c314.shtml), republished by the Southern News Network, confirmed the same timing and outcome.

## A Mega-Constellation in the Making

The satellites belong to the GW constellation operated by China SatNet (China Satellite Network Group), a state-owned enterprise leading the country's flagship satellite-internet programme. According to [IT之家](https://m.ithome.com/html/928564.htm), the GW plan is China's first giant satellite-internet initiative and the world's second-largest planned low-orbit constellation after SpaceX's Starlink, with a projected total of 12,992 satellites.

The system uses a two-layer architecture — a very-low orbit below roughly 500 kilometres plus a near-Earth orbit at about 1,145 kilometres — a design intended to balance low latency against wide coverage. Reporting has projected that a backbone network and nationwide coverage could be established around the end of 2026.

## From Experiment to Industrial Cadence

The 30th-batch milestone matters less for the number of satellites lofted than for what it signals about cadence. China has shifted toward low-cost, mass-produced, short-cycle launches, and the Hainan site has repeatedly cleared multiple batches within months.

The sequencing tells the story. Batch-launches began in earnest in late 2025; by 26 December 2025 the Hainan site had completed ten consecutive successful launches. Batches 18 and 19 followed in mid-January 2026, and batch 20 flew on 13 March 2026 — the first GW deployment after the annual Government Work Report.

That report explicitly named "accelerating the development of satellite internet" as a key task for the year, and the country's 15th Five-Year Plan outline reportedly lists satellite-internet infrastructure among 109 major projects across six areas. Together, the policies frame satellite internet as future national critical information infrastructure underpinning 6G and a broader "space-air-ground" digital backbone.

## The Commercial Logic: Beyond Launching Satellites

Industry figures argue the real value lies not in firing satellites into orbit but in connecting and using them. In an interview with [21世纪经济报道](https://www.21jingji.com/article/20260320/herald/3fe528f6ef07a7a2eb63bc8177356e7f.html), Wu Minhua, chairman of satellite core-network firm Zeyou Technology, described the sector's shift from policy-driven to market-driven growth.

"In the past, satellite communication was an island," Wu said, arguing that dense low-orbit launches and the spread of direct-to-phone satellite links are pushing the industry toward "a network of everything" and integrated satellite-terrestrial systems. He noted that core networks — the "brain" that handles user access, resource scheduling and service support — are what turn hundreds of satellites from "space information islands" into a functioning network.

Wu also pointed to the demand side: government and emergency communications as an absolute necessity, alongside maritime, remote-area and low-altitude economy applications where ground base stations leave coverage gaps. Zeyou, which supplies the core network for the Tiantong-1 system, reported that satellite-internet business accounted for about 7.15% of its revenue in the first three quarters of 2025.

## Analysis: A Strategic Race With High Stakes

The 30th batch illustrates how China's satellite-internet effort has matured from demonstration flights into an industrial routine. Where early missions tested hardware and procedures, the current phase tests supply chains, launch turnaround and cost control — the unglamorous mechanics that determine whether a constellation of thousands of satellites is economically viable.

The strategic dimension is hard to overstate. Low Earth orbit is a finite and contested resource, and the pace of deployment determines how much of the orbital and spectrum envelope a country can claim. China SatNet's GW plan and Shanghai's separate Qianfan ("Thousand Sails") constellation together represent a national bet that satellite broadband will become as essential as terrestrial mobile networks.

That bet is also an industrial one. Chinese media and analysts have described 2026 as a year of "triple resonance" of policy, industry and demand for the low-orbit sector, with listed companies positioning around the GW and Qianfan supply chains in antennas, terminals and Ka-band user equipment. The commercial test will be whether downstream demand — direct-to-phone service, maritime connectivity, emergency response and low-altitude applications — grows fast enough to justify the buildout.

## What's Next

With batch 30 in orbit, attention turns to whether China SatNet can sustain its launch tempo through the remainder of 2026 and deliver on projections of a nationwide backbone by year's end. The next milestones to watch are the pace of subsequent batch launches, the commercial rollout of direct-to-phone services, and how Chinese operators position themselves against Starlink in overseas markets — where, as Wu suggested, the pitch is less about selling data plans than about helping governments build sovereign, controllable networks.

For now, the message from Hainan is one of steady momentum: another batch, another successful orbit insertion, another step toward a Chinese broadband network in the sky.
