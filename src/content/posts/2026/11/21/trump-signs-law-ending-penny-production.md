# Trump Signs Law Ending the Penny After Nearly 250 Years

President Donald Trump on Friday signed the bipartisan Common Cents Act into law, formally directing the federal government to stop minting the one-cent coin and closing the book on nearly 250 years of continuous penny production, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/white-house/trump-formally-ends-minting-penny-nearly-250-years-production-rcna600580).

The measure, H.R. 10167, directs the Department of the Treasury to halt production of the penny for general circulation while permitting continued production for collectors, according to the [White House statement](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2026/11/h-r-10167-common-cents-act/). The signing formalizes a production halt that had already been in place since November 2025, when the U.S. Mint struck its final circulating penny in Philadelphia.

## Why Washington Ended the Penny

The decision rests on simple arithmetic. Over the past decade, the total production cost of a penny has risen from 1.3 cents to 3.69 cents per coin, according to a [Treasury Department FAQ](https://home.treasury.gov/news/featured-stories/penny-production-cessation-faqs). The U.S. Mint lost roughly $18 million minting pennies in 2024 alone, and it projects an immediate annual savings of $56 million in reduced material costs by stopping production.

"Washington has a spending problem, and it shows up in places big and small," said Rep. Lisa McClain (R-Mich.), the bill's author, in a [statement from the House Financial Services Committee](https://financialservices.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=411256). "If the federal government is spending nearly four cents to make a penny worth one cent, something is broken."

McClain, the House Republican Conference chairwoman, has pegged the total annual savings at $85 million — a higher estimate than the Mint's $56 million figure, likely reflecting broader program costs beyond raw materials. Both the House and Senate passed the measure by voice vote, signaling no notable opposition to a rare piece of bipartisan consensus.

The bill's path was lengthy. An earlier version cleared the House Financial Services Committee in July 2025, the full House passed it in September 2026, and the Senate followed later that month before Trump's signature finalized it on November 20.

## What Changes for Consumers

The penny remains legal tender "for all debts, public charges, taxes, and dues," and the roughly 114 billion pennies already in existence will not disappear. The Federal Reserve will continue recirculating them "for as long as possible," Treasury said.

For cash transactions, the law establishes a symmetrical rounding procedure for when exact change isn't available. Totals ending in 1, 2, 6, or 7 cents round down; those ending in 3, 4, 8, or 9 cents round up. Transactions totaling exactly 1 or 2 cents may round up to a nickel. Rounding does not apply to electronic payments, checks, gift cards, or split payments.

"It's meant to eliminate those awkward situations where, if you buy something for 99 cents and you hand over a dollar bill, the retailer doesn't give you anything back because they don't have pennies," Steve Kenneally, senior vice president for payments at the American Bankers Association, [told USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/money/personal-finance/2026/10/01/common-cents-act-pennies-coins-value/92020168007/).

The law also resolves a quieter legal problem. In about a dozen states and some cities, rounding cash transactions to a nickel was illegal under laws requiring equal treatment of all customers, according to the National Retail Federation. The Common Cents Act effectively overrides those rules so merchants can round consistently nationwide — a federal preemption of state consumer-protection law that critics may yet challenge.

## Consumer Trust and the Nickel's Future

Rounding has bred suspicion. When the National Association of Convenience Stores surveyed consumers, 25% said they believed retailers would profit from rounding up, while only 10% thought customers would benefit; the remaining 65% correctly guessed it evens out. Some merchants have responded by rounding every transaction down to build trust, losing money in the process.

"Right now, you have businesses that are having to implement these rounding procedures without much certainty or clarity," Dylan Jeon, vice president of government relations at the National Retail Federation, told USA Today.

The law also takes aim at the nickel, which is an even bigger money-loser: the government spent 13.78 cents to make each nickel in 2024, a loss of nearly 9 cents per coin. The legislation allows nickels to be made with cost-saving materials — an inner layer of zinc with an outer layer of nickel — and requires Treasury to notify Congress before eliminating any other coin.

## A Coin With Deep Roots

The penny's history stretches to 1787, when the first cent was struck with a sundial design created by Benjamin Franklin. Congress passed the Coinage Act in 1792, and the U.S. Mint began producing the coin the following year. It cycled through designs — the Flying Eagle, the Indian Head — before Abraham Lincoln's profile arrived in 1909 to mark his 100th birthday. The most recent "Union Shield" reverse debuted in 2010.

Even as its purchasing power collapsed, the penny carried outsized cultural weight, embedded in idioms from "pinch pennies" to "penny wise and pound foolish." U.S. Treasurer Brandon Beach, present when the last circulating penny was struck, was careful to stress continuity. "We're saying goodbye to the penny today, but let me just be crystal clear... it's still legal tender," he said at the time.

## What to Watch

The transition will test retailers and point-of-sale system providers, who must update software and train staff to handle rounding transparently. Treasury has said it is working with vendors to ensure systems calculate rounding and sales taxes correctly.

Beyond the practical adjustments, the penny's demise raises a larger question: whether the nickel is next. Coin experts expect it eventually will be, for the same fiscal reasons that doomed the cent. For now, collectors will still be able to buy limited-edition pennies, and the final circulating coin — a small bronze artifact of a lost monetary era — is slated for auction.

After nearly 250 years, the smallest unit of American commerce is officially a piece of history.
