# Iran Widens Gulf Attacks, Striking US Base in Kuwait

Iran widened its regional military campaign across the Persian Gulf on Wednesday, targeting the Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait and launching several attacks on Bahrain—an expansion of a nearly nine-month-old war into new Gulf states and the first strikes on US facilities in Bahrain in roughly two months. According to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/iran-us-kuwait-gulf-attacks-bahrain-b1f0b530cffe951e6fcc1e27b2e1af94), the early-morning attacks appeared to be retaliation for an American strike on an Iranian island the day before.

The strikes came as Gulf nations scrambled to intercept incoming fire from Iran and its allies, and as Washington weighed whether to restart major combat operations. Explosions were also heard in Saudi Arabia, where the kingdom said it intercepted missiles and drones fired by the Iran-backed Houthis.

## A Tit-for-Tat Cycle

The attacks followed a familiar pattern. A US fighter jet struck a Revolutionary Guard missile launch site Tuesday on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz with "precision munitions," a US military official said. That strike was itself a riposte to an Iranian attack on an American radar installation in Bahrain earlier on Tuesday.

Iran said its recent attacks—including those on other US facilities in Bahrain on Sunday and Friday—were in response to US drone surveillance operations and an attack on Iranian communications towers. "Bahrain has now suffered four Iranian attacks in six days, ending a weeks-long respite," Agence France-Presse [reported](https://www.freemalaysiatoday.com/category/highlight/2026/11/19/gulf-countries-under-fire-after-us-hits-iran-island).

In the assault on Ali Al Salem, the Iranian military said its "combat and kamikaze drones" targeted US air defense systems and missile and drone launch platforms at the base, according to Iran's state broadcaster. Kuwait's military said its air defenses intercepted drone attacks and did not immediately report any damage. The Kuwaiti Foreign Ministry condemned the repeated Iranian attacks as "criminal," saying they threatened the country's security and stability.

Bahrain's military said it intercepted a number of attacks from Iran but gave no information on any damage or casualties. Bahrain's Interior Ministry issued two early-morning alerts urging people to take shelter, followed by an "all clear." Bahrain hosts the US Navy's 5th Fleet.

## A Base Already Battered

The widening strikes land on infrastructure that has already absorbed heavy punishment. Ali Al Salem Air Base had been struck at least 30 times by Iran during the war, damaging or destroying at least 15 aircraft hangars, numerous warehouses and 10 buildings near a residential area, according to [an AP satellite-imagery analysis](https://apnews.com/article/iran-war-us-base-damage-trump-26e0f05e01d6f56ff46f7c1a14384f7c). That same analysis found that, through August, more than 115 drone or missile strikes claimed by Iran or Iranian-backed militias damaged or destroyed structures on 26 military bases and sites with US equipment, causing billions of dollars in equipment damage.

The war began on February 28, 2026, when Israel and the US attacked Iran. Since then, Iran has regularly fired on American facilities and interests in neighboring Gulf countries, energy infrastructure and shipping. Its strikes have focused first on early-warning systems before hitting aircraft and buildings.

Damage assessments in the Gulf remain opaque. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao told a US Senate committee he could not offer a full accounting in Bahrain because "it's a nonpermissive area for us to go," though he previously said Iran "blew the hell out of Bahrain."

## Diplomacy Under Pressure

The escalation is unfolding against an intense diplomatic tug-of-war. American allies in the Middle East have accelerated efforts to prevent a resumption of all-out war, as pressure grows from Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu for the Trump administration to launch a joint air campaign on Iran. Government and security officials from the Gulf countries, as well as Pakistan and Turkey, have engaged in concerted diplomacy, including phone calls with senior US officials, to avert renewed war, according to four regional officials who spoke on condition of anonymity.

Qatar's prime minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani, met with Trump at the White House the prior week in a bid to bring the US and Iran back to the negotiating table. The Gulf states have been concerned that a recent US visit by Israel's Mossad chief, Roman Gofman, would tilt Trump toward restarting the war.

Qatar, which has served as a mediator, called the latest attacks a "dangerous escalation" and warned they undercut efforts to find a peaceful solution. Saudi Arabia voiced its "strongest condemnation" of the attacks on Bahrain, Kuwait and an Emirati oil tanker, calling for an end to the violence and negotiations to resolve the crisis.

## Calibrated Escalation

Analysts broadly agree Iran is pursuing a strategy of deliberate, calibrated escalation rather than triggering an all-out war. "Iran appears to be pursuing a strategy of deliberate, calibrated escalation," Danny Citrinowicz, a researcher at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies and a former Iran specialist in Israeli military and intelligence services, wrote on X. "The objective is not necessarily to trigger an all-out war, but to steadily increase the military, economic, and political costs of maintaining the blockade."

Hamidreza Azizi, a senior Iran analyst at the International Crisis Group, warned on X that "the accumulation of military incidents across multiple fronts increases the risk of miscalculation and escalation beyond what either side originally intended."

Much hinges on the US naval blockade of Iranian ports, which has squeezed Tehran's economy even as American escorts help restore some oil tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz—a waterway that carried a quarter of global oil flows before the war. Iran's army chief, Amir Hatami, warned of a "more devastating" response to the US and its allies, saying it would "know no geographical boundaries," according to Iran's Fars news agency.

## Economic and Human Costs

The maritime front remains dangerous. UAE state-owned oil company ADNOC said Wednesday that one of its tanker crew members was missing after an attack in the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday, while 11 others were injured and receiving medical care. Ships transiting Hormuz have come under near-daily fire from Iran, which insists they use only routes it has authorized.

For residents of the Gulf, the renewed violence has revived anxieties that had begun to ease. "The explosions seemed to be coming from far away, but hearing them again after a period of calm was both surprising and worrying," said Hani Mohammed, 40, an Egyptian resident in Kuwait. "People had started to readjust to a normal life. But what happened has revived feelings of anxiety."

## What to Watch

Beyond Kuwait and Bahrain, the conflict now spans the Strait of Hormuz, Saudi Arabia and Yemen. Saudi officials have briefed allies that the conflict with the Houthis could last another six months, according to a diplomat familiar with the matter. The Houthis control much of Yemen, including the capital Sanaa, and have made territorial gains along the Red Sea coast; a four-year truce dissolved in July.

The path forward depends largely on two variables. Citrinowicz argued there are "two clear paths to de-escalation: a negotiated agreement or a US decision to lift the blockade." Should neither materialize, the risk of a broader war—one that could engulf oil facilities, power plants, desalination infrastructure and undersea cables—will keep mounting. Meanwhile, the commander of US Central Command, Adm. Brad Cooper, met in Tel Aviv on Wednesday with Israel's military chief of staff, Eyal Zamir, and Gofman, underscoring the competing pressures shaping Washington's next move.

For now, the Gulf is bracing for what comes next.
