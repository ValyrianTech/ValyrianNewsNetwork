# No Ban on Killer Robots, Just Two More Years of Talks

A week of negotiations at the United Nations in Geneva has ended without an international ban on lethal autonomous weapons — the systems widely known as "killer robots" — leaving the fast-developing technology unregulated at the global level. The 128 states party to the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) instead agreed only to "make a start on developing an international instrument" on such weapons, with that work to be completed by 2028, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/21/vn-bereiken-overeenstemming-tijdens-conferentie-over-killerrobot/).

The outcome, announced by conference chair Julien Thöni after five days of talks that ran into the early morning hours, amounts in practice to roughly two more years of consultations — with no concrete, binding prohibitions on the use of autonomous weapons for now.

## A Last Chance That Slipped Away

Supporters had framed the Seventh Review Conference, which opened on 16 November, as the last best chance to set ground rules before AI-assisted weapons become too advanced and widespread to control. The CCW meets only once every five years, and campaigners warned that the window for meaningful regulation was closing fast.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres opened the conference by urging delegates from more than 120 member states to negotiate a legally binding instrument with "clear prohibitions and restrictions." "New technologies, including autonomous weapons systems, present new humanitarian, legal, ethical and security challenges," he said. "I urge you to seize this moment. Take action to prevent the use and spread of these merciless weapons," [UN News reported](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/11/1168810).

That goal was not met. Guterres had hoped negotiations on an international accord would begin in 2026.

## Consensus as a Choke Point

The central obstacle is the CCW's rule that all decisions must be approved by consensus — meaning a single country can block any agreement. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/16/nx-s1-6042548/un-killer-robots-ai-weapons-drones), the United States, Russia and Israel indicated they would oppose legally binding rules, with Washington proposing a five-year timetable for non-binding guidelines instead. Nearly 100 countries, including Ireland, Italy, Brazil, Mexico, France and Germany, had pushed for negotiations on a binding ban.

The procedural fight began almost immediately. [Geneva Solutions](https://genevasolutions.news/peace-humanitarian/russia-s-delay-tactics-derail-un-talks-on-autonomous-weapons-in-geneva) reported that Russia blocked approval of the conference's agenda within minutes of the opening, objecting to the longstanding practice of allowing observers such as the European Union to speak. Chair Thöni suspended the conference and moved talks into informal consultations from Tuesday.

"It's another case of consensus paralysis," said Anna Hehir of the Future of Life Institute. Beatrice Fihn, founder of Lex International, accused resistant states of "weaponising consensus, on both procedural and substantive issues, to ensure that there will be no outcome this week."

The deadlock echoed an earlier setback. At a CCW expert session in September, states had already reached consensus on a non-binding text that created no legal obligations and contained no prohibitions — a draft rights groups said had been "substantially diluted" in its final hours, [Reuters reported](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/states-reach-agreement-at-autonomous-weapons-talks-in-geneva-4890192).

## Why It Matters

Lethal autonomous weapons use sensors, algorithms and artificial intelligence to select and engage targets without a human giving the final order to kill. Limited forms already exist — for instance, defensive systems on warships — but a more autonomous generation is emerging in active conflicts, including in Ukraine, Sudan, Gaza and the Middle East.

The International Committee of the Red Cross warns that machines cannot read the contextual signals that distinguish a soldier still fighting from one who is wounded or surrendering. "The risks are incredibly high that there will be a misidentification of a target," ICRC legal adviser Georgia Hinds told NPR. The UN's human rights chief, Volker Türk, has argued that "a machine must never have the power to decide whether a human being survives or dies."

Over the next two years, the work will focus on clarifying which autonomous weapons can be prohibited under international humanitarian law and which may remain permitted but subject to rules.

## Disappointment and a Search for Alternatives

NGOs reacted sharply. Stop Killer Robots called the result a "watered-down mandate" that does not genuinely clear the path to a binding accord, while Human Rights Watch called the outcome "disappointing." Brazil's ambassador Frederico Meyer, a proponent of stricter rules, voiced the frustration of ban supporters: "Technology evolves faster than diplomacy. Diplomats are very slow."

With consensus blocked in Geneva, states and campaigners are weighing routes outside the CCW — taking decisions to the UN General Assembly, invoking a mechanism to negotiate a new protocol, or hosting standalone talks, as Norway did in 2006 with the Oslo Process after the CCW failed to address cluster munitions.

## What's Next

For now, the weapons remain unregulated at the international level. The key questions are whether the two-year process toward 2028 produces anything binding or repeats the pattern of ever-weaker texts, and how quickly autonomous systems will be deployed on battlefields before rules exist. In the interim, national legal reviews, export controls and procurement terms may end up doing more to constrain the technology than any treaty — a stopgap that leaves the hardest decisions to individual governments rather than to international law.
