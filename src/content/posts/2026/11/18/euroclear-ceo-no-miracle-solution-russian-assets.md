# Euroclear CEO: No Miracle Solution on Russian Assets

The head of Euroclear has said there is still no miracle solution to the question of roughly €185 billion in frozen Russian central bank assets held at the Brussels-based clearing house, warning that any plan to mobilise the money would not eliminate the risk of bankruptcy for the institution, damage the eurozone's reputation, or leave Belgian taxpayers exposed.

In an interview with Belgian daily [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/782489/article/2026-11-13/la-patronne-deuroclear-au-soir-sur-les-actifs-russes-il-ny-toujours-pas-de) published on 13 November, CEO Valerie Urbain framed the assets as "Europe's biggest bargaining chip" and argued that "the best use of the Russian assets is to integrate them into peace negotiations."

## The Scale of the Exposure

European Union countries immobilised some €210 billion ($236 billion) of Russian central bank assets after Moscow's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, according to [Reuters](https://live.euronext.com/en/financial-news/no-miracle-solution-frozen-russian-assets-euroclear-ceo-tells-soir). Euroclear holds about €185 billion of that total — more than 80% of its own balance sheet.

That concentration is why Belgium, home to Euroclear, has been the most vocal opponent of proposals to disburse the assets on behalf of Ukraine. The liability sits disproportionately on Belgian shoulders: lawsuits, potential claims for the full asset value plus interest and damages, and the systemic risk to a financial institution that clears more than the entire global GDP every month.

Urbain told Le Soir that transferring only the assets and not the corresponding liabilities would bankrupt Euroclear. Any transfer, she argued, would require permanent guarantees from EU member states until the war ends or until Euroclear's repayment obligation to Russia is extinguished.

## A Recurring European Fault Line

Some European nations — notably the Baltic states, Poland and the Nordic countries — have pushed for the Russian assets to be used to fund Ukraine. The European Commission's earlier concept of a "reparations loan," backed by German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, sought to leverage the assets for a large zero-interest loan to Kyiv.

Belgium resisted the plan throughout 2025, with Prime Minister Bart De Wever demanding risk mutualisation, legal certainty and transparency. As the research record shows, his objection crystallised in a simple image: the assets were a "poule aux œufs d'or" — a golden-egg-laying hen that would stop producing if slaughtered for a single meal. Defence Minister Theo Francken later described Belgium's refusal as "non-negotiable."

The reparations-loan plan ultimately collapsed at the December 2025 summit run, and EU leaders pivoted instead to joint debt to raise €90 billion for Ukraine, leaving the €210 billion in Russian assets immobilised. Yet the issue has not gone away: several member states continue to push to revisit the question, prompting Belgium to restate its categorical opposition.

## The Legal War

Behind the political debate sits an escalating legal battle. Russia has weaponised its courts against Euroclear, with a Moscow arbitration court ordering the clearing house in May 2026 to pay roughly $250 billion in damages over the frozen assets, later authorising the Russian Central Bank to claim €217 billion in damages and interest. Russian courts rejected Euroclear's appeal in July, and its cassation appeal was rebuffed in October — a fact disclosed in its third-quarter results.

Euroclear says these rulings are not recognised under EU law and has set aside a €162 million provision to cover Russian claims. It continues to fight for its interests, including before the Belgian courts. The judgments are not enforceable within the EU, but they sustain legal uncertainty and headline risk around a systemic institution.

## What Euroclear Has Already Given

Amid the standoff, Euroclear's shareholders have agreed to pass the interest earned on the frozen assets — nearly €8 billion so far — to the European Commission for Ukraine. That arrangement has allowed Belgium to point out that it is already contributing the windfall from the immobilised funds without touching the principal.

Urbain also said Euroclear has not been targeted by a cyberattack so far, despite a recent wave of cyber incidents affecting several European countries, including Belgium. The reassurance comes as financial infrastructure operators across the continent have heightened vigilance.

Financially, Euroclear remains robust: in the first nine months of 2026 it posted a net profit of €903 million, up 7%, on adjusted operating profit of about €1.3 billion, up 10%.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is whether the new scenarios circulating in European capitals can satisfy Belgium's demand for ironclad, EU-wide guarantees — or whether the bloc will again set the assets aside in favour of other funding mechanisms. Urbain's intervention is a reminder that the money is not a free resource: using it carries systemic, legal and reputational risk that no mechanism has yet resolved.

For Kyiv, the frozen billions remain a tantalising but legally fraught source of support. For Brussels, they are leverage to be preserved rather than spent — a bargaining chip whose value may lie precisely in keeping it on the table.
