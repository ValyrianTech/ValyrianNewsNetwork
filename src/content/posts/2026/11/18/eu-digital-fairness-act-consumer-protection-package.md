# EU Moves to End Abuses in Online Commerce

The European Commission has proposed the most sweeping overhaul of EU consumer protection law in a generation, targeting the manipulative design tactics, subscription traps and misleading influencer marketing that have come to define online shopping. The **Digital Fairness Act (DFA)**, presented in Brussels on 11 November 2026, arrives alongside a revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation (CPC) Regulation that would hand the Commission direct powers to investigate and fine large-scale cross-border breaches—penalties reaching up to **6% of a company's worldwide annual turnover**.

The package, framed by [L'Echo](https://www.lecho.be/economie-politique/europe/economie/achats-forces-abonnements-pieges-influenceurs-l-europe-veut-mettre-fin-aux-derives-du-commerce-en-ligne/10699394.html) as Europe acting to "put an end to abuses in online commerce," aims to give the bloc's 450 million consumers the same protection online as offline. According to the [European Commission](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_2407), the measures are expected to save EU consumers up to €13.7 billion a year by preventing some €88.4 billion in losses between 2025 and 2035.

## A Rulebook Built for an Analog Era

The proposals respond to a well-documented gap. The Commission's 2024 Digital Fairness Fitness Check concluded that consumer rules drafted for the offline world no longer fit a digital marketplace shaped by sophisticated behavioural design. Enforcement today runs through national authorities via the CPC Network, which has carried out more than 30 coordinated actions since 2020 but lacks the tools to act swiftly against practices that cross borders as easily as they cross screens.

"The Single Market lives through its 450 million consumers. Our laws must keep up with the way people live, shop and do business today," said Henna Virkkunen, Executive Vice-President for Tech Sovereignty, Security and Democracy. "This reform will make everyday life simpler, fairer and safer for consumers."

## What the Digital Fairness Act Would Change

At its core, the DFA confronts the commercial architecture of the modern internet. The Commission's proposals include:

- **A ban on "dark patterns"**—manipulative tactics such as fake "almost sold out" claims that pressure consumers into purchases they might not otherwise make.
- **Transparent pricing**, including for purchases made with virtual in-app currencies and dynamic pricing for cultural and sports events. In virtual queues, companies would have to show the current price in real time alongside the highest possible price.
- **Fairness by design**, giving consumers the ability to switch off addictive features that encourage compulsive spending, such as persistent prompts. The proposal would also ban gambling-like mechanics for minors—pay-to-win and pay-to-progress systems of the kind seen in FIFA Ultimate Team-style loot boxes.
- **Influencer marketing transparency**, requiring content creators to disclose commercial content and prohibiting the exploitation of minors' trust.
- **Restrictions on unfair personalised ads and prices** targeting minors or exploiting sensitive data.
- **Easier cancellation** of digital contracts and subscriptions, ending unwanted automatic renewals and free trials that morph into subscription traps. Consumers would gain the right to speak to a human in customer service rather than being confined to a chatbot, along with new rights when using an AI agent to purchase on their behalf.

A separate simplification directive would make targeted amendments to the Consumer Rights Directive, streamlining pre-contractual information requirements for repeat in-app purchases and clarifying how consumers can cancel digital media subscriptions within the withdrawal period.

## Enforcement Gets Teeth—and a Debate

The CPC Regulation revision may prove the more consequential of the two texts. It would give the Commission direct investigative and fining powers in large-scale cases with a major impact across the Single Market—a competence that currently rests with national authorities. Non-EU companies would be required to appoint a legal representative in the EU to cooperate with regulators.

"Strong consumer protection is the foundation of competitiveness," said Michael McGrath, Commissioner for Democracy, Justice, the Rule of Law and Consumer Protection. "We are not making it harder to do business, we are making it harder to do business unfairly."

Business groups are less convinced. In a [statement](https://www.businesseurope.eu/publications/press-statement-businesseurope-reacts-to-the-eu-consumer-package/), BusinessEurope Director-General Markus J. Beyrer warned that the DFA "risks adding new burdens to an already crowded EU rulebook" and raised "serious concerns" about proposed rules shifting the burden of proof. The lobby group called for "an immediate moratorium on new regulatory burdens," arguing that "more regulation does not automatically mean better protection."

## A Crowded Rulebook

The package lands in an already dense field of EU digital law. It is designed to work alongside the Digital Services Act, which allows fines of up to 6% of turnover, the Digital Markets Act (up to 10%), the AI Act, and the newly proposed EU Kids Act, which shifts responsibility for children's online safety onto companies. The two files—the DFA and the Kids Act—are expected to be negotiated side by side.

That overlap is a central concern. As [Euronews](https://fr.euronews.com/2026/11/11/lue-devoile-un-digital-fairness-act-contre-pieges-dabonnements-et-designs-addictifs) reported, industry figures received the proposals with caution. Isabelle Roccia, managing director of IAPP Europe, questioned whether the DFA brings genuinely new tools or simply compounds complexity: "Does the DFA introduce new tools that organisations, consumers and regulators don't already have in their toolbox?"

Monika Zdzieborska, a lawyer at Clifford Chance, identified three fault lines likely to shape the negotiations: consistency—whether the DFA fills genuine gaps or adds another layer atop existing law; defaults—whether addictive features should be opt-in rather than opt-out; and proportionality—whether an e-commerce site, a streaming service, a game and a social network should be regulated the same way.

Civil society welcomed the direction but pressed for stronger obligations. Itxaso Domínguez de Olazábal of European Digital Rights argued the rules still lean too heavily on individuals finding the off switch. "The Commission has spent years studying how companies manipulate us online," she said. "Now comes the uncomfortable part: telling those companies to stop. The Parliament and Member States have a lot of work ahead to give this law the teeth it deserves."

## The Wider Stakes

There is a political dimension beneath the consumer-protection language. Centralising enforcement would shift regulatory power away from national capitals—notably Ireland, which hosts the European headquarters of Apple, Google, Meta and Microsoft. As [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2313329/europe-wants-to-ban-dark-patterns-in-apps-and-loot-boxes-for-minors/) noted, a recent UN experts' report raised concerns about tech firms' "undue influence" over Ireland's privacy regulator.

The proposal also cuts against a broader current. Since 2024, EU leaders have called for simplification and a lighter regulatory touch; 2026, by contrast, has brought a wave of platform-related proposals, fuelling fears of overregulation. Whether the DFA is seen as necessary surgery or regulatory creep may determine how quickly it survives the legislative process.

## What Happens Next

The proposals now go to the European Parliament and the Council of member states, where they will be negotiated—and likely amended—before becoming law. Key questions remain open: whether centralised enforcement powers survive scrutiny from member states, whether addictive design features will be off by default, how liability will work when AI agents shop on a consumer's behalf, and what support will offset compliance costs for small online traders who lack legal teams.

For consumers, the ambition is unmistakable. For businesses, the message is that the era of competing on the ability to exploit human psychology may be drawing to a close. "Shopping has never been more convenient, but convenience must never come at the expense of fairness," McGrath said. The task now falls to lawmakers to decide just how far that principle will reach.
