# Femina to Haenel, Goncourt to Jaenada in all-male season

The French autumn literary prize season delivered its headline verdicts in early November 2026, crowning two of the country's most respected novelists—and igniting a debate about who French literature's most prestigious awards actually recognise. Yannick Haenel won the Prix Femina on 4 November for his novel on the solitude of teaching, a day after Philippe Jaenada took the Prix Goncourt, France's most coveted literary honour.

## A Goncourt for an unsolved crime

Jaenada, 62, won the Goncourt on 3 November from the steps of the Drouant restaurant in Paris, the traditional venue since 1914, for *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel* (Flammarion), as [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/goncourt-2026-le-prestigieux-prix-a-ete-attribue-a-philippe-jaenada-pour-son-livre-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel-11809864). The book is a sprawling investigation into the unsolved 1949 murder of Louise Cansot, a 29-year-old Breton woman who became a Paris model and whose body was found on the banks of the Seine.

Jaenada conducts the inquiry in the first person, drawing on the methods of Simenon's Commissaire Maigret as he sifts through police archives and revisits the victim's haunts. Académie Goncourt president Philippe Claudel said the novel "makes visible a woman who was made invisible, a victim, a young victim whom the police did not much bother about." Jaenada succeeds Laurent Mauvignier, who won in 2025.

The victory is a culmination for a writer known for archival crime investigations such as *Sulak* and *La Serpe*—the latter of which earned him the Femina in 2017. "For me it is an accomplishment, it is an honour," Jaenada said, describing the experience to France Inter as something that "really resembles vertigo."

## Haenel and the solitude of teachers

The following day, a jury of 12 women awarded the Femina to Haenel, 59, for *La solitude des professeurs est infinie* (Gallimard), a narrative drawing on his own years teaching in the 1990s. Its protagonist, Jean Deichel—the author's double—is a young trainee French teacher arriving at a collège in Mantes-la-Jolie, a Paris suburb, where he confronts stress and the hard realities of the job.

Haenel dedicated the prize "to teachers, to high-school and middle-school pupils," accepting it amid an ongoing student protest movement in France, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576). He said he felt the need to write about the solitude of teachers after the assassinations of teachers Samuel Paty in 2020 and Dominique Bernard in 2023, both killed in jihadist attacks.

The book captures teaching's fleeting joys—"when a face lights up furtively, when a pupil shows understanding, when a lesson is a success"—while insisting on its difficulties. "Whatever the pedagogues say, it is violence that comes first in middle schools; and being a teacher means halting its unleashing," Haenel writes.

The Femina win capped a remarkable two days for Haenel, who had been the Goncourt runner-up the day before. He previously won the Prix Interallié in 2009 for *Jan Karski* and the Prix Médicis in 2017 for *Tiens ferme ta couronne*, and writes columns including for the satirical weekly *Charlie Hebdo*. In the same Femina round, the American Laura Kasischke won the foreign-language prize for *Baignade surveillée*, and Jennifer Tamas took the essay prize for *Barbe Bleue contre-enquête*.

## An all-male season, and a backlash

Beneath the celebration ran a pointed controversy. All five major French literary prizes of the season went to men: the Goncourt to Jaenada, the Renaudot to François-Henri Désérable, the Femina to Haenel, the Médicis to Philippe Vasset and the Grand prix de l'Académie française to Sébastien Lapaque.

On 6 November, more than 200 women authors published a tribune in *Libération* denouncing what they called the "sexism" of the system, as [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-en-france-plus-de-200-autrices-denoncent-dans-une-tribune-le-sexisme-du-systeme-11811966). Signatories included Nobel laureate Annie Ernaux, Virginie Despentes and Anne Godard. "We express our anger at this system of literary rewards whose functioning, peculiar to France, does not seem to us at all capable of reflecting the richness and diversity of the literature being written today," the authors wrote in the [tribune](https://www.liberation.fr/idees-et-debats/tribunes/le-vieux-systeme-de-lentre-soi-patriarcal-fonctionne-a-plein-plus-de-200-autrices-denoncent-le-sexisme-des-grands-prix-litteraires-20261106_OICJ6TRW3VHHLIZX6RRAJIAPK4/).

The tribune said the juries had "accomplished the heroic feat of rewarding only men" in a season that had been described as open, with many acclaimed books by women. It criticised the "old system of patriarchal insularity," noting that jury members are co-opted and largely serve for life, and called for "rotating, gender-balanced juries." Over 124 years, the Goncourt has been awarded only 13 times to books by women.

## The Académie pushes back

Claudel defended the prize's record. "I read texts, and I do not read sexes," he said, noting that the number of books by women and men submitted is now roughly equal. He said he was among the first to deplore the imbalance, and that under his tenure the jury grew from two to four women out of ten members. He argued the Goncourt does not claim to reward the "best" book but reflects the view of a jury of established writers.

## AI casts a shadow

The season was also shaped by an artificial-intelligence controversy. Author Thélyson Orélien had been presented as a Goncourt favourite, but his novel *C'était ça ou mourir* was withdrawn from the selection and eliminated from other prizes over suspicions of AI use. The affair prompted the Académie to announce a rule change to explicitly exclude any work produced with AI, specifying that "the Goncourt prize crowns a work written by a human being."

Jaenada himself disclosed using AI three times in his winning novel—to hide a ChatGPT-generated sentence as a game with readers, to mock the tool's absurd answers, and once where it genuinely helped his research. "You cannot push back AI, as if it did not exist," he said. "You must use it, but first you must acknowledge it, and above all tame it—that is, not let yourself be replaced."

## What to watch

The twin crowns cement a strong season for two veteran novelists whose work turns on memory, crime and the lives of ordinary people. But the louder story may be the pressure building beneath the prizes themselves. With a reform debate over rotating juries now in the open and the Goncourt preparing to codify its AI rule before year's end, the question is no longer only who wins—but how, and by whom, the winners are chosen.
