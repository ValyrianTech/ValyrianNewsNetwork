# China Unveils Four Finds Tracing Yangtze Civilization

China's National Cultural Heritage Administration (NCHA) on Wednesday announced four new archaeological findings that together trace the origins and development of civilization along the Yangtze River, the country's longest waterway and one of its great cultural cradles. The findings, released at a work meeting in Beijing on the "Archaeology China" flagship research program, span an extraordinary chronological range — from a Paleolithic campsite dated roughly 300,000 to 50,000 years ago to a cemetery holding the most concentrated cache of Qin dynasty bamboo slips ever unearthed.

According to [Xinhua News Agency](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261118/ac40752dab8c40bfa4f4d4967abbb0eb/c.html), which reported the announcement, the four sites are the Taohuahe site in Suining, Sichuan; the Fanshan site at Liangzhu in Hangzhou, Zhejiang; the Lingtoushang site within the Qiaomailing complex in Jiujiang, Jiangxi; and the Laohudun cemetery in Yunmeng, Hubei. The meeting was thematically focused on tracing the origins of Yangtze River civilization (长江文明溯源), a research line the state has promoted as part of a broader effort to document China's deep past.

## A Deep-Time Narrative in Four Sites

The four findings are notable less for any single discovery than for their combined sweep. Together they span the Paleolithic, the Neolithic, the Bronze Age and the early imperial period, constructing a narrative of continuous human and cultural development in the Yangtze basin stretching back hundreds of millennia.

At the Taohuahe site, the NCHA described the most densely concentrated Middle Pleistocene open-air campsite yet found in the Yangtze basin, dated to approximately 300,000 to 50,000 years before present. Xinhua reported that the site features a distinctive standardized large flake chopper industry, alongside complex camp organizational forms and evidence of spatial planning capability — qualities the authority said are "of great significance for studying global early human evolution."

The site had first come to prominence following rescue excavations in 2022. Reporting at the time, [China.com.cn](http://cul.china.com.cn/2023-01/13/content_42232730.htm) noted that the excavation revealed three phases of Paleolithic culture and produced hand axes, picks and heavy scrapers, prompting experts to describe it as a major breakthrough in Chinese Paleolithic archaeology.

## Liangzhu's Royal Precinct

At the Fanshan site at Liangzhu, the authority announced the discovery of enclosure walls, platforms and building remains around what is already recognized as the highest-ranking elite cemetery of the Liangzhu culture period yet found. The new structures, Xinhua reported, reveal an integrated layout combining burial areas, ceremonial activity spaces and peripheral protective facilities — evidence, the NCHA said, for exploring "the ceremonial expression and institutionalized development of Liangzhu royal power."

The findings build directly on work reported the previous year. As [Guangming Online](https://m.gmw.cn/2025-10/20/content_38353096.htm) reported in October 2025, archaeologists had identified a rectangular wall structure around the Fanshan royal tombs, with carbon-14 dating of the east and south walls placing them at roughly 5,000 years old — contemporaneous with the Fanshan Tomb No. 12, which yielded the celebrated Liangzhu Jade Cong King and Jade Yue King.

The NCHA also emphasized the structural connection between the elite cemetery and the site's water facilities. The earthen ridge on which Fanshan sits, Xinhua reported, was both a key component of the Liangzhu water conservancy system and an important support for the spatial layout of the capital — evidence, the authority said, of the unified planning and coordinated construction of the water system, capital and high-ranking tombs.

## A Crossroads on the Yangtze

The third finding, the Lingtoushang site within the Qiaomailing complex in Jiujiang, Jiangxi, is one of the largest platform mounds in a large pre-Qin settlement complex located in the transitional zone between the middle and lower Yangtze. The NCHA reported that the site contains diverse settlement cultural elements from the late Neolithic and early Shang periods, reflecting frequent cultural transmission, interaction and integration between the middle and lower reaches of the river, and revealing close ties between the Central Plains and the middle Yangtze in cultural exchange and resource development and control.

## Qin Slips and Grassroots Governance

The fourth finding, the Laohudun cemetery in Yunmeng, Hubei, dates from the late Warring States period to the early Western Han dynasty and contains the most concentrated collection of Qin dynasty bamboo slips (秦简) yet unearthed. According to Xinhua, the materials recovered cover politics, military affairs, literature, medicine, folklore and art, and provide "the latest historical materials for studying the historical process of Qin-Han unification and grassroots social governance models."

The discovery extends Yunmeng's standing as a center of Qin-era written records. The county is renowned in Chinese archaeology as the site of the Shuihudi tombs, excavated in 1975, where more than 1,000 bamboo slips were recovered in the first major discovery of its kind in Chinese archaeological history.

## Analysis: A River at the Center of the Story

The grouping of these four findings under a single "Yangtze civilization origins" banner is itself significant. Chinese archaeology has long been dominated by a Yellow River-centric model of civilizational origins; by foregrounding the Yangtze, the NCHA advances a multi-regional origins thesis in which the great river basins developed as co-equal cradles of Chinese civilization. The chronological breadth of Wednesday's announcement — from the Middle Pleistocene to the early Han — serves that narrative by presenting the Yangtze basin as a theater of unbroken human and cultural development.

Two themes recur across the findings. The first is institutionalization of power: the Fanshan material, with its royal tombs, enclosure and coordinated water system, supports arguments about early state formation, hierarchy and ritual-institutional development. The second is governance and administration: the Laohudun slips connect archaeology to the history of administrative capacity, from local governance in the Qin-Han period to present-day interest in state capacity.

The releases also appear tied to policy. The NCHA has recently issued its Archaeology Work 15th Five-Year Plan and held a press conference on promoting high-quality archaeological development during that period, framing Wednesday's results within a broader national push.

## What's Next

For Taohuahe, dating work was still underway at the time of earlier reporting, with more precise dates to be confirmed by optically stimulated luminescence. For Fanshan, the new structural evidence is likely to sharpen scholarly debate over how early Liangzhu's elite burial system became institutionalized. And for Laohudun, conservation and study of the bamboo slips will take years, with their contents promising fresh insight into how the Qin and Han states governed at the local level.

What unites the four is a single, if sweeping, question: how far back, and through what stages, did one of the world's great river civilizations take shape. Wednesday's announcement offered four more pieces of an answer.
