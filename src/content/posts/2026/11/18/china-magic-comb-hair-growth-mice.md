# China's 'Magic Comb' Restores Hair in Mice

A comb-shaped bioelectronic device developed by scientists at Beijing's Beihang University restored hair growth by nearly 100 percent in mice with pattern baldness, according to a peer-reviewed study that offers a striking — if still distant — new approach to treating hair loss. The device, dubbed "Detangle," achieved roughly 97.85 percent regrowth over 28 days, far outpacing the topical treatment minoxidil, which managed about 70 percent in the same experiment, the [South China Morning Post (SCMP)](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/science/article/3374723/chinese-scientists-magic-comb-restores-hair-growth-nearly-100-mice-study) reported. The caveat that accompanies every headline about a mouse-model breakthrough applies in full: no human data exists yet, and clinical trials lie ahead.

## Why it matters

Androgenetic alopecia (AGA), the most common form of hair loss, is driven by hair follicles' sensitivity to androgens such as dihydrotestosterone (DHT). The condition affects more than 1.5 billion people worldwide, causing progressive hair loss with what the researchers describe as psychological and quality-of-life burdens. Existing treatments are imperfect: oral drugs such as finasteride can cause systemic side effects, while topical treatments like minoxidil suffer from limited efficacy and poor skin penetration. Closing that efficacy-and-penetration gap is precisely what the Detangle project set out to do.

## How the comb works

The device, described in [Nature Communications](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-79122-3), combines two established biomedical techniques in a consumer-friendly format. Its electronic teeth carry height-differentiated electrodes that generate a pulsed electric field, stimulating hair follicles as the user combs. Because the teeth alternate in length, the researchers report, the resulting field is about 2.63 times stronger than it would be with electrodes aligned in a row. The alternating shorter teeth also serve as drug-delivery modules, using a process called electroporation — which temporarily raises cell-membrane permeability — to drive therapeutic molecules deep into the skin. According to the paper's abstract, the system generates a field that penetrates up to roughly nine times deeper into hair follicle regions than conventional external application.

## Where the 'nearly 100%' figure comes from

That headline number deserves close reading. The full Detangle treatment pairs electrical stimulation with two experimental biological payloads: a VEGFA plasmid that stimulates blood-vessel growth around follicles, and an SRD5A2 siRNA that suppresses the androgen-related hormone that shrinks them. [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/scientists-invented-an-electric-comb-and-tests-in-mice-show-it-promotes-rapid-hair-growth) laid out the results across the 28-day trial on shaved back skin of mice with testosterone-induced hair loss:

- Untreated controls regrew about 1 percent of their hair.
- Electrical stimulation alone produced roughly 40 percent regrowth — less than minoxidil's 70 percent and finasteride's 50 percent.
- Electrostimulation plus SRD5A2 siRNA reached about 85 percent; with the VEGFA plasmid, about 90 percent.
- The full combination — stimulation plus both payloads — hit approximately 97.85 percent.

The distinction matters. As a delivery-and-stimulation platform on its own, the comb underperforms standard drugs. Most of its dramatic success depends on delivering experimental gene therapies deep into the follicle. The researchers themselves framed the result in those terms, writing that the approach "accelerates hair growth nearly 100 percent within a month in the AGA mouse model, surpassing the outcomes achievable with conventional oral or external medications."

## The gap between mice and men

The study's abstract states the Detangle system "proves safe and effective for deep tissue delivery in dermatology," but that conclusion rests on mouse data alone. ScienceAlert noted that the results are "a hugely promising proof of concept," while cautioning that further research must demonstrate the techniques are safe and effective in humans. Key questions remain unanswered: how durable the regrowth would be, how repeated electroporation and gene delivery would affect the scalp over time, and what a commercial device might cost. There is no public information yet on plans for human clinical trials, regulatory approval, or commercialization, and the authors declared no competing interests, suggesting no disclosed commercial partnership.

## What to watch

The work reflects China's expanding output in bioelectronics and biomedical engineering, with Beihang University and collaborating institutions — including Anhui Medical University, Peking University Cancer Hospital and City University of Hong Kong — producing high-profile, open-access papers in the Nature portfolio. Whether the Detangle concept advances will depend on the same test that has humbled many hair-loss breakthroughs before it: human trials. Until then, the "magic comb" is a compelling laboratory result — and a reminder that in hair-loss research, the distance between a mouse's back and a human scalp can be very long indeed.
