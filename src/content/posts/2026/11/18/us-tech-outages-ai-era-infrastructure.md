# US Tech Outages Test AI-Era Infrastructure

In the space of six days, some of the most powerful institutions in the United States suffered a remarkable run of system failures. Microsoft's productivity tools, the nation's air-traffic control network, JPMorgan Chase's online banking, Apple's cloud services and even a classified National Security Agency communications network all went dark in quick succession, according to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34501995). The cluster has sparked public alarm and forced officials to answer a pointed question: are these isolated glitches, or signs that critical digital infrastructure is buckling under the demands of the AI era?

As of Wednesday, no evidence linked the incidents, and no cyberattack had been confirmed. The dominant view among officials and experts was that the failures reflected brittle, over-stretched systems and rushed modernization rather than a coordinated attack.

## A Week of Cascading Failures

The sequence began on Nov. 12, when a software defect in a Microsoft data center crippled Outlook, Teams and Copilot. At its peak, more than 80 percent of Microsoft's network gateway servers were unresponsive, and the incident ran nearly two days. A preliminary post-mortem traced the problem to a bug introduced in July but never detected, triggered when Microsoft adjusted Teams traffic routing while some servers lacked capacity.

The next day, the Federal Aviation Administration halted all US departures for more than four hours, disrupting more than 11,000 flights. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said switches and routers in the air-traffic communications system were overloaded by data. The agency later traced the disruption to efforts to upgrade copper wire with fiber optic cable and a defective router in Arkansas that confused systems nationally — a local fault that cascaded across the country.

On Nov. 16, JPMorgan Chase customers nationwide reported failures in online banking and the Chase app, along with delayed Zelle payments. The bank confirmed that a software bug in an on-premise hardware system slowed transaction processing and said it was a technical fault, not a cyberattack.

The following day brought the most sensitive incident. The NSA's classified internal communications network went down, classified as a "code yellow" event — the second-highest level in a three-tier alert system. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/17/nsa-national-security-classified-communications-network-down) reported, the agency said it experienced a "temporary degradation" during scheduled routine maintenance, adding that mission-critical capabilities remained fully operational. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton attributed the outage to installing software patches to fix major vulnerabilities discovered the prior month.

That same day, Apple suffered a massive outage affecting the App Store, iCloud and payments. Internal services, including the employee portal, authentication system and VPN, were also hit — even the company's internal outage-tracking site went down. Apple said an internal system update caused the disruption and that it was not a cyberattack.

## Strain, Not Sabotage

As [CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/17/politics/tech-outages-conspiracy-theories-trump) analyzed, the incidents spawned a wave of online speculation, amplified by social media algorithms that feed on doom. But officials and experts pointed to a more mundane explanation.

"We are not under attack," Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said in a CNBC interview, describing the incidents as the product of aging technical infrastructure and the upgrade process. Deploying new technology on top of old computer systems, he said, can simply overload them.

Tech consultancy Qualaix chief executive Marva Bailer described legacy systems as a "Frankenstein's monster": AI firms and third-party vendors keep bolting on new components and functions, introducing ever more failure points. The timing compounds the risk — the holiday travel and shopping season raises transaction volumes and the pressure to update systems before December.

Sen. Mark Warner, vice chair of the Senate Intelligence Committee, offered another angle: AI is helping identify vulnerabilities across huge numbers of systems at unprecedented speed, forcing rapid patching that strains systems never designed for that pace of change.

"Where most people see conspiracy, I almost always encounter incompetence," CNN tech contributor Jacob Ward said. "Now when we just try to do something like a routine upgrade, we wind up skunking the whole system."

Joe Slowik, director of threat research at Dataminr, called the cluster rare and worrying. "We rely too heavily on infrastructure whose stability was already shaky, and we under-invest in redundancy," he said.

## The Verification Gap

Despite the reassuring official consensus, some lawmakers and security experts urged caution. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries demanded that the administration brief lawmakers, saying the public deserves transparency. Sen. James Lankford of Oklahoma said on Fox News that the number of incidents demanded an investigation, noting that China is known to have penetrated the US telecom system.

Former Defense Department analyst Mike Wiacek told The Guardian that early assurances may have come too quickly. "Four major outages in under a week ... look suspiciously like what I'd expect one to look like," he said. "What concerns me most is how quickly officials ruled out an attack. You can't compress months of forensic work into a few hours."

That ambiguity — the growing difficulty of telling a glitch from a cyberattack — is itself a source of public anxiety, CNN's John Miller has written. And the no-cyberattack conclusions rest largely on operators' own statements, with limited independent forensic confirmation in public reporting.

## What to Watch

President Donald Trump downplayed the events. "It's life," he told reporters. "When you're big and powerful and you have all the money, things like that happen. It's fine." Vice President JD Vance urged calm but added, "you have to be prepared for everything."

The episode feeds a broader debate over US critical-infrastructure resilience, AI's dual role in both exposing and exploiting vulnerabilities, and public preparedness. Whether any incident is later reclassified as malicious remains open. Meanwhile, the structural risks — under-investment in redundancy and over-reliance on legacy systems layered with new AI tooling — are unlikely to fade before the December holiday peak. Experts warn that is exactly when the next cascading failure could strike.
