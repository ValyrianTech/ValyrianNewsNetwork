# Belgium's Rental Crunch Drives Tenants to Falsify Payslips

Real-estate agencies across Belgium are reporting a sharp rise in prospective tenants falsifying payslips and other documents to stand a chance of securing a home, as intense competition for scarce rental properties pushes applicants to misrepresent their finances. The trend, reported by the [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/18/krapte-op-huurmarkt-doet-kandidaat-huurders-sjoemelen/) on the basis of reporting by the Mediahuis newspapers, lays bare the strain on housing access in a market where dozens of candidates now chase a single property.

Major agencies Dewaele, ERA and Trevi all confirm a clear pattern. Dewaele Vastgoedgroep says it now encounters "a falsifying candidate for virtually every rental property." Trevi Vastgoed, which has tracked suspicious or falsified applications in Brussels for years, reports that the share has doubled between 2021 and 2026 — from one in ten applications to one in five.

## A market under unprecedented pressure

The fraud is a symptom of a rental market that has tightened dramatically. According to the annual [Dewaele Huurbarometer](https://news.bereal.be/huis-huren-kost-voor-het-eerst-meer-dan-1000-euro), a market analysis based on 11,000 rental contracts concluded between 2018 and 2024, 16 percent fewer new rental contracts were signed in 2024 than the year before, even as 10 percent more candidates applied. Three years ago there were on average 12 candidates per rental property; today there are 34. In Antwerp province that figure rises to 47, and outliers of 100 applications for a single property are no longer exceptional.

"For every property that is rented out, 33 candidate tenants are left empty-handed," said Filip Dewaele, managing director of Dewaele Vastgoedgroep, in the press release accompanying the barometer. "The shortage on the rental market is historically large and demands urgent action. Otherwise we are heading for a rental crisis."

The pressure extends to prices. For the first time in history, the average rent for a house in Flanders exceeded €1,000 per month (€1,007, up 3 percent year-on-year), while apartments rose faster, up 5 percent to an average €841. With average net income in Flanders just under €2,800, affordability has become a defining problem.

## Detection is difficult — and GDPR is no help

The sector's biggest challenge is verification. Applicants are commonly — though not legally obliged — asked to present proof of identity and proof of income, in practice often a payslip. But intercepting forged documents has become far harder.

"Intercepting false payslips is difficult. They all look real thanks to AI," Kristophe Thijs of the CIB, the Belgian professional institute of estate agents, told VRT NWS. "So many still slip through the net. The only check you could do as an agent is to approach the employer, but under GDPR they may not disclose anything about this."

Red flags during fraud screening include unusual white spaces and inconsistent fonts, according to the VRT NWS report. Trevi's data shows the problem is concentrated in large cities and most acute in Brussels. "Five years ago we barely heard of it, now much more, and more in cities than in the countryside," Thijs added. Estate firms must also comply with anti-discrimination and data-protection legislation, further narrowing their room to probe applicants' claims.

## Root cause is scarcity, not technology

The sector is almost unanimous that AI is the enabler, not the driver. "Thanks to AI it is child's play to tamper with documents, but it is primarily the shortage on the rental market that drives candidates to do so," VRT NWS summarized, reflecting views echoed across the industry.

The competitive dynamics reinforce exclusion. As financially strong dual-income earners increasingly enter the rental market, singles and those with uncertain incomes struggle even more. Landlords seek the "best" tenant — reliable, pays on time, maintains the property.

"For many landlords, a good tenant is not a luxury but a necessity," Dewaele said. "Homeowners who rent out their property often have a loan on it and must themselves meet their obligations to the bank. Given the unprecedented competition among candidate tenants, some applicants present their financial situation as better than it really is. That leads to a lot of frustration, disappointment and lost time."

## Legal grey zone with real consequences

Under Flemish law, forging documents is not a trivial matter. According to Wonen in Vlaanderen, the Flemish government housing agency, the Flemish rental housing decree contains nothing specific about tenants falsifying data — but the practice still carries criminal weight.

"Such fraud can be qualified as forgery (valsheid in geschrifte), and thus a criminal offence," said spokeswoman Dorien Van Cauwenberge, as reported by VRT NWS. Verenigde Eigenaars, the association of property owners, advises landlords who detect falsification to inform the tenant.

The practical reality is an enforcement gap: tenants risk prosecution, yet agents are constrained by privacy rules from verifying income claims rigorously, and many forgeries go undetected.

## Policy pressures and what to watch

The fraud trend sits within a broader affordability debate. Flemish Minister of Housing Melissa Depraetere (Vooruit) has pledged to spend the full budget for social housing construction and announced financial penalties for municipalities that fail to build enough. But analysts argue penalties alone will not fix the shortage, given that permitting takes years.

In a [De Tijd opinion column](https://www.tijd.be/opinie/column/wooncrisis-vereist-stevige-maatregelen/10683244.html), chief economist Ivan Van de Cloot of Stichting Merito argued that solving the housing crisis requires a smoother permitting policy and a targeted improvement of the investment climate, noting that for singles a rental home can swallow 55 percent of income. "More supply through faster procedures and targeted, market-friendly measures is the only sustainable path to affordable housing," he wrote.

For tenants, the incentive to embellish is structural: when 34 candidates compete for one property, a slightly inflated payslip can look like the difference between a home and homelessness. For landlords and policymakers, the open questions are how to verify claims within GDPR limits, whether forgery detection will become standard practice, and whether supply reforms will ease the pressure that drives people toward fraud in the first place. Until then, the sector warns, the line between desperation and criminality will keep blurring.
