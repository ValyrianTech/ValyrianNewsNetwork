# Bitcoin Falls Below $70,000 as Strong Dollar Bites

Bitcoin fell below the $70,000 threshold on Friday 13 November, its first dip under that level since August, as a resurgent US dollar and persistently high inflation drove investors away from riskier assets. The world's largest cryptocurrency tumbled as much as 5.4% to $67,628, its lowest since 19 August, according to [HLN](https://www.hln.be/economie/bitcoin-zakt-voor-het-eerst-in-maanden-onder-de-70-000-dollar~a2f3da59/). Ethereum, the second-largest digital asset, also slipped below $2,000.

The move underscores how deeply macroeconomic forces now shape a market once pitched as an uncorrelated hedge against inflation and fiat currency.

## Context: A Crypto Market in Macro's Grip

Bitcoin's decline is not an isolated crypto story. It reflects a broader rotation toward safe-haven assets as the Federal Reserve signals further tightening. US inflation came in higher than expected, reinforced by elevated oil prices, and traders now expect the central bank to raise interest rates again to curb price pressures.

Higher rates make yield-bearing, low-risk instruments such as US Treasury bonds attractive. Because of that high rate, it is worthwhile to hold dollars — for example in US government bonds, which are safe investments, as HLN explained. That demand lifts the value of the greenback, which moves inversely to bitcoin.

## Key Developments: From Record High to Half Its Peak

The sell-off has been building for weeks. Bitcoin had already lost 3.8% on Thursday 12 November before the sharper Friday drop. A year earlier, in October 2025, bitcoin had reached a record of over $126,000 — meaning it is now worth just over half of that peak, [NU.nl](https://www.nu.nl/economie/6416446/bitcoin-zakt-onder-70000-dollar-hoge-amerikaanse-inflatie-schrikt-af.html) noted.

Higher borrowing costs limit the room to invest and speculate, HLN reported. Saving yields more again, and risky investments become less attractive — a classic flight to quality that drains liquidity from speculative markets like crypto.

Two political shocks have compounded the downdraft. After Democratic gains in the US midterm elections, markets expect stricter oversight of the crypto sector, adding a policy-risk premium. Meanwhile, the surprise resignation of Nvidia's long-serving chief executive Jensen Huang on 12 November rattled technology stocks. [Nvidia](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-CEO-Transition/default.aspx) confirmed that Huang stepped down as president, CEO and board member effective immediately, with CFO Colette Kress appointed interim CEO. Given crypto's tight ties to tech and AI-driven market sentiment, the shock rippled across digital assets.

## Analysis: Why the Dollar and Bitcoin Move in Opposite Directions

The episode illustrates a structural shift in how bitcoin trades. Once marketed as "digital gold" and an uncorrelated store of value, it now behaves increasingly like a high-beta risk asset — falling when the dollar strengthens and when monetary policy tightens.

Three reinforcing dynamics are at play. First, monetary policy: a hawkish Fed lifts the dollar index and mechanically depresses dollar-denominated assets like bitcoin. Second, a risk-off rotation: safer fixed-income returns lure capital away from speculation. Third, regulatory risk: the prospect of tougher US crypto oversight adds an extra discount to prices.

The stakes extend beyond crypto enthusiasts. As US politics turns on economic pain — the familiar "it's the economy, stupid" framing — financial markets of all stripes absorb the consequences. For Belgian and European investors with exposure to digital assets, the message is that crypto is no longer a world apart from the macro cycle.

## What's Next

The immediate question is whether the Fed follows through with another rate increase, and how aggressively. A further hike would likely keep pressure on bitcoin and Ethereum, while any signal of a pause could offer relief. Investors will also watch how the new Nvidia leadership steadies the tech sector, given its outsized influence on broader market sentiment. For now, the crypto market remains firmly in the grip of the dollar — a relationship that its earliest advocates never anticipated.
