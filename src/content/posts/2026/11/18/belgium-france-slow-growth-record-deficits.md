# Belgium and France: Slow Growth, Record Deficits

The European Commission's autumn economic forecast has placed Belgium and France in an uncomfortable double bind: both are projected to post some of the eurozone's weakest growth while simultaneously ranking among the bloc's largest public-deficit offenders. Presented on Tuesday by Economy Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis, the forecast prompted a direct public call on Paris and Brussels to bring their public finances back onto a downward debt trajectory.

Belgium is forecast to grow just 0.7% in 2026 and 0.8% in 2027, while France is expected to expand by only 0.4% and 0.6% over the same years — against a eurozone average of 1.2% in both years, according to [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/783313/article/2026-11-17/conjoncture-la-belgique-et-la-france-cancres-de-la-croissance-championnes-des). The French figures represent a sharp downgrade from the Commission's spring forecast of 0.8% and 1.1%. Italy, at 0.7% in 2027, slots between the two laggards.

## A Shared Fiscal Problem

The growth shortfall is compounded by a fiscal one. Both countries carry general government deficits above 5% of GDP and face widening spreads on their sovereign borrowing. According to [Boursorama, citing AFP](https://www.boursorama.com/actualite-economique/actualites/deficit-la-commission-europeenne-appelle-paris-et-bruxelles-a-redresser-leurs-finances-publiques-a89ca8885adf54c20d9efb5202fea61d), the Commission projects French deficits of 5.5% in 2026 and 5.4% in 2027, and Belgian deficits of 5.2% and 5.5% over the same period. On unchanged policy, the two countries would share the largest deficit in the eurozone; only Poland fares worse across the wider EU, at -7.2%.

Public debt tells a similar story. Belgium's debt is projected to climb from 111.2% of GDP in 2026 to 113.4% in 2027 and 115.8% in 2028, keeping it the fourth most indebted EU member. France's debt is expected to exceed 123% of GDP by 2028. Of the four EU states above the 100% threshold — Italy, Greece, France and Belgium — only Greece is on a declining path, [7sur7 reported](https://www.7sur7.be/economie/deficit-record-dette-en-hausse-lue-met-la-pression-sur-la-belgique~a9e4aba2/). The eurozone average stands at 91.3%.

## Dombrovskis Turns Up the Heat

Dombrovskis used the forecast's presentation to warn explicitly about the widening gap between French and Belgian borrowing rates and the German benchmark. "On the budgetary situation in France and Belgium, we have seen a sharp rise in the spreads between borrowing rates… particularly in France's case," he said. "It is essential to ensure that the deficit and debt are on a downward trajectory."

The Commissioner balanced the warning with reassurance, noting that demand for French and Belgian sovereign bonds remains sufficient and that the Commission sees "no imminent risk to financial stability." For Belgium specifically, he stressed the urgency of a federal budget deal: "It is very important that the budget be adopted and that the deficit be put on a downward trajectory."

## Belgium's Budget Impasse

The forecast lands at an awkward moment for Brussels. Belgium's federal government has yet to agree on the budgetary effort required through 2029 to remain on the fiscal path negotiated with the EU, and the country missed the 15 October deadline to submit its budget plan to the Commission. As a result, the 2027 projections include only measures already announced by the federated entities — the regions and communities — rather than any new federal consolidation.

A temporary reprieve exists, but it comes with an expiry date. The Commission's flexibility allowing member states to raise defence spending should keep Belgium within its agreed trajectory — but only until the end of 2028. From 2029, the derogation clause lapses and Belgium must find a way to close the budget gap, according to a source at the Berlaymont, the Commission's headquarters.

## An Interest Burden That Crowds Out Room to Manoeuvre

The widening deficit is being driven in part by surging debt-service costs and military spending. Belgium will devote 2.9% of GDP to interest payments in 2027 and 3.1% in 2028. Interest payments alone will equal half of Belgium's 2027 deficit — a self-reinforcing dynamic in which debt service crowds out productive investment.

The picture is somewhat brighter on Belgium's labour market, which the Commission describes as more resilient than its public finances. Employment is set to grow 0.8% over two years, against 0.4% in the eurozone, while unemployment eases to 6.3% from 6.6%. Growth is projected to rebound to 1.2% in 2028, and inflation to fall to 2.5% from 3.6% — though still above the eurozone average of 2.1%.

## The French Political Fallout

The downgrade is already reverberating in Paris. French Economy Minister Roland Lescure acknowledged the following day that the government's own 1% growth forecast for 2027 is "at risk" — "rather to the downside" — and that additional effort beyond the 43 billion euros of savings already planned may be needed to hold the deficit at 5% of GDP, a level he described as "not negotiable," [AFP reported](https://www.boursorama.com/actualite-economique/actualites/la-prevision-de-croissance-de-1-pour-2027-est-a-risque-reconnait-lescure-1413adb06621627fe38f39df0cd61db2).

## What to Watch

The Commission is expected next week to detail country-specific budget recommendations and to review the Excessive Deficit Procedures under which both Belgium and France already operate, as part of the autumn European Semester cycle. Both countries therefore face formal oversight — and the prospect of sanctions — if they fail to correct their deficits.

The broader lesson is one of a two-speed eurozone. Southern economies, notably Greece, have corrected their public finances, while core members Belgium and France now lag on both growth and fiscal discipline. Whether the two governments can convert the Commission's warnings into credible budget plans — Belgium's still unwritten, France's under pressure before it is even adopted — will determine how much of a premium investors demand to hold their debt.
