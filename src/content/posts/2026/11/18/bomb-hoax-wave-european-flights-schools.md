# Bomb Hoax Wave Hits European Flights and Schools

A coordinated wave of false bomb threats swept across Europe in November, disrupting dozens of passenger flights, shutting down media outlets and forcing the evacuation of hundreds of schools. Among the affected flights was a LOT aircraft travelling from Warsaw to Brussels, which landed safely at Brussels Airport (Zaventem) after a threat was received, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/). No explosives were found in any of the incidents, and no one was harmed — but the scale of the disruption has rattled security services and revived debate about who is targeting Europe and why.

## A week of disruption

The aviation wave peaked on Thursday 5 November, when at least 27 passenger flights across more than a dozen countries were grounded, diverted or escorted by fighter jets. Belgium's federal police carried out a risk analysis on the Warsaw–Brussels jet and concluded no additional measures were needed, according to the airport press service. A Ryanair flight between Pescara, Italy, and Charleroi was also hit after the word "bom" ("bomb") was found written in one of the aircraft's toilets; passengers were disembarked and screened with the help of explosive-detection dogs before the alarm was lifted.

Elsewhere the response was more dramatic. An Aer Lingus Airbus A320 travelling from Dublin to Rome was diverted to a French military airbase at Évreux, west of Paris, where French fighter jets intercepted and escorted the aircraft before its 157 occupants landed safely. Finnair and Aegean flights were escorted into Milan and Rome, a Wizz Air jet from Milan to Warsaw diverted to Vienna, and Hungary scrambled fighter jets three times, including for a Brussels-to-Sofia service. According to an [Al Jazeera explainer](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/6/hoax-or-bomb-threats-what-we-know-about-flight-disruptions-across-europe), flights departing from 16 countries were affected, and at least 17 more were disrupted by midday on Friday 6 November.

The aviation wave bled into civilian targets. On 6 November, buildings of several Irish media outlets were evacuated after what were described as "very serious threats", including public broadcaster RTÉ, which switched to pre-recorded programming. Sky News in the UK and France's TF1 also received threats.

## Then the schools

Days later, on 11 November, the campaign shifted to education. Roughly 6,300 of Italy's 7,476 state schools received a single identical bomb-threat email, prompting Italy's government to convene an emergency meeting and Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni to consult the intelligence services. Hundreds of schools were initially evacuated. As [BBC News reported](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwz0k79pr8z2o), more than 700 schools in Scotland and Northern Ireland also received threatening emails with similar wording.

Meloni said checks so far had shown no evidence of "concrete danger" and that the reports appeared "rather linked to a strategy intended to cause alarm and concern." Italy's Education Minister Giuseppe Valditara said the incidents were probably "a cyber-attack, not a genuine risk." The email was signed in the name of a purported "Mikhail Andropov, leader of Vilayat of Misoginistan", an almost certainly fictitious persona — the same Russian name used days earlier in threats sent to European media outlets, [VRT NWS noted](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/11/bommeldingen-scholen-europa/). One Milan headteacher told Italian media the message "seemed almost comical", and that the sender's email address had already been flagged as spam.

## Who is behind it?

No group has credibly claimed responsibility, and no country has been officially blamed. The European Commissioner for Transport said it is "too early to link the incidents together." Russia has "emphatically denied" involvement, and there is no public evidence tying the threats to Russia, Iran or any proxy group.

That has not stopped speculation. Aviation-security experts and Russia analysts have publicly argued the pattern fits a suspected Russian "hybrid" or "shadow war" campaign, pointing to a broader wave of disruption in 2026 — cyberattacks on supermarket refrigeration systems, drone incursions at airports, and threats against blood banks and railways. Russia expert Jan Balliauw of the Egmont Institute told VRT that calling it hybrid war is "too cautious", describing it as a "shadow war." But Ghent University's Prof. Tim Haesebrouck cautioned on VRT that "there is currently no proof that it is Moscow. It could just as well be environmental activists, or a group linked to Iran."

Italy's Defence Minister Guido Crosetto struck a similar note, warning against assuming state involvement: "It is not always the work of other countries or hostile entities. There are also attention-seekers and madmen." According to BBC News, a misogynistic extremist group briefly linked to the threats later denied any involvement.

## Analysis: a cheap, disruptive tactic

What unites the incidents is not a confirmed perpetrator but a method: a single email or phone call, sent to many targets at once, that forces an expensive and visible response. Philip Baum, a visiting professor of aviation security at Coventry University, told Al Jazeera that each flight disruption can cost an airline up to $250,000. False threats are illegal, but the cost of sending one is close to zero — an asymmetry that makes them attractive to anyone seeking to sow alarm or test Europe's defences.

The pattern matters for Belgium in particular. The country's airports and one of its main carriers appeared in both phases of the scare, and its federal police were drawn into the initial risk assessment. Eric Schouten, CEO of aviation-security firm Dyami, told VRT that Europe should not panic but must accept that "this will for now be part of Europe" and become more resilient — describing the incidents as "temporarily the new normal."

## What to watch

The central question remains unresolved: whether a single actor, a state or a loose network is behind the waves, and whether the flight and school campaigns are connected. Authorities across Europe are investigating the source of the messages, with Scotland's cyber-crime unit liaising with international partners. Two risks now stand out — the cost to airlines and public confidence if the threats continue, and the danger that a false alarm somewhere along the chain will one day coincide with a real one, testing the credibility of the very systems designed to keep passengers safe.
