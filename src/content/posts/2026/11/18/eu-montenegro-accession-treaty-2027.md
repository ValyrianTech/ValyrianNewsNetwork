# EU Eyes Montenegro Accession Treaty by Next Summer

The European Union is targeting an accession treaty with Montenegro by June 2027 and full membership by 2028, according to Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos, who told Flemish public broadcaster [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/europese-unie-wil-in-juni-volgend-jaar-toetredingsverdrag-ondert/) that the Balkan country is "on its way to becoming the next new member of the EU." If realised, Montenegro would become the bloc's first new member since Croatia joined in 2013.

The timeline sits alongside the European Commission's annual enlargement policy report, which casts enlargement as a renewed strategic priority. Montenegro, Albania, Moldova and Ukraine are named as frontrunners, while Serbia faces both a difficult progress report and a fresh opening after a landmark opposition election win in October.

## Montenegro: The Frontrunner

The Commission expects to conclude accession negotiations with Montenegro by the end of 2026. "The accession treaty is already being prepared," Kos said. Under the plan, the treaty would be signed in June 2027, then ratified by all 27 member-state parliaments over roughly a year, paving the way for membership in 2028, [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/eu-says-montenegro-on-track-to-become-blocs-next-member-state-by-2028/a-79855011) reported.

"This is testament to Montenegro's sustained efforts to advance on the European path and implement the reforms needed to turn EU membership into reality," the Commission said in its report. Montenegro still must implement reforms, particularly in fundamental rights and financial services.

Kos downplayed the country's small size, noting it has roughly the population of Antwerp - about 630,000 people. "I am sure we will bring the population on board," she said.

One lingering risk is Croatia, which has been slowing Montenegro's accession over bilateral disputes stemming from the 1990s Yugoslav wars. According to a document sent to EU governments and [seen by Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/14/croatias-wartime-grievances-threaten-montenegros-hopes-of-joining-eu-by-2028), Zagreb has set out eight demands, including compensation for around 300 Croatian nationals detained at the Morinj prison camp between October 1991 and August 1992. Croatia is blocking progress in four areas - transport, foreign relations, fundamental rights and home affairs - and each member state holds a veto over accession chapters, giving Zagreb significant leverage. For now, however, the Commission appears largely unconcerned.

## The Other Frontrunners

Beyond Montenegro, Kos identified three more frontrunners. Albania is roughly a year behind and aims to close all negotiating chapters by the end of 2027. "In the past year Albania really jumped into action with all kinds of reforms," Kos said, though she noted the open question of how street protests against the government will affect its progress.

Moldova, a candidate only since 2022, has already opened 25 of its 33 negotiating chapters this year. Its path remains complicated by Russia's reaction and a divided domestic electorate split between pro-Russian and pro-EU camps.

Ukraine presents the most complex case. Fighting a war of survival, it is nonetheless held to the same standards as other candidates - a bar the Commission acknowledged is "not easy" for Kyiv to meet given that the war complicates the functioning of its parliament. Kos hopes all negotiating chapters with Ukraine will be opened by the end of 2026, and she believes membership within five years is possible.

"I believe that should be possible if we help Ukraine," she said. "Although that will also depend on how long the war lasts."

## Serbia's Fresh Start

Serbia was once the second-most advanced candidate after Montenegro, but regressed under the government of Aleksandar Vučić. The Commission's progress report is strict on Belgrade. In late October 2026, however, the student-led "Students' List" defeated Vučić at parliamentary elections, a result DW dated to 25 October, ending 14 years of his dominance.

Kos said she has already received the new leadership in Brussels. "Now Serbia has the chance for a fresh start," she told EU lawmakers. "If they are serious and really want to carry out major reforms, then things could move very quickly with Serbia."

## Analysis: Enlargement as Geopolitics

The renewed push marks a shift from more than a decade of "enlargement fatigue" that followed Croatia's 2013 entry. Today, enlargement is increasingly framed as a security and stability tool - a way to counter Russian and other influence across the Western Balkans and the Eastern neighbourhood. That logic explains the emphasis on bringing candidate countries "on board" rather than holding them at arm's length.

Yet the path is uneven. Ukraine's timeline hinges on a war with no clear end; Moldova faces Russian pressure and internal division; Albania's progress could be disrupted by domestic unrest; and Montenegro's timetable could still be slowed by Croatia's veto. The bloc has also floated "safeguards and special terms" for new members, raising fears among some candidates of a second-class membership.

"The significant progress achieved by some candidates over the last year allows us to turn the promise of enlargement into reality," European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said. "Step by step, we are building a more complete, stronger Union."

## What to Watch

Kos framed the moment in historic terms, comparing it to the 2004 "big bang" when ten countries joined at once. "I have the feeling that more and more European leaders realise it is important to bring all those countries on board, if we want to protect ourselves in these geopolitical times," she said.

The key milestones ahead are the conclusion of Montenegro's negotiations by the end of 2026, the signing of its accession treaty in June 2027, and the opening of all chapters with Ukraine. Whether the EU can convert momentum into membership - and manage the vetoes, wars and internal politics along the way - will define the bloc's next chapter.
