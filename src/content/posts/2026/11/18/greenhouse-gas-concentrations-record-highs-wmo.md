# Greenhouse Gas Concentrations Hit Unprecedented Record Highs

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases all reached new records in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) has reported, warning that the planet is now locked into decades of additional warming. According to the UN agency's [Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high), carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide each climbed to unprecedented highs — a finding the WMO released deliberately ahead of the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye, which enters its final week.

## A New Year, Another Record

The globally averaged surface concentration of CO2 reached **426.8 parts per million (ppm)** in 2025, or 153% of the pre-industrial baseline of 1750. Methane hit **1,948 parts per billion (ppb)**, or 267% of the 1750 level, while nitrous oxide reached **339.1 ppb**, 126% above pre-industrial times.

"You could say: another year, another record. Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett, as [reported by Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779336/article/2026-10-27/une-nouvelle-annee-un-nouveau-record-la-concentration-gaz-effet-de-serre-atteint). "The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue. CO2 has a very long lifetime and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries."

The bulletin measures *concentrations* — the amount of gas that accumulates in the atmosphere — rather than annual emissions. This distinction matters: about half of the CO2 emitted each year remains in the atmosphere, while the rest is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans. Even if emissions fell sharply, concentrations would keep rising for years to come.

## Growth Rate and the El Niño Factor

CO2 growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, accelerating from roughly 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year during the 2011–2020 decade. From 2024 to 2025, CO2 rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024.

According to WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova, that more moderate rise reflects the recovery of land carbon sinks after the strong 2023/2024 El Niño. But she cautioned that a 2.8 ppm annual increase "is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," and that a new powerful El Niño developing in 2026 is likely to push concentrations even higher.

## The Less Visible Gases

The bulletin also turns a spotlight on two potent but less abundant trace gases tied to the energy transition. Sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), used as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution, has an atmospheric lifetime of over 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO2 — and its increase in 2025 was a record high. Molecular hydrogen, increasingly used as a clean-energy carrier, is not a greenhouse gas but influences atmospheric chemistry and rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024.

Read together, the WMO warns, these gases show how the shift to clean energy could, if unmanaged, "undermine the climate benefits of the energy transition."

## Why It Matters Now

The timing is pointed. The WMO released the bulletin to inform negotiators at COP31, held from 9 to 20 November 2026 and framed around turning climate commitments into concrete action. By the date of this report, the summit is in its closing days — making the record concentrations the scientific backdrop to any final text.

For Belgium and its EU partners, the bulletin reinforces the stakes of domestic climate policy. With the last 11 years the warmest on record and the trend set to continue, the gap between the stated ambition of the Paris Agreement and the physical reality of the atmosphere has rarely been starker.

## What to Watch

The question now is not whether concentrations will rise again in 2026 — the WMO expects they will, driven by fossil fuel burning and a resurgent El Niño — but by how much, and whether COP31 can translate the science into durable commitments. On current trajectories, the next bulletin may once again open with the same four words: another year, another record.
