# Meta's 'Project Gryffindor' Sought More Kids on Instagram

Internal Meta documents show that an Instagram program publicly presented as a school-safety initiative was conceived and pitched inside the company as a way to grow its teenage user base — an effort codenamed "Project Gryffindor," after a *Harry Potter* school house. The previously unreported documents, obtained through discovery in a sprawling child-safety lawsuit, were detailed by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/18/nx-s1-6037808/meta-instagram-schools-teens-safety) on Wednesday and raise fresh questions about the social media giant's push to recruit its youngest users.

## A safety program with a commercial blueprint

Meta launched the initiative in 2025 under the public-facing name "School Partnership Program," then [expanded it significantly](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/expanding-instagram-school-partnership-program-help-teens-stay-informed/) in September 2026, saying nearly 17,000 schools had signed up. The program encourages teens to display a school banner on their profiles and establishes a student directory that lets users find and connect with classmates. School-linked accounts can also post Stories and notes visible to verified students and parents within a school community.

Meta frames the initiative around safety. The company says the program protects students from bullying, offers educators a direct channel to report concerns, and helps families stay informed about school activities. In a statement to NPR, Meta spokesperson Liza Crenshaw called the reporting a mischaracterization of "a positive program that helps thousands of U.S. schools protect their students online," adding that the company reviewed more than 90% of bullying and harassment reports from participating schools within 48 hours over the past year.

The internal record tells a more commercial story. In a 2024 [Project Gryffindor presentation](https://www.documentcloud.org/documents/28920133-meta-project-gryffindor/), the company described embedding in schools as a route to increase user "acquisition," "friending," and "invites." "We believe network connections will fuel organic and unattributed acquisition," the deck states, referring to the addition of new users.

An earlier 2023 internal presentation, titled "Unlocking New Networks Through School Communities," put the goal plainly: the school program would "give teens a new reason to join / find more value in IG," or Instagram.

## Years in the making

The documents suggest the strategy dates back far longer than the public program. As early as 2015, internal records show Meta was planning relationships with schools to acquire teen users. A 2023 company presentation asked whether Meta could "use school networks as a lever for acquisitions," noting that competitors were already leveraging school integrations.

Meta has viewed the struggle to attract teens as a strategic danger. According to other internal documents submitted as evidence, the slowdown in teen Instagram growth after TikTok launched in the U.S. in 2018 amounted to "an existential threat for not just IG but the company overall."

The internal ambitions extended beyond teens themselves. One document observed that "teens strongly influenced preteens' understanding of what and how frequently to share on Instagram." Both Instagram and Facebook require users to be at least 13. A separate internal Facebook presentation on retention underscored the value of younger users: "Tweens (approximate age 10-12) are special. People who join Facebook as tweens have the highest long term retention out of all age groups."

A 2025 [legal brief filed on behalf of school districts](https://www.documentcloud.org/documents/28920132-meta-plaintiffs-brief/) cited a 2015 internal comment that captured the engagement mindset: "One of the things we need to optimize for is sneaking a look at your phone under your desk in the middle of Chemistry." The same brief described how Meta targeted push notifications — "school blasts" — to students at specific schools to "incrementally increase teen MAP [monthly active persons]," and how the company built the capability to determine when teen users were at school using location and school-specific data.

The gap between that internal positioning and Meta's public messaging is stark. In its September 2026 announcement, the company said: "We don't want social media to be a distraction from school."

## Safety experts warn of a predator loophole

Online safety experts told NPR that the program's design may make teens more vulnerable rather than safer. Paul Raffile, a cybersecurity investigator, said school-affiliated profiles could create "a loophole that scammers, sextortion scammers, as well as groomers, would obviously exploit."

Raffile explained that identifying a victim's school is "actually written into the script that the scammers use," and that blackmailers later threaten to send compromising images to everyone in the school, including teachers and coaches. A compromised account with an active school profile, he said, can expose directories of clubs and campus groups — "a sextortionist's dream."

John DeMay, whose 17-year-old son Jordan died by suicide in 2022 after a sextortion blackmail scam on Instagram, said the program runs counter to years of digital-safety education. "This goes against all the internet safety training that parents and teachers have been trying to instill in their kids all these years," he said, warning that social platforms lack the security to keep bad actors out. Meta did not respond to NPR's questions about sextortion and cybersecurity concerns.

Sacha Haworth, executive director of the Tech Oversight Project, was more pointed. "Meta's latest attempt to trojan-horse their tech into the school day shows the business strategy remains the same: maximize their profits at our kids' expense," she said.

## Partnership claim disputed

Meta's September 2026 release said the program was developed "in partnership" with the International Society for Technology in Education and the Association for Supervision and Curriculum Development. ISTE+ASCD disputed that framing, saying it did contract work to build a process for educators to report inappropriate posts — work that concluded in 2025. Crenshaw responded that Meta was "proud to work with ISTE on the initial development" of the program but that the organization has not been part of the work since.

## A legal backdrop of defeats

The disclosures land at a fraught moment for Meta. In August 2026, the company [agreed to a settlement worth up to $17.1 billion](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/26/nx-s1-5944781/meta-settlement-child-safety-lawsuit) with dozens of state attorneys general over claims that Facebook and Instagram were designed to encourage compulsive use among young people and that the company misled the public about the risks; Meta denied the allegations. Months earlier, a [Los Angeles jury found](https://www.npr.org/2026/03/25/nx-s1-5746125/meta-youtube-social-media-trial-verdict) that YouTube and Meta designed their platforms to be addictive to young users, and Meta lost a separate two-phase case brought by the New Mexico attorney general, a verdict it is appealing.

The documents at the center of the NPR story emerged from discovery in the broader multidistrict litigation against Meta, TikTok, Google, and Snap, brought by children, families, school districts, and state attorneys general.

## What to watch

The revelations arrive as regulators and lawmakers intensify scrutiny of how social platforms court minors. Two questions now loom: whether the school-banner and directory design deliberately exposes affiliations that sextortion scammers rely on, and whether the disclosures prompt fresh inquiries from state attorneys general or Congress. For parents and districts already enrolled in the program, the more immediate concern may be simpler — learning that a safety initiative was, in the company's own internal words, a recruitment channel all along.
