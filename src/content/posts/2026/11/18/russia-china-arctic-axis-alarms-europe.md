# Russia and China's Arctic Axis Alarms Europe

Moscow and Beijing are building a "Polar Silk Road" across the Far North, pairing Russian ports and shipping lanes with Chinese capital, demand and technology. The deepening partnership is alarming Western capitals—and pushing the European Union to reorient its Arctic strategy away from climate policy and toward security, according to an [analysis by RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719) produced for its "Vu d'Europe" series.

The logic is driven by a changing climate and a changing world. Melting ice is opening shipping routes that were long unnavigable, while the Arctic seabed sharpens resource appetites. The US Geological Survey estimates the region holds nearly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and 13 percent of its undiscovered oil.

## A Corridor Takes Shape

The recent visit to China by Nikolai Patrushev—an aide to Vladimir Putin and chairman of Russia's Maritime Board—illustrated the shared ambition to deepen maritime cooperation in the High North. According to the Asia Times, cited by RTBF, talks focused on linking China's "Maritime Silk Road" with Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor, developing Russian ports, exchanging container-terminal automation technology and conducting joint maritime research.

Cooperation has expanded considerably over the past decade. In 2023 the two countries signed a key Arctic cooperation agreement. Chinese actors now co-finance major extraction projects such as Yamal LNG and are significantly expanding their own fleets of nuclear icebreakers. In its 2018 strategy, Beijing already presented itself as a "near-Arctic state" with legitimate interests in shipping, resource exploitation, scientific research and regional governance.

"China aspires to world superpower status. One of its attributes is domination, or at least presence, in geopolitically important areas of the world, and the Arctic is unquestionably one of them," said Vlastislav Bříza, a security expert at Charles University in Prague.

## The Northern Sea Route

The Northern Sea Route dramatically shortens distances between Europe and East Asia. The journey from Shanghai to Hamburg takes about 18 days, compared with 30 to 35 days on traditional routes via the Suez Canal.

The route's appeal is growing as other corridors are disrupted. With 80 percent of world goods still moving by sea, tanker freight rates have hit record levels because of the conflicts in Iran and Ukraine, pushing Asian refiners to seek alternatives to costlier US oil. Despite the high costs of essential icebreaker assistance, the Northern Sea Route is now cheaper than routes through the Red Sea. For Moscow, it also offers a safe way to export its "black gold" without the difficulties Western restrictions impose in European waters.

In 2026, Russian oil exports via the Northern Sea Route rose by nearly 50 percent year-on-year: roughly 20 million barrels were delivered to Asia between July and mid-October, Modern Diplomacy reported, citing Reuters. Gazprom Neft plans to increase its deliveries by about 35 percent this year. Putin claims freight volume could double by the end of the decade to 70 million tonnes a year, boosted by Rosneft's Vostok Oil megaproject.

Asia Times cautions, however, that flows through the Suez Canal remain incomparably larger, and that Arctic traffic is hampered by ice, high insurance premiums, sparse infrastructure and a short navigation season. Even so, the route's geopolitical and geo-economic importance remains paramount.

## A First Regular Container Service

In August, Chinese carrier Sea Legend launched the first regular weekly container service across the Arctic, realizing Beijing's long-sought Polar Silk Road ambition, the South China Morning Post reported. South Korea and India—whose supply chains have also been strained by conflict—are showing interest in the icy shortcut to Europe.

"For Moscow, Chinese capital, shipping demand and technology could help transform an ambitious Arctic strategy into a functional logistics system. The geopolitical consequences will undoubtedly be closely watched in Washington," Asia Times wrote.

## A Militarized North

As the ice retreats, the way into the Arctic opens not only to commercial vessels but also to military submarines from northern naval bases. Russia controls roughly 53 percent of the Arctic coastline, and its Northern Fleet operates in the region, which also hosts significant nuclear installations. Under Putin, Moscow has reactivated Soviet-era bases and deployed advanced coastal defense systems such as the Bastion, Arctic-adapted S-400 air defense and fighters armed with hypersonic missiles, according to the Polish outlet Defence24.

"Russia is expanding its military presence in the North. Moscow has reopened Soviet bases and is testing new weapons in the region," warned EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas. "Drone incursions, GPS jamming and the sabotage of undersea cables undermine the security of the Arctic."

Western intelligence and military analysts warn that Beijing is preparing to follow suit by strengthening its capacity to deploy its own nuclear submarines in the region.

## Europe's Security-First Pivot

In October, the European Commission presented its own Arctic strategy with four priorities: strengthening security and resilience, combating climate change, and prosperity for Arctic inhabitants and communities. According to Le Monde, the EU was forced to reorient its Arctic priorities from climate to security because of rising regional tensions.

"The Arctic is one of the places where the tectonic plates of geopolitics collide," said European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. "When we see that sovereignty, territorial integrity and the rules that underpin the stability of the region can no longer be taken for granted, we must act and change our strategic posture."

Jozef Síkela, the EU commissioner for international partnerships who prepared the strategy, stressed that the Arctic is warming up to four times faster than the global average and has become "the object of genuine geopolitical rivalry."

The plan calls for strengthening Europe's icebreaker fleet—the continent currently has only about ten such vessels—and for intensifying surveillance of potential sabotage and the movements of Russia's "shadow fleet." A new Centre for Maritime Security would extend shadow-fleet monitoring from the Mediterranean and Baltic to the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean, though Kyiv Independent noted that an EU official said it will not be operational immediately.

Other measures appear aimed at reducing dependence on Washington. Brussels foresees a "sovereign and secure" undersea cable between Greenland and the European continent, part of a broader "pan-Arctic" cable linking Canada, Japan and South Korea, and aims to guarantee satellite connectivity via the IRIS2 constellation, designed to compete with Elon Musk's Starlink.

## The Icebreaker Gap

NATO faces a hard capability problem. Finland and Sweden's membership has shifted the Arctic Council balance—seven of its eight members are now in NATO—but the Alliance's deterrence remains insufficient, Defence24 argued.

"NATO's problem is not geography but the lack of specialized operational platforms and adequate means to physically express its power in these waters. The United States suffers a serious shortage of operational icebreakers: only two aging vessels, whereas Russia has more than forty, including powerful Arktika-class nuclear icebreakers," the outlet wrote.

NATO is reinforcing its presence through the Arctic Sentry framework initiative, launched in February 2026, which unifies maritime and air patrols, intelligence-sharing and military exercises. Sweden and Germany are among the countries participating, deploying fighter jets.

## What to Watch

The central question is whether the Sino-Russian Arctic partnership can move from ambition to execution—Asia Times notes that many proposals remain largely unimplemented plans. Europe's ability to close the icebreaker gap, the operational launch of its maritime security center and Washington's response to the deepening axis will shape the balance of power in the High North for years to come.
