# How MR Won and Vooruit Holds Out on Belgium's Budget

Belgium's federal budget negotiations have been deadlocked for weeks, and a VRT NWS [timeline](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/17/begroting-tijdlijn-de-wever-koning-bouchez-vooruit/) published on 17 November lays out exactly how that happened: the francophone liberal MR secured its key demands, while the Flemish socialist party Vooruit has become the only coalition partner still refusing to sign off. The standoff has pushed Prime Minister Bart De Wever to offer his resignation to King Filip, who has so far declined to accept it.

## A coalition under strain

The federal government — the so-called "Arizona" coalition — brings together five parties across Belgium's linguistic divide: De Wever's N-VA, the socialists of Vooruit, the Christian democrats of CD&V, the francophone liberals of MR under Georges-Louis Bouchez, and the francophone centrists of Les Engagés. Their task has been to find roughly €10 billion in savings to bring the federal budget in order. By mid-November, about €9 billion had been identified — and the final billion proved the hardest to find.

## The turning point: VAT reform dropped

According to VRT NWS, the decisive shift came halfway through October, when De Wever removed a proposed VAT reform from the table. That reform had been the core of MR's objections, and shelving it tilted the balance decisively toward the francophone liberals. From that moment, Vooruit found itself increasingly isolated, left as the only party that found the budget plans "indigestible."

## The night the talks collapsed

On the night of Sunday 2 November, the talks failed outright. De Wever concluded that no deal was possible and announced he would offer his resignation to King Filip. Political journalist Bart Verhulst described the development as a "dramatic turn" and, in [VRT NWS's account of the collapse](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/02/geen-begrotingsakkoord-politieke-crisis-overzicht/), said: "If you can't call this a political crisis now, I don't know what is."

The underlying fault line was familiar: Vooruit, backed on some points by Les Engagés and CD&V, wanted more "effort from the strongest shoulders" — higher contributions from large wealth and high incomes — and resisted cuts to benefits, healthcare and pensions. N-VA and MR pushed back against higher taxes.

## The King's careful hand

King Filip chose not to accept the resignation. On Monday 2 November he held it "in advisement" and began consultations with the coalition party chairmen, keeping the pressure on negotiators without dissolving parliament. On Friday 6 November the King formally refused the resignation and sent De Wever back to the table, urging him to avoid a "harmful political and budgetary crisis."

Political scientist Carl Devos of Ghent University explained the logic behind that decision. "That would mean game over," he said of accepting the resignation, adding that "nobody in this country believes new elections would offer a solution." The King then granted De Wever a further week on 16 November, with the Prime Minister due to report back on 23 November.

## Vooruit's escalating pushback

The isolation has sharpened Vooruit's rhetoric. On 16 November, Vice-Premier Frank Vandenbroucke gave combative television interviews attacking De Wever and particularly Foreign Minister Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés. He also suggested that an "index jump" — a one-off freeze in welfare indexation — was on the table.

That claim drew a sharp rebuttal from Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem of CD&V, who said Vandenbroucke should not "create unnecessary unrest with incorrect information," insisting that no index jump was actually under discussion. Vooruit chair Conner Rousseau defended his party's stance: "Taking responsibility also means protecting people from something that is unfair."

## The fiscal stakes

The impasse carries real economic costs. The European Commission's [Autumn 2026 forecast](https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/economic-forecast-and-surveys/economic-forecasts/autumn-2026-economic-forecast-shows-continued-growth-amid-higher-inflation_en), published on 17 November, underscored how important it is for Belgium to adopt a budget. Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis stressed that Europe "must safeguard our public finances" even as its economy holds up better than expected. Belgium already carries one of the eurozone's highest debt and deficit burdens.

De Wever is determined to avoid a budget built on "provisional twelfths" — the mechanism that lets government keep spending at prior-year levels when no budget has been approved. It is an outcome he has called unacceptable, and one that political observers increasingly see as his fallback risk.

## What to watch

A year ago the same coalition faced a nearly identical impasse, eventually reaching a €9.2 billion deal on 24 November 2025 after De Wever asked the King for a 50-day extension. The question now is whether history repeats itself — or whether the resignation offer, this time stated explicitly, signals something worse. De Wever reports back to the King on 23 November, and the opposition is already moving: Vlaams Belang has filed a motion of no confidence, PVDA has called for a protest, and Groen has criticized the government's handling of the talks.

For now, the arithmetic is unchanged: MR has what it wants, Vooruit still refuses, and Belgium's budget hangs on whether the last billion can be found before the deadline runs out.
