# ICE 'Made a Mockery of Court Orders,' Federal Judge Rules

A federal judge held U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials in contempt of court on Wednesday, ruling that the agency repeatedly violated judicial orders by re-detaining immigrants in Colorado just moments after courts had ordered them released.

U.S. District Court Judge Charlotte Sweeney issued the 15-page ruling on Nov. 18, accusing ICE of adopting a new "policy" of immediately re-detaining petitioners following judge-ordered release — conduct she wrote "made a mockery of court orders mandating petitioners' immediate release," according to [CPR News](https://www.cpr.org/2026/11/18/ice-immigration-agents-contempt-of-court/).

The ruling caps weeks of escalating tension between Colorado's federal bench and the immigration agency, and it amounts to a formal warning that stiffer penalties could follow if the practice resumes.

## The case at the center

The ruling stemmed from the detention of Ramon Quintero Alvarez, who was born in Mexico and entered the United States with his mother between 1993 and 1994. He has not left the country in more than 30 years and told the judge he has lived in Wyoming for 12 years with his two American-born sons.

In an October hearing on a writ of habeas corpus — a legal mechanism that allows detainees to challenge their confinement — the federal government, represented by the U.S. Attorney's office, did not challenge the facts and failed to show cause why Alvarez should not be released by Oct. 27. He was released from immigration custody — but was immediately re-detained after taking only a few steps to freedom.

Sweeney called the re-detention unlawful.

## 'This should shock any reader'

Testimony from ICE officials under oath proved central to the ruling. Nichole Black, assistant field office director for ICE's Enforcement and Removal Operations, told the court she received a "direct order" from her supervisor that she could "re-detain" immigrants "that had some type of criminality."

In her ruling, Sweeney noted Black said she believed it was permissible to "simply escort someone to a public area and immediately re-detain them despite a court order to the contrary."

"This should — and in the court's opinion, must — shock any reader," Sweeney wrote.

Black's supervisor, Nathan Rasper, testified that he believed he was obligated to "make lawful arrests," and that he had heard he could re-arrest people based on a policy in Minnesota. But he also told the court — at a hearing earlier this month that CPR News [reported on](https://www.cpr.org/2026/11/09/federal-judges-ice-court-orders/) — that he had stopped re-arresting people who were ordered released.

That testimony, Sweeney wrote, spared the government from greater consequences.

"Given the (government) cured the contempt by ceasing the practice at issue, the court declines to take any further action," she wrote.

## At least 18 affected, likely more

At least 18 people in Colorado have been held longer than they should have been or re-arrested within minutes of their judge-ordered release in the past six weeks. Immigration attorneys estimate the true number is higher, because many immigrants represent themselves in court.

The ruling drew heavily on longstanding constitutional principles, invoking Supreme Court precedent and citing a 1979 case. "The government of the United States has been emphatically termed a government of laws and not of men," Sweeney wrote. "No man is above the law."

U.S. Attorney Peter McNeilly, who represents the federal government in Colorado, had no comment on the ruling.

## What's next

Judges possess broad contempt powers, including the authority to impose fines or order conduct stopped. Sweeney declined to exercise those powers here because the government had ceased the practice — but her pointed language left little doubt that she would revisit the matter if violations recur.

For ICE, the ruling adds to mounting legal scrutiny of its enforcement practices nationwide, raising questions about whether agency directives are being adequately communicated to field offices and whether court orders are being read at all. For the immigrants caught in the middle, the ruling offers a measure of accountability — but no guarantee that similar re-detentions won't happen again.
