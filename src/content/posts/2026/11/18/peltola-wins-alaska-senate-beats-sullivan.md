# Peltola Wins Alaska Senate, Toppling Dan Sullivan

Democrat Mary Peltola has defeated two-term Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in Alaska, the [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/alaska-senate-peltola-sullivan-congress-f1fd4d243bf764ec46ff94db4c792953) declared on November 18, 2026, completing a remarkable political comeback and sealing her party's control of the Senate. The result caps an extraordinary Democratic sweep in a state President Donald Trump repeatedly carried by wide margins, and it handed the party a 56-44 majority when the new Congress convenes in January.

The Alaska contest was the last U.S. Senate race resolved in this year's midterms. Peltola's victory gave Democrats a ninth flipped Republican-held seat — their biggest midterm gain in nearly 70 years — and delivered the party's largest Senate advantage in 16 years.

## A Comeback Rooted in Alaska

Peltola previously held Alaska's lone U.S. House seat, becoming the first Alaska Native to serve in Congress. She lost her bid for a second full term to Republican Nick Begich in 2024 but remained central to Democrats' plans to retake the Senate in 2026. With this win she becomes the first Alaska Native elected to the Senate — only the fifth Democrat since statehood to represent Alaska in that chamber, and the first since Mark Begich, whom Sullivan defeated in 2014.

In a statement, Peltola said voters sent a clear message that "the status quo in D.C. is not working for Alaska."

"Over the course of this campaign I've spoken with elders in tears about empty fish racks; families struggling to find housing they can afford; and parents in the Bush faced with the impossible choice of heating their homes or putting food on the table," she said. "Today we take a monumental step forward in addressing those issues and putting Alaska first."

## A Race Roiled by a Name and a Lawsuit

The campaign's final stretch was dominated by allegations that Peltola's team helped recruit a retired teacher named Dan J. Sullivan to run in order to siphon votes from the incumbent. Trump called for Peltola's prosecution after The New York Times first reported the allegation less than a week before the November 3 election; Peltola denied any involvement.

"I knew nothing about any other candidates on the ballot except for me and the incumbent," Peltola told supporters in Juneau days before the election. The second Sullivan, listed on the ballot as Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. with no party affiliation, has denied any contact with her campaign — while Sullivan appeared as "Dan S. Sullivan, Republican and incumbent."

At Trump's direction, the Justice Department sued to block certification of the race if the incumbent lost, pending an investigation into possible voter confusion. Peltola called the lawsuit "flagrant corruption." A federal judge [dismissed the case](https://apnews.com/article/alaska-senate-dan-sullivan-peltola-investigation-b35acffc8b55ef58679d9b8395760f98), though the Justice Department appealed. Alaska's attorneys argued the state should conduct its elections "without unwarranted interference by the United States," noting the ballot distinguished the two Sullivans and that ranked-choice voting would let confused voters rank them. The Justice Department also confirmed a criminal investigation, but Alaska's U.S. attorney would not say what potential crimes authorities were examining.

## A Message Built on Affordability

During the campaign, Peltola tapped into Alaskans' frustration with high fuel and food costs — burdens that hit hardest in remote communities relying on diesel for electricity and heat and generally reachable only by air or water. She peppered social media with images of her travels around the state, working to separate herself from national Democrats behind an "Alaska first" platform.

"Alaskans who know me know that I'm not liberal or radical," Peltola said. "I am only interested in working for Alaska and working for the best interest of Alaskans, in as nonpartisan of a way as I possibly can."

The strategy resonated. Sam Schimmel, who is Alaska Native, told the AP in Anchorage after Peltola's win that "people across the country don't understand what it means to grow up in a place where there isn't running water, where there's only one store, where gas costs $17 a gallon, where heating fuel is not a guaranteed thing." He called having someone who understands that in the Senate "an enormous achievement."

Michael Dunn, also in Anchorage, said he voted for Sullivan but hopes Peltola succeeds. "She put in the effort," he said. "So hopefully she puts in the effort in D.C. for us."

## National Significance

The [nine-seat Senate sweep](https://apnews.com/article/senate-democrat-flip-alaska-aa623c1c32cb493c21633a1a6a140578) is the Democrats' largest midterm gain since 1958, when the party netted 15 seats during Dwight Eisenhower's second term. The 2026 wave also flipped seats in South Carolina, Texas, Kansas and Mississippi, and Democrats reclaimed the House, picking up roughly 42 seats.

The 56-44 majority — the largest since Democrats held 58 seats at the close of the 111th Congress in January 2011 — gives the party substantial leverage to confirm judges, advance legislation and check the Trump administration. That such a result unfolded in a presidential second midterm, typically punishing for the incumbent party, underscores the depth of Republican erosion even in conservative strongholds.

Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer called the outcome hard to imagine even months earlier, noting that few thought 51 Democratic seats was possible at the start of the cycle, according to the AP.

## What to Watch

Several questions remain open. The Justice Department's criminal investigation continues, and it is unclear what, if any, consequences could follow for Peltola or the second Sullivan, both of whom deny wrongdoing. The DOJ's appeal of the dismissed certification lawsuit is also unresolved now that the race has been called.

Meanwhile, the U.S. House race in Alaska — where independent Bill Hill is challenging Republican Rep. Nick Begich — had not yet been called. And Peltola must now balance her "Alaska first" brand against the priorities of a strengthened Democratic caucus, as voters in the Bush and along the Kuskokwim watch to see whether her promises on affordability translate into action in Washington.
