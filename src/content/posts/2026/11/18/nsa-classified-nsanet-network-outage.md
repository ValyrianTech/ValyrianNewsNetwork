# NSA's Classified NSANet Goes Down During Patching

A highly classified communications network used by the National Security Agency to circulate top-secret information went offline on Tuesday, prompting the spy agency to alter staffing at its Fort Meade, Maryland, headquarters, according to current and former officials. The NSA publicly attributed the disruption to "temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance," while the nation's top intelligence official linked it to a scramble to patch newly disclosed software vulnerabilities.

There was no immediate evidence that a cyberattack caused the outage, according to multiple people familiar with the matter.

## A Network Central to the Agency's Work

The affected system, known as NSANet, allows NSA employees to securely send and receive classified information internally. Because much of the agency's work—including surveilling foreign spies and terrorists—is classified, a disruption of the network would be a severe impediment to daily operations.

The [New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/17/us/politics/nsa-down-network-outage-nsanet.html) reported that the issue appeared tied to a software patch, and that NSANet had been unstable for weeks—fueling frustration inside a workforce that numbers in the tens of thousands.

The NSA issued a statement characterizing the incident as routine. "The National Security Agency experienced temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance," the agency said, according to the [Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/nsa-computer-network-down-22dc6be1bf1d1f5f86a7167f016e9526). "While some internal systems were affected, mission-critical capabilities remain fully operational and our ability to defend national security continues. NSA's infrastructure is designed with multiple layers of redundancy to maintain operations during planned maintenance or unexpected events."

The agency did not detail the cause or the full scope of the disruption.

## Staffing Tightened as the Day Unfolded

According to [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/17/politics/highly-classified-national-security-communications-network-goes-down), which first reported the outage, the situation at Fort Meade shifted repeatedly over the course of the day. A voicemail inbox used by NSA employees described a "code yellow" alert on Tuesday morning—the second-highest of three main levels governing whether workers report to campus—directing non-emergency personnel to arrive in a staggered manner.

By Tuesday evening, the inbox briefly reverted to "code green," telling employees to report as scheduled. But later that night it escalated to "code orange," closing the campus to most employees and reverting to essential personnel only until further notice. It was not immediately clear what prompted the reversal.

A source told CNN that restoration of the network was ongoing but not yet complete. A former senior NSA official described the disruption as serious but limited in effect.

"It could impact analysis and reporting, but for highest priority requirements they would find a way to work those," the former official told CNN. "So it is alarming, but likely not crippling." The official noted that NSANet "is more of a network of networks, and it's unlikely that a full outage exists given the many redundancies in place."

## The PHP Patching Angle

Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton attributed the NSA outage to the agency applying a software patch to address major vulnerabilities discovered the previous month. "The patching that has gone on following that has caused some of these systems to not come back up when you patch them as expected," Clayton said in an interview. "That is what has happened at the NSA today."

The vulnerabilities were in PHP, a programming language that powers roughly 70% of the world's websites. AI tools helped researchers exploit the flaws, setting off a global scramble by governments and corporations to apply fixes in recent weeks. The NSA itself had urged organizations to install the patches.

Clayton said there was no sign that any of the recent technology outages, including the NSA's, resulted from a cyberattack. Sen. Mark Warner, the Democratic vice chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee, echoed that assessment while pointing to the pressures created by fast-moving threats.

"AI is making it possible to identify vulnerabilities across enormous numbers of systems at unprecedented speed, creating enormous pressure on companies and government agencies to patch those vulnerabilities immediately, often all at once, putting tremendous strain on systems that weren't built to handle that pace of change," Warner said in a statement.

## Part of a Troubling Cluster

The NSA episode arrived amid an unusual run of technical failures across American infrastructure. Late last week, a massive systems failure triggered a nationwide ground stop of flights—the longest since the Sept. 11, 2001, attacks—which the Federal Aviation Administration traced to a single router in Arkansas. A sweeping Microsoft Outlook and Teams outage knocked users offline for hours, and a Chase Bank disruption on Monday rippled out to customers at Bank of America, Capital One and Wells Fargo. United Airlines also suffered an internal systems outage that delayed flights.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio, speaking to reporters while traveling with President Donald Trump in Asia, insisted there was "zero evidence" that the scattered incidents were linked to hostile activity. Trump's communications were not affected by the NSA outage, officials said, citing multiple backup networks for reaching the president securely.

The Five Eyes intelligence-sharing alliance—the United States, Britain, New Zealand, Australia and Canada—was not affected by the NSANet disruption, according to a source from one of those countries and a former U.S. intelligence official.

## Resilience Under Strain

For all its disruption, the episode showcased the redundancies built into U.S. signals-intelligence infrastructure. Mission-critical capabilities reportedly remained operational, intelligence sharing with close allies continued, and presidential communications were unaffected. The [New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/11/17/us-news/classified-us-communication-network-is-down-but-nsa-says-no-evidence-of-cyber-attack-report/) reported on the outage and the agency's assurances that it saw no evidence of a cyberattack.

Still, the clustering of failures has fueled public anxiety about the reliability of the systems that keep the country running—and raised a counterintuitive risk: that the urgent, defensive work of patching vulnerable software can itself cause operational disruption. This is not without precedent. In 2000, a technology failure at the NSA downed computers for nearly three days and interrupted intelligence processing at Fort Meade.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is the full restoration of NSANet and how many systems beyond it were affected. The escalation to code orange—closing the campus to most employees—suggests the agency treated the situation as more than a minor hiccup, even as officials stressed that the cause was benign.

Longer term, the incident sharpens scrutiny of how government agencies manage the breakneck pace of patching in an era of AI-accelerated vulnerability discovery. Lawmakers are likely to press for details on what exactly failed during the maintenance window and whether the NSA's patch-management practices need revision. With the workforce already frustrated by weeks of instability, the outage may also become a case study in the hidden costs of securing aging digital infrastructure—where the cure, for a time, can look a lot like the disease.
