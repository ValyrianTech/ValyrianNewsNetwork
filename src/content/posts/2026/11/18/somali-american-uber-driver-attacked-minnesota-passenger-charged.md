# Uber Driver Strangled, Run Over; Passenger Charged

A Somali American Uber driver was strangled with a seatbelt and then run over by his own car in a Minneapolis suburb early Saturday, according to authorities, an attack that has left the 51-year-old father of five in critical condition and ignited a furor over hate-crime designation and driver safety. Hennepin County prosecutors on Tuesday charged the passenger, 25-year-old Samit Apurva Patel of Hopkins, with attempted second-degree murder and first-degree carjacking.

![Composite image of Samit Apurva Patel and Mohamed Yusuf](https://alphanews.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Uberattack-1.jpeg)

The victim, Mohamed Yusuf, suffered broken ribs, a broken leg, spinal injuries, two collapsed lungs and significant internal bleeding from organs that had to be removed, court documents show. His family told [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/18/man-charged-over-brutal-attack-on-somali-american-in-minnesota) he is in a coma, and his son later said he had undergone five surgeries.

## How the attack unfolded

According to a criminal complaint detailed by [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/minnesota-uber-driver-attacked-somali-american-72c9c7be7b3c01096e7ec2aa9d7831a1), Yusuf picked up Patel shortly before 1 a.m. Saturday in a parking lot near the NE Moose Bar & Grill in Northeast Minneapolis. Yusuf was on a phone call with his brother — also an Uber driver — as the two often talked while working.

On that call, Yusuf's brother heard the passenger ask the driver's name. When Yusuf answered "Mohamed," the passenger replied, "Fuck, I don't like that name," the complaint states. Yusuf told his brother it was fine because the rider was drunk. Minutes later, near a patch of gravel pits in Hopkins, the brother heard Yusuf scream in Somali that he was being choked with a belt. After a pause, Yusuf screamed that he was being run over by the car.

Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty described the attack as "incredibly brutal." She said Yusuf was "apparently pulled out of the car and was standing there when Patel drove over him and might have circled back and driven over him again."

Patel's parents called 911 that night for a wellness check, reporting their son had come home intoxicated and belligerent. When officers arrived, they found Yusuf's damaged, still-running car in the driveway, its system still connected to the call with Yusuf's brother. Officers located Yusuf in a roadside ditch using location data from his smartwatch and a drone with thermal imaging.

## A hate crime? The legal question

Yusuf's family called the attack "a pure hate crime," with a relative telling the Somali Brief, as cited by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/15/somali-american-uber-driver-attack-minnesota), that "our politicians created this hostile environment." Minnesota state Senator Omar Fateh said Yusuf should be "home safe with his family, not fighting for his life after an attack fueled by hatred towards his Somali identity."

But Moriarty said there was not enough evidence to prove the attack met Minnesota's bias-crime standard, which requires showing the victim was targeted because of a protected characteristic such as race or religion. That decision "does not diminish the pain that the family and the Somali community is feeling right now," she said, adding that prosecutors could amend the charges as the case develops.

Defense attorney Dave Risk, who is not involved in the case, told the AP that because the pair likely met randomly through the ride-hailing app, proving intentional bias would be difficult. Moriarty noted Patel may be suffering from paranoia or mental-health issues, and that the motive remains unknown.

[Alpha News](https://alphanews.org/hopkins-resident-charged-in-connection-to-weekend-assault-on-somali-uber-driver/), a Minnesota outlet with a conservative editorial stance, reproduced the complaint's timeline in granular detail — including GPS data and the recorded dialogue — and framed the hate-crime decision as a rebuke to those who had blamed "racist right-wing rhetoric" for the attack. Patel is being held on $1 million bail.

## A community already on edge

Minnesota is home to the largest Somali population in the United States, and the attack landed during a period of intense friction. It followed a midterm campaign in which President Donald Trump made anti-Somali claims a rallying cry, and it came amid aggressive federal immigration enforcement in the Twin Cities.

"We feel as a community we're under threat, constantly," Jaylani Hussein of CAIR-Minnesota told the AP, describing "a disinformation and demonization campaign that has been going on now, and it's coming from every corner."

For fellow drivers, the case hit close to home. "As a Muslim, as an immigrant, as a driver and as a Somali community, it affects me. I'm scared now, more than before," said Muhiyadin Yusuf, a Twin Cities ride-hailing driver. Uber said it was "shocked and horrified" and has banned the rider from its platform, and SEIU Local 26, which organizes rideshare drivers, said it was "shocked, angered and saddened."

## What's next

The case now moves through Hennepin County courts, where prosecutors have left open the possibility of adding a bias enhancement if new evidence emerges. Yusuf remains in critical condition with what the complaint describes as a poor prognosis; a GoFundMe launched by his family had raised more than $140,000 within days.

The attack has also sharpened a national conversation about the vulnerability of ride-hailing drivers who work alone with strangers. Some drivers say they have curtailed night shifts, added cameras or stopped driving altogether — a safety debate that platforms and state regulators may soon be forced to confront.
