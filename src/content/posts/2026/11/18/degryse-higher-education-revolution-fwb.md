# Degryse's Higher Education Revolution in Wallonia-Brussels

Belgium's French-speaking higher education system is facing its most sweeping overhaul in more than a decade. Elisabeth Degryse, Minister-President of the Federation Wallonie-Bruxelles (FWB) and the minister responsible for higher education, has drafted a landmark "Parcours étudiant" decree that would scrap the traditional blocus study periods, reorganise the academic year into five bimesters, and introduce a compulsory first-year competency assessment, [Le Soir reported](https://www.lesoir.be/783355/article/2026-11-18/nouveau-calendrier-fin-des-blocus-test-lentree-degryse-fait-sa-revolution-dans) on 18 November. The reform, intended to replace the much-criticised Décret Paysage, is slated to take effect in the 2028-2029 academic year.

The draft decree, obtained by the newspaper, merges two long-debated tracks that previous governments had struggled to reconcile: a rewrite of the rules governing student success and financing, and a realignment of the academic calendar. The result, observers say, amounts to a structural reset of how universities, hautes écoles and art colleges organise teaching and evaluation.

## A calendar built around rest, not relentless exams

The most visible change concerns the rhythm of the academic year. Under the draft text, the current three-quadrimester structure and the August resit sessions would be abolished. In their place, the year would be organised into five two-month bimesters, with continuous assessment and genuine rest periods built in, [Le Soir detailed](https://www.lesoir.be/783357/article/2026-11-18/enseignement-superieur-le-decret-parcours-veut-en-finir-avec-les-secondes).

Crucially, students would no longer face January exams after revising through the Christmas holidays. Evaluations would take place earlier, before the end of December, followed by two full weeks of holiday, with the same pattern repeating across the year and the academic year concluding in late June or early July.

"Today, many students feel they are permanently in exams and classes," Degryse told Le Soir. "The August session has become a full-fledged assessment session. But I think everyone needs real moments of pause. Teachers also need time to do research."

That rationale echoes comments the minister made a year earlier, when she described the reform as a way to break what she called an endless cycle for students while freeing time for academic research, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/y-aura-t-il-prochainement-un-accord-sur-une-reforme-des-rythmes-dans-l-enseignement-superieur-11608132).

Denis Dufrane, director-president of the Haute École en Hainaut, framed the change as a matter of student wellbeing. Many students now hold side jobs during so-called holidays, he noted, leaving them "never truly able to breathe in the current calendar." Putting students in conditions favourable to rest, he said, "can only favour their success."

## The competency assessment: support or hidden selection?

The most sensitive element is the compulsory competency assessment, or "bilan de compétences," for first-year students. According to [Le Soir's report on the measure](https://www.lesoir.be/783361/article/2026-11-18/enseignement-superieur-un-bilan-de-competences-obligatoire-pour-les-etudiants-de), the assessment would be organised during the opening week of the academic year, from 2028-2029, allowing institutions to identify difficulties early and offer reinforced support.

The initiative stems from the government's coalition declaration, which pledged an obligatory assessment enabling students to "evaluate their level of competence and become aware of gaps to be filled." Its urgency is underscored by stubborn completion figures: only 23% of FWB bachelor's students graduate on time, against a 43% average across OECD countries.

Yet the framing is contested. While the draft describes a diagnostic, non-selective exercise held after enrolment, the reform's headline billing as an "entrance test" has fuelled concern that it could evolve into a de facto selection mechanism in a system built on open access. Flanders imposes an aptitude test with mandatory remediation that leads roughly 30% of students to change their study choice; the FWB has never had such a compulsory gate.

## Universities warn of lost teaching time

Rectors and academics have broadly welcomed the pedagogical ambition but warn that the calendar shift carries risks. Françoise Smets, rector of UCLouvain, said the goal should be a rhythm that favours lasting learning rather than "a blocus where you study fast fast to forget quickly."

Others are more cautious. Frédéric Schoenaerts, vice-rector for education at the University of Liège, warned that squeezing the calendar could force institutions to cut teaching weeks, potentially losing the equivalent of ten to fifteen credits of instruction. "Maintaining our standards of teaching quality is capital," he said.

Anne-Sophie Nyssen, rector of ULiege and president of the CReF rectors' council, urged the government to allow time for a proper transition, warning that imposing the reform too quickly would be "quite simply impossible."

Student representatives have adopted a wary stance shaped by memory of past failures. Adam Assaoui, president of the FEF students' federation, recalled that a similar push three years ago collapsed over unresolved trade-offs. "If we want to move forward without taboos, students must get something in return," he said, cautioning that scrapping the resit session is unthinkable as long as some students still sit a dozen exams in a single session.

## A legacy file, and a political test

Replacing the Décret Paysage, adopted in 2013 by socialist minister Jean-Claude Marcourt and later amended under Valérie Glatigny, has become one of the most anticipated files of the legislature. By bundling success rules, calendar reform and the competency assessment into a single decree, Degryse is betting that a coherent package can succeed where piecemeal efforts failed.

The 2028-2029 entry-into-force date leaves a transition window for the 2027-2028 year, though the text remains a draft and has yet to be formally tabled. The immediate backdrop is tense: the announcement came a day after a few thousand students marched in Brussels to denounce deteriorating study conditions.

The FWB's chronic on-time completion crisis, strained student-teacher ratios and budget pressures all raise the stakes. Whether the reform delivers on its promise of calmer, more effective study will depend on details still to be settled: exactly what the competency assessment measures, what follows a weak result, whether any resit pathway survives, and how large-cohort programmes such as first-year psychology implement continuous assessment at scale.

For now, Wallonia-Bruxelles has a blueprint for its most ambitious education reform in a generation, and a minister determined to see it through. The debate over how fast and how far to go is only beginning.
