# China Launches Yaogan-50 04 Remote Sensing Satellite

China successfully launched the Yaogan-50 04 remote sensing satellite into orbit on Friday evening, adding another node to its steadily expanding civilian Earth-observation network and extending a notably dense stretch of space activity.

According to [Xinhua News Agency](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261127/40ede3a652f1429fa3fbfaee70a2e416/c.html), the satellite lifted off at 21:05 Beijing time on November 27 aboard a Long March 6A (CZ-6A) carrier rocket from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center in Shanxi Province. The satellite entered its planned orbit, and the mission was declared a complete success.

## A Civilian Mission With Practical Applications

Xinhua reported that the Yaogan-50 04 is designed primarily for land and resources surveys, crop yield estimation, and disaster prevention and mitigation. These applications place the satellite firmly within China's civilian Earth-observation portfolio, supporting agricultural planning and emergency response.

The mission marks the 682nd flight of the Long March family of launch vehicles, a milestone that underscores the maturity and high cadence of China's state-run launch program.

## The "Hybrid" Rocket Behind the Launch

The launch was carried out by the Long March 6A, a medium-lift vehicle developed by the 8th Academy of the state-owned China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation (CASC). According to [Kuaikeji, as reported by Sina Finance](https://finance.sina.com.cn/tech/roll/2026-11-27/doc-inkaewtz3092872.shtml), the rocket stands about 50 meters tall with a takeoff mass of roughly 530 tonnes and uses a two-and-a-half-stage configuration.

Its defining feature is a "hybrid mode" that pairs liquid oxygen and kerosene core stages—favored for high performance and long burn times—with solid rocket boosters, which offer high thrust and simpler maintenance. It is China's first solid-liquid "bundled" medium-lift rocket, which conducted its maiden flight on March 29, 2022, and can accommodate single, multiple, stacked, and side-mounted payload configurations.

## A Steady Launch Cadence

Friday's mission came just one day after China launched the Yaogan-49 01 group of satellites. That mission lifted off at 17:38 Beijing time on November 26 from the Xichang Satellite Launch Center aboard a Long March 2D rocket, marking the second consecutive day of remote sensing satellite launches.

The Yaogan-50 series itself has been rolling out steadily through 2026. The 02 satellite launched on March 15 and the 03 satellite on October 23, both from Taiyuan and both aboard Long March 6A rockets, with the same stated civilian purposes as the 04 satellite.

## Analysis: Routine Launch, Broader Signal

On its face, the Yaogan-50 04 mission is a routine, unremarkable launch—another civilian remote sensing satellite in a series that has become familiar to space watchers. Yet the cadence tells a larger story. Two remote sensing launches on consecutive days, alongside several Yaogan-50 series deployments within a single year, illustrate a launch pipeline that has become dependable and nearly continuous.

That rhythm matters for more than prestige. A dense constellation of Earth-observation satellites improves revisit times over Chinese territory, strengthening the data available for agriculture, land management, and disaster response—areas of growing importance as the country confronts climate-related risks and food-security pressures.

At the same time, observers note that the Yaogan designation has, in the view of some foreign analysts, potential dual-use characteristics, even as Chinese official sources describe each mission strictly in civilian terms. As is standard for the program, Xinhua did not disclose detailed orbital parameters or technical specifications for the satellite.

## What to Watch

The immediate takeaway is continuity: China's space program shows no sign of slowing its launch tempo as 2026 draws to a close. Whether the Yaogan-50 series continues its rollout and how the broader remote sensing constellation evolves will be worth tracking in the months ahead—along with the 683rd flight of the Long March family, which is likely not far behind.
