# China, Russia Militaries Hold Strategic Consultation

The Chinese People's Liberation Army (PLA) and the Russian Armed Forces held a strategic consultation by video conference on November 26, pledging to deepen military ties and strengthen coordination on international and regional security, according to [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/world/20261126/85c69fae5e9b4e6495fe7920d5f75e76/c.html), citing the Ministry of National Defense.

The announcement was brief and deliberately general. In a single-paragraph statement, the two sides said they exchanged views on "implementing the consensus of the two heads of state" and on "promoting the continuous deepening and expansion of military relations," and that they "conducted an in-depth exchange of views on the international and regional situation." The two militaries, the statement said, "will further strengthen communication and coordination to jointly safeguard international and regional security and stability."

## A Routine but Telling Signal

The consultation is framed as a continuation of a recurring high-level defense dialogue mechanism rather than a response to any specific event. The terse, formulaic language is characteristic of Chinese military releases on such talks, which typically avoid operational detail and keep strategic messaging broad.

The reference to "the consensus of the two heads of state" ties the military channel directly to the political track between President Xi Jinping and President Vladimir Putin. It signals that defense engagement remains a structured, leadership-directed component of the broader bilateral relationship.

The video-conference format — rather than an in-person meeting — is notable, though the statement did not name the officers who led the talks or specify where they were held.

## Context: A Sustained Tempo of Engagement

The consultation extends a busy stretch of China-Russia defense diplomacy through late 2026. On November 3, Central Military Commission Vice Chairman Zhang Shengmin met visiting Russian Defense Minister Andrei Belousov at Beijing's Diaoyutai State Guesthouse, where both sides pledged to strengthen strategic communication and deepen military cooperation, as [China's Ministry of National Defense](http://www.mod.gov.cn/gfbw/qwfb/16497821.html) reported.

That meeting coincided with two milestones frequently invoked by officials: the 30th anniversary of the China-Russia strategic partnership of coordination and the 25th anniversary of the Treaty of Good-Neighborliness and Friendly Cooperation. Earlier in the year, the two navies conducted Joint Sea-2026, the 12th iteration of an exercise series launched in 2012, near Qingdao — a continued showcase of maritime coordination.

The November 26 consultation also came just days after an unusual diplomatic moment: the APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting in Shenzhen, where Xi, Putin, and U.S. President Donald Trump reportedly held a first-ever trilateral meeting on the sidelines. Against that fluid great-power backdrop, the China-Russia military channel appears to be consolidating rather than loosening.

## Analysis: Coordination, Not Alliance

Analysts caution against reading the consultation as a step toward a formal military alliance. As the [Russian International Affairs Council (RIAC)](https://russiancouncil.ru/en/analytics-and-comments/columns/military-and-security/china-russia-military-cooperation-strategic-implications-for-global-stability-the-middle-east-and-pa/) noted in a November 23 analysis, both countries "retain independent foreign and security policies, and their partnership does not impose an automatic obligation to participate in each other's conflicts."

Instead, the relationship is best understood as deepening coordination and mutual strategic reassurance. Joint exercises, naval operations, air-force exchanges, military education, and technological cooperation have expanded considerably, RIAC observed, providing "both with additional strategic options and strengthens their ability to respond to changing international security circumstances."

The deliberate ambiguity in the November 26 statement — no named participants, no specific agenda items, no concrete deliverables — is itself a signal. It preserves strategic flexibility while affirming continuity, allowing external observers to interpret the relationship's trajectory without Beijing or Moscow committing to specifics.

## What to Watch

Several questions remain open. The statement did not identify which officers led the consultation, nor did it specify which regional issues were discussed under the broad heading of "the international and regional situation" — likely candidates include Ukraine, the Middle East, and Asia-Pacific security, but none were confirmed. A matching Russian readout may yet surface with additional detail.

What is clear is that the military-to-military channel remains active and leadership-endorsed. Coming so soon after the Shenzhen trilateral meeting and the Belousov visit, the consultation underscores that Beijing and Moscow intend to keep their defense dialogue on a steady footing — even as the wider diplomatic environment shifts around them.
