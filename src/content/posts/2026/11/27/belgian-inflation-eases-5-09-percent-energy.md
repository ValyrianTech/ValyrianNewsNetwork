# Belgian Inflation Eases to 5.09% as Energy Still Bites

Belgian inflation fell to **5.09 percent in November 2026**, down from 5.26 percent in October, the Federal Public Service Economy reported on 27 November. Yet the modest decline masks a stubborn underlying reality: energy remains the dominant force pushing up the cost of living, with core inflation — which strips out energy and unprocessed food — sitting at a much lower 2.47 percent, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/27/inflatie-november/).

The figures mean everyday life in Belgium was 5.09 percent more expensive this November than in the same month a year earlier — a slowdown from October's three-year high, but a sharp acceleration from September's 4.69 percent, both reported by VRT NWS.

## Energy Does the Heavy Lifting

The headline number tells only part of the story. Energy products alone are running **33.69 percent higher** than a year ago, up from 33.22 percent in October, VRT NWS reported. Natural gas has surged **65.6 percent** year-on-year, heating oil **38.1 percent** and electricity **25.6 percent**.

Month-on-month, the pressure is still building: natural gas rose 7.7 percent and electricity 3 percent between October and November, even as motor fuels fell 7.5 percent over the same period.

The root cause, according to VRT NWS, is geopolitical: "the war in the Middle East in particular is fueling energy prices and thus inflation," the broadcaster noted. On top of the energy-driven pressure, households are paying more for household appliances and clothing as well.

That energy inflation dwarfs the general cost of living, creating a bifurcated picture: households squeezed by heating and electricity bills, while the prices of some everyday goods actually fell. Motor fuels, food, and even holiday villages and campsites were all slightly cheaper than a year earlier.

## The Wage Storm on the Horizon

Perhaps the most consequential figure buried in the report concerns wages. Based on the November data, the HR services firm Acerta calculated that roughly **1 million Belgian workers will receive automatic wage increases in January 2027** through the country's wage indexation system, VRT NWS reported.

Among them are some 600,000 white-collar employees covered by joint committee PC200, whose wages are currently estimated to rise by **4.41 percent**. Workers in agriculture and horticulture, food processing, goods transport and hospitality are set to see increases of around 4.17 percent.

The mechanism links pay, benefits and pensions to the health index, cushioning workers against inflation but creating a feedback loop that can entrench price pressures — a perennial concern for employers and the central bank alike.

## A Near-Miss on the Spilindex

The November figures came close to triggering an even broader round of indexation. According to the index tracker [Loonindex.be](https://www.loonindex.be/index/november-2026), the health index stood at 105.41, with the smoothed health index at 102.25 — tantalizingly short of the **spilindex threshold of 102.29**. Only a further 0.04 percent increase would have tipped the measure, which would have indexed public-sector wages, benefits and pensions.

Citing the current projections of the Federal Planning Bureau, Loonindex.be reported that the spilindex is expected to be crossed in **December 2026** — meaning the January indexation may not be the last word on wage costs heading into the new year.

## What to Watch

The November headline decline is real but fragile. It rests on softer food and fuel prices, while the structural drivers — an energy shock rooted in war and a tight electricity market — remain firmly in place. Core inflation easing from 2.80 percent to 2.47 percent offers genuine reassurance that underlying price pressure is cooling, but it is the energy line that will determine whether Belgian households feel relief.

For policymakers, the convergence of high energy costs, automatic wage indexation and an imminent spilindex crossing creates a delicate balancing act. The question going into 2027 is not whether Belgian wages will rise — they almost certainly will — but whether the buying power those increases restore can keep pace with the energy bills arriving at the same time.
