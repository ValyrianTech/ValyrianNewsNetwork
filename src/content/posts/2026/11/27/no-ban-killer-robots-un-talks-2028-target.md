# No Ban on Killer Robots as UN Talks End With 2028 Target

Five days of negotiations at the United Nations in Geneva ended without an international ban on lethal autonomous weapons systems — the machines widely known as "killer robots." Instead, the 128 member states of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) agreed to begin "elaborating" a possible international instrument over the next two years, with a target date of 2028.

The outcome, announced in the early hours of Saturday 21 November after talks that ran through the night, fell far short of what nearly 100 states had demanded: binding negotiations toward a legally enforceable ban. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/21/vn-bereiken-overeenstemming-tijdens-conferentie-over-killerrobot/), no concrete rules on the use of the weapons were adopted, and several NGOs responded with open disappointment.

## What Was at Stake

The weapons at issue are systems that, using artificial intelligence, can independently select and attack targets without a human giving the final order to fire. That capability sits at the centre of one of the most consequential ethical and legal debates in modern warfare: whether machines should ever be allowed to decide who lives and who dies.

Supporters of firm controls entered the conference at the Palais des Nations — held from 16 to 20 November under the presidency of Swiss Ambassador Julien Thöni — calling this the last best chance to agree rules before the technology outpaces diplomacy. As [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/16/nx-s1-6042548/un-killer-robots-ai-weapons-drones) reported ahead of the talks, some experts warned the world was only months away from weapons that can choose targets and make the decision to kill autonomously.

## A Consensus Barrier and a Blocking Minority

The structural obstacle is the CCW's rulebook. Decisions under the convention require consensus, meaning a single state can veto an agreement. A small group of countries that already possess or are developing the technology — notably the United States, Russia and Israel — used that leverage to resist legally binding rules.

As [Deutsche Welle](https://www.dw.com/en/killer-robots-talks-at-un-end-with-no-legally-binding-ban/a-80008584) reported, the arms-producing giants were among those opposing the mandate, while Washington floated a five-year timetable for non-binding guidelines instead. More than 50 states — including Ireland, Italy, Brazil, Mexico, France and Germany — had called for a strict timetable toward binding rules, according to NPR. UN Secretary-General António Guterres, the International Committee of the Red Cross and Pope Leo XIV had all backed a treaty.

The process was also marked by procedural friction. Diplomats said Russia's objections over procedure led to some formal sessions being cancelled, and talks over the final wording ran through the night. In the end, some states rejected the word "negotiate" altogether; the agreed text refers instead to the "elaboration" of an instrument — a deliberate softening that leaves open whether the eventual document will be legally binding.

## The Chair's Case — and the Campaigners' Fury

Thöni framed the result as proof that multilateralism still functions. "It shows that even in today's deeply divided world, multilateral diplomacy can still deliver concrete results," he told reporters. He described more than a decade of talks as having produced a significant step for humanitarian disarmament, and said the coming process should determine which weapons can be banned under international humanitarian law and which can be permitted but regulated.

Campaigners saw it very differently. Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of more than 300 organisations, called the outcome a "watered-down mandate that fails to chart a clear path towards a legally binding instrument," as [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/21/nations-agree-to-draft-rules-for-killer-robots-at-arms-control-summit) reported, citing AFP. The group added that "in the final hours, a small number of states weakened the mandate, introducing uncertainty about the legal status of the final document."

Nicole van Rooijen, the coalition's executive director, warned that delay carries a human cost. "Delaying the creation of a legally binding instrument puts civilians and humanity at risk," she said, noting that autonomous weapons are already being deployed in combat, including AI systems that choose targets. Verity Coyle, deputy director for arms at Human Rights Watch, put it bluntly: the outcome "does not provide the urgent action needed to agree new rules — but the technology is racing ahead."

Brazilian ambassador Frederico Meyer, a prominent supporter of stricter rules, captured the frustration of the pro-regulation camp. "Technology evolves faster than diplomacy," he said. "Diplomats are very slow."

## Why the Red Cross Is Worried

The humanitarian concern is not abstract. The laws of war — the Geneva Conventions — require fighters to distinguish a soldier still fighting from one who is wounded or in the process of surrendering, judgments that depend on rapid contextual reading of a chaotic battlefield. The ICRC doubts that current AI can reliably make them.

"The risk is that, based on the technology we are seeing, they are not able to understand in the same way that a human is the contextual signals," Georgia Hinds, a legal adviser with the Red Cross, told NPR. "And so the risks are incredibly high that there will be a misidentification of a target."

Rights groups also warn of proliferation. As the technology becomes cheaper and easier to militarise, the danger grows that autonomous systems could be adopted by terrorist groups or repressive law-enforcement agencies. In theory, a swarm of autonomous drones could be programmed to kill everyone wearing a certain colour or speaking a certain language.

## Analysis: Language as Substance

The shift from "negotiate" to "elaborate" is not cosmetic. It lowers the ambition of the mandate and defers the central question — whether the eventual instrument will be binding — to a process that will not conclude until 2028. Supporters frame the two-year work programme as tangible progress after a stalemate stretching back more than a decade. Critics frame it as kicking a dangerous can down the road while the technology races ahead.

The deadlock also exposes a deeper flaw in the CCW architecture. Designed in the 1990s as a flexible framework that could add new protocols as weapons technology evolved — it produced a ban on blinding laser weapons in 1995 — its consensus rule now hands a veto to any single state. That same dynamic has stalled other UN disarmament fora, and it is why frustrated states once took the landmine issue outside the UN process entirely to forge the 1997 Ottawa Convention.

That precedent is the quiet question hanging over Geneva. If the CCW cannot deliver binding rules, like-minded states may eventually abandon it for a standalone treaty — one that the major military powers would likely refuse to join.

## What's Next

Between now and 2028, states will work to categorise which autonomous weapons are prohibited under international humanitarian law and which are permitted but regulated. Whether that effort yields a binding instrument or merely voluntary guidelines remains the central unresolved question — and the fault line between the nearly 100 states that wanted a ban and the few that blocked one.

Guterres had hoped negotiations on an international accord would begin in 2026. That goal was not met. He has warned that allowing fully autonomous weapons to kill would be "crossing a moral red line." For now, that line remains uncodified — and the machines keep advancing.
