# Belgium's Audit Office Casts Doubt on €16.4bn Savings Plan

Belgium's Court of Audit has raised serious doubts about the federal government's flagship budget strategy, concluding that many of the measures announced at the time of the 2025 budget — intended to generate some €16.4 billion by 2029 — are uncertain, delayed, or difficult to calculate. The finding, published in the audit institution's 183rd Book, Part III, undercuts the central fiscal narrative of Prime Minister Bart De Wever's governing coalition.

According to [Het Laatste Nieuws](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/rekenhof-ziet-heel-wat-onzekerheden-bij-begrotingsmaatregelen-van-2025-die-16-4-miljard-euro-moeten-opbrengen~adff72f3/), the Court's review of the federal government's 2025 budget policy found that a raft of reforms and savings — spread across the federal administration and social security — carry a total projected impact of €16.4 billion in 2029, but that their actual yield is far from assured.

## A fiscal picture already under pressure

The scrutiny lands against a deteriorating budgetary backdrop. Belgium closed 2025 with a government deficit of 5.2% of GDP and gross debt of 108.1% of GDP, both breaching EU fiscal rules; the deficit had been 4.4% in 2024. The [Court of Audit](https://www.ccrek.be/nl/publicatie/183e-boek-deel-iii) reports that the deterioration stems mainly from Entity I — the federal government and social security — whose deficit rose from 2.7% to 3.7% of GDP, though that is still better than the 5.5% initially forecast.

The Court notes that federal expenditure in 2025 totalled €98.8 billion, of which €53.4 billion was transferred to other policy levels and €45.4 billion was own spending. Additional defence outlays and new policy carried a cost of €3.5 billion.

## The €16.4 billion question

At the heart of the report is the coalition agreement's package of reforms and savings. The total planned impact is €16.4 billion by 2029, of which €9.1 billion falls on the federal government and €7.3 billion on social security. The Court's own summary text cites €16.3 billion for the same trajectory — a minor discrepancy likely reflecting rounding or revisions between draft and final figures.

What matters more, the auditors say, is reliability. "Several measures were postponed, spread over time, or re-estimated," the Court states, as quoted by HLN. Among the specific measures flagged are those concerning migration, the reform of the *leefloon* (integration income), and the reorganisation of the federal public services.

Defence has been another moving part. Spending was increased significantly to meet NATO's 2% of GDP norm, overseen by Defence Minister Theo Francken — a commitment that adds to the budget's cost base even as savings elsewhere remain uncertain.

## Tax reform: hard to pin down

The Court also concludes that the impact of the government's tax reforms remains difficult to determine. Cited examples include the fiscal regularisation (a form of tax amnesty), the tightening of conditions for the DBI deduction on definitively taxed income, and the tax on securities accounts.

These are precisely the kind of revenue measures that are sensitive to taxpayer behaviour and administrative execution — factors that make budget projections inherently fragile.

## A pattern of scepticism, not a one-off

This is not the first time the Court of Audit has challenged the government's arithmetic. In May 2025, as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/05/16/begroting-terugverdieneffecten-regering-de-wever-rekenhof/) reported, the Court was already harsh, warning that the so-called *terugverdieneffecten* — indirect revenues the government expects to flow from its own measures, such as pushing the unemployed back to work — were "insufficiently substantiated" and at "very great risk" of being substantially overestimated. The government's 2025 plan had projected €7.8 billion in such return effects by 2029.

The Court then questioned the assumed 78% employment rate, calling it "an unsubstantiated objective" rather than a calculated outcome, and warned of double-counting: nearly €2 billion already booked in 2029 from limiting unemployment benefits in time could not be re-counted as return effects. It also flagged doubts about fiscal revenue estimates and the financing of extra military spending.

At the time, De Wever's office responded that "a budget always contains uncertain factors" and that the government would adjust during budget reviews "as always happens." The opposition was less measured: Groen's Stefaan Van Hecke said the budget was "built on quicksand," while the PS's Pierre-Yves Dermagne called the report "deadly."

## Why it matters

The significance goes beyond accounting. The Court is Belgium's independent fiscal watchdog, and its direct statement that measures worth some €16.4 billion are uncertain or hard to calculate undermines the credibility of the government's consolidation trajectory. It is not a judgment that the measures are wrong in principle, but that they may not be delivered on schedule — and execution risk, rather than design, is the recurring theme.

With a deficit far above the EU's 3% threshold and debt above 100% of GDP, Belgium is exposed to European Commission scrutiny and pressure for corrective action. Unreliable savings projections raise the risk of non-compliance and could force additional consolidation measures at the next budget review.

Politically, the report hands fresh ammunition to critics and adds pressure on coalition partners, notably Vooruit, which has been pressing its own budget demands. For a prime minister who campaigned on restoring Belgium's public finances, the finding is an uncomfortable reminder that promises on paper are not the same as euros in the treasury.

## What to watch

Three questions now loom: which specific savings measures were re-estimated downward, and by how much; whether the government will confirm the findings and announce corrective action at the next budget review; and how the European Commission weighs the Court's conclusions in its assessment of Belgium's fiscal path. For now, the €16.4 billion remains an ambition — one the auditors say is still very much a work in progress.
