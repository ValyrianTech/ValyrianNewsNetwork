# Republicans Rethink Tariffs, Handing Democrats an Opening

WASHINGTON — Democrats hoping to blunt President Donald Trump's tariffs when they retake control of Congress may find partners in an unlikely place: among his fellow Republicans. After devastating midterm losses centered on rising costs, many Republicans say they are open to reconsidering at least some of the import taxes that have become a central plank of Trump's [global trade agenda](https://apnews.com/article/trump-tariffs-republicans-congress-a438d0649e3b905804eafb63e9803bb3).

In interviews with more than a half-dozen congressional Republicans since the Nov. 3 election, most said they were willing to review tariffs on a case-by-case basis, expressed a desire for greater predictability, and said Congress could reclaim its constitutional authority over tariffs. They were reluctant, however, to broadly curb the president's own tariff powers — and acknowledged that Trump could veto any repeal legislation.

Whether Republicans actually buck the president on tariffs could signal whether election losses and Trump's lame-duck status shake what has been nearly lockstep fealty to him.

## A "Rethink" Takes Shape

The shift is already visible in the party's rhetoric. "A rethink is indeed in order," said Sen. Todd Young, R-Ind. "I'll be a bipartisan voice for making sure we have the most consumer friendly tariff approaches and I think there will be a whole lot of receptivity within the administration to my approach."

Young, who faces reelection in 2028, has said the tariff structure is "overly broad, and as a result, it is negatively affecting too many American factory floors, farms, small businesses, and family budgets." Rep. Michael Guest, R-Miss., struck a similar note, saying "there may be some areas that I would agree with the Democrats."

"I would personally be opposed to just a blanket restriction on the president's ability to impose tariffs," Guest said. "But I would be willing to look at it on a case-by-case basis based on what the Democrats may bring forward."

Others are looking inward at how Congress ceded power in the first place. Sen. Mike Rounds, R-S.D., said lawmakers over the years delegated tariff authority to the executive branch, and "some of that has cascaded." He added: "There's nothing wrong with Congress taking a second look at whether or not those authorities should be withdrawn, modified, at least reconsidered, or limited."

## The Economic Cost at the Center

The reconsideration follows a midterm election in which the cost of living dominated. Rep. Steve Womack, R-Ark., said tariffs "have played a role in some of the affordability issues," adding that he has "producers in my district that lament the fact that they're paying higher for goods that can't be sourced domestically."

Trump made tariffs a defining feature of his second term, calling himself "Mr. Tariff" as he imposed broad levies on allies and adversaries alike. He insists the tariffs will revive American manufacturing, though there is scant evidence they have done so — and tariffs often raise costs for consumers in the form of a tax. According to [Yale University's Budget Lab](https://apnews.com/article/trump-tariffs-democratic-congress-canada-veto-588eba3bcb688301cbcef3847c79db9b), the average U.S. tariff has risen from about 2.5% when Trump returned to office in January 2025 to 11.2% today, costing the average household roughly $900 a year.

Voters noticed. About two-thirds of U.S. voters said Trump's tariffs had hurt the national economy or their local economy, while only about 2 in 10 said they had helped, according to [The AP Voter Poll](https://apnews.com/projects/elections-2026/voter-poll/), a survey of more than 100,000 midterm voters. Republican voters were more mixed: about half of those who backed the GOP candidate in their local House race said tariffs helped the national economy, while about 3 in 10 said they hurt.

Yet the party is not uniformly ready to turn on the president's signature policy. Sen. Josh Hawley, R-Mo., warned of an overcorrection. "I bet a lot of my Republican colleagues will be happy to join Democrats and get rid of a bunch of them, which makes me just a little nervous that it's going to be a dramatic pendulum swing," he said.

The White House signaled no retreat. Spokesman Matthew Middleton said the administration "is committed to rebuilding the American communities left behind by previous administrations and will continue fighting to lower costs, expand opportunity, and make life more affordable for hardworking American families."

## Canada Tariffs as the First Test

If Democrats and Republicans unite on the issue, tariffs on Canada could emerge as an early test case. Previous repeal efforts won support from a handful of Republicans, and Democrats — including incoming Senate Finance Committee Chairman Ron Wyden of Oregon — have already identified the Canada tariffs as a top target.

Sen. John Kennedy, R-La., who is up for reelection in 2028, said he distinguishes the Canadian levies from tariffs on countries like China, which he supports. "Tariffs with respect to Canada don't make sense to me," he said.

Wyden said that if Democrats succeed in winning Republican backing to repeal the Canadian tariffs, "we'll have some momentum for going after other tariffs." His House counterpart, incoming Ways and Means Chairman Richard Neal of Massachusetts, called rescinding the Canada tariffs "a good starting point" but said "taking up the tariffs discussion in totality is really important, largely because the way they've been used in a punitive manner."

## The Veto Looms

No hurdle is as formidable as Trump's veto power. Overriding it requires a two-thirds majority in both chambers — 67 votes in the Senate — a steep climb even with Democrats poised to hold a 56-seat majority and full control of Congress.

Sen. Rand Paul, R-Ky., a fervent tariff opponent, said he would back repeals, but only if they were attached to must-pass legislation. "On its own, he'll just veto it," he said. That reality means any meaningful challenge to the tariff regime likely rides on must-pass bills such as government funding measures or the renewal of the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement, which Young said works best "if Congress is actively involved and not patted on the head."

Democrats also remain divided on trade. Some want a return to the lower-tariff policies Trump dismantled; others want to keep wielding tariffs to counter unfair foreign practices and pressure adversaries like China. As former trade official Ryan Majerus put it, "It can't just be: We don't like what Trump's doing."

## What to Watch

The coming weeks will test whether Republican discomfort with tariffs hardens into votes to constrain or repeal them — or remains rhetorical openness that collapses against presidential power. A repeal of the Canada tariffs reaching the floor would be the clearest signal of how many Republicans are willing to break ranks. And any move by the administration to soften its tariff approach could preempt Congress before the fight fully forms. For now, the opening is real but narrow.
