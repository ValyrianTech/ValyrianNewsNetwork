# Biden Memoir Sales Fall Far Short of Obama, Clinton Books

Former President Joe Biden's new memoir sold roughly 21,000 copies in its first week on sale — a modest figure that, while enough to top The New York Times nonfiction bestseller list, landed far below the opening pace set by comparable books from his Democratic predecessors, according to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/joe-biden-memoir-obama-clinton-be240fbad570702200e0963d8fd43059).

The numbers, compiled by book-industry tracker Circana, offer a stark measure of how Biden's public appeal has diminished since he left office in January 2025, and how a book seen as unrevealing about his most consequential decisions has struggled to find an audience.

## A Fraction of Predecessors' Sales

"Promise Me, America," published November 17 by Little, Brown and Company, sold around 21,000 copies in its first week, per Circana, which tracks roughly 85 percent of the physical book market. The figure is measured against openings that were orders of magnitude larger.

Barack Obama's "A Promised Land" sold more than 800,000 copies within 24 hours of its 2020 release and topped 1 million by the end of its first week. Bill Clinton's "My Life," released in 2004, surpassed 1 million sales within its first two weeks.

The disparity extends to the broader Biden family. Former first lady Jill Biden's "View from the East Wing," published in June 2026, has sold around 46,000 copies, according to Circana — still more than double her husband's opening week. By comparison, White House memoirs by Michelle Obama and Hillary Clinton were both million sellers within a month of release.

## Lukewarm Reviews and Limited Promotion

The book's reception was shaped by two factors, according to AP. Critics found the memoir largely unrevealing about Biden's time in office and about his 2024 decision not to seek reelection amid widespread concern about his age and health.

A New York Times review carried the headline ["Reflecting on 2024, Joe Biden Finds No Faults of His Own."](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/13/books/review/promise-me-america-joe-biden.html) Critic Jennifer Szalai wrote that the book "reads like a mix of cover letter and campaign speech — one that's more than 400 pages long," and argued that Biden "is so preoccupied with burnishing his legacy that he gets awkward and defensive when addressing the two issues many readers will be most curious about: his handling of the war in Gaza, and his decision to run for a second term."

Sales may also have suffered from the 84-year-old Biden's limited promotion of the book; he is battling stage 4 prostate cancer, AP reported.

## A Telling Advance

The sales gap was foreshadowed by the size of the deal Biden struck to write the book. According to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promise_Me,_America), citing reporting from The Wall Street Journal and The New York Times, Biden sold the memoir rights for a roughly $10 million advance — well below the roughly $15 million Clinton and $60 million Obama reportedly commanded for their memoirs.

That publishers priced Biden's book at a fraction of his predecessors' reflects differing expectations of its commercial appeal. The 448-page memoir, released three days before Biden's 84th birthday, covers his presidency and his contested withdrawal from the 2024 race.

Despite the modest sales, the publisher, Little, Brown and Company, released a statement saying it was "immensely proud" of the book topping the New York Times nonfiction bestseller list.

## What the Numbers Reveal

The first-week figures are widely read in the publishing industry as a proxy for a former president's ongoing cultural and political relevance — and by that measure, Biden's standing has cooled considerably. Where Obama and Clinton remained pop-culture fixtures whose books became events, Biden's memoir arrived with less fanfare, less promotion, and a narrative that critics say declined to reckon with the controversies that defined his final years in office.

Biden's earlier book, "Promise Me, Dad" (2017), about his son Beau's illness and death, was well received and struck a more personal chord. The contrast with "Promise Me, America" underscores how the political memoir — a genre that rewards reflection and humility — can stumble when an author is seen as defending rather than examining his record.

## What to Watch

Whether the book develops "legs" beyond its opening week remains an open question. Circana's figures track mostly physical copies, leaving e-book and audiobook sales unaccounted for, and second-week and cumulative numbers will offer a clearer picture of the memoir's ultimate reach.

For now, the early returns make one thing plain: in the marketplace of post-presidential memoirs, Biden's book is competing in a different weight class than the ones his Democratic predecessors wrote — and the gap says as much about his diminished public standing as it does about the book itself.
