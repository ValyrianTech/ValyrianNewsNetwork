# Court: Social Media Firms Not Liable in Buffalo Shooting

New York's highest court has ruled that major social media companies cannot be held liable for the racist 2022 mass shooting at a Buffalo supermarket, a decision that shields platforms from lawsuits alleging their content and design helped radicalize the gunman who killed 10 Black people. In a 4-3 decision issued Monday, the state's Court of Appeals upheld a lower court's dismissal of claims brought by survivors and victims' relatives, [The Associated Press reported](https://apnews.com/article/buffalo-supermarket-shooting-lawsuits-39338cead3894bb06ae673aa9dfa07a1).

## The Ruling

The Albany-based court held that the four lawsuits "fail to establish that the shooter's actions were a natural and foreseeable consequence of these defendants' decisions about how to design and operate their platforms." Writing for the majority, the judges concluded that the gunman's conduct was so extraordinary as to sever the causal chain as a matter of law, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/legal/government/social-media-companies-not-liable-buffalo-mass-shooting-new-yorks-highest-court-2026-11-23/).

The ruling affected Facebook and Instagram owner Meta, Google and YouTube parent Alphabet, and Reddit. Under different reasoning, it also upheld dismissal of claims against other messaging and social media apps as well as Amazon, which owns Twitch, the livestreaming service the gunman used to broadcast the attack. The lower court being upheld had found the platforms immune under Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, the federal law that broadly shields online companies from liability over user content.

## The Dissent

Three judges disagreed, arguing the survivors and families had built enough of a case to proceed. In an opinion by Judge Jenny Rivera, the dissenters pointed to the gunman's own diary, in which he described himself as an online "addict" whose "mental health has seriously degraded." Rivera wrote that no federal or state law required the court to "slam the courthouse door on plaintiffs' claims."

## The Attack and Its Aftermath

Payton Gendron, who is white, absorbed racist conspiracy theories online before opening fire at the Tops Friendly Market on May 14, 2022, killing 10 Black people and wounding three others. He was 18 at the time. The people killed ranged in age from 32 to 86: Celestine Chaney, Geraldine Talley, Katherine Massey, Pearl Young, Andre Mackneil, Ruth Whitfield, Roberta Drury, Margus Morrison, Aaron Salter and Heyward Patterson.

The lawsuits accused the companies of offering platforms engineered to promote extreme content and encourage compulsive use and of helping to radicalize Gendron. Gendron told a court in 2023 that he "believed what I read online and acted out of hate." The now-23-year-old is serving a life sentence without parole after pleading guilty to state-level murder and hate-motivated terrorism charges.

## What It Means

For the plaintiffs, the decision is a major setback. Matthew Bergman, who represents victim Heyward Patterson's family and estate, said the families "deserve accountability when dangerous product design choices contribute to real-world harm" and that they were assessing their legal options.

The case had been closely watched because it sought to establish that platform design itself—not merely user speech—could give rise to liability, a theory central to other pending lawsuits involving minors, addiction and violent radicalization. The ruling reinforces strong protections for social media companies against claims of real-world harm and may chill similar product-design suits, though plaintiffs could still refine causation arguments or pursue new legal theories.

The decision also intersects with a broader national debate over algorithmic amplification, online radicalization and efforts to reform Section 230. Separately, jury selection is ongoing in Gendron's federal hate crime trial, which carries the possibility of a death sentence if he is convicted; he has pleaded not guilty. That proceeding is likely to keep the radicalization narrative—and calls for platform accountability—in public view.
