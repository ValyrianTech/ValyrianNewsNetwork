# The Names Beneath the Waves

A long-form feature published by Xinhua on November 27 argues that the names of South China Sea islands and reefs carry a thousand years of Chinese history — and that this layered toponymy amounts to evidence of discovery, settlement, and administration. Written by Sanya-based correspondent Zhao Yingquan after more than two decades of reporting from the region, the piece is filed as culture but reads as a carefully constructed sovereignty narrative, blending memoir, literary essay, and historical argument in a single voice.

The article, [published by Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261127/c1c11c7624024b9ea46be73970ba5bb5/c.html), opens not on a reef but in grief. In April 2006, the fishing vessel *Qiong Qionghai 03012* was attacked at the Nansha (Spratly) archipelago's Southern Shoal; four men from Tanmen, Hainan — Ke Jingqin, Li Xuanxing, Ke Weixi and Chen Yichao — did not return. Mai Bangfen, then head of the Qionghai Tanmen Fishermen's Association, showed the author a *Genglubu*, the hereditary navigational manuscript kept by local fishing families. "Maozhi, Yulin, Nailuo, Kugui — these names are made familiar by fishermen with their lives," Mai is quoted saying. "If the names are forgotten, the door opens."

## Two Brushes, One Sea

The article's central rhetorical device is what it calls "two brushes." The scholar's brush wrote the classical names that reached the dynastic histories: "Zhanghai" (the Rising Sea) in the Qin and Eastern Han, when Yang Fu's *Yiwu Zhi* recorded "Zhanghai Qitou, water shallow and full of lodestones." By the Song dynasty, Zhou Qufei's *Lingwai Daida* spoke of "Changsha and Shitang stretching tens of thousands of li," while Zhao Rushi and Wang Xiangzhi gave the region the paired names "A Thousand Li of Sand" for the Xisha (Paracel) islands and "Ten Thousand Li of Stone Pond" for the Nansha.

The fisherman's charcoal pen wrote smaller, earthier names: Maozhi ("cat reef"), Yulin ("fish scales"), Nailuo, Kugui. Zhang Zhengsheng, a professor at South China Normal University's School of Geography, explains the "dislocated expression" between the two registers: scholars recorded what trade and voyages revealed and so wrote coarsely, while fishermen, who lived on the sea, knew each reef's position and character intimately. "Their ordering was not longitude and latitude," the article notes, "but wind, fish, and how many *geng* — watches — it took to get home."

## Layers of Memory

The piece then climbs through successive strata of naming: the literary and imperial names; the voyages of Zheng He, which left behind Zheng He Reef, Jinghong Island, Mahuan Island and Feixin Island; the reign titles Yongle and Xuande, pressed directly into coral; and finally the layer of ships and guns. In 1909, Qing naval commander Li Zhun cruised the Xisha with the vessels Fubo, Guangjin and Chenhang — three islands now bear those names. In 1946, the Republic of China sent the warships Taiping, Yongxing, Zhongjian and Zhongye to recover the Xisha and Nansha, and the ships again became places: Taiping Island, Yongxing Island, Zhongjian Island, Zhongye Island.

"Layered names are exactly 'who came first, who lived long, who administered, who returned,'" the author writes. The load-bearing artifact in his account is the *Genglubu*, a national-level intangible cultural heritage item. Zhang Shuoren of Hainan Tropical Ocean University calls it "a guide to South China Sea operations that Hainan fishermen earned with the blood and sweat of generation after generation." The author adds his own gloss: it is also the sea's "book of milk names," the source from which the official names later grew.

## The 1983 Standard Names

The factual spine of the feature is the April 1983 publication, by the China Committee on Geographical Names, of 287 standard names for South China Sea islands and reefs. According to the article, 126 fishermen's vernacular names — 42 for the Xisha and 84 for the Nansha — were retained under the principle of "name follows the owner," and of 172 old names published in 1947, 164 remain in use. Zhang Zhengsheng notes that while many vernacular names were adopted, a number of features kept earlier designations rather than fully replacing foreign names.

That detail matters, because the article's subtext is the contest over naming itself. Chen Xiangmiao, director of the South China Sea History and Culture Research Institute at the National Institute for South China Sea Studies, argues that China has officially published geographic names for the region three times — in the 1930s, 1940s and 1980s — following a logic of "discover first, claim, then name," and implementing administrative jurisdiction through official designation. "So-called renaming changes only others' maps, not our history," he says. Zhang Zhengsheng is blunter still: revitalizing these place-names, he argues, is significant for safeguarding maritime rights and demonstrates that the Chinese people first discovered, named, developed and administered the islands.

## What the Names Carry

It is worth being precise about what this article is and is not. It is a cultural feature — memoir and essay more than reportage — restating a position the Chinese state holds as settled historical fact. The claims about "effective administration" are asserted as established state position, not adjudicated legal findings; the 2016 arbitration ruling in Manila, which China rejects, went the other way. The article does not name the countries it accuses of "illegally renaming" reefs, using euphemism instead. Its evidentiary weight rests on genuine, well-documented facts — the 1946 warship recovery, the 1983 standard names, the *Genglubu*'s heritage status — woven into a rhetorical frame that treats naming as proof of title.

The piece is also unmistakably of its moment. It is illustrated with 2024 photographs of Chinese Coast Guard and naval vessels on patrol at Ren'ai Jiao (Second Thomas Shoal), the site of a persistent China-Philippines standoff over a grounded vessel. Its publication comes amid continued friction over a South China Sea code of conduct.

Zhao ends on the water, aboard the *Sansha 2* bound for Qinglan Harbour after a month at sea, standing at the stern and hearing names in the wind — "like a roll call, like recognizing kin." Whether one reads the feature as elegy, advocacy, or both, its closing note is the one the author seems most to want remembered: "The tide keeps faith for a thousand years; the South China Sea, home and nation." That sentence, he insists, was not written by him. It was written by the sea.
