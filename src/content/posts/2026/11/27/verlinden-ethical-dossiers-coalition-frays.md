# Verlinden Readies Ethical Dossiers as Coalition Frays

Belgian Justice Minister Annelies Verlinden (CD&V) told the Chamber of Representatives on Thursday that draft legislative texts on three sensitive ethical dossiers — extending the abortion time limit, regulating surrogacy, and permitting euthanasia for people with dementia — are ready for the government to discuss. But the announcement lands in the middle of a paralysing budget crisis, and it is far from clear whether Prime Minister Bart De Wever's coalition can turn paper into law.

The texts were promised by a self-imposed 1 December deadline set by the inner cabinet in June, as [Het Laatste Nieuws reported](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/minister-verlinden-heeft-ontwerpteksten-klaar-over-abortustermijn-euthanasie-en-draagmoederschap~a3f67ea7/). Verlinden said she had passed word to De Wever earlier on Thursday. She attached a significant caveat: a series of points remain on which there is still no political consensus inside the government. "It is now up to the government to make the further political choices," she said.

## A compromise everyone resents

The most concrete of the three files is abortion. The coalition agreement settled on extending the legal limit from 12 to 14 weeks — a compromise championed by Verlinden's Christian Democrats. Most other parties and experts favour 18 weeks, and opposition bills to that effect were kept off the table because they lacked sufficient support.

Groen MP Meyrem Almaci did not disguise her view of the 14-week deal, calling it "a horse-trading" in the Chamber. The compromise reflects the arithmetic of government formation rather than any ethical consensus — and it has left both sides of the argument dissatisfied.

## The package problem

Beneath the procedural language lies a deeper dispute over tactics that has divided the coalition for months. Vooruit, the socialist partner, insisted in June that all three dossiers be treated as a single package tied to a firm deadline, wary that CD&V would deliver only the least contentious compromise — the 14-week abortion limit — while letting euthanasia and surrogacy stall.

As [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/06/17/abortus-discussie-regering/) at the time, CD&V denied any formal linkage was ever agreed and accused Vooruit of using the issue to threaten the government's survival. From CD&V's vantage point, coupling the files makes the whole agenda hostage to its hardest items: euthanasia for people with dementia, in particular, is legally delicate and could take years to get right.

For Vooruit the coupling is a guarantee; for CD&V it is a trap. Neither side has resolved the question, and both now read the same June decision very differently.

## Opposition cries foul

Opposition MPs were unconvinced that "ready" means ready. DéFI's François De Smet put the objection sharply: "You come and tell us the texts are ready, but they aren't there, and you tell us there's an agreement except on the points where consensus is still needed. So there is no agreement yet."

With the exception of Vlaams Belang, the opposition pressed Verlinden on whether the 1 December deadline would genuinely be met, and several members demanded that parliament be allowed a free vote on ethical matters. PVDA's Sofie Merckx declared it "time for an alternative majority," while Anders MP Katja Gabriëls framed free voting as a democratic principle. Separate opposition bills on extending the abortion term are due before the Chamber's Justice Committee on 9 December.

## The context behind the files

The three dossiers are classic Belgian "ethical questions" — issues of conscience that cut across the left-right divide. Belgium legalised euthanasia in 2002 but the law does not clearly permit it for people with advanced dementia who are no longer competent, even where they signed an advance directive; ethicists argue this can force people to end their lives prematurely while still competent. On surrogacy there is still no comprehensive framework — roughly 150 children are estimated to have been born through the practice in Belgium over the past 30 years, with reform efforts focused on an altruistic model that protects the surrogate and involves judicial oversight, as [deMens.nu detailed](https://demens.nu/nieuws/euthanasie-abortus-en-draagmoederschap-waar-staan-we-vandaag/).

## A government in crisis

None of this can be separated from the wider collapse in the coalition's fortunes. The De Wever government — an "Arizona" coalition of N-VA, Vooruit, CD&V, MR and Les Engagés — has been consumed since autumn by a budget standoff. On 2 November De Wever offered his resignation to the King after another night of failed talks, and the deadline has kept slipping as [VRT NWS chronicled](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/17/begroting-tijdlijn-de-wever-koning-bouchez-vooruit/). Vooruit remains the last coalition party to reject the budget plans, and De Wever has floated a "Christmas deal or elections" ultimatum.

That backdrop makes the ethical timetable look optimistic at best. A government fighting for its survival has little appetite for the most divisive social legislation on the books, and if the coalition falls, all three files could freeze indefinitely.

## What to watch

The 9 December Justice Committee meeting is the next flashpoint: if Vooruit MPs were to vote with the opposition for an 18-week limit, it could either force the government's hand or hasten its collapse. The deeper question is whether Verlinden's texts are "ready" only in form or also in substance — and whether a coalition on the brink can make the political choices she has now handed to it.
