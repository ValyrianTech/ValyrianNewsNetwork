# Young Republicans Tell Their Party: 'Grow Up'

FORT WORTH, Texas — Three weeks after midterm elections that [devastated the Republican Party](https://apnews.com/article/midterm-election-house-senate-democrats-republicans-trump-f126e12e05b579a327b98481c0ae2226), several dozen of its younger members gathered at a Texas brewery to sort through the wreckage. Their message to party leaders was blunt and near-unanimous: grow up.

The Monday meeting, organized by the Tarrant County Young Republicans, put a grassroots face on a national reckoning inside the GOP. The chapter's last leader resigned shortly before the midterms because she could not bring herself to vote for some of her party's candidates. Then Tarrant County — once a conservative stronghold — flipped blue.

"Everyone has someone to blame, and that finger is never pointed at ourself," said Derrick Wilson, who heads the Texas Young Republican Federation, the coalition that includes the Tarrant County chapter. "So we're going to have to get past that and figure out, what did we do wrong?"

## A Stark Reckoning After a Blue Wave

The stakes are difficult to overstate. Democrats captured control of both chambers of Congress in a decisive rebuke of President Donald Trump and his party, and in Texas they won the U.S. Senate race and the governor's office — [their first statewide victories](https://apnews.com/article/texas-democrats-republicans-talarico-hinojosa-6e285484736b5116629a3b6e37e1dda5) in more than three decades.

The reasons for the drubbing were visible in the room. Some attendees said they were bored with "owning the libs" and put off by meme-driven political messaging they saw as flippant in the face of real problems. Many felt Trump did not follow through on his promises. And others offered their leaders the same advice: take themselves seriously, be humble.

The event opened with someone Wilson introduced as a "survivor" of the midterms. State Appeals Court Judge Brian Walker, a Republican who won reelection, delivered a withering assessment of how the party had stumbled down the wrong path with Trump-style belligerent politics led by the "loudest" and "craziest" voices.

## Affordability at the Center of the Anger

Perhaps the sharpest frustration was economic. Robert Coe, a 29-year-old in a black cowboy hat, voted for Trump three times but now gives the Republican Party a 4 out of 10, citing little progress on affordability.

"Me and millions of other Americans are just tired of that, and we're not seeing any viable solution to it," Coe said. "It's almost as if the people in power don't even want to try and solve it."

Coe said he wanted party leaders to "grow up" and has been put off by "the high school level name-calling, the infighting on an almost childish level." Citing Trump's screeds against talk show hosts, he added: "There is no decency."

Wilson, the state federation chair, tied the party's troubles directly to the cost-of-living squeeze facing younger voters. "Young people wanted to buy homes and start families," he said, "then they look on the news and they see Trump say, 'I don't want housing prices to go down, I want them to go up.'"

## A Clash Over Tone — and Direction

Andrew Gochis, a 29-year-old coach and teacher who has worked in politics, voted for a mix of candidates rather than straight-ticket Republicans. He said the party had conflated being respectful with being weak. Republicans have to "do better," he argued, at standing on their principles — on deportations or opposition to abortion — while still treating immigrants and women who get abortions as "a human being that has value."

Not everyone agreed. Stephen Nesbitt, a 26-year-old former U.S. Marine, was a pointed outlier. "The Republican Party needs to get meaner," he said. "We need to get stricter, we need to triple down." He listed non-Christians, immigrants and communists as the "enemy" — a view that meshes with some who believe the Make America Great Again movement did not go far enough.

Wilson wants something different. He is pushing the party to "return to its roots" of "fiscal and social conservatism" and says the organization is now in "listening mode" to be better prepared ahead of 2028 — a strategy he felt was missed before the midterms.

Trevor Carr, 25, attended for the first time because he saw the midterms as a repudiation of the party's old guard and wanted a role in its future. He did not vote in the midterms because he doubted Trump would fulfill his promises. He wants a party less focused on loyalty to one man and more committed to an ideologically sound platform.

While Carr said Trump's approach was once useful for shaking up the system a decade ago, "it's served its purpose, and it's time to move on to be a serious political movement."

## A Question of Identity, Not Just Strategy

The Fort Worth gathering captures a party wrestling with questions deeper than any single election result. It reflects long-running tensions between the Reagan-era "three-legged stool" of fiscal, social and defense conservatism and Trump's personality-driven, grievance-based politics. National polling pointed to the headwinds: most voters saw Trump as more focused on his own fortune than their needs, and saw him elevate flawed, loyalty-first candidates, [according to AP VoteCast and the AP Voter Poll](https://apnews.com/article/trump-2026-midterms-voters-young-voters-hispanic-voters-democrats-congress-ba1689d8402415d26ad32f4fa80ab2f9).

For younger Republicans, the priorities are clear: affordability, authenticity and tone. The meeting was not a scientific sample of young Republicans nationwide, and its voices ranged from calls for a return to principle to demands that the party get tougher. But the gravitational pull was unmistakable — away from performance and toward substance.

## What to Watch

Wilson's "listening mode" framing signals how the party intends to regroup before the 2028 presidential cycle. The central question is whether the GOP can translate grassroots frustration into a coherent message that speaks to housing, prices and family life — and whether it can do so while managing the faction that wants the party to get meaner, not grow up.

For now, the young Republicans at the brewery have done what their leaders have not: named the problem out loud, and pointed the finger inward first.
