# COP31 Collapses as EU Rejects Draft Omitting Fossil Fuels

The COP31 UN climate summit in the Turkish resort city of Antalya ended without an agreed final decision after the European Union and a coalition of countries rejected a draft text that failed to explicitly reference a transition away from fossil fuels. Belgium was among roughly twenty countries that, in the summit's final days, pushed to reinstate language phasing out oil and gas - a demand that ultimately went unmet as the talks collapsed.

The failure makes COP31 only the third climate conference in history to close without an agreement, after The Hague in 2000 and Copenhagen in 2009, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/liveblog/eu-verwerpt-akkoord-dat-voorligt-we-kunnen-geen-beslissing-ste~1794235749946/). It was also the longest COP ever held, running more than 30 hours past its scheduled close.

## A battle over a single phrase

The deadlock centred on whether the final text would name fossil fuels as the main driver of climate change. Three years earlier in Dubai, parties had agreed to "transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems" - a commitment that the 2026 draft, produced by the Turkish presidency, reduced to an indirect cross-reference rather than an explicit restatement.

As [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/cop31-a-antalya-la-belgique-et-une-vingtaine-de-pays-haussent-le-ton-pour-reinscrire-la-sortie-du-petrole-et-du-gaz-dans-le-projet-d-accord-11818457) reported, Belgium joined about twenty like-minded nations determined to put the phase-out back at the heart of the negotiations, announcing they would - or already had - drawn up a roadmap to turn the pledge into concrete action. A second group, including France, Panama and the Solomon Islands, issued a public letter demanding the final decision mention a "clear transition away from fossil fuels" and "clean, immediate electrification."

Belgium's federal climate minister, Jean-Luc Crucke, refused to treat the fight as lost while the text could still change. "The final text is not yet approved. It changes hour by hour and it remains a demand of Europe... it goes well beyond Europe. So we will fight to the end," he said, according to RTBF.

The summit's focus on electrification became a flashpoint of its own. The draft set a goal of raising electricity's share of total final energy consumption to 35 percent worldwide by 2035 - the so-called "35x35" target. But critics argued it did not specify how that electricity would be generated, making it an insufficient answer without a parallel commitment to move away from fossil fuels.

## EU delivers the decisive no

The turning point came on the summit's final day, when the EU formally rejected the proposed "Antalya Pact." Speaking for the bloc, Irish climate minister Darragh O'Brien said the EU was "deeply disappointed" and would not back a text that failed to follow the science or close the gap between pledges and the action needed to bring 1.5C back within reach.

"We will not support an agreement that does not even mention fossil fuels, the main cause of climate change. We will not accept a step back in time. And we will not accept a climate conference that greenwashes fossil fuels," O'Brien said, as VRT reported.

After the EU's objection, Panama, Vanuatu, the Marshall Islands, Norway and Belize followed. COP president Murat Kurum then confirmed that no consensus could be found and that the decision would not be adopted. Other, largely technical decisions were still finalised, but the central cover decision failed.

## Petro-states held the line

Saudi Arabia and Russia led opposition to any direct reference to fossil fuels. Russia argued that supply-side measures risked "energy poverty" and price shocks, warning that the phase-down was moving too fast, while Saudi Arabia positioned itself as a voice for developing countries and defended carbon capture technology against EU criticism.

Developing nations, meanwhile, pressed for clarity on climate finance, with adaptation funding demands - including a proposed $120 billion by 2035 - left unresolved in the final text. Russia openly accused the EU of failing to commit to additional finance for poorer countries.

UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said he was "deeply disappointed," warning that "we cannot solve the fire while pouring oil on it," and that those who profit from the planet's destruction "must never dictate the terms of the fight to save it."

## A summit that hit the wall

The collapse drew sharp recriminations and renewed questions about the COP process itself. Belgian climate scientist and former IPCC vice-chair Jean-Pascal van Ypersele called the failure "symbolic of the current geopolitical situation," in which the United States stands out by its absence, China stays in the background, and the EU talks ambitiously but does not always act consistently.

Still, van Ypersele saw possible value in an "electroshock" to relaunch the process. "I don't think there's really an alternative," he said, arguing the COP framework must continue even in difficult times.

UNFCCC Executive Secretary Simon Stiell acknowledged the process "fell short" but stressed that without the Paris Agreement, global warming would be heading toward 3.5-4C rather than roughly 2.6C. Environmental groups were blunter. Greenpeace's delegation head, Jasper Inventor, called the outcome "a gift to the fossil sector," even as the organisation claimed it had helped block "a deal tailor-made for the fossil fuel industry."

Questions also mounted over the summit's unusual dual-presidency arrangement, under which Turkiye held the COP presidency while Australia led the formal negotiations. Critics noted that both hosts have significant fossil-fuel interests - Australia is the world's second-largest coal exporter, and Turkiye plans to triple oil output to one million barrels a day by 2030.

## What comes next

The failure of the cover decision also sank the mitigation work programme and other linked items, leaving their futures unclear. Crucke argued the COP system cannot remain unchanged: "The COP must continue... but a system where a few countries can do everything to block an agreement cannot last."

Attention now turns to COP32, to be hosted by Ethiopia in Addis Ababa in 2027, where negotiators will face the same fundamental question that stymied Antalya: whether the world's climate process can deliver a text that names - and confronts - the fossil fuels driving the crisis.
