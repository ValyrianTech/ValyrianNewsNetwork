# Chinese Brain Implant Lets ALS Patient Speak

A woman with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) has become the first patient to communicate through a new brain-computer interface (BCI) designed specifically for Mandarin, Chinese researchers announced this week. By simply thinking of the words she wanted to express, the patient — identified only as "Ms. Li" — generated on-screen text and synthesized speech in her own cloned voice, according to [Xinhua News Agency](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261127/bd0e1493aac54e6594278afc41336ab9/c.html).

The surgery was performed in September at Huashan Hospital, affiliated with Fudan University in Shanghai, as part of China's first clinical trial of a wireless, implantable, Chinese-language BCI. The results were presented at a group press briefing on November 26. "I am honored to be the first user of this new technology," Ms. Li said through the system. "Today I am no longer afraid of the disease."

## Why the Chinese language posed a unique challenge

Most advanced speech-decoding BCIs have been developed for English. According to Lu Junfeng, deputy director of Fudan University's Institute of Modern Languages and an associate chief physician at Huashan Hospital, English distinguishes word meaning primarily through combinations of phonemes. Mandarin adds another layer: it is a tonal language in which pitch directly carries meaning — a hurdle English-only systems never face.

To address this, the Fudan-led team developed what it calls a "dual-target" BCI system. The device relies on two sets of ultra-flexible electrode implants placed in two key brain regions: one collects neural signals tied to phoneme-related articulation, the other gathers signals tied to tone-related articulation. A dual-stream decoding strategy then fuses the two streams of information. The system uses a 512-channel minimally invasive implant, according to [IT之家 (IT Home)](https://www.ithome.com/1/033/101.htm).

## Measured performance

After roughly two months of adaptation and training, the reported decoding metrics are striking. In a speaking state, the system achieved real-time Chinese character decoding accuracy above 95 percent at about 80 characters per minute. In a silent-thought state — where the patient merely imagines the words — accuracy exceeded 90 percent at roughly 110 characters per minute, IT之家 reported. The output was also passed through voice cloning to restore Ms. Li's own voice.

Those numbers compare favorably with existing assistive communication methods. Scalp electroencephalography (EEG) and eye-tracking systems rely on character-by-character typing and typically manage fewer than 40 characters per minute. An invasive BCI that directly decodes speech-motor cortex signals, the researchers suggest, may eventually approach the speed of natural human speech.

## A patient whose mind stays clear

ALS progressively destroys the motor neurons that control movement, speech and eventually breathing. As Lu noted, there is no cure, and the disease steadily strips away a patient's ability to move and speak — "but the patient's mind remains clear, and their perception remains sharp." That locked-in trajectory makes restoring communication one of the most compelling clinical applications for BCI technology.

"The clinical trial has preliminarily verified the safety and efficacy of the technology," said Mao Ying, president of Huashan Hospital. He added that the research offers not only a new communication possibility for patients with articulation disorders but also "a new path for building a Chinese-oriented BCI technology and knowledge system."

## An interdisciplinary effort

The project brought together linguistics, neuroscience, clinical medicine, and engineering. Zhang Menghan, director of the Institute of Modern Languages and project lead of the National Science and Technology Major Project on Brain Science and Brain-Like Intelligence, described the collaboration model: linguistics clarified the core challenges of Chinese expression and provided corpus design; brain science supplied functional localization and signal-parsing foundations; clinical medicine handled implantation and trials; and engineering and artificial intelligence managed device development, signal processing, and speech output.

The work drew on contributions from the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Center for Excellence in Brain Science and Intelligence Technology, the Chinese BCI startup Step Medical, and ShanghaiTech University, and was supported by the "Brain Science and Brain-Like Intelligence Research" national project and the Shanghai Key Laboratory of Brain-Computer Interface Clinical Trials and Translation.

## Analysis: a domestic push in a global race

The milestone is significant on two fronts. Scientifically, it demonstrates that a language as structurally different from English as Mandarin can be decoded from neural signals — a result that broadens the potential reach of BCI technology to the world's most-spoken first language. Strategically, it underscores China's ambition to build a self-sufficient BCI ecosystem. Brain-computer interfaces were listed as a "future industry" in China's 2026 Government Work Report, and regulators have moved to shape standards for the sector, developments that position domestic players against Western pioneers such as Neuralink.

That competitive framing is not incidental. Chinese industry partners like Step Medical have been advancing ultra-flexible electrode technology and expanding invasive BCI trials, part of a broader effort to localize a technology with profound medical and strategic value. The Fudan achievement sits at the intersection of fundamental linguistics research and clinical implementation — a combination that may prove as important as any single technical benchmark.

The caveats remain real, however. The results come from a single patient over roughly two months, and "preliminary" verification of safety and efficacy is a starting point, not a conclusion. Long-term durability, the reliability of wireless transmission, and the device's performance across a wider and more diverse patient population all require further study.

## What's next

The team says it will work to improve everyday interaction, streamline the device's portability and operation, and broaden clinical validation. It also plans to extend the technology beyond ALS to people with language impairments caused by stroke and other conditions — a population Mao said could number in the millions across China. The researchers estimate the technology could reach more patients within two to three years.

For Ms. Li, the advance has already changed her outlook. "I have rekindled hope for life," she said through the system, "and I sincerely hope this research matures soon to help more patients in need."
