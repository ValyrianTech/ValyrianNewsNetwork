# COP31 Ends Without a Deal on Key Political Decisions

The COP31 UN climate summit closed in Antalya, Turkey, on the evening of 22 November without adopting its central political text, the so-called "Antalya Pact," after two weeks of talks and a two-day overrun made it the longest climate conference on record. According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20261123/dd34253be20e45e793580b9e6ab3a67b/c.html), negotiators did reach a series of decisions on adaptation, mitigation, finance and a just transition, keeping the multilateral process moving — but failed to bridge deep divisions on the summit's core political signal.

The flashpoint was fossil fuels. The draft cover decision declined to reaffirm the COP28 commitment in Dubai to "transition away from fossil fuels," which more than 130 countries had demanded, and instead leaned on contested provisions on carbon capture and storage (CCS). The result, as [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/22/cop31-climate-talks-set-to-end-in-deep-division-as-key-deal-rejected) reported, was a dramatic final plenary in which the EU, the UK, small island states and other vulnerable developing nations refused to endorse the text, while others — including Saudi Arabia, the Africa group, China and Pakistan — declined to reject the wider package.

## An Unprecedented Dual Presidency

COP31 opened on 9 November under an unusual "dual presidency": Turkey served as official host, with Environment Minister Murat Kurum as COP President, while Australia led the negotiations through co-president and climate minister Chris Bowen. The arrangement followed a contest between the two countries over the hosting role that was resolved through compromise, and it shaped a summit marked by long deadlocks and competing centre of gravity.

When the gavel came down, Kurum acknowledged the impasse directly. "It is clear that there are parties in this hall who oppose this text," he said. "We recognize that there is no consensus on this document. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted." He insisted the presidencies had "not imposed this text on anyone" and had not issued a "take it or leave it" ultimatum.

## The Fossil-Fuel Fault Line

At the heart of the collapse lay a dispute over the language of energy transition. COP28 in Dubai produced the first explicit call to move away from fossil fuels; reaffirming it in Antalya would, in the view of its supporters, have confirmed the direction of travel for investors, banks and manufacturers. Speaking for the EU, Ireland's environment minister Darragh O'Brien rejected the draft in terms that drew applause from the hall, saying it "failed to meet the moment" and that the bloc would not endorse a decision that "does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change" or that "greenwashes fossil fuels." Panama, the Marshall Islands and Norway raised similar objections.

Saudi Arabia defended the inclusion of CCS, while the Africa group, China and Pakistan did not block the broader package despite reservations. Pakistan told the conference that "climate action is not a menu from which we can select only what suits us," and Ghana said Africa "has always chosen to build multilateralism rather than walk away from it."

Australia's Bowen defended his stewardship of the talks — "Australia left nothing on the field" — but conceded that the red lines the presidencies tried to reconcile were "unbridgeable."

## A Target Endorsed, Then Blocked

Analysis from the Turkish think tank [YDAM](https://ydam.org.tr/the-future-was-put-on-hold-at-cop31-in-antalya) points to the fate of Turkey's signature "35 by 35" initiative — a proposal to raise electricity's share of global final energy consumption to 35 percent by 2035 — as a telling illustration of how the summit unravelled. The European Commission had put its endorsement of the target on record on 17 November, yet the EU ultimately joined those blocking the wider package into which the target was folded, because Brussels wanted electrification tied to a renewed commitment to phase out fossil fuels. In the EU's judgement, endorsing a weaker text carried a greater political cost than letting the summit close without a deal.

The final failure also exposed a financial rift. Developing countries questioned how they were expected to pay for the transition being asked of them, and pledges to boost adaptation funding lacked a credible agreement on burden-sharing. African countries objected to announcements of large future increases when the baseline against which they would be measured remained unclear.

## Disappointment and a Call for Reform

UN Secretary-General António Guterres voiced deep frustration. "I am deeply disappointed that COP31 has ended without consensus on an overarching outcome," he said, adding that "there may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science." UNFCCC Executive Secretary Simon Stiell conceded the outcome "certainly fell short today" but stressed that a significant number of agenda items had passed with "real progress and concrete results."

China, which had worked to make the summit a success, was also "deeply disappointed," according to the head of its delegation, Vice Minister of Ecology and Environment Li Gao. He argued that countries should not lose confidence in multilateralism, warning that "mutual recrimination would not build trust," and said that "global climate governance has never been a smooth path."

The breakdown has intensified calls to rethink how UN climate summits make decisions. The failure at COP31 to reach a keystone agreement "has exposed the need to reform how the UN's climate summits make decisions," [The Guardian reported](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/23/failure-cop31-calls-urgent-reform-un-climate-summits), with former US vice-president Al Gore saying it had been "clear for years that the COP process is in need of significant reform." Because decisions can only be made by consensus, a small number of objecting states can block a text backed by the majority; Panama even circulated an open letter calling for a path toward simple-majority voting.

## Shifting Coalitions and What Comes Next

The divisions in Antalya did not fall along a simple "West versus the rest" line. China was prepared to accept the texts despite reservations, while several island states joined the EU in opposing them — a reminder that coalitions are likely to reconfigure issue-by-issue in future talks. Critics also pointed to wealthy nations' own records. Mohamed Adow of Power Shift Africa, who called it "the worst COP ever," said Europe "cannot wash its hands of the wreckage it helped create," accusing governments of demanding fossil-fuel roadmaps from others while expanding fossil fuels themselves. Bill Hare of Climate Analytics read the standoff differently, calling it "a real line in the sand moment" in which countries were "standing up to fossil fuel interests and saying no."

For the host, the summit brought mixed results. Turkey signed agreements worth $5.1 billion with four international development banks and two memoranda of understanding with China — one on renewable energy envisaging roughly $15 billion in investment, the other on environmental and climate cooperation — even as the diplomatic prize of an overarching pact slipped away.

The stakes extend beyond the negotiating hall. Extreme weather in 2026 — heatwaves, the Nepal glacier collapse, and droughts and floods worsened by El Niño — claimed tens of thousands of lives, sharpening criticism of the slow pace of diplomacy. With COP32 scheduled for Ethiopia in 2027, the central question is whether governments can make the consensus system decide faster without silencing the most vulnerable states it was designed to protect — or whether smaller "coalitions of the willing" will increasingly press ahead outside the UN process. In Antalya, the science was never in doubt; the politics were.
