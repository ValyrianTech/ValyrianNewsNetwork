# China's 400-Year-Old Min Opera Courts a Younger Stage

Min Opera, a traditional Chinese opera form from Fujian Province with more than 400 years of history, is being retooled for younger audiences through livestreaming, immersive study courses and open-air performances, according to a [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/culture/20261127/fe43e031956f49a1bb652c42b91c89ca/c.html) photo feature published on November 27. The piece profiles a provincial push to keep the heritage art form — inscribed on China's first national intangible cultural heritage list in 2006 — alive and relevant.

## What the story shows

Also known as Fuzhou Opera, Min Opera is rooted in the Min capital's cultural traditions and carries a distinctly local flavour. In recent years, the report notes, opera troupes across Fujian have experimented with new ways of reaching young people.

Those efforts take several forms. Troupes have built immersive Min Opera study programmes that combine performances, body-movement instruction and intangible cultural heritage handicraft activities. They have also leaned on "Weekend Opera Encounter" (周末戏相逢), a Fujian cultural-benefit brand that brings excerpt operas, master-guided appreciation and interactive sessions into historic buildings, streets and parks — deliberately breaking down the walls of the theatre so that residents, especially the young, can encounter the art during their leisure time.

The photo sequence tracks a particularly digital turn. On November 23, young performer Yang Jiamin of the Fuzhou Min Opera Art Inheritance and Development Centre livestreamed makeup and costume techniques and interacted with online fans ahead of a performance at the Fuzhou Min Opera Theatre, where excerpts of *"The Strange Tale of the Yang Family"* were also streamed. Earlier, on November 19, children on their autumn holiday took part in a holographic opera study programme, "Rong Melody Illusion · Opera Reflecting Flowing Light," launched by the Fujian Provincial Experimental Min Opera Theatre and others.

## Voices of transmission

Zhou Hong, a nationally recognised Min Opera inheritor and president of the Fujian Provincial Experimental Min Opera Theatre, framed the effort as a generational mission.

> "We must use opera to pass on [the tradition] to people, to guide people, and to retain people — to promote the passing of the Min Opera torch," she said. "Young performers should shoulder the mission of cultural inheritance."

The article's editorial framing pairs tradition with innovation: "Only by holding to the orthodox can one innovate" (守正方能出新), it suggests, describing how performers deepen their command of classic repertoire while exploring youth-oriented expression through short video, livestreaming, campus shows and site-specific street performances.

## Analysis: heritage, policy and the digital pivot

The feature is a textbook example of China's state-led intangible cultural heritage system at work. Since the 2006 designation, Min Opera — like Peking Opera, Kunqu and Yue Opera — has been supported through officially recognised "representative inheritors" tasked with transmission, alongside campaigns such as "heritage into campuses" and youth study programmes. The holographic study course points to a broader official willingness to marry traditional arts with immersive technology to attract younger audiences.

What the report captures well is the *reach* strategy: meeting young people on their screens, in their parks, and during their holidays. Livestreaming makeup tutorials, in particular, is a low-barrier entry point — a performer's craft becomes shareable content that can draw first-time viewers toward the theatre itself.

## What to watch

The Xinhua piece is celebratory and uncritical, and it leaves several natural questions open — not because they are unimportant, but because a single official source does not address them. Among them: whether livestream audiences actually convert into ticket-buying theatregoers; the demographic makeup of real audiences; and how institutions balance the tension between "preserving the orthodox" and "innovating" without diluting the form.

Those questions matter for Min Opera's long-term health, and they are the follow-up angles that will determine whether a 400-year-old tradition truly finds a durable new stage — or just a larger digital shop window.
