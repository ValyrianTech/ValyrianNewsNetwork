# LA Schools Block Google AI as Tech Giant Threatens Search

Los Angeles school officials have quietly disabled Google's AI-generated search summaries on school-issued student devices, making the nation's second-largest district the biggest to push back against the tech giant's insistence that its search product and artificial intelligence cannot be separated. The move directly answers a Google policy that schools which opt out of AI will lose access to Google search entirely.

The Los Angeles Unified School District, which enrolls roughly 500,000 students, has made no formal announcement of the block, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/google-artificial-intelligence-schools-search-engine-lausd-762a613b11bd316384430112cc223ed1). The change caught parents and students by surprise and marks the sharpest escalation yet in a national fight over AI in classrooms, student safety, and the leverage of a company whose Chromebooks and learning tools are deeply embedded in American education.

## A Quiet Block, A Loud Message

The block removed "AI Overview" — the summarized answers that appear atop many Google search results — from student devices. LAUSD said it "temporarily suspended" the feature to ensure student experiences are "age-appropriate and safe," and said Google "did not play a role in these recent improvements." The district appears to have achieved the block using an unnamed third-party filter, the method of which it has declined to detail.

The timing was pointed. Parent activists weighed in at a meeting of the Los Angeles school board's AI committee on Friday, Nov. 13. By the following Monday, AI Overview was gone.

"It was the talk of the playground," said Bridie Lee, a leader of Schools Beyond Screens LA, a parent group that has pushed for screen-time limits and tech oversight. "We're thrilled LAUSD did this. AI is seeping into everything. It's dangerous. It's hurting learning. And it's a corporate overreach into public schools. It's Big Tech attempting to strongarm tech policy without anyone's consent."

## The Ultimatum at the Center of the Fight

The standoff traces to a November policy shift by Google. In early November, the Mountain View, California-based company said in a [blog post](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-learning-era-updates-2026/) that it would turn off Gemini, Gemini Notebook, and search tools by default for K-12 schools, letting administrators opt in tool by tool. Google framed the change as giving schools more control over AI in their classrooms.

But there is a catch: because search is now integrated with AI, schools that opt out of AI lose Google search altogether. Chris Phillips, Google's vice president and general manager of education, told [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/03/us/google-search-schools-restrict-ai.html) that "search and AI are deeply integrated," and that students at schools banning AI "will not have access to search, and they'll see a message from their school that indicates it's not available."

That framing has drawn fury from critics who see an ultimatum dressed up as choice. Former Federal Trade Commission chair Lina Khan accused Google of "using its search monopoly to coerce schools into adopting AI," describing the move as evidence of "the danger of allowing an unregulated monopolist to control an essential service." LAUSD school board member Kelly Gonez called it "a very cynical effort to really hold school districts hostage," noting the timing followed the nation's two largest districts taking a strong stand to protect students from AI.

## Google's Deep Roots in the Classroom

Google's leverage is not accidental. Beyond low-cost Chromebooks — the most widely used student laptops in the country — Google's suite of education apps, including Google Docs, Classroom, and Gmail, has become integral to classwork, homework, and communication among students and teachers. Google says 150 million students and teachers worldwide use Google Classroom, according to AP.

The dispute follows months of escalating tension. In August 2026, Google turned on its Gemini chatbot by default for many K-12 students using Google Classroom, a feature previously limited to users 18 and older. The backlash was swift. In September, New York City and Los Angeles — the country's two largest districts — announced one-year moratoriums on student use of generative AI on school devices. New York's ban applies to all elementary and middle school students; Los Angeles banned all students in grades K-12 from using AI on school devices.

LAUSD had already disabled Google's separate "AI Mode," a chatbot-style search interaction, at the start of the school year. Educators and staff retain access to both features.

## Pressure From Inside Google

The company's stance has even divided its own workforce. Hundreds of Google employees signed an internal petition urging leadership to let schools turn off AI features "without disrupting their ability to use search as a whole." According to the petition, reported by the [Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/business/story/2026-11-20/google-workers-urge-company-to-let-schools-use-search-without-ai), the decision had made Google "look like we're punishing schools for not using AI."

"Many students, educators and parents have concerns about AI in the classroom, as is shown by many school districts who have placed moratoriums or bans on such technology," the petition said. "It is our job as Googlers to respect the user, and listen to them when they have concerns about our products."

Google has made some concessions. It has pointed to a forthcoming "Guided Learning" tool that walks students step by step through problems rather than handing them quick answers, part of what the company calls support for "productive struggle." But the option many parents and teachers want most — the ability to use Google search without any AI, much as it worked before 2025 — is not on offer.

## Why It Matters

The dispute is a test of whether public institutions can push back against the market power of a company woven into the fabric of American schooling. LAUSD's block is a proof of concept: a large district found a technical workaround to Google's integration of AI and search, apparently without the company's help.

But it may be fragile. Board member Nick Melvoin acknowledged the district's low profile was deliberate, worried that "the more that we share what's possible, maybe Google will find a workaround. Will they create a version that can't be blocked?" He added: "We don't know if this is a fix for a day, a week or indefinitely."

The stakes extend beyond one district. New York City schools Chancellor Kamar H. Samuels has warned that "if Google doesn't reconsider their position, we will explore other search options," and officials in Chicago are also weighing alternatives. Fairfax County Public Schools in Virginia developed plug-ins to strip AI summaries for student searches.

Education researchers argue the outcome matters for how a generation learns. Stephen J. Aguilar, an associate professor at the University of Southern California's Rossier School of Education, called Google's move "reckless," saying there is "no evidence that AI-enabled search is better, and in many instances, there is evidence that it can undermine the necessary friction needed to teach digital literacy and critical thinking." Heather Schwartz, a RAND researcher, pointed to "too much evidence" of "cognitive offloading and eroded performance" when AI replaces a student's first attempt at the work.

## What to Watch

For now, LAUSD has won a round by acting quietly and technically rather than loudly and politically. The question is whether Google patches the workaround, whether other large districts follow Los Angeles' lead, and whether the pressure — from parents, educators, and even its own employees — pushes Google to offer what it has so far refused: a way for schools to keep search while leaving AI at the door.

As LAUSD's chief information officer, Jack Kelanic, put it at the school board meeting, to applause: "We have choices. We can change the default search engine literally overnight." Whether Google gives schools a reason to exercise that choice may define the next phase of the fight.
