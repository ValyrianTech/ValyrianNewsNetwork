# China Bans Startup Ads on Cable and IPTV TVs

Televisions across China can no longer greet viewers with advertisements at boot. On November 27, the National Radio and Television Administration (NRTA) brought two departmental regulations into force that prohibit startup advertisements on cable television and interactive network television (IPTV) and require set-top devices to power on straight to full-screen live broadcast. According to [CCTV News](https://news.cctv.com/2026/11/27/ARTISXmtsmYX031OeXxiZkVE261127.shtml), the rules took effect the same day, formalizing a clean-up of the TV industry's long-criticized fee and interface problems into binding national regulation.

The two instruments are Order No. 17, the Regulations on the Management of Cable Broadcasting Operations Services, and Order No. 18, the Interim Regulations on the Management of Interactive Network Television Operations Services. Both were reviewed and passed at the NRTA's executive meeting on October 23, 2026, and published the same week, giving operators roughly a month to prepare before implementation.

## A "live-first" mandate

At the center of the new regime is what the regulator calls the "live-first principle." Operators must ensure that a television powers on directly to full-screen live broadcast, provide an entry point for channel replay and catch-up viewing, and must not play startup advertisements. Smart televisions are required to support prioritized presentation of live channels and to offer a convenient settings entry so that users can choose at power-on whether to enter live TV or the interactive smart homepage.

That requirement quietly reorders the economics of the TV screen. Startup ads have been a lucrative slot for operators and device makers alike, capturing a captive audience in the seconds between switching on and watching. The NRTA's language leaves little ambiguity: the boot sequence belongs to the viewer, not the advertiser.

## Free zones and clearer pricing

The regulations also take aim at the so-called "matryoshka" (套娃) fee model, in which paid content is buried behind multiple, confusing paywalls. Operators are now required to set up a "free zone" on the homepage that prominently recommends quality free content, to streamline the number of paid channels and columns, and to clearly disclose program fee information. App-zone operations and subscription-payment management must also be standardized.

The rules further demand that operators keep optimizing terminal performance to shorten power-on time and simplify the remote control, including a one-button switch of the live source. The regulator encourages voice remotes, phone-based remotes and artificial intelligence tools to improve the viewing experience.

## Who is covered — and who is not

The scope of the two regulations is deliberately narrow. They cover cable TV and IPTV operations, which together reach roughly 205 million actual cable television households nationwide. They do not extend to Internet TV (OTT) services, meaning the regulations do not directly prohibit startup advertisements embedded in smart TV manufacturers' own operating systems. That carve-out leaves a significant gap: a viewer who boots a smart TV through a manufacturer's own interface may still encounter the very ads the rules bar on broadcast-controlled pipelines.

## Enforcement and penalties

Compliance is not left to goodwill. Operators that fail to ensure live-first presentation, power-on-to-full-screen-live or the absence of startup ads face orders to correct from county-level or higher radio and television authorities, along with warnings or public criticism. In serious cases, the regulator can impose fines ranging from 10,000 to 100,000 yuan.

Whether the penalties bite will depend on how vigorously local authorities enforce them. The fines are modest relative to the advertising revenue at stake, and the rules rely on county-and-above broadcast bureaus to police operators in their jurisdictions.

## What to watch

The immediate effect for consumers should be a simpler, faster and ad-free boot experience on cable and IPTV, with clearer pricing and a dedicated free-content zone — a direct response to years of public complaints about cluttered interfaces and layered fees. For the industry, the regulations curtail a startup-screen advertising model that had become a reliable revenue stream.

The open question is whether the regulator will eventually extend the ban to OTT services and smart TV makers' own operating systems. For now, the November 27 rules draw a firm line around broadcast-controlled pipelines — and leave the connected-TV living room to a separate, still-unresolved debate.
