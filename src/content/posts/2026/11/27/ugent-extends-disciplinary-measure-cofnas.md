# UGent Extends Disciplinary Measure Against Cofnas

Ghent University (UGent) has extended by three months the disciplinary "order measure" (ordemaatregel) imposed on its controversial American postdoctoral researcher Nathan Cofnas, keeping him on remote work and barred from teaching while a formal misconduct investigation continues. The university announced the extension on 26 November, days before the existing measure was due to expire.

"UGent confirms that the order measure regarding the postdoctoral staff member is extended by 3 months," the university said in a brief statement, [according to VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/26/gent-universiteit-ugent-cofnas-rassenrealist-omstreden-onderzoek/). "The disciplinary preliminary investigation is ongoing. In the interest of confidentiality, we are not providing further details."

## A case that has gripped Belgium

The extension marks the latest chapter in one of the most polarising academic-freedom debates in Belgium this year — a saga that has drawn in race science, the death of a Black Cambridge professor, and even US-Belgium diplomatic tensions.

Cofnas, a self-described "race realist" who argues that intelligence differences between racial groups are partly biological, was appointed in early 2026 to UGent's Department of Philosophy and Moral Sciences on a research project about the "birth problem of liberalism." His hiring, announced in March, triggered immediate controversy given his prior statements on intelligence and race — views widely characterised by experts as pseudoscience.

After he left Cambridge's Emmanuel College in 2024 amid racism accusations, roughly 300 UGent staff and students emailed rector Petra De Sutter asking her to reconsider the appointment, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/03/17/filosoof-nathan-cofnas-portret-rassenrealisme-aanstelling-ugent/). The university declined to intervene, stating the hiring followed the rules, and Cofnas began work.

## The Arday affair and the disciplinary turn

The case took a tragic turn in the summer. In late July, Cofnas published a blog post accusing Jason Arday — a Black professor who in 2023 became the youngest Black professor in Cambridge's history — of plagiarism. Arday resigned on 5 August amid the resulting media storm; about a week later he was found dead at his home. His family says he took his own life under the pressure.

Cofnas acknowledged his role in the sequence of events. "I set in motion a series of events that led to a tragic outcome," he told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/08/21/nathan-cofnas-reactie-jason-arday-ugent-cambridge/), while insisting that "this was about DEI [diversity, equity and inclusion] and Cambridge. Arday played only a supporting role."

In the aftermath, UGent opened a disciplinary preliminary investigation. Cofnas was briefly suspended on 20 August, and when that preventive suspension lapsed after the maximum seven-day period, the university imposed the three-month order measure: remote work, no teaching, and no campus presence beyond his appointment.

## Polarised reactions

The measure drew sharp international pushback. Hundreds of academics — figures range from 800 to 931 — signed an open letter calling the sanctions retaliation and defending Cofnas's right to hold controversial views. US Ambassador to Belgium Bill White weighed in on social media, defending Cofnas as a "whistleblower" and warning that US relations with UGent would be reviewed.

Rector De Sutter rejected accusations of political interference. Speaking on VRT NWS's *De Afspraak*, she said some 80 disciplinary complaints had been filed against Cofnas and dismissed critics' claims as "framing." She has argued that academic freedom is essential but not unlimited — a theme she returned to in her opening address at the start of the academic year.

"The freedom to think differently must be cherished," she said, per VRT NWS. "Freedom is essential but not without obligation. It is not absolute or unlimited; it is conditional and ends where it clashes with the rights of others."

The case has also spilled into campus life. An April lecture by Cofnas at a right-conservative student association ended with him being pelted with beer and yoghurt and a fight breaking out. In September, emeritus vice-rector Freddy Mortier was booed at a graduation ceremony after questioning the suspension, prompting the dean to apologise, and De Sutter was jeered by protesters at the start of the new academic year.

## A separate legal track

Beyond the university's internal process, Belgium's Equal Opportunities Centre (Unia) prepared a complaint alleging that Cofnas's statements — which rank people by origin — may violate the country's anti-racism law, VRT NWS reported.

## What's next

The extension keeps Cofnas off campus and out of the classroom while the disciplinary investigation runs its course. Several open questions remain: what specific findings and possible sanctions the inquiry will produce, how Unia's discrimination complaint will proceed, and whether the US diplomatic dimension will escalate.

For UGent, the case is a stress test of how a university balances its duty of care toward the academic community against its commitment to free inquiry. The fact that the measure was extended — rather than allowed to lapse — suggests the institution is not treating the matter as resolved, and that a heavier sanction, up to dismissal, may yet be on the table.
