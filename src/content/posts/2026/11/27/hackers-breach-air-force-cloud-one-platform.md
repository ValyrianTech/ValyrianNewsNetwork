# Hackers Breach Air Force's Flagship Cloud One Platform

Hackers have penetrated the U.S. Air Force's flagship cloud computing platform, Cloud One, in an intrusion that could expose a vast range of sensitive tactical, personnel and logistics data, according to [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/hackers-breach-us-air-force-flagship-cloud-one-computing-platform-2026-11-27/), which first reported the breach on Friday. A U.S. official confirmed the hack to [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/hackers-attack-penetrate-air-forces-computing-platform-rcna611559) but could not provide additional details about the timing or the attack's impact.

The intrusion was discovered within the past month or so during work to upgrade the system, two people familiar with the matter told Reuters, and the discovery has led to disruption across the platform. Investigators have called in U.S. Cyber Command, the military's cyber force, to assess the damage.

## What Cloud One Is — and Why It Matters

Initiated in 2017, Cloud One was designed as a one-stop shop for the Air Force's IT needs, combining the resources of four major cloud providers — Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle and Alphabet — to power scores of applications. Among the systems it supports are personnel and pay databases, logistics and maintenance tools, and two combat-relevant platforms: Cloud-Based Command and Control, which collates air defense radar and other data feeds from across North America, and the Theater Integrated Combat Munitions System (TICMS), which tracks bombs and missiles across more than 200 Air Force locations worldwide.

"A breach here is a big deal," one of the sources told Reuters. Cloud One is among the U.S. military's biggest cloud platforms, according to two of the sources.

One source said the intruders exploited a software vulnerability to reach the system's **control plane**, which the source likened to a "universal remote." In theory, attackers could use that access to check the operational status of military aircraft or track the movement of spare parts — though it remains unclear whether the hackers actually did so in this case. Reuters could not identify the specific vulnerability exploited, the identity of the hackers, the full scope of the intrusion, or whether any data was stolen.

The Air Force declined to discuss the details. "The Department of the Air Force does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities, or operational status of specific networks and systems," a spokesperson said in a statement. "We continuously take prudent measures to protect department information, systems, and mission capabilities. Ensuring the security, resilience, and availability of our most critical war fighting assets remains a top priority."

## A Range of Disruption

The discovery triggered significant operational fallout. According to Reuters, efforts to migrate back-office applications to Cloud One — a longstanding military priority — have been paused. Updates to applications already running on the platform were blocked, leaving Air Force personnel unable to apply fixes just as a new batch of serious software vulnerabilities was disclosed across the technology industry. A fifth source said some users had their administrator rights revoked.

All of the sources spoke on condition of anonymity. SAIC was the prime contractor for Cloud One until 2025, when Leidos took over. SAIC did not respond to a request for comment, and Leidos referred questions to the Air Force.

The breach is a striking reversal for a platform whose leadership once projected confidence. In a 2023 commentary carried by the online publication FedScoop, Cloud One's leadership touted what they called a "100% success rate" in beating back a "staggering" number of attempts to penetrate the system, writing: "There is no room for error when it comes to security."

## One Breach in a Wave

The Cloud One intrusion is the latest in a string of recently disclosed cyber incidents against U.S. federal systems. As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/27/hackers-breach-us-air-force-cloud-computing) reported, the breach comes days after news that a CIA database had been accessed and amid disclosures involving the FBI, the Department of Homeland Security, the Treasury Department, the Pentagon and the Department of Agriculture.

The pattern extends well beyond the Air Force. Hackers breached the FBI's National Crime Information Center, a database used by federal, state and local authorities to share information on warrants and background checks — a hack President Trump publicly acknowledged. Federal officials have also grappled with a Pentagon data breach affecting 2.76 million living people and 294,000 deceased individuals, and a leak of DHS personnel information that the hacktivist group "Department of Peace" claimed responsibility for.

The most recent incident before the Cloud One disclosure concerned the nation's spy agencies. According to [Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/cybersecurity/2026/11/cia-odni-unclassified-intelligence-system-hacked/417438/), the CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence discovered a breach of an unclassified system used to analyze public and commercially available information. A person familiar with the matter said it disrupted an unclassified network but did not affect classified systems and has since been resolved. ODNI told Bloomberg there had been "no disruptions to critical capabilities or classified systems."

That incident was reportedly linked to a tool known as ARTEX — an AI program developed by a Chinese cybersecurity engineer known as "Autumn-27" that automatically finds and breaks into computer systems and has become popular among criminal hackers. It is unclear whether any of the breaches are connected.

## Anxiety Over AI-Powered Intrusions

Reuters framed the Cloud One breach against "heightened anxiety over AI-powered intrusions" — a particularly acute concern for cloud-based networks because AI models can swiftly detect weaknesses across wide surface areas. The ability to map and exploit vulnerabilities at machine speed is changing the economics of cyber offense, making sprawling, interconnected platforms like Cloud One attractive and difficult-to-defend targets.

The fact that the Air Force discovered the breach during an upgrade — rather than through routine monitoring — suggests the intrusion may have persisted undetected for some time, a hallmark of sophisticated campaigns. Access to a control plane, if confirmed, would be especially serious, because it could allow an adversary to observe or even manipulate operational data rather than merely steal static records.

## What to Watch

For now, the central questions remain unresolved: who carried out the attack, whether it was state-sponsored, what specific vulnerability was exploited, whether data was exfiltrated, and what the operational impact on Air Force readiness has been. Attribution in cyber intrusions is notoriously difficult and often takes months.

The broader concern is systemic. With at least seven federal breach disclosures in rapid succession, the Cloud One incident raises pointed questions about the adequacy of U.S. government cyber defenses and whether the string of intrusions reflects shared vulnerabilities, common adversaries, or simply the difficulty of securing an expanding federal cloud footprint. Watch for whether Congress or oversight bodies launch investigations, whether attribution emerges, and how quickly the Air Force restores full functionality to one of its most important computing platforms.
