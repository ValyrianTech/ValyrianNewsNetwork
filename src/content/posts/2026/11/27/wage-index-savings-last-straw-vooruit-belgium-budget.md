# Wage Index Savings Were the Last Straw for Vooruit

Belgium's federal budget negotiations reached a breaking point this week over proposed savings to the country's wage indexation system, with the socialist party Vooruit declaring the measures the "proverbial last straw" that pushed it to set a hard ultimatum to its coalition partners. The dispute, detailed by [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/26/deze-voorstellen-over-de-index-waren-de-druppel-voor-vooruit/), caps months of deadlock over Prime Minister Bart De Wever's search for roughly 10 billion euros in savings to rein in one of the eurozone's largest deficits.

## The numbers behind the break

In De Wever's latest negotiating table, according to VRT NWS, some 1.1 billion euros in savings were to be drawn from the wage index — the mechanism that automatically raises wages in line with inflation. Of that total, 400 million euros was to come from an index reform being negotiated by employers and workers within the Group of Ten, the social partners' consultation body. The remaining 700 million euros had to be found on top of that, through measures the government itself would impose.

It is that second component that Vooruit objects to most. The social-partner track has been under way for months at the government's request, mandated by the coalition agreement, which asks employers and workers to produce advice on reforming both the wage index and the 1996 wage norm law — with a deadline of the end of this year. A reform could loosen both systems at once: sectors in difficulty could see wage costs indexed more slowly, while booming sectors could gain more room to raise pay.

Vooruit says it is willing to accept an index reform agreed by the social partners — the 400 million euro component. Its objection is to the additional 700 million euros of government-imposed measures layered on top, and to the reliability of the 400 million figure itself. Sources close to the Group of Ten described that estimate bluntly: "That is simply guesswork."

## What was on the table

The concrete measures behind the extra 700 million euros, according to several government sources cited by VRT NWS, would include switching civil servants, pensioners and benefit recipients to the index system of the private sector's largest joint committee, PC 200 for white-collar workers. Under that system, the full index increase would occur only once a year, on 1 January.

Today, the 2 percent index increase takes effect every time the health index (the so-called spilindex) is exceeded, three months later. Bundling the increase into a single moment would dampen public spending during inflation spikes, the N-VA's reasoning goes; another governing party argues it would also remove an inequality from the index system.

A second idea would calculate the health index not on the basis of the last four months but on a 12-month average, slowing indexation. Combined with the first proposal, some workers — teachers, for example — would see their wages indexed much later than today, the socialists warn.

Rousseau never used the word "indexsprong" — the politically charged "index jump" — at any point. But calculations circulating within the party conclude the effect of the measures could be comparable. That is something the party says it cannot defend again, particularly after the earlier introduction of the "centenindex," the cents index, which Vooruit already swallowed. As [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/05/29/pensioenhervorming-en-centenindex-blijven-zwaar-op-de-maag-ligge/) at the time, the government pressed ahead with that reform despite a social-partner alternative it had rejected — a decision that unions and even some coalition partners criticised sharply.

## A competing interpretation

The gap between the two coalition partners is not only about money but about what an index reform should mean. N-VA expects a reform built around calculating energy prices over the last 12 months rather than four months within the index — a proposal that sources say was lifted from a social-partner proposal made earlier in the year in a very different context, and which the government had rejected at the time.

Vooruit, by contrast, insists the social-partner reform concerns only a loosening of the index and the wage norm law. That interpretive gap fuels mutual mistrust, and it narrows the government's actual room for manoeuvre considerably.

## The ultimatum

The standoff crystallised on 25 November, when Vooruit chair Conner Rousseau held a press conference setting out two demands to continue negotiating: an extra contribution from the wealthiest citizens, and leaving the wage index alone. As [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/25/vooruit-stelt-ultimatum-bij-begroting-eerst-garanties-over-inde/), Rousseau warned that if the other four coalition parties disagreed, it was their right to seek an "alternative majority" with someone else.

"People are tired of the theatre," Rousseau said. "It can't drag on for six more weeks. If you can't reach an agreement on 10 billion euros in eight months, how will you find 13 or 14 billion euros in four weeks?"

His framing shifted the burden of the government's survival onto his partners. As political journalist Ivan De Vadder put it, "the fall of the government is now being pushed back onto the other four parties." Professor Carl Devos of Ghent University, speaking on VRT NWS's Villa Politica, noted that Vooruit itself seems not to really expect the other parties to agree, since they have refused for months to move on these points.

Rousseau nonetheless pledged that, as long as no alternative majority exists, Vooruit will loyally execute the coalition agreement and continue to support the premier — including on the ongoing Engie energy deal, which the party backs. "Our door is open," he concluded. "But without a millionaires' contribution and without protection of the index by the government, you won't get in."

## Little room, fragile arithmetic

De Wever's margin is now very thin. Vooruit is essentially making everything depend on a possible social-partner agreement on the index; anything beyond that appears taboo. Other parties voice cautious hope that there is still room to negotiate. CD&V points out that much has already been removed from the table on the index at its own request — for instance, the non-indexation of pensioners' holiday pay, which no longer appears in the latest two tables. "Which is also proof that much is being adjusted and can still be adjusted around the index," the party said.

Yet the structural bind remains. Belgium's public debt is close to 110 percent of GDP, placing it alongside Greece, Italy and France among the most indebted EU countries — the pressure that drives De Wever's hunt for savings. The Arizona coalition of N-VA, Vooruit, CD&V, MR and Les Engagés is the only configuration with a working majority, and several parties say they do not want elections. The five are, in effect, condemned to each other.

The government already offered its resignation to King Filip earlier this month; the king declined to accept it and asked De Wever to keep negotiating. The same scenario played out a year earlier, when a budget deal was eventually reached after the king granted extra time. Whether that history repeats itself now depends on whether the wage index can be pried loose from the deadlock — or whether, for Vooruit, it has already proven the one concession too many.

## What to watch

Attention now centres on the Group of Ten and whether a social-partner reform can materialise before the end-of-year deadline — and, crucially, whether its substance matches what each coalition partner thinks it agreed to. Absent that, the question becomes whether the other four parties can credibly threaten an alternative majority that none of them appears to want. For now, the wage index — the mechanism that puts money in Belgian workers' pockets every time prices rise — has become the fault line on which the government's future rests.
