# CCTV Finds Sulfur-Fumigated Angelica Persists Despite Ban

A CCTV investigation has found that sulfur-fumigated Angelica root (baizhi), a common medicinal herb and culinary spice, remains widely sold in China despite an explicit regulatory ban, raising fresh questions about food-safety enforcement along the country's spice supply chain. The report, aired on CCTV's Weekly Quality Report on 1 November 2026, documented treated product openly on sale in Qingdao markets and on e-commerce platforms, and found that packaging claiming to be "sulfur-free" is often unreliable.

## A Banned Additive That Still Sells

Under China's national food additive standard GB 2760-2024, only star anise (八角) among spices may legally be sulfur-fumigated, with a sulfur dioxide (SO2) residue ceiling of 0.15 g/kg. All other spices, including baizhi, are barred from sulfur-based additives. Yet enforcement appears to be faltering. According to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34381423), which summarized the CCTV broadcast, the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) has issued 21 food-sampling bulletins this year, and 19 of them named "baizhi SO2 exceeding the standard"—a cumulative 32 batches.

The practice endures for a simple reason: appearance. Merchants say sulfur-fumigated baizhi is whiter and looks better, which is the primary reason it still finds buyers. Fumigation also helps prevent insects and mold. Some merchants claimed the treated spice makes braised foods (卤味) more fragrant—a justification that CCTV said is unsupported by any scientific research.

## Hidden Stock and Mislabeled Products

The investigation found the loopholes operate both offline and online. In Qingdao's agricultural markets, merchants continued to secretly stock sulfur-fumigated baizhi beneath their counters even after a rectification campaign, according to The Paper. On e-commerce platforms, the problem is one of labeling fraud. CCTV reporters purchased six products marketed as "sulfur-free" and had them tested; two of the six showed SO2 residue—a failure rate of 30%. A baizhi product labeled "premium sulfur-free" (特级无硫) tested at 191 mg/kg, while one advertised as "naturally sun-dried" measured 1,070 mg/kg, dozens of times a normal level.

## Why the Practice Persists

A consumer advisory published in July by China Consumer News and carried by [CNR](https://food.cnr.cn/gstj/20260702/t20260702_527685868.shtml) offers context on the root causes. Wang Bei, a food-science expert at Beijing Technology and Business University, explained that SO2 is a legal additive in most countries and that compliant use leaves minimal residue. Star anise's sulfur treatment, she said, is a climate-driven necessity in humid Guangxi, where traditional drying is slow and prone to mold. Baizhi and other spices have no such industrial rationale—violations are driven purely by appearance and shelf-life considerations, typically by small workshops and repackagers seeking to mask damp or blackened raw material.

The advisory also highlights a dual-standard confusion. The 2025 Chinese Pharmacopoeia permits SO2 up to 0.4 g/kg for mao shanyao as a medicinal material, but a 0.2 g/kg food limit applies once the same item is sold as food—a gap that can trip up producers of dual-use medicinal and culinary ingredients, a category that includes baizhi.

## Health Consequences

CCTV's report warned that sulfur fumigation degrades baizhi's active medicinal components by roughly 2.5 times and that long-term consumption may damage the liver and lungs. High-dose SO2 exposure can cause nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Experts including Wang Bei caution that studies showing organ damage involve occupational or high-dose exposure far above normal dietary intake, but that risk rises with chronic high-dose consumption. Her advice to consumers: avoid unnaturally pure-white baizhi, smell for a sour or sulfur odor, buy pre-packaged products from reputable channels, and soak the spice in 30-40°C water for 30 minutes, changing the water two to three times, to remove more than 80% of residue.

## A Recurring Pattern

The angelica case is not isolated. In March, reports exposed "super-sulfur star anise" still being sold at wholesale in Nanning, Guangxi, fumigated at night and marketed the next morning. In October, a "sulfur osmanthus" scandal was confirmed by official notice, with treated product selling at roughly 100 yuan per jin versus more than 600 yuan for the sulfur-free variety. A July sweep of six SAMR bulletins covering 12,920 batches found 244 non-compliant batches, 30 of which exceeded SO2 limits; baizhi was the most-flagged spice, mostly from leading e-commerce platforms.

## What to Watch

National television exposés in China have historically triggered local crackdowns, product recalls, and e-commerce audits. The open questions now are whether SAMR or Shandong and Qingdao regulators will launch a formal probe—none had been announced as of the broadcast—and whether named e-commerce platforms will be required to delist non-compliant listings or verify "sulfur-free" claims. The deeper challenge is structural: standardized drying, supply-chain traceability, and clearer alignment between medicinal and food standards would do more than periodic crackdowns to close the gap that keeps sulfur-fumigated baizhi on shelves.
