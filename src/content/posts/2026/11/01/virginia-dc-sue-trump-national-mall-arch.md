# Virginia and D.C. Sue to Block Trump's National Mall Arch

The Commonwealth of Virginia and the District of Columbia filed a 45-page lawsuit on Saturday to block construction of President Donald Trump's proposed 250-foot-tall triumphal arch on the National Mall, calling the project "flagrantly unlawful" and warning that building it would inflict "irreparable harm" on both jurisdictions.

The suit, filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia by Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones and D.C. Attorney General Brian Schwalb, is the latest legal challenge to a project whose plans the Trump administration unveiled in October 2025. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/31/nx-s1-6024694/virginia-dc-sue-trump-arch), construction could begin as soon as the following week, giving the filing unusual urgency.

## A Race Against the Groundbreaking

The attorneys general argue that the arch — planned for a patch of grass between Arlington National Cemetery and the Lincoln Memorial, at roughly twice the height of the Lincoln Memorial itself — would snarl traffic between the District and Virginia and impose severe, unrecoverable costs on both jurisdictions during a three-year construction period.

"Both jurisdictions will need to take immediate and costly steps to assess, respond to, and mitigate these harmful effects," the attorneys general wrote. "These costs are severe and unrecoverable—particularly because Defendants have refused to take them into account or adequately consult with either jurisdiction during their hurried and preordained process to approve the Arch and start construction."

As [AP reporting carried by Breitbart](https://www.breitbart.com/news/virginia-and-dc-sue-over-trumps-arch-adding-to-legal-challenges-as-government-wants-work-to-begin/) detailed, the two officials warned that "as soon as Defendants break ground on this project, they will inflict irreparable harm on both the District and Virginia." The filing notes that the site sits at the center of a major traffic artery connecting the two jurisdictions, on an island straddling the Potomac River, and that construction would "produce pollution and stormwater runoff in the Potomac, interfere with emergency services, and pose numerous other challenges."

The lawsuit raises particular concerns about how traffic disruptions could affect emergency responses in a region where joint operations are routine — including the January 2025 midair collision between a military helicopter and an airliner that killed 67 people.

## A Growing Stack of Lawsuits

The Virginia-D.C. filing is now the third lawsuit seeking to halt work on the monument. Three Vietnam War veterans and an architectural historian filed the original challenge, and U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan is already weighing whether to issue an order pausing the project until that case is resolved. She held a hearing on Friday but did not immediately act, saying she would move expeditiously.

Chutkan also ordered both sides in the new case to confer and agree on a proposed briefing schedule for a temporary restraining order motion by Saturday evening.

The administration, for its part, has signaled it intends to press ahead. In a recent court filing, it said it planned to seek final approval of the project at Thursday's meeting of the National Capital Planning Commission — and possibly begin construction the same day, despite the then-two active lawsuits pending. On Friday, the government submitted a 106-page plan for the arch describing a structure more than 12 stories high, featuring a massive bronze statue of Lady Liberty with wings and a torch bearing an eternal flame, an observation deck, an "occupied roof," and two mezzanines. Inside are rooms for program space, offices, a staff lounge, circular staircases, multiple elevators, and a fire command center.

## A Wider Campaign to Reshape the Capital

The arch is one of several projects through which Trump has sought to remake Washington during his second term. As [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/17/nx-s1-6001826/president-trumps-widening-stamp-on-d-c-landmarks) in October, these include transforming East Potomac Golf Links into a championship course, a helipad in front of the White House, changes to the reflecting pool near the Lincoln Memorial, and a new ballroom rising from the site of the demolished East Wing of the White House — which was torn down without congressional approval.

That pattern has made courts the primary vehicle for opponents. "People are sort of worried that if they don't stop things before they start, they might lose them forever," NPR's Rachel Treisman said. "People feel kind of powerless to stop it."

Critics of the arch emphasize that the site is federal land requiring multiple approvals, that one of two federal arts commissions gave final design approval in May 2026 — before the design was even finished — and that Congress never authorized the project. Supporters, by contrast, frame the arch as a legitimate tribute to the nation's 250th anniversary and to veterans.

"Some officials and lots of people are not necessarily inherently opposed to the idea of an arch that honors the 250th or honors veterans and service members," Treisman noted. "They're just saying this isn't the right spot for it."

## The Battle Over Process

Beneath the dispute over traffic and aesthetics lies a broader argument about executive authority. Trump has previously defended the arch as a "military complex" designed to hold weapons and snipers, though the most recent plans do not include military elements.

"The reason that Vietnam veterans are suing over this and the reason that a lot of Democratic lawmakers are opposed is because the president never got approval from Congress," Treisman said. "Whether or not you'll ever golf on that course or drive past this arch, it raises bigger questions about process and respect and transparency that they say all Americans should care about."

That sentiment was echoed by visitors to the capital. "It seems to me that he's just deciding by himself without any kind of input from the rest of the government on what's happening," said Wayne Brown of Texas. "And, you know, that doesn't seem right to me." Others were more supportive. "I say, build the monuments, and hey, people will come see them," said Nick Churchill of Michigan. "I don't think he's doing everything selfishly. I think it's just for the people of America."

NPR said it reached out to both the White House and the Interior Department for comment on the lawsuit on Saturday but did not receive a response.

## What's Next

The immediate question is whether Judge Chutkan will grant a temporary restraining order before construction equipment arrives. The National Capital Planning Commission's Thursday meeting looms as the administration's next procedural milestone — and potentially the moment work begins.

But the longer fight may be decided at the ballot box. As Treisman observed, the pivotal moment for Trump's push to reshape Washington could come in the midterm elections just weeks away, which will determine control of Congress. For now, the arch's fate rests where so much of the Trump-era policy agenda has landed: in the courts.
