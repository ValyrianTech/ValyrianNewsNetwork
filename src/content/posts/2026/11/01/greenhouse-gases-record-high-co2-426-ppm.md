# Greenhouse Gases Hit Record Highs as CO2 Tops 426 ppm

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases all reached unprecedented highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) reported, locking in additional long-term warming even as countries push to curb emissions. According to the [WMO's Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22](https://wmo.int/resources/publication-series/greenhouse-gas-bulletin/no-22), released on 27 October 2026, the globally averaged surface concentration of carbon dioxide (CO₂) rose to 426.8 parts per million — 153% of the pre-industrial level of 1750.

The finding, first reported in the Belgian daily [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/779336/article/2026-10-27/une-nouvelle-annee-un-nouveau-record-la-concentration-gaz-effet-de-serre-atteint), comes just weeks before the COP31 UN climate conference opens in Antalya, Türkiye, where negotiators will seek to turn climate pledges into concrete action.

## A Record for the Third Major Gas

Methane (CH₄) reached 1,948 parts per billion, or 267% of its 1750 level, while nitrous oxide (N₂O) climbed to 339.1 ppb — 126% above the pre-industrial baseline. CO₂ alone accounts for about 66% of the warming effect from long-lived greenhouse gases, while methane contributes roughly 16%.

Crucially, the Bulletin measures atmospheric concentrations — the accumulated burden of these gases — not annual emissions. About half of all CO₂ emitted each year is absorbed by land ecosystems and oceans, while the other half remains in the atmosphere. Because CO₂ persists for centuries, concentrations can keep rising long after emissions begin to fall.

"Another year, another new record. Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies," said [WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett](https://wmo.int/secretariat/ko-barrett/record-greenhouse-gases-trap-more-heat-future). "The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue."

## Growth Has Tripled Since the 1960s

The pace of accumulation is itself accelerating. According to the [WMO press release](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high), CO₂ growth rates have tripled since the 1960s — from an average of about 0.8 ppm per year to roughly 2.4 ppm annually in the 2011–2020 decade. When the Bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO₂ level stood at 377.5 ppm, meaning concentrations have climbed roughly 49 ppm in two decades.

Between 2024 and 2025, CO₂ rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024. WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed the more modest increase to the recovery of land carbon sinks and reduced fire emissions following the strong 2023/2024 El Niño, which had dried vegetation and fuelled wildfires.

That respite may prove short-lived. Tarasova warned that a powerful new El Niño is expected to push concentrations higher again in 2026 by weakening the ability of land and oceans to absorb CO₂.

"Every additional part per million matters because it traps additional heat. In effect, we are supercharging the ocean and the atmosphere," Barrett said in comments carried by [ESG News](https://esgnews.com/wmo-greenhouse-gas-record-2025/). "That means that heatwaves, tropical cyclones, and rainstorms are intensifying faster than our historical baselines prepared us for."

## The Energy Transition's Hidden Risks

The Bulletin also flagged two lesser-known but potent gases tied to the shift toward clean energy. The 2025 increase in sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆), used as a spark suppressant in electrical equipment, hit a record high. SF₆ has an atmospheric lifetime of more than 1,000 years and a global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO₂.

Molecular hydrogen (H₂) concentrations rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024, reflecting growing use of hydrogen as an energy carrier and storage medium for sectors such as steelmaking and shipping. Hydrogen is not itself a greenhouse gas, but leakage from production facilities, pipelines and fuel cells can alter atmospheric chemistry and indirectly contribute to warming.

"The energy transition is essential and welcome, but as with every transition, it has to be done in a smart way and informed by environmental observations," Barrett said.

## A Backdrop for COP31

The Bulletin is one of WMO's flagship publications, produced specifically to arm UN climate negotiators with authoritative science. Its release sets a sobering tone for COP31, which runs from 9 to 20 November in Antalya and will launch the second global stocktake of climate action under the Paris Agreement.

The report's core message is that emissions cuts alone cannot quickly reverse the atmospheric buildup already underway. Even a rapid decline in emissions would leave CO₂ concentrations elevated for generations, committing the planet to warming that is largely irreversible on human timescales. With a powerful El Niño expected to compound the problem next year, the pressure on negotiators to convert promises into measurable action has rarely been sharper.

What remains to be seen is whether the record — a refrain of "another year, another new record" — can finally be broken in the other direction.
