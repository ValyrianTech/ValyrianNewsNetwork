# Seven Workers Rescued From 24m Under Vilvoorde Viaduct

Seven construction workers spent roughly three hours dangling about 24 metres above the ground beneath the Vilvoorde viaduct on Saturday afternoon after their suspended work platform jammed, before firefighters using a ladder truck brought them safely down. No one was injured.

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/01/arbeiders-vast-op-24-meter-hoogte-onder-viaduct-van-vilvoorde/), the workers were aboard a work lift performing renovation duties under the viaduct — a section of the R0 Brussels ring road — when the platform failed. They remained stranded high above the ground until emergency crews reached them.

## The rescue

The fire brigade of the Brandweerzone Vlaams-Brabant West received the emergency call at 16:30, but the lift had in fact been stuck since around 14:00. "The call only came in at 4:30 p.m., but apparently the lift had already been stuck since 2 p.m.," spokesperson Wouter Jeanfils said. "With the help of a ladder truck we were able to quickly free all the people from their precarious position."

Shortly after 17:00, all the workers had been evacuated, Jeanfils confirmed. "No one was injured."

## Cause of the incident

The [Ring TV](https://www.ringtv.be/mobiliteit/arbeiders-vast-op-24-meter-hoogte-onder-viaduct-van-vilvoorde-defecte-werklift-oorzaak) regional broadcaster reported that a technical defect left the work lift blocked and unable to descend. The contractor responsible, a subcontractor working for the Flemish agency De Werkvennootschap, was unable to lower the platform, prompting the call to emergency services.

"The subcontractor could not get the lift back down," said Marijn Struyf, spokesperson for De Werkvennootschap. "The fire brigade was then called to bring the workers down. Fortunately no one was injured."

## A round-the-clock construction site

The incident unfolded at one of Belgium's most significant infrastructure projects. The Vilvoorde viaduct, which closed the Brussels beltway when it opened in December 1977, is undergoing a total renovation that began in August 2023 and is scheduled to run until the end of 2031 — a project of roughly eight years.

The scope is vast: the amount of steel being added is comparable to that of the Eiffel Tower, more than 4,000 kilometres of welds must be laid, and about 300 kilometres of steel is manually ground before conservation. Workers are also removing asbestos-containing coating under strict safety procedures. To keep the project on schedule, work proceeds around the clock, seven days a week — which is why the crew was present on a Saturday afternoon.

The heaviest traffic disruption is expected from spring 2027, when the works move to the top deck and lanes will be closed in each direction.

## Analysis: height, hazard and emergency response

Saturday's rescue highlights the inherent risks of working at height on a live, heavily trafficked construction site. The three-hour gap between the lift jamming and the emergency call is notable: the subcontractor first attempted to resolve the fault internally before escalating. That the workers were recovered without injury reflects both the professionalism of the fire brigade and the value of a rapid, well-equipped response — but it also raises questions about how quickly such malfunctions should be reported.

For De Werkvennootschap, the incident is a reminder that safety oversight on a project of this scale extends well beyond the steel and concrete. As the renovation moves into its most disruptive phase in the coming years, maintaining rigorous protocols for machinery and emergency escalation will be as important as keeping the timeline on track.

## What's next

The viaduct renovation continues through the winter, with work proceeding around the clock. Motorists should watch for further lane changes as the project advances toward its 2027 peak. For now, the seven workers are safe — and the fire brigade's ladder truck has added another chapter to the viaduct's long history.
