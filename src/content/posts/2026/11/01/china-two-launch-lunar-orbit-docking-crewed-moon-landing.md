# China to Land Astronauts via Two-Launch Lunar Docking

China's crewed lunar exploration program will adopt a "two launches, lunar orbit rendezvous" architecture to put Chinese astronauts on the Moon, a senior space engineer announced at a symposium in Beijing, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261101/372c4f6fe7c942ab8fa02f044cfa93f7/c.html). The plan relies on two separate rocket launches whose spacecraft meet and dock in lunar orbit, a step toward China's stated goal of a crewed Moon landing before 2030.

Wang Xiang, deputy chief designer of China's manned space program and chief designer of the lunar exploration program, detailed the approach at the 7th Manned Space Symposium, which opened on October 31 at Beihang University and drew more than 800 experts.

## How the mission would unfold

Under the architecture, two Long March-10 rockets would separately send the Lanyue lunar lander and the Mengzhou crewed spaceship into Earth-Moon transfer orbit, Xinhua reported. The lander is launched first; the crewed spacecraft follows, and the two craft rendezvous and dock in lunar orbit.

Astronauts then transfer from the Mengzhou spaceship into the Lanyue lander and descend to the lunar surface to carry out scientific work. After completing their tasks, they ascend in the lander and perform a second rendezvous and docking with the Mengzhou in lunar orbit before returning to Earth in the crewed spacecraft.

According to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34375545), citing Global Times reporting, the first crewed lunar mission is planned to consist of three astronauts, two of whom will set foot on the lunar surface.

## Why two launches

Wang framed the lunar-orbit "handshake" between the two craft as a deliberate engineering choice. "First, this plan matches existing technical capabilities and infrastructure; second, developing the spaceship and lander separately clarifies the division of labor between the two craft and facilitates standardized development, forming a long-term, reusable series/model lineup," he said.

The approach echoes the architecture used by NASA's Apollo program and its current Artemis effort, which likewise relies on separate launches for a lander and crew vehicle. Rather than pursuing a single super-rocket capable of lifting a combined spacecraft-and-lander stack directly to the Moon, China is matching the plan to its available launch capacity.

## Testing in the homestretch

Development of the primary flight hardware—the Long March-10 carrier rocket, the Mengzhou crewed spaceship and the Lanyue lander—has progressed smoothly, per Xinhua. Major tests already completed include the Mengzhou's zero-height abort and maximum dynamic pressure escape tests, the Lanyue lander's landing-and-takeoff test, and a low-altitude demonstration and validation flight of the Long March-10.

Wang highlighted that the Long March-10 low-altitude verification flight also achieved China's first rocket re-entry recovery, giving human spaceflight a catalytic role in the country's push toward reusable rocket technology.

These milestones build on a program that has been steadily advancing. As [People's Daily](https://paper.people.com.cn/rmrb/pc/content/202503/04/content_30060082.html) reported in early 2025, the main flight products—including the Wangyu lunar landing suit and the Tansuo crewed lunar rover—were then in the prototype development stage, with related facilities at the Wenchang launch site under construction.

## The challenges ahead

Separating the two craft into distinct launches introduces its own difficulties. The two launches occur at different times, and the relative position of Earth and the Moon changes between them, complicating the timing needed for a precision rendezvous and docking some 380,000 kilometers from Earth.

Wang said China's ability to mobilize resources nationwide is the fundamental advantage for solving these problems. Under a unified development framework, he said, the program has established system plans, technical standards and quality requirements, and adopted an integrated design approach that emphasizes verification of interfaces between subsystems.

He also stressed continuity with China's robotic lunar efforts. The crewed landing and the unmanned Chang'e exploration program share much in common in key technologies, infrastructure and talent, and achievements from the [Chang'e program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Lunar_Exploration_Program)—which has included lunar orbiters, landers, rovers and sample-return missions, as well as a robotic lunar orbit docking during Chang'e 5—will feed into the crewed effort.

## What to watch

Looking further ahead, Wang described a philosophy of "cognition first, capability first," arguing that a prolonged survey phase is needed to understand the Moon step by step before moving into resource development and utilization.

The next concrete milestones will be the first flights of the Long March-10, the Mengzhou spaceship and the Lanyue lander. Beyond the initial landing, China has signaled ambitions to build an International Lunar Research Station at the lunar south pole beginning around 2031 with partner countries—an effort that the new two-launch architecture's emphasis on a reusable "series/model lineup" appears designed, at least in part, to support.

For now, the formal detailing of the mission architecture signals that China is moving from design toward flight-article testing, keeping its before-2030 target in view. Open questions remain over the exact landing date within that window, the identity of the first crew, and precisely how many uncrewed test flights will precede the historic attempt.
