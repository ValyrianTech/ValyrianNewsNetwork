# Russia and China Build a Polar Silk Road in the Arctic

Moscow and Beijing are knitting together a "Polar Silk Road" across the rapidly thawing Arctic, linking China's Maritime Silk Road to Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor — a project that is redrawing the map of global shipping and unsettling Western capitals. The push crystallised in October with a visit to China by Nikolai Patrushev, an aide to Vladimir Putin, whose proposals, [reported](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/) by Asia Times, covered joint port development, the exchange of container-terminal automation technology and collaborative maritime research.

That the world's largest country and its most populous are deepening cooperation is not new. What is new is the speed and the stakes: climate change is opening sea lanes once impassable to commercial traffic, and the Arctic's seabed is sharpening appetites. According to USGS estimates cited by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719), the region may hold nearly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and 13% of its undiscovered oil.

## A Route That Shortens the World

The economic logic of the Northern Sea Route (NSR) is compelling. The Shanghai–Hamburg journey via the Arctic takes roughly 18 days, compared with 30 to 35 days through the Suez Canal. Russia recorded 37.9 million tonnes of cargo on the NSR in 2024, including 3.09 million tonnes of transit cargo — a record high. Putin has said freight volumes could double by the end of the decade to 70 million tonnes annually, helped by Rosneft's giant Vostok Oil project.

Growth is already visible. Russian oil exports via the Arctic rose nearly 50% in 2026 compared with the previous year, with about 20 million barrels delivered to Asia between July and mid-October, according to [Modern Diplomacy](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/10/19/russia-ramps-up-arctic-oil-exports-as-global-shipping-crisis-disrupts-trade-routes/), citing Reuters. In August, the Chinese shipping firm Sea Legend launched the first weekly container service across the Arctic, [The South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/week-asia/economics/article/3364859/india-joins-china-and-south-korea-push-arctic-shipping-routes) reported — a concrete step toward Beijing's long-held Polar Silk Road ambition. South Korea, which completed its first commercial Arctic round trip in 2026, and India are both showing interest.

The urgency is sharpened by disruption elsewhere. With conflicts in Iran and Ukraine pushing tanker freight rates to record levels, the NSR — though riskier, costlier and seasonal — has become cheaper than Red Sea routes. For Moscow, it also offers a way to export its oil without the obstacles posed by Western sanctions in European waters.

## Two Needs, One Corridor

The partnership is driven by complementary needs. Russia wants Chinese capital, shipping demand and port-automation technology to turn an ambitious Arctic strategy into a functioning logistics system. China wants an alternative maritime route that reduces its dependence on the Strait of Malacca and a foothold in a region it has described, since its 2018 policy, as that of a "near-Arctic state" with legitimate interests in shipping, resources, science and governance.

"China aspires to global superpower status. One attribute of that is dominance — or at least a presence — in geopolitically important zones of the world, and the Arctic is unquestionably one of them," Vlastislav Bříza, a security expert at Charles University in Prague, told Czech Television's "Vu d'Europe" programme.

The commercial veneer conceals a strategic shift that worries Western analysts. "For Moscow, Chinese capital, shipping demand and technology could help transform an ambitious Arctic strategy into a functioning logistics system. The geopolitical consequences will undoubtedly be watched closely in Washington," Asia Times wrote.

## The "Boring" Parts Matter Most

Analysts caution against both dismissing the project as Russian bravado and inflating it into a Sino-Russian maritime bloc. The proposals discussed in October remain "largely unimplemented plans," Asia Times stressed, and commercial barriers — ice, high insurance premiums, thin infrastructure and a short operating season — are formidable. NSR volumes remain tiny compared with the Suez Canal.

Yet the least dramatic elements may prove the most consequential. Port automation, digital shipyards, common technical standards and research cooperation are, in Asia Times' words, "the building blocks of 21st-century maritime power: whoever controls the software, data, equipment and logistics architecture of ports can shape trade without ever firing a shot."

The relationship, however, is increasingly asymmetrical. Sweden's head of military intelligence, Thomas Nilsson, warned in an interview with [The Times](https://www.thetimes.com/world/europe/article/china-take-advantage-russia-the-arctic-fgfx6k8ww) that China will "use" Russia to gain a foothold in the Arctic. Moscow, which once resented Chinese influence there, now depends on Beijing to finance its war effort through oil and gas sales and for deliveries of military equipment and dual-use goods such as microprocessors.

## A Militarised North

Security concerns are mounting. Russia controls roughly 53% of the Arctic coastline and is expanding military capacity beyond the Kola Peninsula, reopening Soviet-era bases. According to [Defence24](https://defence24.com/geopolitics/cold-war-at-the-top-of-the-world-the-militarisation-of-the-arctic-and-the-sino-russian-alliance-on-the-northern-sea-route), it has deployed Bastion coastal defence systems, Arctic-adapted S-400 air defence and hypersonic-missile-armed fighters.

"Russia is expanding its military presence in the North. Moscow has reopened Soviet bases and is testing new weapons in the region," EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said. "Drone incursions, GPS jamming and the sabotage of undersea cables are undermining Arctic security."

The West's deterrent is thin. The United States operates just two ageing icebreakers against Russia's more than 40, including powerful Arktika-class nuclear vessels. "Without the physical capacity to operate in ice-covered waters, Washington's declarations in favour of freedom of navigation in the Arctic remain mere diplomatic notes," Defence24 observed. NATO's "Arctic Sentry" initiative, launched in February 2026, aims to unify maritime and air patrols and intelligence-sharing, with Sweden and Germany among those deploying fighter jets.

## Europe's Answer

In October, the European Union unveiled its own Arctic strategy, built on four priorities: security and resilience, climate action, and prosperity for Arctic communities. Flagship measures include expanding a European icebreaker fleet and creating a maritime security hub to track Russia's "shadow fleet," which is increasingly difficult to monitor.

"The Arctic is one of the places where the tectonic plates of geopolitics collide," European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said. "When we see that sovereignty, territorial integrity and the rules underpinning the region's stability can no longer be taken for granted, we must act and change our strategic posture."

But the strategy faces an implementation gap. Andreas Raspotnik, director of the High North Center at Nord University, called it "a good starting point" while noting it says nothing about how flagship projects will be financed or realised, according to [High North News](https://en.highnorthnews.com/politics/the-eus-new-arctic-strategy-a-good-starting-point-says-researcher/1123158). [Le Monde](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2026/10/20/eu-unveils-new-arctic-strategy-in-face-of-us-russia-threats_6760261_4.html) noted that the EU has been forced to pivot its Arctic priorities from climate toward security. The bloc also diverges sharply from Washington: EU Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen insisted that "oil, coal and gas must stay in the ground," even as the Trump administration loosens environmental rules to maximise extraction in the US Arctic.

## What to Watch

The Arctic is emerging as a test of whether the West can match ambition with capability. Several questions loom: whether the Patrushev proposals translate into financed infrastructure or remain, as Asia Times cautioned, "largely unimplemented plans"; how quickly the EU turns its strategy from wish list into funded reality amid budget negotiations; and whether Russia's growing dependence on China erodes its control over its own Arctic gateway.

For Beijing, the northern corridor promises redundancy rather than a replacement for Suez — a hedge against the chokepoints of a fragmenting maritime world. For the EU and NATO, the challenge is stark: in the Arctic, as Kallas put it, "icebreakers are the measure of power." The coming decade will show whether the West is willing to build them fast enough.
