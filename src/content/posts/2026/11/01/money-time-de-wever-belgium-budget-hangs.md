# 'Money Time' for De Wever as Belgium's Budget Hangs

Belgium's federal budget negotiations have entered their decisive phase, with Prime Minister Bart De Wever (N-VA) facing the starkest choice of his tenure: clinch a hard-won deal on the 2026/2027 budget or see the country fall back on "provisional twelfths" — an emergency spending mechanism he has vowed never to accept. Political scientist Nicolas Bouteca of Ghent University (UGent) told [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/01/politicoloog-bouteca-over-begroting/) on Sunday that the night of Monday 2 to Tuesday 3 November is "money time" for the prime minister.

## The Stakes: €10 Billion by 2029

The "Arizona" coalition government must find structural budget savings of about €10 billion by 2029 to keep spending in line with the EU expenditure norm and to reassure financial markets. As [HLN](https://www.hln.be/binnenland/de-wever-zet-bilaterale-gesprekken-om-13-uur-verder-regering-zoekt-nog-altijd-10-miljard-euro~a9cb0fe2/) reported, the government is still searching for that full €10 billion, with the clock running down on the parliamentary calendar.

Failure carries a heavy price. Roughly two months are needed to approve budget laws in Parliament, meaning the window to act — if the budget is to take effect from January — is now extremely tight. Without a timely agreement, the government cannot vote the budget through this year and must fall back on a *noodbegroting* with provisional twelfths, under which the state may spend only one-twelfth of the previous year's budget each month. Belgium already resorted to this mechanism for the first quarter of 2026.

## A Coalition Stretched to Its Limits

The Arizona coalition spans a wide ideological range — from the right-wing N-VA to the left-wing Vooruit — turning every euro of fiscal adjustment into a zero-sum contest. Vooruit, the sole left-wing party in the seven-party coalition, has hardened its position in recent weeks, demanding that "the strongest shoulders contribute more."

According to Bouteca, "Vooruit absolutely wants to win a trophy, but it is looking for it right in the electoral heart of the N-VA." Party chairman Conner Rousseau has targeted roughly €1.5 billion in taxes on large fortunes, arguing that "you shouldn't go looking for every euro from large fortunes among pensioners or in healthcare." Vooruit is also seeking to make management companies (*managementvennootschappen*) less attractive — a move that strikes directly at the N-VA's entrepreneurial base.

"Initially the party kept to the sidelines, but in recent weeks it is showing its teeth," Bouteca said. "A trophy will have to be found that Vooruit — the only left-wing party in the coalition — can wave around."

## Flashpoints on the Table

Several dossiers remain deeply contested. Chief among them is De Wever's proposal to cap sickness benefits at two years for people with burn-out or moderate depression. Vooruit and Health Minister Frank Vandenbroucke oppose the measure, and legal experts have warned that distinguishing mental from physical conditions may conflict with anti-discrimination law.

The MR has proven equally willing to dig in. Party chairman Georges-Louis Bouchez vetoed a proposed VAT increase from 21% to 22%, which Bouteca argues would have been the "least damaging tax." He suggests De Wever now regrets the concessions made to the MR: "According to economists, a VAT increase would be the least damaging tax. You get a lot of money in the till, and you get a compromise with a vision behind it: shifting the burden from labour to consumption... Now there is little vision behind it."

## 'Failure Is Not an Option'

Despite the mounting obstacles, Bouteca was careful not to write off a deal entirely. "Failure is not an option," he said — though he added, "Yet I am less convinced than a few weeks ago that it will still succeed."

The prime minister has been conducting bilateral talks at the Wetstraat 16 residence with his vice-premiers: Vincent Van Peteghem (CD&V), Maxime Prévot (Les Engagés), David Clarinval (MR) and Frank Vandenbroucke (Vooruit). On Sunday, he continued with Vandenbroucke and Clarinval, a meeting of the full Kernkabinet possibly to follow. On 29 October, the coalition agreed to intensify talks, meeting daily from 14:00 into the evening. A full inner-cabinet meeting on Saturday did not take place; only bilateral contacts occurred.

Tensions have flared along the way. On 30 October, Vandenbroucke and Clarinval reportedly clashed sharply, with the Vooruit vice-premier declaring that he was "not afraid of elections." Coalition partners accused Rousseau of "driving us straight into the abyss" — though no partner currently wants to bring the government down.

## What's Next

All eyes now turn to the night of 2–3 November, the decisive window Bouteca has identified. Chamber Speaker Peter De Roover (N-VA) has said approval "can still practically succeed," while acknowledging it would be a difficult exercise.

If the talks fail, Belgium faces a renewed period of fiscal limbo: an emergency budget, potential pressure from credit-rating agencies and financial markets, and a real — if currently unwanted — risk of government collapse or early elections. As Bouteca's framing makes plain, the question is no longer whether to save €10 billion, but who will bear the burden — and whether a coalition this broad can agree on an answer before time runs out.
