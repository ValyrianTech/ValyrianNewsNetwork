# Daubechies: AI Can End 'Dull, Tedious' Math Teaching

For generations of students, mathematics has meant one thing above all: memorising formulas and grinding through mechanical drills without ever understanding why. Ingrid Daubechies wants that to change—and she believes artificial intelligence is the catalyst.

"I hope that as AI becomes better at handling tedious, dull work, we can thoroughly reform math education," the Wolf Prize laureate said in an interview with [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34381316) on 31 October, on the sidelines of the 2026 World Laureates Forum in Shanghai. "I hope the AI era will become the end of that dull, tedious way of teaching mathematics."

## A Lifetime of Making Math Beautiful

Daubechies, the James B. Duke Distinguished Professor Emerita of Mathematics at Duke University, is one of the most influential applied mathematicians alive. Known as the "mother of wavelets," she constructed the "Daubechies wavelets"—mathematical tools that made wavelet analysis practical and now underpin JPEG 2000 image compression, CT and MRI scanning, and digital cinema.

Her work earned her the 2023 Wolf Prize in Mathematics, making her the first woman to receive it since the award's establishment in 1978. In September 2026, she was named the winner of the 2026 World Laureates Award "Intelligent Science or Mathematics Prize," as [21st Century Business Herald](https://www.21jingji.com/article/20260910/herald/ec3bebd9a2efcdb72fa8e3db43ce506c.html) reported, recognising her contributions to applied harmonic analysis, wavelet theory and image compression.

## The Poetry Analogy

Daubechies is unusually direct about what she sees as the failures of conventional math teaching. When people tell her they had a miserable experience with the subject at school, she apologises.

"I say, 'I really regret that you didn't meet an interesting math teacher who could spark your interest,'" she told The Paper, "because that is exactly the result of a teacher failing to truly convey the essence of mathematics."

She compared rote instruction to teaching poetry appreciation by drilling only technique—rhyme, metre and rhetorical devices—while ignoring the soul of the poem. Mathematics, she stressed, contains its own beauty and is deeply creative.

Her conviction is grounded in decades of practice. As [Shanghai Observer](https://www.163.com/dy/article/LACUKQK8055040N3.html) reported from her 28 October campus talk at Tongji University, she has long insisted that math in the AI era needs more "human flavour." Wavelet analysis is not merely abstract—she has used it to help restore the Van Eyck Ghent Altarpiece, separating overlapping X-ray images of the double-sided panels, and to verify the authenticity of works by Van Gogh and Rembrandt.

## Caution From the Same Stage

Daubechies' optimism about AI as a teaching aid is not shared uniformly by her peers. At the same forum, Richard Schoen, a Stanford professor emeritus and fellow Wolf laureate, warned that although AI has made major breakthroughs in mathematical research this year and is a valuable tool, its efficiency carries a hidden cost.

"Sometimes the inefficiency of math research is valuable," Schoen said, arguing that the process of discovering why an approach fails generates insights that a machine-generated answer may skip entirely.

Shing-Tung Yau, the Fields Medalist who leads Tsinghua University's Yau Mathematical Sciences Center, struck a similar note of qualified optimism. Launching the Yau Mathematical and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory Alliance on 11 October, he warned that proper use of AI could yield epoch-making scholarship, but that mishandling it "could bring catastrophic consequences"—and called for Chinese scholars to develop original AI math models.

Daubechies herself is clear-eyed about the technology's limits. In her Tongji talk, she noted that many of the math proofs OpenAI has published are incorrect and difficult for human mathematicians to read. The calculator, she suggested, offers the proper analogy: once machines could do arithmetic, humans stopped doing it by hand and redirected their energy elsewhere.

## Teaching Students to Use the Tool

The practical implication, she argues, is that schools should not ban AI but teach students to integrate it into their learning.

"We must teach students to use AI as part of learning, rather than simply banning it," she said. The goal is not to hand over the thinking, but to free students from the mechanical labour so they can focus on genuine understanding—grasping the ideas mathematics conveys and what learning it can offer.

Her words carry weight beyond the classroom. The 2026 forum has become a showcase for the debate over how AI is reshaping both mathematical research and the way the subject is taught. Zhai Hui, a Tsinghua University professor, told the Future Scientists Conference that the boundary between human and machine research is dissolving, predicting that "almost all scientific research in the future will be conducted jointly by carbon-based and silicon-based life."

## What to Watch

Daubechies' remarks arrive as AI math tools grow more capable by the month, and as institutions from Tsinghua to Duke race to define how the technology should be used. Whether her vision of classrooms focused on insight rather than drill becomes reality will depend less on the technology itself than on the teachers who deliver it—and on how quickly curricula can be redesigned.

For now, her message is a challenge to educators: the dull, rote lessons that turned so many students away from mathematics may finally have a worthy replacement. The question is whether schools will seize it.
