# Supreme Court Weighs Catholic Pre-K Funding Case

The U.S. Supreme Court is set to hear oral arguments in a case that pits the Catholic Church against a taxpayer-funded preschool program serving all 4-year-olds in Colorado, testing whether religious schools can accept public money while refusing to comply with state nondiscrimination rules. The dispute, *St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy*, centers on whether Colorado may require religious providers to abide by a law barring discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity as a condition of participating in its Universal Preschool Program, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/01/nx-s1-6000367/supreme-court-colorado-catholic-preschool-religious-freedom-lgbtq).

## Context: A Program Built for Every Child

Colorado launched its Universal Preschool Program in 2023, offering roughly $6,300 per eligible 4-year-old per year for 15 hours a week, funded by a tobacco tax. To ensure that virtually every child could attend, the state invited private and faith-based providers to join — provided they agreed not to discriminate on eight characteristics, including sexual orientation and gender identity.

"The law really was about implementing a program that said we want every kid, no matter where you live, to be able to have access to universal preschool," Steve Fenberg, who served as president of the Colorado state Senate when the program was enacted, told NPR. "The caveat is that you can't discriminate, and you need to allow all students to participate."

Dozens of religious schools joined under those terms, including at least 35 Christian schools, one Muslim school and three Jewish schools, and Catholic Charities continues to participate.

## The Dispute

The Archdiocese of Denver and two parishes — St. Mary Catholic Virtue School in Littleton and Wellspring Catholic Academy in Lakewood — challenged the requirement, arguing it forces them to choose between their religious beliefs and access to public funds. They are represented by the Becket Fund for Religious Liberty and its CEO, Mark Rienzi.

"They're only excluding religious schools that are organized around beliefs that Colorado doesn't like," Rienzi told NPR. "And that's the constitutional problem." The archdiocese wants an exemption from the anti-discrimination law so it can decline to admit children of gay or transgender parents.

Lower courts sided with Colorado. A federal district court and the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals held that the state's rules are neutral and generally applicable, a standard rooted in the 1990 precedent *Employment Division v. Smith*. The Supreme Court granted certiorari in April 2026 but declined to reconsider *Smith* itself, meaning the ruling will turn on whether excluding Catholic preschools amounts to religious discrimination under the First Amendment.

The archdiocese says the fallout has been severe. In a *Denver Post* op-ed, Archbishop James R. Golka wrote that preschool enrollment across the archdiocese has fallen about 20% and two preschools have closed — one serving "minority and low income families." He called Colorado's policy a "tragedy" that "threatens the broader culture."

Not all Catholic educators agree. Tim Uhl, a Catholic school director who filed a brief siding with Colorado, told NPR he does not accept the alarm. "We do admit students that are not Catholic and families that are not Catholic," he said, adding that "diversity of opinion and diversity of thought is part of an educational experience."

## Analysis: A Doctrinal Shift

The case lands at a moment of profound change in the Court's religion jurisprudence. For decades after the 1940s, the justices emphasized Thomas Jefferson's "wall of separation between church and state." Roughly a decade ago, a new conservative majority began pivoting toward protecting the free exercise of religion.

"It's literally the opposite of what the law was not that long ago," Michigan State University law professor Frank Ravitch told NPR. "It is just a complete shift."

Recent precedents have steadily widened religious access to public funds. In *Trinity Lutheran v. Comer* (2017), *Espinoza v. Montana* (2020) and *Carson v. Makin* (2022), the Court held that states cannot flatly exclude religious schools from public grant or tuition programs. None, however, resolved whether states may attach nondiscrimination conditions — the precise question now before the justices.

The plaintiffs lean heavily on *Fulton v. City of Philadelphia* (2021), which held that a neutral rule loses its neutrality if the government grants secular exceptions but denies religious ones. They note the pre-K program already contains carve-outs, such as income limits for Head Start and sibling-priority admissions, and argue that denying a religious-belief exemption is therefore discriminatory.

Colorado counters that its rule bars excluding children for who they are — based on disability or income — rather than granting preferences the constitution requires equal treatment for.

Douglas Laycock, an emeritus professor at the University of Texas at Austin and the University of Virginia, conceded to NPR that the archdiocese is discriminating but framed the question as one of religious liberty. "The question is whether that discrimination is a protected exercise of religion," he said. He drew a distinction with race, which he said occupies a "unique" place in U.S. history — a nod to *Bob Jones University v. U.S.* (1983), in which the Court stripped a religious school of its tax-exempt status over racially discriminatory policies.

## Broader Stakes

The implications stretch well beyond preschool. A ruling for the archdiocese could invite religious organizations to seek exemptions from antidiscrimination rules while receiving public money — in foster care, homeless shelters and other publicly funded services. Berkeley Law dean Erwin Chemerinsky framed the central uncertainty succinctly: "The question is, how far will the Supreme Court go in requiring the government to pay for religious schools?"

Related battles are already brewing. A federal judge in Oklahoma recently allowed a taxpayer-funded Jewish charter school to open with $2.6 million in state funding, a figure projected to reach $8.3 million by 2030; its fate remains in limbo pending appeal. The Court was evenly split on religious charter schools last term but dropped the case after Justice Amy Coney Barrett recused herself. She has not recused in the Colorado case, so a full court will decide it.

## What's Next

Oral arguments are scheduled for Nov. 3, 2026, with a decision expected by late spring 2027. For now, the case stands as one of the most consequential church-state tests in years — a bellwether for how far the Court's conservative majority will go in reshaping the boundary between public funding and religious practice. Families, school systems and state legislatures across the country will be watching closely for signals about what "universal" public education is permitted to require of the institutions that deliver it.
