# National Symphony Fights to Survive Kennedy Center Closure

When the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts abruptly shut its doors on Sept. 15, it left one of America's most storied orchestras effectively homeless. The 95-year-old National Symphony Orchestra — which has performed at the Kennedy Center since the venue opened in 1971 — is now a musical nomad, scrambling across the Washington, D.C., region while the future of its parent institution remains in doubt, [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/01/nx-s1-6005501/national-symphony-orchestra-kennedy-center).

The closure was the culmination of months of sinking ticket sales, artist boycotts, and a federal court battle over President Trump's attempt to add his name to the center's facade. Trump took control of the institution in early 2025 after firing members of its board of trustees and its president, Deborah Rutter. The question now is whether the orchestra can survive the collapse of the institution to which its finances are bound.

## A Shock to the System

The news landed like a thunderclap. "It was absolutely unexpected," said Music Director Gianandrea Noseda, the Italian conductor who has led the orchestra for nine years. "The news of the closing of the Kennedy Center came like a cold shower. And my first reaction was, 'What will we do now if we don't have our home?'"

The orchestra's vulnerability is structural. Since 1986, its finances have been inseparable from the Kennedy Center's: the center owns the orchestra's endowment and handles its fundraising, marketing, and accounting. That arrangement, designed to rescue a then-struggling NSO, now leaves it exposed to the center's dysfunction.

Because the NSO needed budget approval from the Kennedy Center board, its 2026-27 season was not announced until mid-August — roughly six weeks before opening night and just 11 days before the center locked its doors. The orchestra opened its season on Sept. 26 at the Music Center at Strathmore in North Bethesda, Maryland, one of six alternative venues secured across the region.

Anne Midgette, former classical music critic for The Washington Post, said she had feared the worst. "I was very concerned that there might not be an NSO season. I was concerned there might not be an NSO — because the NSO is so bound up with the Kennedy Center."

## A Season at Half Scale

The financial picture is grim. This season the orchestra will perform about 60 concerts, roughly half its typical slate of some 175 events a year. Ticket revenue is expected to fall by about half and donations by a quarter, partly because of deep cuts to the Kennedy Center's development department. NSO classical concerts had already been drawing about 41% of capacity, down from 58% the prior year and 72% in 2024. The orchestra has also lost most of its former staff, including executive director Jean Davidson, who departed citing the Kennedy Center turmoil as her prime reason.

"It's a huge setback in economic terms," Midgette said. "It's a much reduced season. On the other hand, it's a triumph that they were able to pull off a season at all under these circumstances."

For the musicians, the uncertainty has been deeply personal. "We were stunned at first, honestly," said Rick Barber, the associate principal double bassist who joined the NSO in 1995. "And the months after that, there was definitely a lot of uncertainty — I would say, anxiety." Yet Barber said the music remains a refuge. "I see the political divisions in our nation. But when I'm playing music, I don't think about those at all."

## The Conductor Who Stayed

Perhaps the orchestra's most stabilizing force is Noseda himself. After Rutter's firing, he could have sought an exit. Instead, he extended his contract through 2031.

"I thought that would have been a little bit too easy," he said. "And may I use the word 'coward' — to leave that situation. When you are a music director, you are a leader, of course. But you have to serve the orchestra, the music and the community."

His public posture has remained relentlessly forward-looking. "The idea [is] to see the glass half full and not half empty. Let's try to reach out to new audiences in different places." Opening night at Strathmore — featuring music by Carlos Simon, Tchaikovsky, and Respighi — drew a packed house.

## The Political Backdrop

The closure capped a remarkable sequence of events at the Kennedy Center. On Sept. 15, the board voted to shut the main building in a meeting that began minutes after a federal judge, Christopher Cooper, again blocked the plan to add Trump's name to the building. Administrators had told the board that the complex's physical plant was unsafe and that its finances were within weeks of bankruptcy, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5969905/kennedy-center-closing-trump-name).

Trump immediately vowed to cancel a planned renovation unless the naming decision is reversed. "The closing will take place immediately, however, the Renovation and Reconstruction ... cannot begin until such time as the D.C. Circuit rules on the Board's approved name," he wrote.

Judge Cooper was blunt in his ruling. "Simply put, Defendants cannot install memorials for President Trump or anyone or anything else at the Kennedy Center without Congress's blessing," he wrote. Rep. Joyce Beatty, the Ohio Democrat who brought the suit, said the law is "extremely clear" that the center is named for President Kennedy "and no one else."

The board has pledged continued support for the NSO, along with continued programming at the smaller REACH extension and offsite, with the aim of a two-year closure that allows the center to "ramp up" in a revitalized building in 2028. Its current members are largely Trump allies with little experience administering a performing arts institution.

## The Acid Test

The NSO's struggle reflects a broader anxiety across American orchestral music, as symphonies nationwide confront financial strain — the Phoenix Symphony recently shut down — and audiences weigh discretionary spending against rising costs.

"The simple realities of buying a ticket and getting yourself to hear something, if you don't have the gas and the groceries are expensive, is more challenging," Midgette said. "We live in a really challenging time for the arts right now."

For Midgette, this itinerant season is a referendum on whether the public truly values the orchestra. "This is the $64,000 question," she said. "How many people really do care? How many people are vitally interested and invested in the orchestra? And I will say that this season, in all these different halls, is going to be a real acid test."

How long Noseda and his players will remain out of their hall remains unknown. But Noseda is betting that the music itself can carry them through. "I suspected that we had to go through challenging times, but I strongly believe in the value of the art, and the music can really still bring people together."

For a 95-year-old institution suddenly without a home, that conviction — and the audiences who show up in all those different halls — may be the thing that decides whether it endures.
