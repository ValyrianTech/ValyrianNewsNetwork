# Eight in Ten Flemish Now Choose Cremation

For the first time, more than eight in ten Flemish people are cremated after death, with the regional cremation rate climbing to 81 percent this year, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/23/cremeren-1-november-vlaanderen-prijs/). The figure, released ahead of All Saints' Day on 1 November, lays bare a widening divide in Belgian funeral culture: burial still prevails in Brussels and much of Wallonia, even as cremation becomes the norm in Flanders.

The milestone carries particular weight on 1 November, the Catholic feast day when Belgians traditionally visit and tend family graves. As more Flemish families scatter ashes on a meadow, at sea or in the Scheldt rather than maintain a physical grave, that annual ritual is quietly changing shape.

## Why cremation is rising

Tom Wustenberghs, chair of the Flemish association of crematoria, attributes the increase to practical considerations rather than cost. A cremation gives the bereaved or the undertaker flexibility in planning a funeral, and services are increasingly held after the cremation itself has taken place.

"There is also the aftercare," Wustenberghs told VRT NWS. "For most people who choose cremation, the ashes are then scattered. On a scattering meadow, usually, but it can also be at sea or in, for example, the Scheldt. In those cases there is no longer a physical grave that has to be maintained afterwards."

Cost, by contrast, appears to be a secondary factor. The latest studies Wustenberghs has seen estimate the price of a funeral at around €6,500, whether it is a burial or a cremation — excluding catering. Whether cremation is cheaper "depends on individual choices," he said.

## A three-speed Belgium

The regional gap is stark. Brussels records a cremation rate of 63 percent, well behind Flanders, though VRT NWS cautions that the Brussels figure is imprecise: a Brussels resident cremated in a Flemish facility is counted in the Flemish statistics, meaning the official Brussels rate understates actual cremation among its residents.

Wustenberghs links the difference to the capital's diversity of faith communities. "According to Islamic rules, it is forbidden to cremate a body," said Mustapha Ouriaghli, an Islamic undertaker from Schaerbeek.

Yet Ouriaghli describes a generational shift within Brussels' Muslim community. The first generation, who moved to the city in the 1960s, often chose repatriation and burial in their country of origin. "But with the second and third generation that is completely different," he said. "Since the corona pandemic we have seen a real switch," with families increasingly opting for burial on designated Muslim plots in Belgium so relatives can remain close to the grave. He warns that not every Brussels municipality is anticipating the rising need for Islamic burial places.

Wallonia remains the most traditional region of the three. Wustenberghs points to both culture and geography: "Wallonia is a lot more traditional in this respect. But the distance to the crematorium, which is greater in Wallonia, also plays a role."

## Cities lead the way

Within Flanders, the trend is uneven. In Ghent and Antwerp the cremation rate sometimes exceeds 90 percent, while it is lower in Limburg and West Flanders. Rural parts of Wallonia similarly lag behind urban centres.

The longer arc is one of steady growth. The Flemish cremation rate stood at roughly 63 percent in 2013 and around 78 percent in 2023, before passing 80 percent this year. Previous figures shared in the Flemish Parliament showed 2025 was the busiest year on record for Flemish crematoria, with 53,115 cremations out of 66,495 deaths.

## What to watch

The diverging numbers raise questions that extend beyond statistics. Brussels faces growing pressure to plan for Islamic burial space as second- and third-generation families put down roots. Wallonia's lower rate may shift as cultural attitudes evolve and access to crematoria improves. And with funeral arrangements a regional competence in Belgium, policy choices on cemeteries, scattering rules and urn requirements could widen or narrow the gap further.

For now, the trend line points one way. In Flanders, the traditional grave is fast becoming the exception rather than the rule — and All Saints' Day is being observed in an increasingly different way.
