# Belgium Now Seizes and Sells Cars of Banned Drivers

Driving a car while banned from the road now carries a much steeper price in Belgium. From 1 November 2026, a vehicle can be confiscated and resold when its owner is caught driving despite a revocation of their right to drive, under a change to a 1968 traffic law passed in March. According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/une-nouvelle-regle-permet-la-confiscation-et-la-vente-du-vehicule-en-cas-de-conduite-malgre-une-decheance-du-droit-de-conduire-11808726), the measure makes confiscation the default outcome for repeat offenders rather than a discretionary option.

## From judicial option to legal principle

The reform amends Article 50, section 2, of the Belgian law of 16 March 1968 on traffic policing. Previously, a police court judge *could* order the seizure of a vehicle when a driver was convicted of driving after a licence revocation that was final or of at least six months — but only where the vehicle belonged to the offender. In practice, that power was applied inconsistently.

Under the law of 3 March 2026, published in the Moniteur Belge on 31 August, the default is flipped. Where the legal conditions are met, the judge must now order confiscation. As [La DH / Belga](https://www.dhnet.be/dernieres-depeches/2026/10/27/nouveau-le-1er-novembre-confiscation-du-vehicule-en-cas-de-conduite-malgre-une-decheance-du-droit-de-conduire-656HZDEGT5H6BNLHDLKGWQV5VI/) reported, once seized the car becomes the property of the State, is sold at public auction, and the proceeds flow to the public Treasury.

The judge is not stripped of all flexibility. He or she may still decline to order the sanction, but only by *expressly justifying* the decision in light of the specific circumstances of the case. A boilerplate formula is not expected to suffice.

## The distinction most people miss

Legal analysis published by [Legalex](https://www.legalex.be/articles/confiscation-obligatoire-du-vehicule-des-novembre-2026-en-cas-de-conduite-malgre-une-decheance) highlights a point frequently lost in coverage of the reform: the confiscation obligation only applies where the vehicle belongs to the offender. A disqualified driver who takes the wheel of a company car, a friend's vehicle, or a rental or leased car does not trigger the automatic confiscation of that vehicle.

That does not leave the owner off the hook. A person who knowingly hands over the keys to someone disqualified from driving commits a separate offence carrying a fine of 100 to 1,000 euros, plus surcharges, under Articles 32 and 49 of the traffic law. The vehicle may also be immobilised or seized during proceedings, and insurers have a right of recourse against the policyholder under Article 47, section 2, of the Royal Decree of 16 April 2018.

The problem is verifying a driver's status in the first place. There is no accessible database allowing an individual, an employer, or a rental company to confirm that a person is not disqualified. The central licence register, known as Mercurius, is reserved for authorities and municipalities. Legalex notes that case law interprets "knowingly" broadly — including negligent failure to check — meaning fleet managers and employers must document how they verify a driver's licence.

## A piece of a wider enforcement push

The measure entered into force the same day as a package of other Belgian changes, from a 2-euro processing fee on small parcels arriving from outside the EU to a revised infant vaccination schedule in the Fédération Wallonie-Bruxelles. The confiscation rule, however, is the one with the sharpest teeth for road users.

It arrives amid a broader crackdown on repeat traffic offenders in Belgium, with lawmakers framing the reform as a deterrent against those who treat a driving ban as a minor inconvenience. The parliamentary proposal was adopted in committee on 27 January 2026 before the full law followed in March.

## What to watch

The law's practical bite will depend on how judges use their remaining discretion — and on how often "motivated" refusals to confiscate stand up on appeal. Enforcement, meanwhile, is handled by local police zones and prosecuted by the various public prosecutors' offices, so the pace of application may vary from one region to another.

For drivers, the message is straightforward: in Belgium, losing the right to drive now means losing the car too.
