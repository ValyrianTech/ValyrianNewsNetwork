# Oil Profits Soar Again as Iran War Lifts Gas Prices

Major oil companies reported another quarter of surging profits as the war in Iran keeps crude and refined-product prices elevated, renewing political attention on the widening gap between energy-industry earnings and what American drivers pay at the pump. ExxonMobil, Chevron and Shell together booked just over $36 billion in third-quarter profit, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/31/nx-s1-6008072/oil-company-profits-gasoline-diesel-prices), a result that has thrust the question of a windfall profits tax back into the national debate just days before the midterm elections.

## What the Numbers Show

ExxonMobil reported third-quarter earnings of $15.4 billion, or $3.72 per share, with adjusted earnings reaching $17.5 billion, or $4.23 per share, according to the company's [official release](https://investor.exxonmobil.com/company-information/press-releases/detail/1213/exxonmobil-announces-third-quarter-2026-results). That more than doubled the roughly $7.5 billion reported in the same quarter a year earlier. Chevron posted net income of $9.9 billion, or $5.06 per share, up about 180% from $3.5 billion a year earlier, while Shell reported $10.8 billion, nearly doubling its prior-year result and marking one of its largest buybacks ever.

The unusual driver this quarter is refining, not drilling. Exxon's refining segment earned roughly $7.2 billion, edging past its production profit of about $7.1 billion, with refining profits up nearly 200% year to date. Chevron's refining division profit surged more than 300% to $4.9 billion, as reported by [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/30/chevron-exxon-cvx-xom-earnings-q3-2026.html), with U.S. refineries running at 98% capacity and processing just over one million barrels a day.

## Why Prices Are High

The supply disruption traces to March 2026, when the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran, taking millions of barrels per day of refining capacity offline, particularly in the Middle East. Ukraine's strikes on Russian refineries compounded the squeeze. Because fuel demand is highly inelastic — needed for heating, driving, farming and trucking — prices climbed sharply. Brent crude has traded around $113 a barrel, compared with roughly $70 before the war began.

The impact at the pump has been severe. Gasoline is roughly $1.20 a gallon higher than a year earlier, and U.S. diesel averaged $6.32 a gallon nationally on Oct. 30 — up more than 70% year over year, according to AAA, after hitting a record high in September.

"The majors are able to sell their barrels that they produce in the U.S. or Latin America or Europe for much higher prices," Dan Pickering, founder of Pickering Energy Partners, told NPR. "So the Middle East's loss is generally the gain of oil and gas companies in the U.S. and Europe."

ExxonMobil CEO Darren Woods acknowledged the strain. "We recognize the impact high prices have on people's lives and economic growth," he said, adding that "with products in short supply, our teams have done everything they can to keep prices down by maximizing production." Woods told CNBC that "refining capacity is very tight today" and that "diesel is the key crunch point here because it is so critical to economic growth." Chevron CEO Mike Wirth said, "What we can do is what we can control, and that's help consumers get as much as they possibly can out of our systems," while warning that "the system is under stress."

## The Windfall Tax Debate

The scale of the profits has revived calls to tax them. Three separate bills in Congress seek to capture a share of the gains, including a Senate measure sponsored by Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.) and House proposals from Brad Sherman and Ro Khanna, both California Democrats. Advocates argue the profits are unearned — driven by geopolitics rather than innovation — and should be redistributed to households squeezed by a cost-of-living crisis.

"The companies that are running this essential sector have been reaping record profits amidst the worst energy supply crisis that we have had in several decades," economist Isabella Weber of UMass Amherst told NPR. "When emergencies generate massive windfall profits," she added, it creates "this crazy situation where the worst of times ends up being the best of times."

Industry executives reject the approach. Woods has previously called a windfall tax a "misguided policy" that penalizes companies trying to succeed in a volatile industry. History offers a cautionary note: the U.S. Crude Oil Windfall Profit Tax of 1980 raised far less revenue than the government had projected, largely because oil prices collapsed in the mid-1980s, and it was repealed in 1988, according to [NPR reporting](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/16/nx-s1-5845818/oil-prices-windfall-tax-iran-war) on earlier attempts to tax oil windfalls.

## Cash in the Bank, Not in the Ground

Rather than plowing profits into new drilling, the majors are returning cash to shareholders and holding reserves. Exxon generated $25.7 billion in operating cash flow and $19.1 billion in free cash flow in the quarter, distributing $9.4 billion through dividends and buybacks, and announced $20 billion in 2027 share repurchases. Analysts say the posture reflects a belief that high prices are geopolitically driven and potentially temporary.

"This is a period where the high prices are driven more by geopolitical events than anything else," Pickering said. "You put the money in the bank and watch to see what happens as we move into 2027."

Wood Mackenzie estimates the global oil and gas industry is on course for a $495 billion cash windfall in 2026, according to its [mid-year outlook](https://www.woodmac.com/press-releases/wood-mackenzie-mid-year-outlook-oil-and-gas-industry-cash-rich-but-capital-cautious-as-nearly-half-a-trillion-dollar-windfall-fails-to-trigger-a-spending-surge/).

## What to Watch

The political temperature is unlikely to cool soon. President Donald Trump said in August that Chevron and Exxon "were making too much money based on a shortage," and the midterm elections on Tuesday will test how much voter anger over fuel costs shapes the outcome. Meanwhile, the Trump administration has loosened rules on dyed diesel and waived sanctions to allow more Russian diesel into world markets, though experts doubt these moves will meaningfully lower U.S. prices in the near term.

With global inventories drawn down and the Strategic Petroleum Reserve at low levels, Shell CEO Wael Sawan warned the refill will not be quick. "Inventory levels are being drawn down very quickly, and it's going to take quite some time to be able to refill that inventory," he said. For consumers and policymakers alike, the question is how long the wartime windfall — and the pain at the pump that fuels it — will last.
