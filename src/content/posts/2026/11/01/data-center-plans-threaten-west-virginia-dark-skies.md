# Data Center Plans Threaten West Virginia Wilderness and Dark Skies

DAVIS, W.Va. — Plans for a vast data center and power plant complex in Tucker County are drawing fierce resistance from residents who say the project would scar a celebrated stretch of mountain wilderness famous for its ski slopes, hiking trails and some of the darkest night skies left in the eastern United States.

The proposal, known as Ridgeline and developed by Virginia-based Fundamental Data LLC, has become a flashpoint in the national debate over the rapid buildout of the energy-hungry infrastructure powering artificial intelligence — a fight that, [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/31/nx-s1-5992790/data-centers-west-virginia-skiing-dark-skies), pits economic development against conservation and has left locals feeling shut out of the decisions shaping their own communities.

## A project on a massive scale

The first phase of Ridgeline is slated to be a roughly 785-megawatt natural gas-fired generating plant, with diesel as a backup fuel, built on a reclaimed coal-mining site. Fundamental Data describes the venture as "a large-scale data and energy infrastructure project designed to support artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and enterprise systems."

The initial footprint covers 291 acres, but plans could eventually expand to as much as 10,000 acres — roughly 15 square miles — potentially spilling into neighboring Grant County, local news reports indicate. Casey Chapman, listed in court documents as the company's "responsible individual," declined to specify the facility's final size to NPR, saying only that "if fully developed … it would be thousands of acres."

The site sits adjacent to the nearly million-acre Monongahela National Forest, near Spruce Knob — the state's highest point — and Blackwater Falls State Park. The region is one of the few remaining dark-sky pockets in the eastern U.S., and Blackwater Falls is pursuing official dark-sky certification.

An analysis by the Northern Virginia Astronomy Club found that Ridgeline's initial footprint could increase sky glow near Blackwater by 528%, transforming its rural night sky into one comparable to a suburban area. The same analysis found only minimal impact on Spruce Knob.

Chapman says the company will use "well-designed" lighting to limit sky glow, but he insists that safety and security take precedence at "an industrial plant that runs around the clock." "Crews need proper lighting to operate and maintain equipment safely," he said.

## Echoes of an extractive past

The fight carries particular weight in a region whose history is bound up with resource extraction. At the turn of the 20th century, Davis was nicknamed "Stump Town" after logging companies clear-cut the valley, and coal mining later left the nearby Blackwater River acidic. A century of regrowth and conservation has only begun to heal the land.

Residents say the data center feels like a new chapter in that same story. "They want to sell us out, … extract our resources and leave us with the mess," said Nikki Forrester, a spokesperson for Tucker United, the grassroots group leading the opposition.

"If you're an outdoor person, this place is paradise," said Chip Chase, who co-founded the White Grass Ski Touring Center 45 years ago. "People come here to get away from data centers. People want dark skies here. People want to have a peaceful, quiet recreational area."

Opponents also cite environmental and health risks. Forrester points to Canaan Valley's bowl shape and temperature inversions, which can trap pollutants near the ground, and criticizes the storage of some 30 million gallons of diesel "right on a creek that feeds into our water supplies." Chapman counters that the storage would be safe and that not all tanks would need to be full at once.

A study commissioned by opponents estimates the plant could cause $19 million to $35 million a year in health-related damages and premature deaths. Chapman says that analysis assumes the plant emits at its maximum permitted level for a full year, when in fact the permit sets an upper limit and "we're going to operate within those limits."

## The fight over local control

The dispute has become as much about who gets a say as about any single environmental metric. In April 2025, West Virginia's Republican supermajority passed House Bill 2014, exempting certified microgrid districts and high-impact data centers from county and municipal zoning and local restrictions on noise, viewshed, lighting and land use.

Signing the measure, Republican Gov. Patrick Morrisey said the state must "continue to focus on economic development and utilizing our many natural resources," adding that companies "will now be able to quickly build, expand, and increase job creation right here in West Virginia."

Opponents gathered more than 1,000 signatures on a petition urging a veto, to no avail. They later challenged the air-quality permit approved by the state Department of Environmental Protection, but their appeal was dismissed in August on jurisdictional grounds; the groups say they will take the case to the West Virginia Supreme Court.

Critics have also raised conflict-of-interest concerns over House Speaker Roger Hanshaw, a Charleston attorney whose firm, Bowles Rice, is listed as counsel for Fundamental Data in the permit dispute. "Here's a guy who's writing laws [and] sponsoring legislation while under contract for one of the people that he's working for," said Davis resident Gary Berti. Hanshaw told NPR that he has never been employed by the company and that it is "public record" his law firm is counsel in a pending litigation matter.

## Jobs versus a way of life

Supporters argue the project offers a lifeline to one of the nation's poorest states. Fundamental Data says Ridgeline would bring thousands of temporary construction jobs and about 500 permanent positions. A Brookings Institution study, however, found that data centers on average produce roughly 100 to 200 permanent jobs.

John Deskins, director of the West Virginia University Bureau of Business and Economic Research, said data centers could be a boon "as long as we're making sure that we follow all the proper procedures around noise pollution, water pollution, [and] other elements of environmental pollution."

Tucker County Sheriff Jake Kopec, who supports the project, says much of the criticism amounts to fear-mongering. "I really believe that you would drive past it and not even know it's there," he said, though he acknowledges the dark skies will be less dark. The county has more immediate needs, he argues: better-paying jobs and tax revenue for essential services. "We have no cops on after midnight in this county, but we serve a million-plus visitors a year."

## What to watch

Davis Mayor Alan Tomson said the developer may have underestimated the community. "This is a very educated community," he said. "Many of the people here that have retired had very high-level scientific and technological careers elsewhere, and they bring that knowledge and expertise to bear on this situation."

Forrester draws hope from the region's history of successful environmental fights, including 1970s campaigns that halted a hydroelectric project that would have flooded Canaan Valley and coal exploration in the Monongahela National Forest. She noted that Morrisey has since proposed a 20-year "responsible data center" plan — a modest step, she said, but something.

The coming months will hinge on the Supreme Court appeal and on whether the company builds out to its full planned scale. For Tucker County, the larger question — whether rural communities have any real say over the AI boom reshaping them — remains unresolved.
