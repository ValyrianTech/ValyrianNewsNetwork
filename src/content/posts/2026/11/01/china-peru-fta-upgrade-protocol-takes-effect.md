# China-Peru FTA Upgrade Protocol Takes Effect

The Protocol to Upgrade the China-Peru Free Trade Agreement officially entered into force on 1 November 2026, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/world/20261101/98dac4a18c784257a1aca5afd6d3c9c5/c.html). The measure deepens trade and economic ties between the two countries by modernizing seven existing chapters of the agreement and adding five new ones, with the stated aim of expanding market access and cooperation under the bilateral framework.

The change formalizes a diplomatic milestone signed nearly two years ago and cements China's position as Peru's largest trading partner, a relationship that grew roughly sevenfold in trade value since the original deal took effect.

## From Template to Upgrade

The original China-Peru FTA was signed in April 2009 and entered into force in 2010, making it China's first comprehensive free trade agreement with a Latin American country. It served as a template for Beijing's subsequent commercial push across the region.

Upgrade negotiations were launched in November 2018 and substantively concluded in June 2024. The protocol itself was signed on 14 November 2024 in Lima, during Chinese President Xi Jinping's state visit to Peru, which coincided with the APEC summit and the inauguration of the Chinese-invested Chancay mega-port north of the capital.

Peru completed its domestic legal process through Supreme Decree No. 012-2026-MINCETUR, and [Andina](https://andina.pe/agencia/noticia-el-protocolo-optimizacion-del-tlc-peruchina-entrara-vigencia-1-noviembre-1097731.aspx), Peru's state news agency, reported that the entry into force was formalized after the exchange of diplomatic notifications between Lima and Beijing was completed.

## What Actually Changes

The protocol modernizes seven existing chapters: rules of origin; customs procedures and trade facilitation; trade in services; temporary entry of business persons; investment; intellectual property; and sanitary and phytosanitary measures.

It adds five new chapters covering standards and conformity-assessment cooperation; competition policy; electronic commerce; global supply chains; and environment and trade. The parties also set future-work clauses on negative-list negotiations for services and investment, intended to raise openness further in those areas.

Among the concrete benefits highlighted by Andina are improved provisions on certificates of origin and direct transport, stronger customs cooperation, and a push on e-commerce. The deal also seeks to promote investment in transport and to encourage Peruvian-Chinese audiovisual co-productions. On intellectual property, it strengthens protection for traditional knowledge and genetic resources.

"The modernization of the FTA will allow us to continue strengthening this strategic relationship, boost the competitiveness of our products and generate new opportunities for Peruvian exporters," said Peru's Minister of Foreign Trade and Tourism, Rogers Valencia.

## Trade Flows Already Accelerating

The economic stakes are substantial. Bilateral trade grew from US$6.4 billion in 2009 to US$50.4 billion in 2025, according to Xinhua. Valencia noted that China accounts for about 35 percent of Peru's trade, making it the country's leading commercial partner and principal export destination.

The momentum carried into 2026. In the first quarter, Peruvian exports to China reached US$10.748 billion, up 42.6 percent year-on-year, according to Andina. Non-traditional exports—a key target of the upgrade—reached roughly US$297 million, up 83.7 percent, driven by higher sales of agricultural and fisheries products. Peruvian imports from China rose 19.4 percent to US$5.003 billion, led by autos and heavy machinery, steel, IT products, and wood and paper.

"After the protocol takes effect, it will help further tap bilateral trade and investment cooperation opportunities, provide a better business environment for enterprises of both sides, and inject new vitality into the economic and trade relations of the two countries," Xinhua quoted commerce authorities as saying.

## A Wider Strategic Arc

The upgrade lands at a charged geopolitical moment. [China en las Américas](https://chinalasamericas.com/2026/07/27/peru-ratifica-acuerdo-tlc-optimizado-con-china-ante-cambio-de-mando-y-nuevos-aranceles-de-eeuu/), a research outlet tracking Beijing's regional engagement, noted that Peru ratified the protocol in July 2026, just before the presidential transition to Keiko Fujimori's government and amid a new round of US tariffs affecting Latin American imports, including Peruvian goods.

That timing suggests continuity in Peru's China policy across administrations even as Washington presses its own trade agenda. The ratification also acquires operational weight alongside the consolidation of the Chancay port, which analysts say could position Peru as a leading logistics node between South America and Asia-Pacific—precisely the transport and supply-chain provisions the upgraded FTA aims to support.

For Beijing, the deal reinforces a narrative it has promoted for years: that it can offer high-standard trade frameworks—including environment and competition-policy chapters—to partners wary of US-led decoupling pressures. The question is whether the upgrade functions as a serious template or a symbolic gesture.

## What to Watch

The protocol's practical impact will depend on implementation. How quickly customs systems and e-commerce rules adapt, whether the promised negative-list talks on services and investment deliver real liberalization, and how US tariff policy reshapes Peru-China flows are all open questions.

What is clear is the direction of travel. A trade relationship that began in 2009 as China's first comprehensive Latin American FTA has now been modernized for a digital, supply-chain-conscious era—and both partners are betting that closer integration serves their interests.

For Peruvian exporters, especially small and medium-sized businesses in agri-food and fisheries, the promise is tangible market access. For China, the prize is a deeper, more predictable foothold in South America's economic geography. Whether that bet pays off will be measured not in the protocol's text, but in the trade figures of the quarters ahead.
