# Judge Tosses FBI Case, Assails DOJ 'Weaponization'

A federal magistrate judge on Monday dismissed the Justice Department's criminal case against two former senior FBI officials accused of lying to Congress about the bureau's investigations into Hunter Biden, delivering a scathing ruling from the bench that accused prosecutors of "weaponization" and of timing the charges to influence the midterm elections held the following day.

U.S. Magistrate Judge Zia Faruqui, sitting in federal court in Washington, D.C., found that prosecutors failed to show probable cause that Nikki Cole Floris and Bradley Benavides knowingly made false statements to the House Judiciary Committee in 2023, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/02/g-s1-151242/judge-tosses-case-fbi-officials-hunter-biden). The ruling came after a days-long preliminary hearing featuring testimony from an FBI agent who investigated the case.

"I am deeply troubled by what has happened here," Faruqui said. "What transpired here cannot be described as anything other than weaponization."

## The Charges and the Underlying Testimony

Floris, 46, of Alexandria, Virginia, led the FBI's Foreign Influence Task Force (FITF) as a deputy assistant director from mid-2019 through mid-2022. Benavides, 56, of Aiken, South Carolina, served as the task force's section chief from mid-2019 through the 2020 election. Both surrendered on arrest warrants on October 23 after prosecutors filed a criminal complaint charging each with making false statements in a matter within the jurisdiction of the legislative branch, under 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(2).

According to a [Justice Department press release](https://www.justice.gov/usao-dc/pr/two-former-fbi-officials-charged-lying-congress-about-hunter-biden-investigations), Floris told the committee she was involved in no discussions about Hunter Biden while at the FITF, and Benavides testified that he only learned of the FBI's possession of Hunter Biden's laptop from reading a Wikipedia article in 2023. Prosecutors alleged that documentary evidence, contemporaneous communications and witness accounts contradicted both accounts.

At the October 23 announcement, U.S. Attorney Jeanine Ferris Pirro said that lying to Congress "strikes at the heart of our democratic system," adding that "when senior FBI agents lie to Congress believing they are above the law, it's time to bring the hammer of justice down on them." FBI Director Kash Patel said one of the two "is on record privately claiming to be 'running a shadow government' during the 2020 election," and warned that "weaponization will never be tolerated at this FBI."

## A Ruling That Read the Calendar

Faruqui's decision turned heavily on timing and process. He noted that the alleged false statements were made in 2023, yet the Justice Department waited three years before pursuing the case — then brought charges roughly ten days before the midterms, despite a departmental policy prohibiting the timing of any action to influence an election.

He also questioned the use of a criminal complaint rather than a grand jury indictment. Complaints, he noted, are normally reserved for situations demanding urgency — an imminent danger, a statute-of-limitations deadline, or a risk of flight — none of which applied here. The only looming event, he said, was the election itself.

"I will not bury my head in the sand and pretend I cannot read a calendar," Faruqui said. "I will not lobotomize myself that way."

As [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/nov/02/judge-tosses-case-fbi-hunter-biden) reported, the judge also said that some of the officials' statements were not actually false and that others could be attributed to "honest mistakes of memory."

## How the Investigation Began

The case had an unusual origin. According to testimony, the probe into Floris and Benavides was opened on Patel's orders the day after President Trump delivered a primetime address on election security in mid-July 2026, in which he repeated false claims about fraud in the 2020 election and urged the FBI to investigate. Patel then tasked the bureau's Kansas City field office with scrutinizing Floris, who had previously cast doubt on an FBI intelligence report claiming that China sought to interfere in the 2020 election to boost Joe Biden over Trump.

The circumstances were sensitive on another level: Floris and Benavides had served on the task force that, before the 2020 election, raised concerns that Russia could run a disinformation operation to help Trump by amplifying claims about Hunter Biden's laptop.

## The Grand Jury Question

Faruqui suggested prosecutors appeared reluctant to bring the matter before a grand jury, perhaps mindful of the Justice Department's recent struggles in the District of Columbia, where grand juries have repeatedly declined to return indictments in high-profile, politically charged cases during the Trump administration.

The dismissal does not end the matter definitively. Prosecutors could seek an indictment through a grand jury, or they could appeal the ruling to the district's chief judge.

The Justice Department signaled it would not back down. A spokesperson said the department "disagrees with the Magistrate's decision, which remarkably appears to suggest that high-ranking government officials are immune from accountability for making materially false statements to Congress." The spokesperson added that the department is "evaluating all options" and that its actions "will continue to be guided by the facts, the evidence, and the law—not politics."

Outside the courtroom, Floris' attorney, Margaret Donovan, was emphatic. "This case should never have been brought. The government had no case," she said, describing the complaint as a "slap-dash" filing and a "Hunter Biden conspiracy fever dream." She added: "But facts still matter in a courtroom. The truth still matters in a courtroom. And that's what we say prevailed today."

## A Broader Pattern Under Scrutiny

The ruling lands at a politically charged moment. Since returning to office in January 2025, the Justice Department has pursued a series of indictments and complaints against figures Trump regards as adversaries, though several high-profile efforts have faltered. The decision also thrusts into public view a tension at the heart of the case: an FBI director who has vowed to root out "weaponization" presiding over an investigation that a federal judge has now labeled weaponization in its own right.

For the bureau's rank and file, the episode carries particular weight. The courtroom included a large contingent of former senior FBI officials who came to support the defendants, and emotional scenes followed the ruling as Floris embraced former colleagues.

The ruling does not resolve the underlying dispute over how the FITF handled the Hunter Biden inquiries, nor does it settle the broader fight over whether the Justice Department's pre-election actions were driven by law or politics. What it does establish is that a federal judge found the government's conduct wanting on both the facts and the calendar.

## What to Watch

The immediate question is whether prosecutors will attempt to revive the case before a grand jury or appeal to the district's chief judge. Either path would prolong a matter that has already become a flashpoint in the wider debate over the independence of federal law enforcement. With the midterm elections now underway, the political consequences of Faruqui's ruling — and of the prosecution it derailed — are only beginning to take shape.
