# Anti-Indian Hate Rises as 'Model Minority' Myth Fades

A 19-year-old man's alleged plot to carry out a mass shooting at a suburban Chicago Hindu temple has thrust a quiet but steep rise in anti-Indian and anti-Hindu hate into public view. Police in Palatine, Ill., say Alexandros Paporakis, a college student and Taco Bell cashier, tried to attack the Sri Venkateswara Swami (Balaji) Temple in Aurora, Ill., about 40 miles west of Chicago, on Oct. 11 — a plot that authorities say was averted only when his AR-style rifle jammed, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/03/nx-s1-6026418/from-model-minority-to-target-anti-indian-hate-on-the-rise).

## A Massacre Allegedly Averted

Paporakis has been charged in Illinois with making a terrorist threat and unlawful possession of a rifle, both felonies. He was ordered detained at an initial court hearing before Associate Judge Andreana Turano, the [Chicago Sun-Times](https://chicago.suntimes.com/crime/2026/10/15/gun-jammed-when-neo-nazi-tried-to-commit-mass-shooting-at-hindu-temple-in-aurora-prosecutors-say) reported. The charges are allegations and have not been proven in court.

According to prosecutors, the plot came to light only because Paporakis could not stay silent. Shortly after the rifle jammed, he sent messages to coworkers at a Taco Bell in Arlington Heights saying the man he tried to shoot was "certainly saved by his God today" and expressing an intent to "retry when I fix this," the Sun-Times reported. Two employees called 911.

A search of his apartment turned up an AR-style rifle, 300 rounds of ammunition, eight loaded magazines, an extended magazine, body armor, a gas can and items bearing Nazi symbols and anti-Indian slurs. In an eight-page manifesto, Paporakis allegedly described himself as "a sociopathic white supremacist Neo Nazi filled with hatred, fantasizing about mass murder nearly every day," referred to nonwhite people as "subhumans," and wrote that he had been planning an attack for two years — settling on the temple two months before.

"The seriousness of this alleged threat cannot be overstated," Palatine Police Chief William Nord said at a press conference.

The alleged attempt coincided with the start of Navaratri, a major nine-day Hindu festival. Among those inside the temple were U.S. Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi, D-Ill., and his family, including his parents. Krishnamoorthi learned of the incident later through media reports and has since led 105 lawmakers in urging FBI Director Kash Patel to investigate it as a federal hate crime.

## A Rhetoric That Moved From the Fringe

Researchers who track hate speech say the Aurora case did not emerge from nowhere. Two decades ago, the dominant narrative about Indian immigrants to the U.S. was glowing, casting a relatively small population as a "model minority." That script has since reversed, said Rohit Chopra, a professor of communications at Santa Clara University and a visiting fellow at Stanford's Center for South Asia.

"Something of this scale … has not been directed at South Asians before," Chopra told NPR.

Research by Prasiddha Sudhakar of the Network Contagion Research Institute traces much of the rhetoric to fringe platforms such as 4chan and Gab, where, around 2022, degrading memes began hardening into full-blown conspiracy theories — including a baseless "Brahmin Organized Government" that echoes the neo-Nazi "Zionist Organized Government" trope.

"It didn't start off as mainstream, maybe even like a year or a few years ago," Sudhakar said. "But what has actually become more important now is that this is reaching many different parts of society in a scale that we've never seen before."

That reach is measurable. Chopra's research for the Center for the Study of Organized Hate found that between January 2025 and June 2026, anti-South Asian hate speech on X was 240% higher than in January 2024. He ties the spikes to flashpoints in the immigration debate: Indian nationals account for more than 70% of H-1B visas, and each news cycle about the program — or a remark from a public figure — has coincided with a surge in online hate.

Sudhakar said the spikes also followed moments of rising Indian American prominence, including Parag Agrawal's appointment as Twitter CEO, Sriram Krishnan's selection as a White House AI policy adviser and Usha Vance becoming second lady.

## From Words to Violence

The concern among researchers is not confined to the internet. In September, a Sikh truck driver was stabbed 17 times at a Wyoming rest stop and survived — an attack advocates cite as evidence of rising rhetoric, though no motive has been officially confirmed. The Department of Homeland Security drew backlash that same month for a "Transformers"-themed post depicting a Sikh man with the words "Get off our roads, you don't know how to drive Mr. Singh," which it later deleted. And Krishnamoorthi said a Florida elected official called for him to be deported and told him to "take my curry and go home."

"This hateful rhetoric is now being translated into violence," Krishnamoorthi said, "and the Trump administration is fanning the flames."

Aurora Mayor John Laesch, speaking at a press conference hosted by civil rights groups, warned against letting fear take hold as the community continued its Navaratri celebrations. "We cannot be afraid as the voice of fascism grows louder," he said, "escalating a race war to distract us from the class war they are simultaneously waging."

## A Community New to Fighting Back

The alleged attack has intensified calls within Indian American and Hindu communities to organize and speak more publicly about racism. But advocates say that is easier said than done. Pushpita Prasad of the Coalition of Hindus of North America noted that Hindu temples do not function like congregations in other faiths.

"We don't belong to temples and we are not part of congregations," Prasad said. "So for us, organizing and finding our voice and sharing information is that much harder."

The community's apparent success — high median incomes and prominent figures from Vivek Ramaswamy to FBI Director Patel — masks a deeper vulnerability, Chopra argued. The South Asian community "does not have significant political power" commensurate with its visible success, he said, in part because many immigrants remain in "immigration limbo." According to Pew Research Center data, two-thirds of the Indian population in the U.S. are immigrants, and nearly half of those are not naturalized citizens — a status that can suppress public advocacy.

"A community that seems well off, at least in media chatter, is not as vocal or as organized as it probably should have been," Prasad said.

## What to Watch

India's Ministry of External Affairs has expressed "deep concern" over the alleged plot, calling it "particularly disturbing" and urging Washington to ensure the safety of Indian nationals in the country. Whether federal authorities open a hate-crime investigation — and how the case proceeds through Illinois courts — will be closely watched. For researchers, the larger question is whether a community long cast as a success story can build the organized voice needed to confront a threat that, by their data, is still climbing.
