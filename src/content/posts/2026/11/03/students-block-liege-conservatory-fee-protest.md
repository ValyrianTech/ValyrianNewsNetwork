# Students Block Liège Conservatory in Fee Protest

Students at the Royal Conservatory of Liège blockaded their institution from before 5:30 a.m. on Tuesday, the first day back after the autumn half-term, in a festive but pointed protest against rising tuition fees, the end of free tuition for young children in municipal academies and reforms to Belgium's artist status.

According to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-mobilisation-se-poursuit-a-liege-le-conservatoire-bloque-par-ses-etudiants-11809821), the students were on site early in the morning, ready for a full day of blockade. Many brought their instruments, and the plan was to keep the action going until at least 18:00, with the intent of disrupting some of the institution's scheduled competitions. The broadcaster reports that the action is part of a broader wave of youth mobilization across francophone Belgium, but with demands specific to the arts sector.

## Three Grievances

The first and most immediate grievance is the sharp rise in the enrolment fee, known in Belgium as the *minerval*. "At the conservatory, last year we went from roughly 200-300 euros to 1,200 euros," said Leïla, a 23-year-old second-year cello student, as reported by RTBF. "It's an enormous cost, with many students ending up juggling two or three student jobs on the side."

The second grievance concerns the end of free tuition in the academies for children under 12. "Far fewer pupils renewed their enrolment, so there are far fewer hours," Leïla added. "We know friends who have the ability to teach and who can't find work."

The third is the reform of the *statut d'artiste* — the special status that supports professional artists in Belgium. "It's getting more and more complicated to access it, and to renew it," Leïla said. The changes to access and renewal of artist status have been contentious in 2024-2026.

## Solidarity With the Lycée Movement

The conservatory blockade is explicitly framed as a show of solidarity with the secondary-school (*lycée*) movement, whose pupils have been blocking schools across francophone Belgium for weeks, according to RTBF. Liège has become the epicentre of that wider mobilization, and the conservatory students positioned their action as part of the same wave of youth protest rather than an isolated dispute.

## A Festive Form of Protest

Rather than a confrontation, the students chose a musical blockade. Many brought their instruments, and a day of activities was planned: a workshop on debt analysis around 10:00, a football match, an orchestral ensemble, a choir and an open *carte blanche* at 18:00 for anyone who wanted to play. Teachers were invited to give masterclasses outdoors, and a physiotherapist was on hand to support the movement.

The tone was summed up by Pauline, a classical guitar student. "We simply want culture and music to remain accessible to everyone," she told RTBF.

## Analysis: A Conflict Over Access

The Liège conservatory blockade is a microcosm of a wider conflict over the funding and social accessibility of culture and education in francophone Belgium. As RTBF reports, the arts sector is exposed on two fronts at once: its students pay the higher-education enrolment fee while its feeder institutions, the academies, lose pupils and teaching hours after the end of free tuition for under-12s. The artist-status reform adds a layer of employment precarity on top.

Choosing the first day back after the half-term break maximized visibility and disrupted competitions. The festive, musical character frames the dispute as one about culture rather than chaos, deliberately contrasting with the sharper confrontations that have marked the wider *lycée* movement in Liège.

The immediate question is whether the Liège blockade spreads to other conservatories or merges with other student actions already under way across francophone Belgium. For the students at the conservatory, Tuesday's instruments are both a protest and a promise: the mobilization, they say, is not over.
