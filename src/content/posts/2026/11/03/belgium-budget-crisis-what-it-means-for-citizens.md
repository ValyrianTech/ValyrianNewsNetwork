# Belgium's Budget Crisis: What It Means for Citizens

Belgium's federal government has failed to agree on a national budget, prompting Prime Minister Bart De Wever to offer his resignation to King Philippe. The monarchy is now holding that resignation "in beraad" — under advisement — while consulting party leaders, leaving the country officially in what VRT NWS political journalist Bart Verhulst called a "political crisis," as [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/02/geen-begrotingsakkoord-politieke-crisis-overzicht/) reported. Amid an outpouring of reader questions, [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/vragen-politieke-crisis/) published an explainer answering what citizens most want to know: why no deal was reached, and how the deadlock will affect them.

## What is actually happening?

At its core, the impasse is a fight over roughly €10 billion in budget savings needed to bring Belgium into line with European Union fiscal rules, according to [Brussels Morning](https://brusselsmorning.com/bart-de-wever-resigns-over-e10b-talks-in-belgium-2026/106392/). The five-party "Arizona" coalition — De Wever's N-VA, the Francophone liberals of MR, the centrist Les Engagés, the Flemish socialists of Vooruit, and the Flemish Christian Democrats of CD&V — split into two camps over how to find the money.

The conservative N-VA and liberal MR pushed for direct spending cuts, including tighter controls on healthcare and public administration. Vooruit, Les Engagés and CD&V argued that between €1 billion and €1.5 billion should instead come from higher taxes on high earners and wealth. "Vooruit wants more effort from the 'strongest shoulders,' just like Les Engagés and CD&V. On the other side you have N-VA and MR — a classic left-right divide," Verhulst told VRT NWS.

Negotiators broke up overnight without a deal. De Wever concluded that agreement was, for now, impossible and went to the King.

## Do we still have a government?

Yes — for now. By choosing to hold the resignation under advisement rather than accept it, King Philippe kept the government in place while buying time for one more attempt at a deal. "The King keeps his decision under advisement and will consult with the party chairmen of the various coalition partners from tomorrow," the Palace said in a statement. De Wever is expected back at the Palace on Friday.

The King's decision to withhold his approval surprised few observers. Accepting the resignation would mean "game over," said political scientist Carl Devos of Ghent University. "No one in this country believes new elections would offer a solution, because you know our history of long government formations." A year ago, [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/belgium-budget-wever-king-government-crisis-debt-d6e28f97ddcf13c3b358201c4d45cf7e) noted, De Wever faced a near-identical standoff and asked the King for more time instead of resigning.

## Why is a budget needed at all?

Belgium is one of the most heavily indebted countries in the 27-nation EU, alongside France and Italy. The European Commission expects the debt to rise to about 110% of GDP this year, AP reported. Under EU rules, member states are meant to keep deficits below 3% of GDP; Belgium's deficit hit 5% last year, with total public debt exceeding €735 billion. The interest on that debt weighs heavily at a time when the government also wants to increase defense spending — and the failure to resolve the talks has already pushed up Belgian bond yields, raising the state's borrowing costs.

## What is a 'budget of provisional twelfths'?

The VRT explainer devotes a section to the mechanism most likely to follow if no deal is reached: a "budget of provisional twelfths." Under this rule, if no annual budget is approved, the state may keep running month by month at the level of the previous year's budget — one-twelfth per month. Services continue, but new spending and investment are frozen.

De Wever wants to avoid this outcome at all costs, VRT NWS reported. But Devos predicted "we'll probably end up there anyway," and noted that even with a deal in the coming days, getting the budget through parliament in time would be difficult. Opposition figures have warned Belgium could be forced to operate under provisional monthly budgets for a second consecutive year.

## How will citizens feel it?

The practical effects are gradual rather than sudden. No annual budget means no room for new policy initiatives, subsidies or investment commitments until a deal is struck — a standstill that, as Groen's Matti Vandemaele argued, carries real costs. Against this backdrop, Belgium has already seen growing student protests and frequent strikes, including several "national action days" led by unions, AP reported. The uncertainty also feeds directly into household and business confidence at a moment of fiscal strain.

## What happens next?

The decisive moment comes on Friday, when the King receives De Wever again after consulting party leaders. Two paths remain: the coalition finds a last-minute compromise and heads to parliament, or the resignation is accepted — tipping Belgium toward a caretaker government and possibly early elections. Given the country's history of protracted government formations, most observers expect a final push to save the coalition rather than a return to the ballot box.
