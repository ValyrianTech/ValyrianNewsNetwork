# Financial Times Crowns Ghent Belgium's Coolest Destination

The Financial Times has named Ghent the "coolest destination in Belgium," casting an international spotlight on the Flemish city's design, art, technology and food scenes. The [feature, published October 30](https://www.ft.com/content/c88a7ee5-55de-4dc1-a043-f7906ef37f5e) in the FT's HTSI autumn travel issue, was picked up by [Het Laatste Nieuws](https://www.hln.be/gent/britse-krant-financial-times-noemt-gent-coolste-bestemming-van-belgie-van-restaurant-elders-tot-stadshal~afbd0969/) on November 3, amplifying the recognition among Belgian readers.

## A City That Colours Outside the Lines

Written by Chris Allnutt with photography by Louis Delbarre, the profile traces Ghent's evolution from a working-class textiles town into a countercultural student hub and now an international design destination. The duo Soulwax—brothers Stephen and David Dewaele, both Ghent natives—dub the transformation "Ghentrification."

"We like to colour outside the lines—that's inherently Flemish," Stephen Dewaele told the FT.

The article opens with a nod to the city's rebellious streak, recalling the 1539 revolt against Holy Roman Empire taxes, after which participants were forced to parade through the streets wearing nooses. Residents adopted the nickname *stroppendragers*—noose-bearers—a procession still restaged annually.

## Design and Institutions in the Spotlight

The centrepiece of the FT's tour is the Design Museum Gent, which reopened in October 2026 after a four-year renovation and is now the largest of its kind in Belgium. Its new wing, designed by architects Carmody Groarke, ATAMA and RE-ST, is built largely from biobased and circular materials—rubble from local demolitions, worn denim and reclaimed timber.

"In the past, a designer had a design and then found the materials for it," project manager Iris Dochy said. "Now it's, 'What do I have and what can I make from it?'"

The profile also spotlights a dense cluster of cultural anchors: the Herbert Foundation, housed in a former frame factory with a three-decade collection of late-20th-century art; the Smak contemporary art museum, which took over a former casino building in 1999; and Kunsthal Gent, an experimental space occupying a deconsecrated church and monastery since 2019.

Philippe van Cauteren, artistic director of Smak, pointed to the pivotal 1986 *Chambres d'Amis* exhibition—when residents opened their homes for installations by artists including Lawrence Weiner and Sol LeWitt—as the moment Ghent became a cultural destination.

## Tech Ambitions and a Decentralising Food Scene

Beyond art and design, the FT highlights Ghent's technology credentials: the city ranks 10th in the world for tech companies per capita. Its standard-bearer is Wintercircus, a 15,000-square-metre technology hub, incubator and cultural event space that reopened in 2024 in a former circus building and Fiat garage.

"We don't believe the future can be built behind closed doors or by the tech sector alone," said Dorien van Steenberge, the hub's CEO.

The FT also maps a food scene pushing beyond the tourist centre—into the northern Bloemekenswijk neighbourhood, home to restaurant Elders, and out toward Dampoort station, where bistro Alberte added a natural wine bar last year. Near the city's red-light district, Café Rendez-Vous opened in June.

"Els and I only say yes when a place genuinely feels right," proprietor Lino Van Reeth said. "That was immediately the case with this building."

## A City Divided by Its Own Icon

The FT does not shy away from controversy. It recounts the polarising reception of the Stadshal, the 20-metre-high City Pavilion completed in 2012, whose wooden roof is punctuated by 1,600 thin windows. "We weep for this sheep pen," said Hans Van Doorne, then-chair of the city's Urban Advisory Council.

Yet the article concludes that the pavilion has "weathered and grown into its surroundings," hosting concerts, markets and ice rinks—the embodiment of the alternative, international spirit the FT calls Ghentrification.

## Pride, With a Dose of Local Skepticism

The international accolade lands amid a more complicated domestic mood. In a separate HLN municipal report, [Ghent scored just 49 percent](https://www.hln.be/gent/gent-met-49-gebuisd-in-hln-gemeenterapport-dit-zegt-burgemeester-mathias-de-clercq-de-netheid-kan-beter-ik-stoor-me-daar-zelf-ook-enorm-aan~a96a6605/), with mayor Mathias De Clercq acknowledging that cleanliness in the city "can be better"—a point echoed in reader comments beneath the FT coverage, some of whom grumbled that Ghent is nicer to visit than to live in.

For the city, the FT feature is nonetheless a valuable endorsement, expected to raise Ghent's profile among foreign visitors. Chris Allnutt travelled as a guest of the city of Ghent and Pillows Grand Boutique Hotel Reylof.

What remains to be seen is whether the international buzz translates into sustainable tourism—and whether Ghent's residents, proud of their stubborn, outside-the-lines identity, will embrace the attention or push back against it.
