# Dioxin Crisis Closed After 27 Years

One of Belgium's biggest food scandals has reached its final legal chapter. On Tuesday, the Ghent criminal court ratified a civil settlement between Willaert Activities—an animal-feed company from Kortemark in West Flanders—and the surviving relatives of the Verkest family, the fat-melting operators at the centre of the 1999 dioxin crisis. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/03/dioxinecrisis-burgerlijke-partij-willaert-activities-afgehandeld/), the agreement is likely the last outstanding case against the Verkest heirs, drawing a 27-year legal saga to a close.

## A Scandal That Shook the Country

The crisis erupted on 27 May 1999, when VRT's Het Journaal revealed that carcinogenic dioxins had entered the Belgian food chain. As [VRT NWS later recounted](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2018/09/11/de-wie-wat-waar-van-de-dioxinecrisis-de-voedselcrisis-die-ons/), contaminated fat containing toxic PCBs had been processed into feed meal. Chickens laid fewer eggs and died en masse, and heavily contaminated eggs and meat had already been eaten widely by the public. The trace led to the Verkest fat-melting plant in Deinze, which had received contaminated fat from the Walloon company Fogra.

The fallout was immense—politically as well as economically. The government failed to bring the crisis under control, and the CVP (now CD&V) suffered a heavy election defeat that reshaped Belgian political relations. The scandal is still regarded as the largest food scandal the country has ever known and helped spur the creation of the Federal Food Agency (FAVV).

## The Legal Path to Closure

The criminal side of the case concluded in 2010, when the Ghent Court of Appeal convicted Jan and Lucien Verkest, sentencing each to two years' imprisonment, half suspended. But the civil damages claims were kept separate and repeatedly postponed. Willaert Activities, which had used contaminated fats to make feed and suffered losses as a result, had filed as a civil party back in 1999.

"But the damages determination for my client was initially postponed—it remained with the judiciary, in other words," said Stijn De Meulenaer, the company's lawyer. "Only upon the reactivation of the file in 2018 did we join as a law firm." A prolonged procedural battle followed over the justified character of the damages claim, fought all the way to the Court of Cassation.

The breakthrough, De Meulenaer suggested, came through weariness rather than any single ruling. "My client wanted to put an end to it for longer—but for that you need two, of course," he said. "Time did much. Eventually fatigue set in on both sides, allowing us to sit at the table to reach an agreement."

## A Burden Lifted, an Amount Undisclosed

Both Jan and Lucien Verkest have died—Jan in 2019, and Lucien in September 2023 at the age of 88. The settlement was therefore reached with their surviving relatives. Its amount has not been made public. "It is certainly not a dream amount," De Meulenaer emphasised, adding that after 27 years "a burden falls from my client's shoulders."

The Ghent court ratified the agreement on Tuesday morning and closed the case.

## Why It Took So Long

The case lays bare the slow pace of Belgian civil proceedings, particularly where mass food-safety scandals are involved. Multiple claimants, deceased defendants and complex questions of causation combined to keep the damages phase alive long after the criminal verdict. A 2018 ruling, in which the Ghent court ordered Verkest and Fogra to pay 24 million euros in damages to the FAVV, provided a major milestone in accountability—but private claims such as Willaert's required separate resolution.

## What's Next

With this settlement, all legal proceedings linked to the dioxin crisis appear to be concluded, though the exact amount remains confidential. Whether any dormant claim might resurface is unclear, but the lawyer considers this likely the final case against the Verkest heirs. For Belgium, the closure is less a dramatic finale than a quiet administrative footnote to a scandal that reshaped its politics, its food-safety institutions and its public memory.
