# Lijian-2 Rocket Aces Second Flight, Lofts Qianfan Satellites

China's Lijian-2 Y2 carrier rocket lifted off from the Dongfeng Commercial Aerospace Innovation Test Area at 8:15 a.m. Beijing time on November 3 and successfully placed the Qianfan polar-orbit group 21 satellites into their designated orbit, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261103/2aeea17092b14c4a883140ba4858cbec/c.html). The state news agency described the flight test mission as a complete success, marking the second flight of the medium-lift vehicle that China hopes will become a workhorse for deploying its low-orbit satellite internet megaconstellations.

The launch was datelined from Jiuquan and reported by Xinhua journalists Li Guoli and Zhang Yan, with photographs credited to Wang Jiangbo. Xinhua did not disclose further details about the payload beyond the group designation, nor the total number of Qianfan satellites now in orbit.

## A New Rocket Built for High-Frequency Launch

The Lijian-2, also known as Kinetica-2, is developed by CAS Space, a commercial spin-off rooted in the Chinese Academy of Sciences. It is a two-and-a-half-stage, liquid-oxygen/kerosene launch vehicle and China's first rocket to use a "common booster core" configuration, in which three essentially identical core stages can be assembled much like building blocks to boost lift capacity while streamlining mass production and reducing costs, according to [China Review News](https://bj.crntt.com/doc/1072/4/7/6/107247654.html).

The rocket has a maximum payload capacity of about 12 tonnes to low Earth orbit, allowing it to launch multiple satellites at once or carry cargo spacecraft resupply missions. CAS Space has positioned the vehicle as a high-capacity, low-cost, high-frequency launcher tailored for megaconstellation deployment, and it has been selected as the primary rocket for China's space station low-cost cargo resupply effort.

Ahead of the launch, Li Qinfeng, deputy chief designer of the Lijian-2 rocket at CAS Space, said the Y2 vehicle had completed assembly and testing at the launch site and had been transferred to its dedicated pad to begin interface checks and full rehearsal, with propellant loading and final launch procedures to follow. He noted that the rocket would carry out a low-orbit constellation deployment mission as planned.

The successful flight follows the rocket's maiden launch on March 30, 2026, which placed the Xin Zhengcheng series satellites and the Qingzhou cargo spacecraft prototype into orbit. CAS Space announced in August that the Y2 vehicle had completed pre-factory work and had originally targeted an October launch, and the rocket was transported to its pad on October 26.

## The Qianfan Constellation and China's Satellite Internet Ambitions

The payload, Qianfan polar-orbit group 21 satellites, belongs to the Qianfan constellation (G60), a low-Earth-orbit broadband network built by Shanghai Yuanxin Satellite Technology. The project envisions more than 15,000 satellites in orbit by around 2030 and is widely described as China's answer to SpaceX's Starlink.

The constellation began deployment with a first batch of 18 satellites launched in August 2024 aboard a Long March 6A rocket from Taiyuan. It has expanded rapidly since, with roughly 200 satellites placed in orbit by late 2026 and a stated goal of initial global coverage within the year. The polar-orbit series is used within the Qianfan network.

## A Crowded Commercial Space Race

The launch comes as China's commercial space sector enters what analysts describe as a dense launch period. Lijian-2's orbital success has been framed as opening the era of heavy-lift commercial rockets in the country, with competitors ramping up their own programs. Oriental Space's Gravity-2 is planned for a first flight in 2026 with roughly 21.5 tonnes of LEO capacity and a recoverable core stage, while Landspace continues to advance its Zhuque-2 and Zhuque-3 series.

Rival megaconstellations are also taking shape. China SatNet's GW constellation targets around 13,000 satellites, and the Honghu network led by Landspace's Hongqing Technology aims for roughly 10,000. CAS Space itself is pursuing an initial public offering on the STAR Market, underscoring the commercial stakes behind each successful flight.

## What's Next

The successful second flight gives CAS Space a proven vehicle just as demand for constellation deployment accelerates. The company has signaled that Lijian-2 will soon carry out its first large-scale, multi-satellite deployment mission for a low-orbit constellation, a step that would test the rocket's ability to sustain the high launch cadence the Qianfan and other megaconstellation programs require.

For China's satellite internet ambitions, the key question is cadence. Building a network of thousands of satellites depends less on any single launch than on a steady rhythm of them, and the Lijian-2's second consecutive success is a signal that the country's commercial launch sector is moving toward that goal. Whether it can match the pace set by established operators, and do so at competitive cost, will determine how quickly the Qianfan constellation closes the gap.
