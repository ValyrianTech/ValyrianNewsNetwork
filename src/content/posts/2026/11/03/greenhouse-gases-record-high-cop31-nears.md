# Greenhouse Gases Hit Record High as COP31 Nears

Atmospheric concentrations of the three main long-lived greenhouse gases linked to human activity all reached new record highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization said, warning that the buildup has locked the planet into further warming for decades to come. Carbon dioxide reached a globally averaged surface concentration of 426.8 parts per million — 153% of the pre-industrial baseline of 1750 — according to the [WMO's Greenhouse Gas Bulletin](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high), released in Geneva on 27 October 2026.

"Another year, another new record," said WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett. "Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies."

The findings land two weeks before negotiators gather in Antalya, Türkiye, for the COP31 UN climate conference, where the science is intended to sharpen pressure on governments to turn commitments into action.

## A Record That Keeps Breaking

The Bulletin reports the accumulated amount of gas remaining in the atmosphere, not annual emissions. That distinction is central: because CO2 persists for decades to centuries, roughly half of every year's emissions stays aloft, meaning concentrations can keep climbing even as emissions slow.

The long-term trend is stark. According to the [WMO Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22](https://wmo.int/resources/publication-series/greenhouse-gas-bulletin/no-22), CO2 growth rates have tripled since the 1960s — from an annual average of 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year across the 2011–2020 decade. When the Bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 measured by WMO's Global Atmosphere Watch network stood at 377.5 ppm, roughly 49 ppm lower than today.

From 2024 to 2025, the global average CO2 concentration rose by 2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge of 2023/2024. Methane reached 1,948 parts per billion, or 267% of the 1750 level, while nitrous oxide hit 339.1 ppb, or 126% of the pre-industrial baseline.

The warming effect tells a parallel story. NOAA's Annual Greenhouse Gas Index shows that radiative forcing from long-lived greenhouse gases rose by 56% between 1990 and 2025, with CO2 accounting for about 81% of that increase. The past 11 years have been the warmest on record.

## Why the Rise Slowed — and Why It Won't Last

The more modest 2024-to-2025 increase reflects a partial recovery of land carbon sinks and reduced wildfire emissions after the strong 2023/2024 El Niño, which had degraded the biosphere through drought, drier vegetation and more fires, [Xinhua reported](https://english.news.cn/20261027/fae5d4b487804cb481563ec9159b6f8a/c.html).

"However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," said WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova. She warned that concentrations will likely rise further in 2026 as fossil fuel burning continues and a powerful El Niño develops, again weakening land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024. The risk of reduced CO2 uptake by the oceans — the natural sinks that absorb roughly half of human emissions — is also growing, a potential feedback loop in a warming climate.

## Two Gases in the Spotlight

The Bulletin also flags two potent but less abundant trace gases tied to the energy transition. Sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), used mainly as an insulator in electricity distribution, has an atmospheric lifetime of over 1,000 years and a 100-year global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO2. Its increase in 2025 was a record high.

Molecular hydrogen, increasingly used as an energy storage medium for hard-to-decarbonise sectors such as steelmaking and shipping, rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024. It is not itself a greenhouse gas but influences atmospheric chemistry and has a strong indirect warming effect — a reminder that even clean-energy technologies carry monitoring risks.

## The COP31 Test

The Bulletin is timed as scientific input for the COP31 conference in Antalya, running 9–20 November 2026, with the [UNFCCC](https://unfccc.int/cop31) convening negotiators to advance national climate plans, finance, adaptation and loss and damage. The summit's Turkish presidency has made electrification a flagship goal, championing a "35-by-35" pledge to lift electricity to 35% of final energy demand by 2035 from about 23% today. Fossil fuels remain the central sticking point: COP30 in Belém ended without agreed wording on the issue.

Against a backdrop of record concentrations, the gap between commitments and outcomes will be hard to disguise. Rising concentrations are the physical driver behind the intensifying extreme weather, sea-level rise and cascading risks that the WMO references — and they continue to set records despite years of climate policy.

## What to Watch

The immediate test is whether the 2026 El Niño, combined with continued fossil fuel burning, pushes concentrations to yet another record. Longer term, the durability of the land and ocean carbon sinks will determine how quickly — or slowly — the atmosphere's load of heat-trapping gas begins to level off. For negotiators in Antalya, the WMO data offer a clear baseline: the curve is still bending the wrong way.
