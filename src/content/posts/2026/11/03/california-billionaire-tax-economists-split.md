# Two Economists Split Sharply on California Billionaire Tax

Californians are voting Tuesday on Proposition 40, a first-in-the-nation one-time 5% wealth tax on billionaires, and the economists who have studied it most closely cannot agree on whether it would enrich or drain the state. Gabriel Zucman of UC Berkeley estimates the measure would raise about $100 billion, while Stanford's Joshua Rauh calculates it would leave California $39 billion poorer, according to [NPR's Planet Money](https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2026/11/02/g-s1-151009/two-economists-studied-californias-billionaire-tax-they-reached-wildly-different-conclusions).

The gap between the two forecasts—more than $130 billion in the balance—has become the defining battle of one of the most expensive ballot fights in California history, and both sides see the vote as a potential template for national and even global tax policy.

## What the Measure Would Do

Proposition 40 would levy a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of California residents worth more than $1 billion, using a retroactive residency cutoff of January 1, 2026. Real estate, pensions, and most retirement accounts are generally excluded, and roughly 90% of the proceeds are earmarked for state-funded health care, including Medi-Cal, to help offset federal Medicaid cuts.

The measure was sponsored by the health care union SEIU United Healthcare Workers West and drafted by tax law professors and economist Emmanuel Saez. Its authors designed it with an eye toward past failures, taxing business holdings—including shares in companies billionaires run—and tying liability to residency on a fixed date, hoping to prevent the wealthy from simply moving away to escape the bill.

## Two Very Different Ledgers

Zucman, who co-authored an analysis of the proposal with Jasper Boll and Saez, estimates that California billionaire wealth reached $2.3 trillion by mid-2026 and grew 144% between 2023 and 2025, largely on the AI boom. Applying a 5% rate and reducing the result by 10% for avoidance and departures, they project roughly $100 billion in revenue with "comparatively minor impacts" on income tax receipts.

Rauh, working with Benjamin Jaros, reaches a starkly different conclusion. He argues that at least seven prominent billionaires—including Larry Page, Sergey Brin, Peter Thiel, and Steven Spielberg—had already left California before the January 1 cutoff, removing roughly $540 billion in wealth and cutting potential revenue by about $30 billion. He also halves the remaining projection to account for constitutional litigation, estimating the tax would bring in only around $30 billion before weighing longer-term losses.

Then comes the punchline of Rauh's analysis: if more billionaires flee in anticipation of future wealth taxes, the state could lose $1 billion to $3.5 billion a year in income tax revenue, which, sustained over time, produces his headline finding—a net cost to California of about $39 billion.

## A Clash Over Assumptions

At the heart of the dispute are disputes over human behavior and the law. Zucman contends that departing billionaires likely did not successfully shed their California tax residency before the cutoff, that the wealthy pay a relatively small share of state income taxes, and that Rauh's model counts hypothetical losses without crediting the revenue future wealth taxes would raise. "That's absurdly one-sided," Zucman told NPR. "You can't just count the loss and not the gain."

Rauh defends his methodology as standard tax scoring and warns that the measure threatens California's startup and tech ecosystem. "Agglomeration effects are fragile," he argued, predicting that investors would pull back from the state if the tax passes.

Yet the pull of Silicon Valley is exactly what Zucman counts on. He points to Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who told Bloomberg he was "perfectly fine" with paying the tax—under which he would owe roughly $8 billion—and to Mark Zuckerberg's reported arrangement with Meta's board to cover his liability without losing control of the company.

A nonpartisan baseline sits between the camps. California's Legislative Analyst's Office has estimated the measure could raise tens of billions of dollars over several years while reducing annual income tax revenue by less than $1 billion from departing billionaires.

## What's Next

The vote's outcome was not yet known as polls closed Tuesday, and even a victory for Proposition 40 would hardly settle the matter. Constitutional challenges over its retroactive and extraterritorial provisions are widely expected, and the fate of a rival measure, Proposition 42—which would bar retroactive and new personal property taxes—could further complicate the picture.

What is clear is that the California experiment has already become a symbol. Supporters, including Sen. Bernie Sanders, cast it as a test of whether the wealthiest can be asked to fund health care for everyone else. Opponents frame it as a warning about capital flight and economic competitiveness.

For now, the two economists' dueling ledgers will stand as the clearest illustration of just how unsettled the answer remains—and how much the nation's coming debate over wealth taxation may hinge on what California decides.
