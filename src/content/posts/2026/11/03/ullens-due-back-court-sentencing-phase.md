# Ullens due back in court as sentencing phase looms

Nicolas Ullens de Schooten is due to return to the defendant's dock at the Walloon Brabant Assize Court on Wednesday, as the trial of the former State Security agent enters its sentencing phase following his conviction for the premeditated murder of his stepmother, Baroness Myriam Lechien. The resumption on 4 November comes after the Court of Cassation rejected a defence recusal request that had suspended proceedings, clearing the way for arguments over his sentence, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/cour-d-assises-du-brabant-wallon-reprise-du-proces-de-nicolas-ullens-reconnu-coupable-d-assassinat-11809728).

The case, which has gripped Belgium for its combination of a prominent family, a fortune measured in billions and a killing carried out with chilling calculation, now turns on a single question: what penalty awaits a man already found guilty of *assassinat* — murder with premeditation.

## A killing rooted in a divided family

The facts are not in dispute. Ullens, 61, acknowledges firing six shots at his stepmother on 29 March 2023 at the family estate in Ohain, part of the municipality of Lasne in Walloon Brabant. According to RTBF, that morning he had gone to the home of his father, Baron Guy Ullens — a businessman and art collector whose family fortune was once estimated at roughly €3 billion — and his stepmother to discuss family and money "without apparent incident." He then retrieved a 9 mm Glock pistol from his van, waited for the couple as they left, rammed their car to block its passage and opened fire. Myriam Lechien, 70, died at the scene; Guy Ullens was slightly injured.

Behind the shooting lay a deep family rift. Ullens felt sidelined in favour of his stepmother and her children, and accused Lechien of having squandered part of the family fortune. Money runs through the file. A crucial voice was nonetheless absent from the trial: Guy Ullens, who witnessed the killing and was directly involved in the family disputes, died in April 2025 aged 90.

## The verdict that divided the jury

The central issue throughout the trial was premeditation — the distinction between *meurtre* (murder) and *assassinat* (premeditated murder). Ullens admitted the shooting but denied premeditating it. On 6 October 2026, after eight hours of deliberation, the jury found him guilty of *assassinat*, with the question of premeditation decided by a knife-edge vote of seven jurors to five, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/meurtre-de-la-baronne-ullens-nicolas-ullens-coupable-d-assassinat-le-jury-a-retenu-la-premeditation-11795981).

Before the jury retired, Ullens had apologised to Lechien's family, to his own relatives and to "the Belgian population," telling the jurors: "Mon sort est entre vos mains" — "My fate is in your hands."

The civil parties welcomed the verdict. Me Laurent Kennes, lawyer for Lechien's family, called it "très raisonnable" and "the only just decision," saying the jury had accepted that Ullens prepared his act and went to the scene with that intention. "Pour nos clients, c'était une réaction très émotionnellement touchante," he said.

The defence saw it differently. Me Jean-Philippe Mayence noted that premeditation had been retained by only seven jurors against five, arguing that a decision reached by a single vote raised a legal question about how the court could rule on that aggravating circumstance.

## The procedural battle

That disagreement escalated into a procedural challenge that suspended the trial. On 7 October, the defence filed a recusal request against the three professional magistrates of the assize court, accusing them of intervening with the jurors on the question of premeditation during the eight-hour deliberation. The court refused to step aside, sending the matter to the Court of Cassation.

At the hearing on 21 October, Advocate-General Damien Vandermeersch urged the Court to reject the request, arguing the procedure had been correctly followed. "The jurors did not call into question the existence of premeditation. The professional judges did not express their opinion before the jurors, but after their decision on the merits had been definitively settled. With the 7–5 decision, the decision was already made," he said, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/assassinat-de-la-baronne-ullens-l-avocat-general-demande-de-rejeter-la-requete-en-recusation-introduite-par-la-defense-11803533).

Mayence denounced a "total incoherence" in the magistrates' conduct, arguing that a discussion had "necessarily taken place" between them and the jurors. "It is not for nothing that magistrates are normally required to speak last. It is precisely so as not to influence the jurors' decision," he said. The Court ultimately rejected the request, and the trial is now set to resume on Wednesday 4 November.

## What the court must now decide

With guilt settled, the court and jury are now set to turn to the sentence when proceedings resume. As a convicted murderer, Ullens faces a maximum of life imprisonment (*réclusion à perpétuité*). The key question is whether the court finds extenuating circumstances that could reduce the penalty to a fixed term.

The *avocate générale* is expected to deliver her sentencing submissions, after which the defence will speak — likely emphasising the family and financial context, Ullens's personality and his background as a former security agent. Before the prosecution speaks, the court must confirm the jury is complete, since the twelve members had not anticipated these extensions; substitute jurors may need to be called if any are unavailable.

Beyond the sentence itself, another possibility looms: the advocate-general noted that the defence could still lodge a cassation appeal after the trial. Such an appeal would not suspend execution of the sentence under Belgian criminal procedure, but it would extend a case that has already consumed more than three years.

For the family of Myriam Lechien, and for a Belgian public that has followed every twist, the question of what a proportionate punishment looks like — for a killing planned and carried out with such deliberation — remains the final, unresolved chapter.
