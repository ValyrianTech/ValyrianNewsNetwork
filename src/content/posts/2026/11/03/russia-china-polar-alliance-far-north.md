# Russia and China Build a Polar Alliance in the Far North

Russia and China are deepening a strategic partnership across the Arctic and Far North, building what Beijing calls a "Polar Silk Road" and turning the rapidly warming region into a fresh arena of great-power competition. The rapprochement, detailed in a [geopolitical analysis published by RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719), carries major implications for global power balances, resource access and security—prompting the European Union to unveil a new Arctic strategy of its own.

The push is driven by geography and economics. The Arctic is warming up to four times faster than the global average, opening sea lanes that were long impassable to commercial shipping while sharpening appetites for the resources beneath the seabed. According to the US Geological Survey, the region may hold close to a third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and 13% of its undiscovered oil.

## A commercial pitch, with strategic intent

In October, Nikolai Patrushev, a close adviser to President Vladimir Putin, visited China for talks that, according to [Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/), focused on linking China's Maritime Silk Road with Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor. The agenda included developing Russian ports, exchanging technology for container-terminal automation and conducting joint maritime research.

Patrushev's pitch was mostly commercial. Russia wants Chinese firms involved in the Northern Sea Route and related corridors, direct container links between Shanghai's Yangshan port and Russian ports, investment in terminals and Chinese digital expertise in port automation. He also proposed cooperation on shipbuilding, maritime education and research. Beijing described maritime cooperation as a "blue driving force" for bilateral relations.

The economic foundation is substantial. China is Russia's largest trading partner, and the two countries' two-way trade exceeded US$200 billion for a third consecutive year in 2025, according to Chinese government data cited by Asia Times. In 2023 the two signed a key Arctic cooperation agreement; Chinese actors now co-finance major extraction projects such as Yamal LNG and are significantly expanding their own fleet of nuclear icebreakers.

## Why the Northern Sea Route matters

The Northern Sea Route (NSR) is the artery at the heart of the partnership. It cuts distances sharply between Europe and East Asia: a voyage from Shanghai to Hamburg takes about 18 days via the Arctic, compared with 30 to 35 days through the Suez Canal. Russia controls the route—including shipping permits, transit fees and a vast icebreaker fleet—while China supplies the cargo, vessels and industrial capacity to develop it.

For Moscow, the route offers a way to deliver more oil and gas to China and to escape Western sanctions imposed over its war in Ukraine. For Beijing, it offers an alternative maritime corridor that reduces dependence on the Strait of Malacca between Malaysia and Indonesia, and potentially gives Chinese firms access to Russian Arctic resources and infrastructure.

In its 2018 Arctic strategy, China described itself as a "near-Arctic state" with legitimate interests in shipping, resource exploitation, scientific research and regional governance. "China aspires to the status of a world superpower. One of the attributes is domination, or at least presence, in geopolitically important areas of the world, and the Arctic is incontestably one of them," Vlastislav Bříza, a security expert at Charles University in Prague, told the Czech broadcaster ČT24.

The commercial logic is beginning to pay off. In 2026, Russian oil exports via the Northern Sea Route rose nearly 50% year-on-year, with about 20 million barrels delivered to Asia between July and mid-October, according to [Modern Diplomacy](https://moderndiplomacy.eu/2026/10/19/russia-ramps-up-arctic-oil-exports-as-global-shipping-crisis-disrupts-trade-routes/), citing Reuters. Disruption at Middle Eastern chokepoints—including the Suez Canal and the Strait of Hormuz—has pushed tanker freight rates to record highs, making the icebreaker-assisted Arctic route comparatively cheaper than paths through the Red Sea. Putin has said freight volumes could double by the end of the decade to 70 million tonnes a year, driven by Rosneft's Vostok Oil project.

In August, the Chinese shipping company Sea Legend launched the first regular weekly container service across the Arctic, realising a long-held ambition, the South China Morning Post reported. South Korea and India have also shown interest in the icy shortcut.

## A reality check on scale

For all the geopolitical attention, the Arctic remains a marginal player in global shipping, and analysts caution against overstating its near-term potential. Flows through Suez still dwarf Arctic traffic, which is hampered by ice, high insurance premiums, sparse infrastructure and a short navigation season.

As [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/27/arctic-shipping-china-and-russia-expand-grip-can-europe-fight-back.html), strategist David Roche of Quantum Strategy & Geonomics estimates Arctic trade accounts for roughly 0.3% of global shipping today and could reach about 1% by 2030. "The significance of the arctic route is for Russian energy shipments to China, which will increase China's control over Russia in terms of Russia's dependence on China to sell its energy to compensate for losses of its gas markets in Europe," Roche said.

Asia Times makes a similar point: a Sino-Russian maritime network is "not ipso facto a military alliance," and Washington "should not regard every commercial port project as a cloaked naval base." The more critical question, it argues, is "cumulative strategic resilience"—the ability to build infrastructure that has economic value in peacetime and strategic utility in a crisis.

## Militarisation and the Western response

Security concerns are nonetheless mounting. Russia controls about 53% of the Arctic coastline, operates its Northern Fleet from the Kola Peninsula and has reactivated Soviet-era bases. According to the Polish outlet [Defence24](https://defence24.com/geopolitics/cold-war-at-the-top-of-the-world-the-militarisation-of-the-arctic-and-the-sino-russian-alliance-on-the-northern-sea-route), Moscow has deployed Bastion coastal-defence systems, Arctic-adapted S-400 air-defence systems and fighters armed with hypersonic missiles.

Western intelligence is watching China's intentions closely. Thomas Nilsson, head of Swedish military intelligence, told The Times that China will "use" Russia to gain a foothold in the Far North. Moscow, which once viewed China's growing Arctic influence unfavourably, now has little choice: it depends on Beijing to finance its war effort and for military equipment and dual-use goods such as microprocessors.

Defence24 highlights a stark capability gap. "The United States suffers from a severe shortage of operational icebreakers: it has only two ageing vessels, whereas Russia has more than forty, including powerful Arktika-class nuclear icebreakers," it wrote. "Without the physical capacity to operate in ice-covered waters, Washington's declarations in favor of freedom of navigation in the Arctic remain mere diplomatic notes."

NATO has moved to respond. Finland's and Sweden's accession means seven of the Arctic Council's eight member states are now in the alliance. In February 2026, NATO launched its "Arctic Sentry" framework to unify maritime and aerial patrols, intelligence sharing and exercises; Sweden and Germany are among the participants deploying fighter jets.

The EU has also acted. On 20 October it unveiled its [new Arctic Strategy](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_2203), built around four priorities: security and resilience, climate, prosperity, and opportunities for Arctic communities. It calls for expanding Europe's icebreaker fleet—the continent has only about ten such vessels—protecting critical undersea infrastructure and creating a new Maritime Security Hub to track Russia's "shadow fleet" beyond the Mediterranean and Baltic into the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean.

"With Russia and China increasing their footprint and ambition, the Arctic has become a front line of global power competition," EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen framed the region as a geostrategic flashpoint: "The Arctic is one of the places where the tectonic plates of geopolitics collide."

## An uncertain path forward

The strategy also seeks to reduce Europe's dependence on Washington, with plans for a "sovereign and secure" undersea cable between Greenland and the continent, part of a wider pan-Arctic link, and the IRIS² satellite constellation to compete with Starlink. On resources, the EU and the United States diverge sharply: Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen insists Arctic oil, coal and gas "must stay in the ground," while President Donald Trump has loosened environmental rules to maximise extraction in the American Arctic.

The broader picture is one of competing networks rather than a single transformative corridor. Academic analysis from the [East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/21/china-adapts-to-a-colder-arctic-reception/) cautions that China's partnership with Russia has so far proven "narrower" than earlier forecasts suggested and that its near-term military capabilities in the Arctic remain limited—while warning that its expanding research, satellite and seabed-mapping activities represent a gradual accumulation of dual-use capabilities that should be monitored rather than dismissed.

As Alicia Garcia-Herrero, chief Asia-Pacific economist at Natixis, put it to CNBC: "Europe cannot realistically challenge Chinese and Russian dominance of Arctic shipping in any meaningful timeframe. Without a sustained industrial push that the EU has only begun to discuss, Europe can raise the commercial and regulatory costs of the route, but cannot prevent or replace the China-Russia partnership that now operates it."

What to watch in the months ahead: whether Patrushev's still-largely-unimplemented proposals turn into concrete port and logistics projects; how quickly the NSR season and volumes expand; and whether Europe's icebreaker and surveillance plans move from strategy documents to deployed capacity. The direction of travel is clear—the question is how far, and how fast, Moscow and Beijing can turn the Far North into a preserve of their own.
