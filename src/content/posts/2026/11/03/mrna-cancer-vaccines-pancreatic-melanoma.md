# Can mRNA Vaccines Extend Cancer's Miracle Stories?

Seven years ago, Donna Gustafson became the first person in the world to receive a personalized mRNA vaccine designed to treat pancreatic cancer — one of the deadliest diagnoses in medicine. Today, at 72, she shows no evidence of the Stage II disease that was supposed to kill her, and she has spent the intervening years hiking, traveling, skydiving and celebrating her 50th wedding anniversary.

"I have had 100% quality of life; I am back to where I was prior," Gustafson told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/02/nx-s1-6009085/mrna-cancer-vaccine-pancreatic-skin) in a profile published November 2. Her case now anchors a larger and urgent scientific question: whether the eye-popping results seen in a handful of patients can be scaled and reproduced across many cancers — or whether her survival represents an outlier the field cannot yet explain.

## A treatment unlike chemotherapy

Gustafson's vaccine was not a preventive shot. It was *therapeutic* and *personalized*: scientists took samples of her tumor, sequenced its unique mutations, and engineered mRNA to train her immune cells to recognize and attack the distinctive mutant proteins, or neoantigens, on her cancer cells.

She received nine doses of the vaccine, along with surgery and chemotherapy, as part of a small Phase 1 clinical trial at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York that enrolled 16 pancreatic cancer patients. The vaccine worked in half of them — and seven of those eight responders, including Gustafson, remain alive years later, according to [MSK](https://www.mskcc.org/news/can-mrna-vaccines-fight-pancreatic-cancer-msk-clinical-researchers-are-trying-find-out).

Pancreatic cancer is diagnosed in roughly 70,000 Americans each year and carries a five-year survival rate of about 13%. Standard treatments — chemotherapy, radiation, targeted therapy and existing immunotherapies — have been largely ineffective against it, which is why the results drew attention.

Perhaps as striking as the outcomes was the experience of receiving the treatment. Chemotherapy, which required hours of infusion and punishing side effects her husband Ed called "barbaric," was far harder on Gustafson than the vaccine itself. "I had no symptoms, no side effects — to the point where I was actually worried that maybe it was not helping," she told NPR.

It was helping — swiftly. Dr. Vinod Balachandran, the surgeon and scientist who ran the trial, measured what he described as a "10,000-fold increase" in cancer-attacking immune cells that stayed elevated years after her final dose. "It just tells us that this response lasts," he said.

That durability is the scientific crux. In the MSK analysis, responding patients produced both CD8+ T cells, which directly attack cancer cells, and CD4+ T cells, which help sustain the immune response — a combination scientists believe an effective cancer vaccine must activate. Some of these cancer-killing cells persisted for up to six years with no sign of slowing.

## A field at an inflection point

The pancreatic data are striking but preliminary: they come from a single Phase 1 trial of just 16 patients, and results must be confirmed at scale. A larger global Phase 2 trial is now testing the vaccine — known as autogene cevumeran and developed by BioNTech and Genentech — in more patients at MSK and other sites worldwide.

What has changed the calculus for the entire field is a separate, far larger success. In October, drugmakers Merck and Moderna reported that their personalized mRNA therapy, intismeran autogene, combined with the immunotherapy drug Keytruda, reduced the risk of recurrence or death by 42% compared with Keytruda alone in patients whose high-risk melanoma had been surgically removed.

Announcing the Phase 3 INTerpath-001 results, the companies [said](https://www.merck.com/news/merck-and-moderna-announce-phase-3-interpath-001-trial-of-intismeran-autogene-plus-keytruda-met-endpoints-of-recurrence-free-survival-rfs-and-distant-metastasis-free-survival-dmfs-in-patient/) the trial met its primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival and a key secondary endpoint of distant metastasis-free survival. At two years, recurrence-free survival was 82.2% for the combination versus 70% for Keytruda alone. It was the first positive Phase 3 readout for an mRNA-based cancer therapy.

As [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/hematology-oncology/20261023/personalized-mrna-vaccine-reduced-melanoma-recurrence-risk-42-after-surgery) reported, the benefit emerged about six months after treatment began and persisted through two years of follow-up, while overall survival data remain immature. Dr. Patrick Ott of the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute called the results "a real advance," telling the outlet: "It is remarkable to see these differences with an entirely new therapy that has a very different way of action."

The melanoma program spans nine Phase 2 and Phase 3 trials across multiple cancers and stages, including lung, bladder and kidney cancers, according to Merck. The same foundational technologies — mRNA, tumor genomics and artificial intelligence — underpin the pancreatic work.

## The caveat that tempers the hope

The optimism is not unqualified. Researchers still do not understand why some mRNA cancer vaccine trials fail while others produce dramatic results in patients like Gustafson — a gap that will determine how broadly the approach works.

That uncertainty is not abstract. BioNTech's BNT113, an mRNA immunotherapy for HPV16-positive head and neck cancer that had received FDA Fast Track designation, [failed to meet](https://www.biontech.com/us/en/home/mediaroom/news/press-releases/2026/10/BioNTech-Provides-Update-on-Phase-2-3-Clinical-Trial-for-mRNA-Cancer-Immunotherapy-Candidate-BNT113-in-Metastatic-HPV16-positive-Head-and-Neck-Cancer.html) its primary progression-free survival endpoint in its Phase 2/3 trial, the company announced in October. The trial continues to evaluate overall survival, but the miss is a reminder that the technology is not a guaranteed winner.

Balachandran frames the ambition carefully. "Well, if you could generate such a strong immune response in the toughest of cancers, could this similarly develop cancer vaccines for a broad array of human cancers?" he asked. It is a question, not yet an answer.

## What's next

Several forces are converging on that question. The National Cancer Institute is making cancer vaccines a priority, partnering with private companies to fast-track research and development, while MSK is building the capacity to manufacture personalized mRNA vaccines on site to widen access to trials, NPR reported.

Cost and complexity remain open challenges. Personalized vaccines require sequencing each patient's tumor and manufacturing a bespoke therapy — a process that typically takes weeks — which makes them more expensive and logistically demanding than off-the-shelf drugs.

For Gustafson, the stakes are personal and already settled. Reflecting on the years the vaccine helped give her, she told NPR she has watched "two more grandchildren be born and grow, many birthdays, holidays and Christmases with my family." She and Ed have worked through a bucket list — skydiving, summiting mountains — with the expectation of more anniversaries to come.

Her story is, for now, a single data point with an outsized pull on the imagination. Whether mRNA vaccines can turn that kind of survival from a rare exception into something routine is the question the coming trials must answer.
