# Flemish Waterway Clears Dying Duckweed in Ghent

The Flemish Waterway authority (De Vlaamse Waterweg) began clearing away dying duckweed at the Sint-Jorissluis lock in Ghent on Monday, as the plant that turned the city's canals a striking green this autumn starts to brown and decompose. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/02/eendenkroos-gent-opruimen-vlaamse-waterweg-bruin/), the operation uses boats to gather the weed and create an artificial downstream current to push it away, in what the authority describes as a pilot project whose success is not guaranteed.

The cleanup targets the one spot in Ghent where the water authority has identified a significant accumulation of the plant, which earlier this autumn drew international attention and was even featured by the BBC.

## A Green Carpet Turns Brown

For several weeks, duckweed has formed a thick green layer on Ghent's inner waterways, generating eye-catching images on social media and attracting worldwide coverage. Many viewers compared the vivid green surface to matcha, the popular Japanese tea. But the "matcha layer" is now gradually turning into a "latte," as the plants die off and change into a brown sludge that can cause odour nuisance.

The problem is concentrated at the Sint-Jorissluis. "At the Sint-Jorissluis the duckweed stays put," Geert Weymeis of De Vlaamse Waterweg told VRT NWS. "The lock creates a level difference, so the push of the water [the current] sits deeper down. What floats on top therefore stays."

The weed has now formed a thick layer and is slowly dying. "Right now nothing is wrong, but in time it can start to stink," Weymeis said.

## How the Cleanup Works

To prevent odour nuisance, the authority is deploying a boat to corral the weed and generate an artificial current. "We'll try to gather the weed with boats and create an artificial current, so we can push it downstream," Weymeis explained.

Whether the approach will work remains to be seen. "We'll certainly do our best, but it's a pilot project. So it remains to be seen whether it succeeds," he said. Weymeis also assured that the operation will not disrupt the neighbourhood.

## Why Decaying Duckweed Matters

Duckweed is a tiny floating aquatic plant that reproduces extremely fast under warm, nutrient-rich conditions, forming dense green carpets on the water surface. It is not harmful to humans. But when large quantities die and decompose, the plant draws oxygen out of the water, which can harm fish and other underwater life. According to De Vlaamse Waterweg, no other nature impacts at the Sint-Jorissluis are known so far.

The plant arrived in Ghent after heavy late-summer rain flushed it from France via Wervik and the Leie, through Kortrijk. High temperatures and nutrient-rich water then allowed it to multiply rapidly. Earlier coverage by [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/06/eendenkroos-gent-haalt-bbc/) noted that the BBC highlighted Belgium's "historic heatwaves" and unusually high September temperatures as ideal conditions for the plant's spread. For a time, extra rain in early October appeared to be washing the duckweed away on its own, but not everywhere.

## Global Attention and Local Headaches

As [HLN](https://www.hln.be/gent/het-groene-tapijt-op-het-water-in-gent-begint-een-geurtje-achter-te-laten-vlaamse-waterweg-laat-eendenkroos-wegduwen-met-boot~a95793ca/) reported, the green carpet went from a curiosity to a creative phenomenon — drawing photographers, AI artists, wakeboarders and even costumed "witches on the water" paddlers — before reaching the BBC and international audiences. The vivid green layer that circled the world in early October is now, in places, turning into brown sludge.

The bloom also had practical consequences. Earlier this autumn, at least one operator of tourist boats suspended longer trips because duckweed threatened to clog filters and overheat engines, though electric boats were unaffected.

## What's Next

With the cleanup framed as a pilot project, the coming days will show whether the boat-and-current method can dislodge the stubborn layer at the Sint-Jorissluis. The authority will continue to monitor oxygen levels, which remain the key environmental concern if the weed keeps decaying in place. For now, the operation is a small, targeted intervention in a saga that has made Ghent's waterways briefly famous — and left behind a reminder of how quickly warm, nutrient-rich water can reshape an urban landscape.
