# Belgium in Political Crisis as Budget Talks Collapse

Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever offered the resignation of his government to King Philippe on Monday morning after overnight negotiations over the 2027 federal budget collapsed, plunging the country into what political observers are openly calling a political crisis. De Wever arrived at the Royal Palace in Brussels at 9:30 a.m. to tender his resignation, a dramatic turn that threatens the stability of the five-party federal coalition.

The core cabinet had convened on Sunday evening shortly after 9 p.m. in a final push for agreement, but talks broke up around 1:30 a.m. without a deal. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/02/geen-begrotingsakkoord-politieke-crisis-overzicht/), the deadlock centred on a single fault line: how much of the budget effort should fall on the "strongest shoulders" through higher contributions from large wealth and income, versus spending cuts.

## A Coalition Divided Along Left-Right Lines

The government — the so-called "Arizona" coalition, named for the matching party colours of its five members — spans Flemish nationalists (N-VA), Flemish Christian democrats (CD&V), Flemish socialists (Vooruit), and the Francophone liberals (MR) and centrists (Les Engagés). That breadth, analysts note, is precisely what makes budget compromise so difficult. Vooruit, Les Engagés and CD&V pushed for a larger contribution from large wealth; N-VA and MR resisted new taxes.

"If you can't call this a political crisis now, then I don't know what is," VRT NWS political journalist Bart Verhulst said. MR Vice-Premier David Clarinval, who left the talks first, pointed to Vooruit as the party blocking an agreement, saying "the volume of taxes remains far too high." Foreign Minister Maxime Prévot of Les Engagés struck a more resigned tone, remarking that listing all the sticking points "would take an eternity."

## The 10 Billion Euro Question

The government has been searching for roughly €10 billion in savings and reforms by 2029 to keep Belgium within EU expenditure rules and avert a debt spiral that the National Bank and Debt Agency have warned could begin snowballing as early as 2028 or 2029. As [De Morgen](https://www.demorgen.be/nieuws/regering-in-diepe-crisis-premier-de-wever-biedt-ontslag-aan-bij-koning-na-nieuwe-mislukking-in-begrotingsopmaak~be9e7348/) reported, De Wever had structured the exercise around about €4 billion in savings and reforms, €1.5 billion in new revenues, €1 billion in technical corrections and a further €2 billion in catch-up operations. Some parties sought to downgrade the effort to €8 billion to secure a deal — a proposal De Wever rejected as contrary to the objective.

Earlier, De Wever had tabled a "debt brake" — an emergency package freezing roughly €4 billion in federal spending, including pensions and tax brackets — as an alternative to a stopgap budget. The other coalition parties rejected it in mid-October.

## What Happens Next

Much now rests with King Philippe, who must decide whether to accept the resignation, hold it in abeyance, or refuse it. "Either the King accepts the resignation, and then the government falls, or the King holds it in abeyance and gives De Wever more time," Verhulst explained. Political scientist Carl Devos of Ghent University predicted the King is likely to hold the resignation in abeyance to maintain pressure on negotiators, warning that simply accepting it means "game over."

"Nobody believes that new elections would offer a solution," Devos said, citing Belgium's history of lengthy government formations. Recent polling suggests he has a point: Vlaams Belang is now the largest party in Flanders and the PS leads in Wallonia and Brussels — a result that could strengthen the political extremes rather than break the deadlock. If the King were to accept the resignation, the government would fall and elections would have to be held within 40 days.

## A Familiar Impasse

Exactly one year ago, the Arizona coalition found itself in a strikingly similar position. In early November 2025, De Wever went to the King during budget talks — but did not offer his resignation, instead requesting and receiving a 50-day extension. An agreement was reached on 24 November 2025 to save €9.2 billion. "That was a kind of pressure tactic then. Now the word 'resignation' is used explicitly," Verhulst noted.

The risk now is that Belgium falls back on "provisional twelfths" for 2027 — a stopgap mechanism allowing the government to spend only one-twelfth of the previous year's budget per month without an approved budget. Devos forecast the country may end up there regardless, noting that even with a deal, getting the budget through parliament in time would be difficult.

Opposition parties were quick to respond. PS chair Paul Magnette said the right-wing government was "showing its true face," protecting the rich at the expense of the rest, while Groen's Matti Vandemaele called De Wever "the prime minister of standstill" and warned a second consecutive year of provisional twelfths would be "a total disgrace." Anders chair Frédéric De Gucht urged negotiators back to the table: "Rome didn't fall to barbarians at the gate. Rome fell because the people within the walls were too busy with themselves. Go back to the table. Today."

For now, Belgium's political future hinges on a royal decision and whether exhausted negotiators can summon one more attempt at compromise. Analysts expect the core cabinet could reconvene as early as Monday afternoon for a final push — or the country could be headed for something far worse.
