# Belgian Horeca Crisis Hits Even Michelin-Starred Tables

Belgium's hospitality sector is in the grip of a deepening crisis that is no longer sparing its most celebrated kitchens. With roughly 1,000 restaurant bankruptcies recorded since the start of 2026 and several Michelin-starred establishments closing or changing hands, industry figures warn that rising costs, shifting consumer habits and looming political measures threaten the entire Horeca sector, from roadside bistros to double-starred dining rooms, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/pour-tout-le-secteur-horeca-c-est-la-crise-de-trop-a-l-heure-actuelle-personne-n-est-epargne-11809246).

"It strikes people's minds, even if sometimes these restaurants close for reasons of their own," said Thierry Neyens, an Arlon native and former president of Horeca Wallonie, reflecting on a 42-year career in the trade. His warning is blunt: in the current climate, no establishment is spared.

## A Sector Under Mounting Pressure

The numbers paint a stark picture. Statbel data covering January to July 2026 recorded 1,193 bankruptcies in the accommodation and food-service sector, including 221 in Brussels and 300 in Wallonia. According to credit agency GraydonCreditsafe, Horeca bankruptcies rose 4.32 percent across Belgium over the first eight months of 2026 compared with the same period a year earlier, [DH reported](https://www.dhnet.be/conso/consommation/2026/09/17/les-faillites-toujours-en-hausse-dans-lhoreca-des-patrons-qui-jettent-leponge-mais-aussi-un-manque-de-recettes-pour-letat-HSNMEKPRSJAFLM3CUEMH6UHDLM/).

Those figures, significant as they are, capture only part of the story: they exclude restaurant closures where no bankruptcy was formally declared.

The causes are layered. Consumers have seen their purchasing power erode and are eating out less often and spending more cautiously. Energy prices struck a sector that is unusually energy-intensive, reliant on kitchens, refrigeration, ventilation and heating running continuously. And since the COVID-19 pandemic, recruitment has become increasingly difficult, with qualified staff such as sommeliers, maîtres d' and kitchen brigades in short supply as workers shy away from split shifts and long hours.

Horeca Wallonie warned as early as April that the energy crisis has a structural impact on a sector made up overwhelmingly of small and medium-sized enterprises and self-employed operators, who face immediate cost increases and struggle to pass them on to customers.

## The Starred Casualties

The crisis has claimed names that once seemed untouchable. La Table de Manon in Durbuy announced an immediate closure, citing an "exceptional situation" and proceedings ongoing within the company. Chef Maxime Collard announced that La Table de Maxime in Paliseul would close definitively at the end of March 2027, after 18 years, [DH reported](https://www.dhnet.be/regions/luxembourg/2026/10/05/maxime-collard-annonce-la-fermeture-de-la-table-de-maxime-a-paliseul-nous-souhaitons-retrouver-un-autre-equilibre-YVM5WP63TBDSNAYIVVY7DFHW4M/). In Brussels, Christophe Hardiquest sold Menssa, while Senzanome closed having been awarded a star only months earlier.

Collard framed his decision in deeply human terms. "We asked ourselves whether we wanted to live the coming years at the same pace. You have to admit that at that tempo, for 18 years, you end up wearing yourself down, physically and mentally," he said. "We love our trade, but we also want simple things like going away for a weekend with our children."

Neyens noted that the very pursuit of gastronomic excellence carries its own financial burden. "You need highly qualified staff such as a sommelier, a maître d', but also in the kitchen. And there is all the 'ceremonial' that drives up costs," he said.

Hardiquest, for his part, was candid about the economics. "This past year, 2026, has been a little complicated in the restaurant business. In any case, it is not the promised land," he said. "We are artisans, but we cannot lose money." He pointed to automatic wage indexation, rising product prices, geopolitics and a lack of political investment in promoting Brussels as a gastronomic destination.

## Structural Squeeze, Not Just a Bad Year

Industry leaders stress that the pressure is structural rather than cyclical. Belgium's automatic wage indexation mechanically inflates employer payroll charges, while high labour costs compound the difficulty of recruiting. Banks frequently refuse credit to hoteliers and restaurateurs, pushing them toward public partners for financing.

Consumer behaviour has shifted too. Tanguy De Turck, chef at Le Vieux Château in Flobecq, mourned both the economics and the culture. "All these closures make me mostly sad. It is still a part of our heritage," he said. "Lunchtimes have changed a great deal. We no longer have the business clientele."

Pascal Devalkeneer of Chalet de la Forêt in Uccle, holder of two stars for 15 years, described the change in his customers' habits: "Clients who perhaps came every two months now come twice a year."

The end of deductible business lunches, designed to close a tax loophole, has been blamed for killing the lucrative midday business-lunch trade, while rising product prices driven by geopolitics, such as fuel costs pushing up fish prices, have added further strain.

Not everyone sees it as an unrelenting decline. San Degeimbre of L'Air du temps in Eghezée, a double-starred chef with nearly 40 years in the trade, argued that closures often reflect personal choices. But he acknowledged the purchasing-power crisis is real, while suggesting the leisure sector can offer clients "a little breath of oxygen."

## A Political Flashpoint

The crisis is colliding with a federal budget squeeze. Prime Minister Bart De Wever's government is considering a new increase in VAT on accommodation as it searches for revenue, a prospect that has alarmed the sector. In October, the Fédération Horeca for Wallonia and Brussels and the Brussels Hotels Association warned that such a move "could trigger a wave of closures, job losses and disinvestment," and the three regional federations demanded that Horeca be explicitly excluded from any new VAT measure, [La Libre reported](https://www.lalibre.be/dernieres-depeches/2026/10/20/budget-federal-lhoreca-bruxellois-et-wallon-redoute-une-hausse-de-la-tva-HQJCEBPYWVFWPFIVZ7DO366YEQ/).

Their message was pointed: "The State hopes for revenue; it will harvest closures and job losses." The warning carries particular weight given that the government doubled VAT on takeaway and delivery in late 2025, a move described in Wallonie picarde as "a hammer blow." The Council of State has issued a non-favourable opinion on the set of measures envisaged.

The sector did receive one reprieve: a smoking ban on café terraces was temporarily postponed, easing pressure on café owners.

## What to Watch

The Province of Luxembourg, a rural and gastronomy-rich region whose starred restaurants are a point of regional pride and a tourism draw, illustrates the stakes. All seven of its Michelin-starred restaurants retained their stars in the 2026 guide as of May, but several have since been affected by the crisis.

For operators such as Caroline Baerten of humus x hortense in Ixelles, survival has meant sacrifice. "It is especially this year that the situation became complicated," she said. "We are in contact with our accountant every two or three months, just to make sure we are not creating unnecessary expenses. We have reduced our own salary, but not that of our employees."

The months ahead hinge on two variables: whether consumer confidence recovers enough to refill dining rooms, and whether the federal government heeds the sector's warnings on VAT. For a trade that has weathered the 2008 financial crisis, pandemic closures and the energy shock, the current convergence of pressures may prove its sternest test yet.
