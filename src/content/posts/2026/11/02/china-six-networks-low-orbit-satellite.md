# China's Six Networks: A Small Satellite's Big Orbit

A 135-kilogram Chinese satellite circling hundreds of kilometers above Earth is being held up as the 'hard tech' proof that China's sprawling 'Six Networks' infrastructure plan can actually work. The Xingrui-11 (XR-11), built by Hunan Saideleite Satellite Technology in the central city of Zhuzhou, returned its first panchromatic image just eight hours after reaching orbit in May 2025—the fastest downlink yet recorded by a Chinese commercial remote-sensing satellite, according to [Xinhua's Economic Information Daily](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20261102/052eef39e51c444181de62adce0bbf99/c.html).

## What the 'Six Networks' Are

The satellite's story is framed by a far bigger undertaking. The Six Networks are a signature infrastructure program of China's 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030), comprising the national water grid, a new power grid, a computing network, a next-generation communications network, urban underground pipe networks, and a logistics network. The plan was emphasized at a July 30 Politburo meeting and is now moving from blueprint toward construction, [state broadcaster CCTV reported](https://www.ndrc.gov.cn/wsdwhfz/202608/t20260824_1407169.html), citing the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC).

Beijing estimates that investment tied to the Six Networks will exceed 7 trillion yuan (about $980 billion) in 2026 alone, [Xinhua reported](https://www3.xinhuanet.com/politics/20261030/0689abcfb7bd4948a41a48ae8f48b66c/c.html), positioning the program as a core lever for expanding demand and stabilizing growth. Within the communications network, satellite internet forms the space-based layer of an integrated 'space-air-ground-sea' system—the part of the grid designed to reach the ocean, deserts, and remote mountains where fiber and towers cannot easily follow.

## Engineering an 'Impossible Triangle'

The XR-11's pitch is not raw scale but efficiency. To keep the satellite light while making it sharp and agile, engineers had to reconcile what the team calls the 'impossible triangle' of satellite control: low cost, fast maneuvering, and quick stabilization—qualities that traditionally pull against one another, since faster attitude control usually demands heavier, costlier hardware.

'Traditional remote-sensing satellites find it hard to balance attitude maneuvering, rapid stabilization, and low cost,' He Xuan, a planning supervisor at Saideleite, told Xinhua. The solution, he said, was an integrated platform-payload design paired with electro-mechanical-thermal co-optimization, allowing the satellite to turn at up to three degrees per second and hold pointing error to one-thousandth of a degree—while keeping total mass at 135 kilograms.

The payoff is visible from orbit. In its first year, the XR-11 completed 5,530 orbits and carried out more than 14,000 Earth-imaging tasks, according to the same report. Its 0.5-meter resolution and 12-kilometer imaging swath—figures detailed by [Hunan Online](https://hunan.voc.com.cn/news/202608/33471780.html)—support uses ranging from natural-resource surveys to marine monitoring and disaster early warning.

## A Factory as Much as a Satellite

The deeper significance lies on the ground in Zhuzhou. Saideleite's plant, backed by 1.5 billion yuan in investment, houses what Hunan Online describes as the only 'pulsating' micro-satellite production line in central-south China, capable of building more than 150 small satellites a year in the 50-to-500-kilogram class, with a standard single-satellite build cycle cut to two to four months.

Around it, nearly 200 related enterprises have clustered, with components such as aerospace heat pipes, electric-propulsion systems, and phased-array antennas sourced inside the park—a supply chain summarized locally as 'upstairs-downstairs is upstream-downstream.' Saideleite holds more than 10 whole-satellite orders, Xinhua reported, and the XR-11 has won repeat purchases, a rarity in commercial space.

## What's Next

The company is now building a Beidou environmental-monitoring constellation, with an initial phase of 18 low-orbit satellites intended to help safeguard the stable operation of China's Beidou navigation system, deputy general manager Tong Xinya told Xinhua. Cooled infrared remote sensing, space-based AI computing, and microwave SAR satellites are also in development.

Yet the report is candid that a single satellite proves a capability, not an industry. As Beijing pushes to knit sensing, communications, navigation, and computing into one orbital network, the same hard questions face every constellation project: launch costs, competition for orbital slots and frequencies, batch-production consistency, and a commercial model that closes the loop between orders and stable revenue. The value of a low-orbit constellation, as the Xinhua report puts it, lies not in a single satellite's parameters, but in the scale of the network. For China's Six Networks, the XR-11 is less a finished product than a first, small stitch.
