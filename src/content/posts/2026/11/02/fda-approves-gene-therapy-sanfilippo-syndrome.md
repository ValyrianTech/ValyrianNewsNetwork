# FDA Approves First Gene Therapy for Sanfilippo Syndrome

The Food and Drug Administration has approved Fayuvi (rebisufligene etisparvovec-hopf), the first-ever treatment for Sanfilippo syndrome type A, a rare and fatal childhood neurodegenerative disease that had no approved therapy to alter its course. The September 17 decision, [announced by the FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-gene-therapy-pediatric-patients-sanfilippo-syndrome-type), follows more than a decade of research propelled in large part by families who raised millions of dollars to keep the science alive — including the family that drew global attention to the disease with a single viral video.

Sanfilippo syndrome type A, also known as mucopolysaccharidosis type IIIA (MPS IIIA), affects roughly one in 70,000 births. Children with the disorder cannot break down heparan sulfate, a molecule that then builds up to toxic levels in the brain and nervous system, causing progressive loss of speech, movement, and cognitive function, along with seizures. Most patients do not survive past their teens.

## A Family Pushed the Science Forward

The O'Neill family of Columbia, South Carolina, learned their daughter Eliza had Sanfilippo in 2013, when she was 3. Her mother, Cara, is a pediatrician who had studied the disease in medical school. "When they said the word, it all kind of came rushing back," she told [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/01/nx-s1-6014739/sanfilippo-gene-therapy-fayuvi). "And it was just this bale of dread, like, oh, my goodness, this is horrific."

Her father, Glenn, described a similar turning point. "It wasn't really until I got home that I realized how quickly the degeneration happened," he said. "And that was really the point where we said, 'Gosh. Whatever we need to do, we need to do it now.'"

The O'Neills founded the Cure Sanfilippo Foundation and, in 2014, commissioned filmmaker Benjamin Von Wong to produce the "Saving Eliza" video, which broke GoFundMe's record at the time by raising $1 million within weeks and soon $2 million. That money helped fund the first human trial, which began in May 2016. Eliza, then 6, was the first patient dosed.

The treatment had already been years in the making. At Nationwide Children's Hospital in Columbus, Ohio, researcher Haiyan Fu had been working on a Sanfilippo gene therapy in mice since the late 1990s, and in 2009 she nearly had to quit for lack of funding. Eight patient foundations stepped in to sustain her work through to the first human trial. "They said, 'You can't leave. Can we give you some of the funds for you to, you know, carry on?'" Fu recalled.

## How the Therapy Works

Fayuvi is a one-time intravenous gene therapy that uses a modified, non-infectious adeno-associated virus, AAV9, to deliver a working copy of the SGSH gene into a patient's cells. That allows the body to produce sulfamidase, the enzyme missing or deficient in MPS IIIA, so that heparan sulfate can be properly broken down.

In an open-label, single-arm, multicenter study in children aged 2 to 5, patients treated with Fayuvi maintained or improved cognitive function compared with an untreated historical control group — a sharp divergence from the disease's expected course of developmental plateau and decline. Ultragenyx, which acquired licensing rights to the therapy in 2022, reports that all 10 children who received the treatment were still able to communicate as they aged, and 9 could still walk and feed themselves.

"The treatment will work best the younger the child is because the more brain is remaining," Ultragenyx CEO Emil Kakkis told NPR. "By the time they're 2 or 3 or 4 years old diagnosed, getting figured out, they've lost a lot of ground."

The FDA granted Fayuvi Orphan Drug, Fast Track, and Breakthrough Therapy designations. Regulators flagged risks including thrombotic microangiopathy and, as with other AAV-based gene therapies, a potential long-term risk that inserted genetic material could integrate into the genome and lead to tumor development. Patients receive corticosteroids starting a day before infusion and continuing for at least eight weeks.

## A Near-$4 Million Price Tag

The approval comes with a question that has shadowed the field of ultra-rare disease medicine: who can actually afford it. Ultragenyx set the wholesale acquisition cost at $3.95 million, according to [BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/fda/ultragenyx-wins-fda-greenlight-for-first-sanfilippo-therapy-priced-at-nearly-4m), making Fayuvi one of the most expensive gene therapies ever, trailing only Orchard Therapeutics' Lenmeldy at $4.25 million. The company said it expects the therapy to be shippable within 30 to 60 days.

Kakkis said Ultragenyx is committed to treating every patient regardless of price. "There should be no logistical location, site or state or other kind of rule that stops us from getting the patient treated," he said. "We understand what [we've] got to do."

The approval was not a straight line. The FDA initially rejected the therapy in July 2025 over manufacturing issues, not clinical data, and Ultragenyx reapplied. It is the company's second gene therapy approval, following Genglycos in August 2026.

## A Milestone, and a Reckoning

The approval is a landmark for patient-driven research. With more than 7,000 rare diseases affecting hundreds of millions of people worldwide, funding tends to flow toward conditions with larger patient populations, and family foundations frequently bridge the gap. Sanfilippo is now a case study in how far that model can go — and where it runs into limits.

For Fu, the news carried mixed emotions. Minutes after the approval was announced, she received a call from Cara O'Neill and messages from other families who had funded her research, some of whom had lost a child along the way. "It shouldn't take this long," she said.

Because the therapy works best in the youngest patients, the push for newborn screening has become more urgent; a diagnosis at age 3, as in Eliza's case, means irreversible damage has already occurred. Long-term durability and safety will also require monitoring.

Eliza is now almost 17 — past the roughly 15-year median life expectancy for the disease. She lost her speech before treatment and it never returned, but she communicates with picture cards and devices, attends school, and this summer swam and learned to float on a pool noodle.

"I just feel proud of my girl," Glenn O'Neill said. "I feel proud of her. It's been a rough road for her, but we also know she's very lucky."
