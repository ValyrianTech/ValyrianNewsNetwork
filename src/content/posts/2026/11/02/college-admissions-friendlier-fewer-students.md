# As Students Grow Scarce, College Admissions Turns Friendlier

American college admissions is undergoing a quiet transformation. Faced with a shrinking supply of prospective students, colleges and universities are working to remake a process long seen as intimidating, secretive and stressful into one that is open, easier—and, in the words of one observer, "downright friendly." At some institutions, the shift has gone further still: faculty members, who once left recruiting almost entirely to the admissions office, are now being enlisted to court applicants directly.

"There's a demographic squeeze," said Owen Zaret, whose 17-year-old son is navigating the process. "It used to be more an ivory tower approach … 'You're lucky if you come here.' And now, I think, for a variety of reasons, that's changed. It's more like, 'We want you to be here.'"

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/02/nx-s1-6007855/college-admissions-change-fewer-students), which reported the story in partnership with The Hechinger Report, the change reflects hard demographic math that is reshaping how colleges fill their seats.

## The numbers behind the shift

Two-thirds of admissions leaders had trouble meeting their enrollment targets in at least one of the last two years, according to a survey by the ratings platform Niche, and 97% expect competition to increase. The underlying cause is a long-term decline in the traditional-age student population: the number of U.S. high school graduates is projected to fall by 13% over the next 15 years, according to the [Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education](https://www.wiche.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-Knocking-at-the-College-Door-final.pdf).

The proportion of high school graduates going on to college is also slipping. It fell from 70% in 2016 to about 63% in 2024, the most recent year for which figures are available, the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/hsgec.htm) reports.

The result is that getting into college is, on average, easier than it used to be—and far easier than many Americans believe. Average first-time undergraduate acceptance rates are nearly 80% at public and 70% at private, nonprofit colleges, up from roughly 68% and 65% a decade ago, according to the National Association for College Admission Counseling (NACAC). Nearly nine in ten private, nonprofit four-year universities admit half or more of their applicants, according to analysis by the [American Enterprise Institute](https://www.aei.org/domestic-policy/education/after-decades-of-competitive-admissions-getting-into-college-has-finally-become-easier/).

"Most of the public actually doesn't understand that the majority of institutions in the United States actually accept the majority of students who apply," said Angel Pérez, NACAC's chief executive.

That misperception is widespread. More than 40% of 18- to 29-year-olds wrongly believe it is harder to get into college than it was for their parents' generation, a [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/02/28/most-in-the-u-s-say-young-adults-today-face-more-challenges-than-their-parents-generation-in-some-key-areas/) survey found. In reality, only 33 colleges and universities nationwide admit 10% or fewer of their applicants, NPR reported.

## Recruiting students who never applied

Colleges are reaching further than ever to find students. Public universities in 17 states now admit high school students who haven't even applied, according to the Education Commission of the States, and more than 150 private colleges make direct admissions offers to students who fill out the [Common App](https://www.commonapp.org/directadmissions), the standardized online application used by about 1,200 institutions. Some schools are also waiving application fees and offering extra financial aid for campus visits.

There is another, less obvious driver behind the friendlier tone. As students apply to more schools—Common App users now average more than seven applications apiece—colleges find it harder to predict how many admitted students will actually enroll. That figure, known as "yield," has declined sharply, falling from 32% to 22% at public colleges and from 24% to 18% at private nonprofits between 2014 and 2024. The upshot: colleges must admit roughly five times as many students as they hope to enroll, and persuade the admitted to follow through.

## The faculty factor

Now a small number of institutions are taking a further step, enlisting faculty to help recruit. At Skidmore College in Saratoga Springs, N.Y., everyone from dining hall workers to groundskeepers is encouraged to be welcoming, and more than three-quarters of admitted students who visited campus last year beyond a weekend open house asked to sit in on a class or meet with a faculty member—about 300 personal visits in all.

"It creates a feeling of what it would be like to be seen, valued and to find a place of belonging on a campus," said Jess Ricker, Skidmore's vice president for enrollment, who joked about admissions' historic reputation as "offices of rejection."

Skidmore, which enrolls about 2,700 students, hasn't suffered the declines many small private colleges face: applications are rising, and its acceptance rate has fallen from 37% a decade ago to 24% today. Yet president Marc Conner said the headwinds affect everyone. "The challenges are real," he said. "Even if the school is doing all right during these times, we're still facing the same headwinds as everybody else."

Other institutions have followed a similar path. At Ohio Northern University, at least one faculty member meets with every visiting applicant. At Washington College in Maryland, faculty sit down with prospective students on designated Fridays and at open houses.

Coaxing faculty to embrace the role hasn't always been easy. "Some faculty didn't want to be part of it," said Johnnie Johnson, Washington College's vice president for enrollment management. "They'd say, 'You go recruit and I'll teach.'" He countered with a frank financial reality—that every enrolled student translates into programmatic funding and raises, and that faculty have watched colleagues at colleges that closed. "They don't want to be next."

## What to watch

There is no hard data yet on whether faculty outreach actually converts interest into applications and enrollment; at Skidmore, the evidence so far is anecdotal. And as the student pool shrinks through 2041, the pressure is only likely to grow—pointing to further easing, expanded direct admission, and deeper faculty involvement in the years ahead. For students and parents, the takeaway may be simpler than the anxiety suggests: the odds of getting in are better than many believe. As Becca Johnson, a Skidmore psychology professor who often meets with applicants, put it: "I think more information is better than less."
