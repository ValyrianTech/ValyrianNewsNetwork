# Two Economists, One Billionaire Tax, Opposite Verdicts

California voters are deciding whether to levy a first-of-its-kind, one-time 5% tax on billionaires, and two of the state's most prominent economists have analyzed the same proposal and reached conclusions so opposite they could describe different planets. According to [NPR's Planet Money](https://www.npr.org/sections/planet-money/2026/11/02/g-s1-151009/two-economists-studied-californias-billionaire-tax-they-reached-wildly-different-conclusions), Gabriel Zucman of UC Berkeley estimates Proposition 40 would raise about $100 billion for healthcare and education. Joshua Rauh of Stanford and the Hoover Institution calculates it would actually cost the state roughly $39 billion.

The gap between those two numbers — around $139 billion — is not a rounding error. It is the entire crux of a ballot fight that has become one of the most expensive in California history, and it exposes how little economists can actually agree on when it comes to taxing extreme wealth.

## What Proposition 40 Would Do

Prop 40 imposes a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of anyone holding at least $1 billion in assets, applied retroactively to individuals who were California residents as of January 1, 2026. According to the [Los Angeles Times' voter guide](https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-10-01/2026-california-election-proposition-40-billionaire-tax-voter-guide), about 90% of the proceeds would go to healthcare — offsetting federal Medicaid and Medi-Cal cuts — with roughly 10% directed to education and food assistance.

The design deliberately avoids the mistakes of older wealth taxes. Real property, pensions, and retirement accounts are generally excluded, while business holdings, stocks, and investments are included — closing the loophole that led France to scrap its own wealth tax in 2018. The retroactive structure was meant to prevent billionaires from fleeing the state before the tax could take effect, and illiquid taxpayers may defer payment.

California's billionaire wealth reached $2.3 trillion by mid-2026, according to Zucman's analysis with Jasper Boll and Emmanuel Saez — equal to all the annual income reported on every Californian's tax return. That hoard grew 144% between 2023 and 2025, largely on the back of the AI boom.

## The Case for $100 Billion

Zucman, a leading advocate for taxing the superrich who helped design the national wealth tax proposals floated by Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Sanders, arrives at his $100 billion figure by taking 5% of that $2.3 trillion base and shaving off roughly 10% for avoidance, evasion, and residency exits.

He argues the pull of Silicon Valley is simply too strong to break. Even if some billionaires leave, the region's concentrated tech ecosystem — which attracted three-quarters of all U.S. AI funding in 2025 — keeps the wealth, and the people who own it, anchored.

"If California says, 'We can tax billionaires. We can tax the wealthiest, most powerful people in the world,' it will be the beginning of not only a broader U.S.-wide movement... but I think it will be the beginning of an international movement," Zucman told NPR.

The appeal of that vision was enough to draw Sen. Bernie Sanders to Los Angeles for a campaign kickoff rally in February, and to prompt Rep. Ro Khanna to call Prop 40 the "most important ballot initiative in America."

## The Case for a $39 Billion Loss

Rauh, a Hoover Institution senior fellow, sees the same tax through a very different lens. His National Bureau of Economic Research working paper with Benjamin Jaros, [published in October](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35218), estimates the tax would raise only about $30 billion — and then, once lost income-tax revenue is counted, leave the state $39 billion in the hole.

Rauh's reasoning rests on three pillars. First, he says seven billionaires — among them Larry Page, Sergey Brin, Peter Thiel, Steven Spielberg, and Travis Kalanick — relocated before the January 1 cutoff, reducing potential revenue by roughly $30 billion. Second, he factors in legal risk: the retroactive reach and the extension of state power over former residents could run afoul of due process in a conservative Supreme Court, so he halves his projected revenue to account for the chance the tax is struck down. Third, he assumes the threat of future wealth taxes drives more departures, and he counts the resulting income-tax losses indefinitely.

"It would not raise $100 billion for the state," Rauh told NPR. "We calculate that Proposition 40 would cost the state of California $39 billion." He warned that "the entire startup ecosystem of California is threatened by taxes like this," predicting investors would pull out if the measure passes.

His study ran 100,000 simulations; in 85% of plausible outcomes, [Reason reported](https://reason.com/2026/10/20/californias-wealth-tax-could-cost-the-state-39-billion/), the net effect on state revenue is negative.

## Where the Numbers Split

The two economists aren't really arguing about arithmetic. They are arguing about human behavior — and about which assumptions deserve to be counted.

Zucman calls Rauh's net calculation "absurdly one-sided," arguing you can't count the losses from hypothetical future taxes without also counting the gains from the wealth tax itself that those tax fears are supposedly reacting to. He also notes that billionaires like Page and Brin pay relatively little income tax anyway, so the projected income-tax losses are smaller than they appear.

Rauh counters that "agglomeration effects are fragile" and that startup founders would face tax bills on illiquid valuations before they ever cash out. Each side accuses the other of building its case on convenient assumptions.

The dispute echoes a longer historical record. Wealth taxes around the world have largely disappointed — even Zucman concedes they have been "by and large, abject failures" — though a recent NBER paper on Norway found that tax increases drove at most about 100 wealthy people to leave, with limited revenue impact. The catch: moving between countries is far harder than moving between states.

The human stakes are visible beyond the economists' models. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang told Bloomberg he was "perfectly fine" paying the roughly $8 billion he would owe — "we chose to live in Silicon Valley," he said. Meta's Mark Zuckerberg reportedly struck a deal to sell nonvoting shares to cover his liability while keeping control, and [agreed to stay in California](https://apnews.com/article/zuckerberg-california-billionaire-tax-meta-stock-shares-56be13ddd072b48578d14a8f9d7eb23f) for eight years.

## A Pricey, Crowded Ballot

The campaign around Prop 40 has been staggering in scale. Opponents — led by a committee called Building a Better California and bankrolled in part by Sergey Brin, who [relocated to Nevada](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/20/nx-s1-5935597/california-billionaires-wealth-tax-sergey-brin) and [spent more than $100 million fighting the measure](https://www.npr.org/2026/08/11/g-s1-138201/california-billionaire-tax-google-sergey-brin) — have poured in sums reported in the hundreds of millions. Supporters raised roughly $32 million, mostly from the healthcare union SEIU-UHW. A Politico poll found the measure narrowly divided, 46% in favor to 43% opposed.

And even if Prop 40 passes, it may not survive. Two competing measures — Prop 41, a special tax audit proposal favored by opponents that could block the billionaire tax, and Prop 42, a tax ban on retirement and personal savings — could render it moot. Gov. Gavin Newsom opposes the measure.

## What to Watch

Voters decide Tuesday, but the deeper questions will outlast the election. Will courts uphold a retroactive tax reaching former residents? Will Brin, Page, and the others be deemed California residents for tax purposes — a question almost certain to end up in litigation? And will Prop 40, should it survive, become the national and international template Zucman envisions — or the cautionary tale Rauh predicts?

What is already clear is the lesson at the heart of the story: when it comes to taxing extreme wealth, the experts themselves cannot agree on what the future holds. Californians are being asked to bet on one forecast or the other.
