# Brussels Flight Path Health Risk Puts Airport in Spotlight

A change to aircraft approach procedures at Brussels Airport has dramatically expanded the number of Brussels residents exposed to aircraft noise, placing the health of a growing population at risk and reigniting a long-running battle over flight routes, environmental impact and the airport's growth ambitions. According to an [RTBF investigation](https://www.rtbf.be/article/la-sante-de-toujours-plus-de-bruxellois-survoles-mise-en-danger-mais-quel-est-le-probleme-de-brussels-airport-11798700) published on 23 October, the number of people affected by the airport's runway 07L approach may now be as high as 458,954 — up from about 161,082 under the previous procedure.

## What Changed

The controversy traces back to July 2025, when runway works and easterly winds pushed landings onto runway 07L. Skeyes, Belgium's air traffic control authority, replaced the older VOR radio-beacon approach with a GPS-based RNAV approach. The switch removed a tight final turn — which Skeyes had flagged in a February 2026 letter as raising "urgent safety concerns" because some pilots were dropping below safe altitude — but the new straight-line route shifted the flight path toward the densely populated centre of Brussels.

The result is a sharp redistribution of the noise burden. Under the old VOR approach to 07L, 161,082 people were impacted; the new RNAV approach reaches roughly 100,000 more, according to a ULB study. A CGX study commissioned by Mobility Minister Jean-Luc Crucke puts the figure at up to 458,954 at the 45-decibel threshold — a figure the ULB's Frédéric Dobruszkes told RTBF is "without doubt closer to reality" given its more precise noise-cone modelling.

## A Human and Health Toll

For residents, the change has transformed daily life. In his Molenbeek studio, Frédérik describes planes passing overhead "every two minutes. It's unbearable!" He says doctors have attributed unexplained weight gain to chronic stress, with his body reacting "as if it were in a state of war."

The World Health Organization recognises that repeated exposure to significant noise raises the risk of hypertension and cardiovascular events — a link RTBF notes in its reporting. The health consequences have also been tested in court: in March 2025, a Brussels court [first condemned the Belgian State](https://www.woluwe1200.be/survol-aerien-pour-la-premiere-fois-letat-belge-condamne-a-la-suite-des-infractions-constatees-par-le-sonometre-place-a-woluwe-saint-lambert/) over 269 noise-standard violations recorded by a sonometer in Woluwe-Saint-Lambert, findings explicitly linked to harm to sleep and well-being.

## A Planning Question

The shift in runway usage is stark and cannot be explained by wind alone. In 2024, runway 01 handled 8,157 landings against 3,184 on 07L. Over the first nine months of 2026, that balance inverted: 5,957 landings on runway 01 versus 9,189 on 07L — the first time 07L has been used more than 01. Critics point to a capacity explanation: runway 01 crosses all other runways, cutting airport throughput by 25 percent, from 67 to 54 movements per hour. With Brussels Airport projecting 75 percent growth by 2040 — from about 200,000 movements in 2025 to more than 350,000 — suspicions have grown that the higher-capacity 07L route was favoured partly for economic reasons. Both Skeyes and Brussels Airport declined RTBF's interview requests.

## Minister's Measures Draw Skepticism

In early October 2026, Minister Crucke announced a package of structural measures: cutting wind-change anticipation from three to four hours down to under 30 minutes, tightening gust handling, raising the easterly-wind threshold from 7 to 8 knots, and lifting the descent start from 3,000 to 4,000 feet with continuous descent. The estimated impact is modest — roughly a 5 percent reduction in landings on 07L and 01.

Acoustician Philippe Brux, CEO of the Modyva consultancy, told RTBF the measures would yield only 1 to 2 decibels overall, since continuous descent already occurs in 80 percent of landings. "It's not much, but it's something," he said. Residents' groups have dismissed them as mere "measurettes."

Crucke defended the airport's economic weight, noting it "supports more than 80,000 families" and is "Belgium's second economic lung." He also insisted that "it is not Skeyes's role to weigh such considerations. Their only concern must be safety." Flemish Budget Minister Ben Weyts, however, rejected the idea of shifting more traffic over Flanders, warning that "there is already an incredible concentration of flights over the Flemish periphery."

## Environmental Justice and the Road Ahead

Dobruszkes raises a sharper question. The most-affected communes — Molenbeek-Saint-Jean, Anderlecht, Jette, Ganshoren — have the lowest median property prices and fewer resources to mount legal challenges. "It is simpler to fly over many poor people than a few rich ones," he said, wondering aloud whether it amounts to "social racism" — while conceding he lacks proof of intent. Crucke rejected the charge as "false, utterly false."

The financial stakes are considerable. Residents' association Free Air 4 Brussels has estimated costs of up to €6 billion, combining property depreciation, lost public revenue and soundproofing — figures that [BruxellesToday](https://www.bruxellestoday.be/actualite/survol-bruxelles-route-crucke-6-milliards-euros.html) reported have not been independently validated.

For the first time, several hundred Brussels families are uniting to launch a judicial procedure against the Belgian State, and the mayor of Berchem-Sainte-Agathe has filed an environmental cessation action. What happens next may hinge on two things: whether Skeyes can be held accountable for its runway choices, and whether a technical fix — restoring the 07L VOR approach augmented with GPS — can meaningfully reduce the number of overflown residents. Until then, for the communities beneath the flight path, the debate over who bears the cost of Belgium's aviation ambitions is far from settled.
