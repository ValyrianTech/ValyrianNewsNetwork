# One in Four Belgians Works Through Illness, Survey Finds

More than one in four Belgian workers says they always go to work even when they are sick, according to a new biennial absenteeism survey by Mensura, the country's largest health and prevention service. The finding, published Monday, marks a dramatic jump from two years ago and raises concerns about a workplace culture of "presenteeism" — working while ill under real or perceived pressure.

The survey of 1,023 employees, conducted by research firm Indiville on behalf of [Mensura](https://news.mensura.be/meer-dan-acht-op-de-tien-werknemers-bereid-bij-ziekte-nog-iets-te-doen-voor-het-werk), found that 27% of workers say they always keep working when ill, up from just 4% in 2024. A further 81% said they had at some point worked while sick, and 82% do not rule out doing something for work while ill if their health allows and the employer facilitates it.

## A High Willingness to Work

The survey paints a picture of a workforce increasingly willing to stay engaged during illness. About 34% said they would do some work if it did not hinder their recovery, motivated mainly by wanting to feel useful. A further 48% would at least consider whether they could still contribute while sick or on incapacity leave.

Resistance to the idea is also eroding. Only 18% of workers believe an employer cannot expect someone to work if their condition allows it — down from 26% two years earlier.

"Over 8 in 10 workers don't rule out doing something for work during illness. That is a strong signal," said Bart Teuwen, an absenteeism expert at Mensura. "As an employer you must be able to respond to employees' willingness. Whoever thinks ahead about what's possible within the organisation can offer a tailored solution in good consultation when it matters."

## The Danger of Presenteeism

But Mensura warns that this goodwill has a dark side. The rise in presenteeism — showing up to work while ill because of internal or external pressure — can ultimately make employees sicker and keep them out of work longer, the service cautioned.

"It is important to keep two things clearly separate: working under pressure while you should actually be recovering, and examining in consultation what is still possible without endangering your recovery," Teuwen said, citing the case of footballer Vincent Kompany. "The first makes people sicker. The second helps them stay connected."

Teuwen stressed that presenteeism is "an experience of coercion" that undermines a healthy balance between workload and capacity. Employees who feel obliged to work while sick risk heavier and longer absences — or, in the case of viral infections, may infect colleagues in the workplace.

## Mental Health Remains Taboo

The survey also flags a worrying decline in workers' willingness to discuss health with managers, particularly on mental health. While 67% feel comfortable talking to their manager about health and well-being in general, only 12% feel comfortable raising mental health problems — a drop of 12 percentage points from 24% two years earlier.

The share of workers who feel comfortable discussing all topics fell from 47% in 2024 to 34%. More troubling still, 14% of all workers talk to no one — not a confidential adviser, occupational physician, or trusted colleague — about their struggles.

"That employees stay silent about their mental well-being to their manager is not the most disturbing figure," Teuwen said. "Worse is that across the entire group of workers, 14% also do not talk to a confidential adviser, the occupational physician, or even a colleague they get along with. Whoever feels they are on their own only seeks help when things become unbearable."

Each organisation must ensure "at least one safe door is open, and that everyone knows how to find that door," he added. One in three people on long-term sick leave became incapacitated due to mental problems, making a safety net essential to prevent things reaching that point.

## A National Challenge

The findings land against the backdrop of a broader crisis. Belgium is among Europe's "sickest" countries, with more than 500,000 people on long-term sick leave and a cost exceeding €9 billion in benefits. The De Wever federal government has made re-integrating the long-term sick one of its most important priorities, introducing new rules in 2026 that oblige employers to begin return-to-work processes earlier.

Yet many companies remain unprepared. About four in ten Belgian firms say adapted work is "practically impossible" in their organisation, and only around 12% have a formal re-integration policy.

## What to Watch

The headline figure — a six-fold rise in the share of workers who always work through illness — is striking, and the survey offers no explanation for the magnitude of the swing, leaving open whether it reflects a genuine shift in workplace culture or a difference in how the question was framed. Either way, the trend raises pressing questions for employers and policymakers alike.

As Belgium pushes ahead with its re-integration reforms, the challenge will be to accommodate workers' willingness to remain engaged without normalising the kind of presenteeism that ultimately damages health and productivity. As Teuwen put it, employer and employee alike risk being "bound to the paper dictate of the sick note" unless organisations think ahead about what is genuinely possible.
