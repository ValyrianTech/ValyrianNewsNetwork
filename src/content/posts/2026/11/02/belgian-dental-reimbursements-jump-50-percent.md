# Belgian Dental Reimbursements Jump Over 50% in Four Years

Reimbursements for dental care in Belgium climbed from just over €1 billion in 2021 to €1.6 billion in 2025 — an increase of more than 50%, according to figures from the National Institute for Health and Disability Insurance (INAMI/RIZIV). The surge, reported by [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/soins-dentaires-pourquoi-les-remboursements-ont-ils-bondi-de-plus-de-50-en-quatre-ans-11808228), comes against a much smaller rise in the volume of care, sharpening a debate over the affordability of treatment and the sustainability of Belgium's health insurance system.

Over the same four-year period, the total number of dental procedures rose by 16%. The gap between spending growth and activity growth sits at the heart of the story: patients are not simply going to the dentist more often, but the cost of each visit — and the share covered by sickness insurance — has grown.

## Why the Numbers Rose So Fast

According to RTBF, several factors explain the divergence. Fees for a range of procedures have been progressively revalued, coverage conditions have been broadened, the number of beneficiaries of the enhanced reimbursement scheme (BIM, or *intervention majorée*) has grown, access to care for young people has improved, third-party payment (*tiers payant*) has expanded, and oral hygienists have entered the system.

Frédéric Bettens, an administrator at the Société de Médecine Dentaire (SMD), the professional dental association, told RTBF that the increases reflect deliberate policy. "Several dental procedures have progressively benefited from tariff revaluations, notably conservative care, removable prostheses and certain preventive acts," he said.

INAMI stresses that the figures measure sickness-insurance spending — the amount reimbursed — and not the total sum paid to dentists. As such, they do not on their own capture how the patient's own bill has evolved, a distinction the agency is careful to draw.

## Where the Money Goes

Conservative care — fillings and similar restorative work — accounted for 37.3% of dental spending in 2025, while preventive treatments made up 27%. X-rays are a notable growth area: more than 4.4 million were reimbursed in 2025 for €105.43 million, a 49% rise in that budget line since 2021.

Bettens pointed to a structural cause of repeated imaging. "The radiological history does not always easily follow the patient, which can lead the new practitioner to take new images even though some exams already exist," he said. The SMD is calling for a shared database giving authorised practitioners access to existing X-rays, arguing that "an X-ray must above all respond to a clinical indication."

The gum-health examination, known as DPSI, has grown at an average of 23% a year since it was introduced in 2009, according to INAMI spokesperson Isabelle Collin. "This sharp rise is driving spending up faster than expected," she told RTBF. In 2025, budget savings cut the DPSI fee from €29.50 to €21.

## A Wave of Reforms

Authorities have introduced a series of measures to control spending without deterring patients from seeking care. From 1 June 2026, panoramic X-rays for adults aged 19 and over are reimbursed only once every three years instead of once every two. "Since 1 June 2026, the rules on panoramic X-rays have changed for adults: from age 19, reimbursement is limited to once every three years, instead of once every two," Collin confirmed.

The same month, the DPSI examination was integrated into the annual oral exam for people aged 18 and over, and a rule that halved tartar-removal reimbursement when no dental care had been reimbursed the previous year was abolished. From 1 July 2026, supplementary fees (*suppléments d'honoraires*) were banned for BIM patients on reimbursable dental and orthodontic care, whether or not the dentist is conventioned.

These changes form part of the [2026-2027 dento-mutualist agreement](https://www.inami.fgov.be/fr/professionnels/professionnels-de-la-sante/dentistes/prestations-prix-et-remboursement/l-accord-national-dento-mutualiste#l-accord-dentomutualiste-20262027), which sets tariffs for conventioned dentists. The first results of the broader nomenclature reform are expected by the end of 2027.

## The Tension: Access Versus Cost

The figures sit within a wider national debate about the financing of Belgium's social security and public health spending. Better coverage improves access to care, but it also raises public outlays — and the measures above are an attempt to curb unnecessary spending on imaging and examinations without penalising patients.

Bettens cautioned against reading growth as wrongdoing. "An increase in activity or number of procedures does not, in itself, constitute proof of fraud," he said, while acknowledging that abnormal use of certain procedures was "explained, at least in part, by certain insufficiently controlled billing practices."

On prevention, he argued that incentives must not become barriers. "Prevention remains essential, but it must encourage patients to consult regularly rather than constitute an additional financial obstacle for those already far from care."

A companion [RTBF investigation](https://www.rtbf.be/article/augmentation-des-frais-dentaires-diminuer-les-remboursements-la-bonne-solution-les-gens-ne-se-soignent-plus-11807351) captured the human stakes, quoting patients who say they have stopped seeking treatment: "People stop getting treated." The tension between containing costs and protecting access is the crux of the debate.

## What to Watch

INAMI has not yet published 2026 spending data, and Collin said it is "too early to assess the effect of the measures that came into force during the 2026-2027 agreement." The coming year will reveal whether the reforms bend the cost curve — and whether they do so without pushing more patients away from the dentist's chair. For now, the numbers point in one direction: dental care in Belgium is becoming both more accessible and more expensive for the public purse, and reconciling the two is the challenge ahead.
