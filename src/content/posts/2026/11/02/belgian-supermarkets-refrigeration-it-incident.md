# Belgian Supermarkets Hit by Refrigeration 'IT Incident'

More than 40 Delhaize supermarkets, 15 Carrefour Belgium stores and about five independent Spar outlets across Belgium saw their refrigeration systems disrupted by an "IT incident" on the morning of Monday 2 November, sending temperatures in coolers and freezers up to around 15°C. Delhaize confirmed the disruption, Carrefour said it was still investigating, and it remained unclear whether the cause was a deliberate cyberattack or an ordinary technical fault, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/02/cyberaanval-delhaize/).

The timing is significant: the incident comes just three weeks after a confirmed cyberattack sabotaged the refrigeration systems of roughly 150 French supermarkets, an event that has focused attention on how vulnerable retail food infrastructure is to digital disruption.

## A warning at 4 a.m.

According to the reporting, Delhaize issued a warning around 4:00 a.m. about sudden temperature rises in its cooling systems. Both coolers and freezer cabinets climbed to about 15°C. A Delhaize spokesperson said the problem was nationwide rather than regional, with stores affected in both Flanders and Wallonia.

The affected Delhaize formats reportedly include Shop & Go stores that adjoin Q8 fuel stations, Delhaize Proxy outlets, certain regular Delhaize supermarkets and stores along motorways. A Delhaize branch on Sint-Paulusstraat in Antwerp displayed an apology "for the inconvenience."

Carrefour Belgium said 15 of its stores were affected and that the group was "still fully investigating" the situation. About five independent Spar operators reported similar problems.

## Cause still unconfirmed

Delhaize said work was under way and that it was being examined how the stores could be brought back into operation. Critically, the cause had not been established at the time of the initial reporting. The phrase "IT incident" is deliberately cautious: it leaves open whether the disruption stemmed from a malicious actor, a software malfunction or another technical failure.

The potential damage could be substantial if refrigerated and frozen stock has to be discarded — a cost that falls directly on retailers and, ultimately, on consumers.

## The French precedent

Three weeks earlier, on the night of 11-12 October, hackers sabotaged refrigeration systems at roughly 150 French supermarkets, [VRT NWS reported at the time](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/10/12/cyberaanval-treft-koelinstallaties-van-supermarkten-in-frankrijk/). In that attack, temperatures in some installations rose as high as 50°C, rendering food unusable. The hackers targeted systems supplied by Danish technology company Danfoss, which provides software to remotely manage refrigeration installations, and hijacked the automatic defrost function — forcing a permanent 50°C defrost cycle that effectively turned fridges into ovens.

The damage was severe. In Haguenau in Alsace, an Intermarché store reportedly lost its entire frozen stock, an estimated €50,000 loss, while a nearby Super U outlet had to destroy fresh products, bringing combined damage to about €270,000. National losses were estimated in the millions of euros. Affected chains, including Coopérative U and Intermarché, set up crisis cells and worked with refrigeration specialists.

At the time, Belgian retail federation Comeos said the impact was confined to France, stating that the problem was situated at Danfoss France and that no issue had been detected for Danfoss Belgium or Belgian supermarkets.

## What remains unknown

Whether the Belgian incident is technically linked to the French attack has not been established, and no direct connection has been reported. The short interval between the two events is notable, but analysts caution against assuming a link before investigators confirm one. It is equally possible that the Belgian disruption is an unrelated fault.

A separate Ahold Delhaize cyber incident in the Netherlands in October — which affected employee data at Albert Heijn, Etos and Gall & Gall — is a distinct event and should not be conflated with the refrigeration issue.

## What to watch

Three questions will shape how this story develops. First, whether Delhaize, Carrefour or Comeos confirm that the incident was a deliberate cyberattack. Second, whether the affected systems involve the same Danfoss software implicated in France, which would raise the prospect of a coordinated campaign against European food retailers. Third, the scale of the losses — the volume of stock that must be destroyed and the cost borne by the chains.

For now, the immediate priority is restoring refrigeration and protecting food safety. But the episode is a reminder that the digital systems underpinning the food supply chain are an increasingly attractive target — and that the retail sector's resilience to such disruptions remains an open question.
