# Floodwaters Swamp the Southwest, Forcing Evacuations

A slow-moving storm system drenched in tropical moisture has unleashed catastrophic flooding across the American Southwest, forcing hundreds of residents to flee their homes, killing at least one person and reshaping Election Day logistics in a Colorado mountain town. Over the past two weeks, more than 20 trillion gallons of water have fallen on the Four Corners states and Nevada, according to calculations by former National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration chief scientist Ryan Maue, as [AP News reported](https://apnews.com/article/arizona-pagosa-springs-colorado-sedona-southwest-flooding-ee6777f8b21234d2243998b66294b92b).

The flooding has swept through northern Arizona, southwestern Colorado and northern New Mexico — a normally arid region where dry creekbeds were transformed into raging torrents. The system is being supercharged by an unusually intense El Niño, the periodic warming of Pacific Ocean waters that pumps moisture into the atmosphere and fuels passing storms.

## Two dead in Flagstaff

The human toll came into focus Monday in Flagstaff, where rain fell nearly nonstop Sunday. Police said Williams Singavarapu, 73, of New York, fell into fast-moving floodwaters that fed into a drainage tunnel near Northern Arizona University. His body was found about 12 hours later, roughly 15 blocks from where he disappeared. A second man was found dead Monday in a Flagstaff storm-water drainage area; authorities said the death appeared weather-related but were awaiting an official cause.

The scale of the rainfall set records. Flagstaff recorded 5.15 inches (13 centimeters) over two days — its wettest Nov. 1 on record — in a city that typically sees only a handful of days a year with more than an inch of rain. Farther north, Workman Creek recorded 7.3 inches (18.5 centimeters) over two days.

"What really stood out to me was that this wasn't localized. It was a very broad system that brought a lot of rain to a lot of places," said Jennifer Tate, a meteorologist with the National Weather Service's Weather Prediction Center.

## A Colorado town braces for a historic crest

The most dramatic standoff played out in Pagosa Springs, Colorado, a town of more than 2,000 people that was nearly wiped out by a flood in 1911. The San Juan River crested at 16.81 feet (5.1 meters) late Sunday before receding Monday, below the roughly 17.8-foot (5.4-meter) level of the 1911 disaster but still well above a 100-year flood that struck just over a year ago.

The town had only just lifted an evacuation order from previous flooding last week before reimposing it Saturday. Officials had warned of "potentially catastrophic flooding," with forecasts at one point projecting a crest above 21.9 feet.

"To put the forecast into perspective, during the devastating 1911 flood that wiped out most of what existed as the town of Pagosa at that time, the San Juan River crested at approximately 17.8 feet," the Archuleta County Sheriff's Office said in a social media post. "This is a potentially catastrophic flood event."

For local business owners, the worry was immediate and personal. Katie German, owner and chef of Bruin Coffee in Pagosa Springs, said she feared the single bridge into town could wash out, cutting off trucks carrying food and fuel.

"I'm worried about my business, worried about my employees, definitely worried for everybody in this town," German said.

## Flooding collides with Election Day

The disaster struck on the eve of the 2026 midterm elections, forcing Colorado officials to improvise for displaced voters. The Colorado Secretary of State's office arranged alternative voting plans for evacuated or marooned Pagosa Springs residents. Voters who received mail ballots can obtain replacements at any vote center statewide, and mail ballots can be returned to any drop box or vote center by 7 p.m. on Election Day. The La Plata County clerk planned special hours Tuesday to assist.

It is a rare collision of natural disaster and civic logistics — one that election administrators across the region will be watching closely as they gauge whether displaced residents can still make their voices heard.

## Rescues from rooftops and swollen rivers

The flooding's reach extended across three states. In Sedona, Arizona, officials ordered residents near Oak Creek to evacuate Sunday as floodwaters trapped multiple people in homes, prompting helicopter rescues from neighborhoods. About 20 homes needed emergency assistance to relocate, according to Yavapai County spokesperson David McAtee. Heavy rain washed out part of a main highway between Flagstaff and Sedona, mangling guardrails and littering the road with boulders and trees.

"I've never seen them rescuing people with helicopters out of neighborhoods," said longtime Sedona resident Michelle Grimm.

In Durango, Colorado, evacuations were ordered along the Animas River, which was closed to kayaks and other watercraft. Police Chief Brice Current warned that sewage and chemicals flushed into the river could make people sick, and that riverside trails and footbridges were shut.

"There is still a lot of people stranded," Current said. "There's a lot of emergency services being utilized today."

In New Mexico, evacuations were ordered along the San Juan River and on the Navajo Nation, including parts of Farmington, where about 600 households were affected at one point. A two-mile stretch of U.S. Highway 64 west of Shiprock was closed, and the village of Chama was evacuated and cut off by road closures. In Tucson, Arizona, crews conducted swiftwater rescues.

"We are getting two or three months of rain in a span of a couple of days," said Aaron Hardin, a National Weather Service meteorologist in Tucson.

## Relief agencies stretched thin

The American Red Cross has deployed almost 140 disaster volunteers, with shelters open across Arizona, Colorado and New Mexico housing more than 280 people Sunday night. Early estimates indicate hundreds of homes destroyed or with major damage. In Farmington, eight-year-old Brycen Bloom and his family took refuge at a Red Cross shelter after fleeing their home along the San Juan River.

"It was really scary," Brycen said. "I thought our house was gonna float away like a boat."

His father, Chris Bloom, praised the volunteers. "Everyone has been so kind and helpful; thanks to the Red Cross for coming out and pulling this all together," he said, according to a [Red Cross statement](https://www.redcross.org/about-us/news-and-events/news/2026/red-cross-responds-to-devastating-southwest-flooding.html).

The flooding is the latest in a punishing 2026 disaster season that has included Southern California wildfires, catastrophic flooding in Texas, Typhoon Halong in Alaska and three super typhoons striking Guam, Saipan and Tinian — a sequence the Red Cross says has stretched response resources.

## A "super duper" El Niño

Scientists attribute the extraordinary rainfall to an unusually powerful El Niño, which is intensifying heavy rains across the southern United States. Experts note that human-caused climate change has already warmed the oceans, making El Niño events more intense than in the past.

"We've had super El Niños in the past, but this is a super duper El Niño," said meteorologist Jeff Masters of Yale Climate Connections, as [AP reported](https://apnews.com/article/el-nino-southwest-floods-colorado-arizona-88cf0ddc26d3ec7e40941fb437f9a4e8).

"The likelihood of big precipitation in the Southwest interior does go up this time of year, but we're seeing it to a degree we actually haven't seen before," said climate scientist Daniel Swain of the University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources.

Randy Cerveny, a meteorologist at Arizona State University, said warmer baseline ocean temperatures amplify each El Niño cycle. "The fact that the ocean temperatures are already warmer than they were, say, 100 years ago, makes these El Niño situations even more intense," he said. "We're not at the peak of the heating in the Pacific Ocean. So yes, we will see more of these particular events."

## What to watch

The El Niño is expected to peak in December, raising the prospect of further rounds of flooding through the winter. Full damage assessments — and the ultimate cost of the disaster — await the water's retreat. Officials have cautioned that the death toll could still change as investigations in Flagstaff continue.

For now, the region's residents face a familiar and painful task: waiting for the waters to fall, then beginning the long work of recovery. And with more moisture likely on the way, the question hanging over the Southwest is not whether the rains will return, but how soon.
