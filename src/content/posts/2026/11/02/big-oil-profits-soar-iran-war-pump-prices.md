# Big Oil Profits Soar Again as Iran War Lifts Pump Prices

ExxonMobil, Chevron and Shell together earned just over $36 billion in the third quarter of 2026, the companies reported this week — another windfall driven largely by elevated crude and diesel prices stemming from the ongoing war in Iran. The results land days before U.S. midterm elections in which fuel costs have become a potent political issue, reviving Democratic calls for a windfall profits tax.

According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/31/nx-s1-6008072/oil-company-profits-gasoline-diesel-prices), ExxonMobil earned $15.4 billion, more than doubling its results from a year earlier. Shell's earnings nearly doubled to $10.8 billion, and Chevron's rose 180% to $9.9 billion.

## A Supply Shock, Not a Demand Boom

The profits are the product of a classic supply shock layered atop two simultaneous wars. Since the U.S. and Israel struck Iran in late February, Tehran has restricted traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes. In parallel, Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries and Houthi attacks on Middle Eastern shipping have taken additional refining capacity offline.

The result is that oil produced outside the Middle East has become far more valuable. Brent crude is trading around $113 a barrel, up from about $70 before the war. "The majors are able to sell their barrels that they produce in the U.S. or Latin America or Europe for much higher prices," Dan Pickering of Pickering Energy Partners told NPR. "So the Middle East's loss is generally the gain of oil and gas companies in the U.S. and Europe."

Refining, rather than drilling, has powered much of the gain. [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/30/chevron-exxon-cvx-xom-earnings-q3-2026.html) reported that Chevron's refining profits surged more than 300% to $4.9 billion, while its U.S. refineries processed record volumes at 98% capacity. Exxon's Energy Products division earned about $7.2 billion, outpacing its production business, as Gulf Coast refineries ran at 100% crude-unit utilization and produced record volumes of diesel.

"Refining capacity is very tight today," Exxon CEO Darren Woods told CNBC. "Frankly, diesel is the key crunch point here because it is so critical to economic growth [with] very inelastic demand." Chevron's Mike Wirth struck a similar note: "The system is under stress. We remain in an environment that is fairly fragile."

## The Cost to Consumers

Those profits have a mirror image at the pump. Gasoline now costs about $1.20 more per gallon than a year ago, according to AAA, and diesel hit an all-time national record of roughly $6.53 a gallon in late September before easing slightly. The pain is especially acute for trucking, farming and freight, sectors that run on diesel and pass costs along to consumers.

Oil executives say they are doing what they can to ease the squeeze. "We recognize the impact high prices have on people's lives and economic growth," Woods said, adding that Exxon has maximized production to keep prices in check. Still, the companies are directing much of their windfall to shareholders rather than new drilling. Exxon announced its highest-ever quarterly dividend and $9.4 billion in third-quarter distributions, while Shell executed one of its largest-ever buybacks. Pickering said the caution reflects the unpredictable duration of the geopolitical shock: "You put the money in the bank and watch to see what happens as we move into 2027."

Shell CEO Wael Sawan warned that high prices may prove more durable than expected. "We're now seeing more and more structural stability in that higher oil price simply because we are seeing that inventory levels are being drawn down very quickly," he said, noting it will take considerable time to refill global stockpiles — including a U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve sitting at low levels.

## Windfall Tax Back in Play

The scale of the profits has reignited a long-running political fight. Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.) introduced the Big Oil Windfall Profits Tax Act in March, which would levy an excise tax on excess oil profits and return revenue to Americans through rebates. The measure has stalled, but the issue has fresh salience as fuel costs weigh on voters.

"The companies that are running this essential sector have been reaping record profits amidst the worst energy supply crisis that we have had in several decades," Isabella Weber, an economist at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, told NPR. "This is a contradiction that, I think, raises fundamental questions about how we organize our economy, how we organize our society, who benefits, who loses."

Woods has previously called windfall taxes a "misguided policy" that penalizes companies navigating a volatile industry.

## What to Watch

The Trump administration has taken stopgap steps aimed at lowering fuel costs before the midterms — temporarily loosening rules on dyed diesel and lifting sanctions on Russia to allow more diesel into the world market — but analysts say the measures are too small to meaningfully move U.S. prices, and significantly cheaper fuel is unlikely to arrive by Election Day. With inventories depleted and the Iran conflict unresolved, the structural case for elevated prices — and elevated oil profits — appears intact for now. The question is how long consumers, and their representatives in Washington, are willing to absorb the difference.
