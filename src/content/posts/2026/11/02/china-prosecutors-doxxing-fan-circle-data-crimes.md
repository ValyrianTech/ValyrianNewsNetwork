# China Prosecutors Target Doxxing, Fan-Circle Data Crimes

China's Supreme People's Procuratorate (SPP) on Monday published eight typical cases on the lawful protection of citizens' personal information, spotlighting the aggressive doxxing practice known as *kaihe* (开盒) and the trade in celebrities' flight and location data that flourishes inside fan circles (*fanquan*, 饭圈). According to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/legal/20261102/649fd8524ef541bc98899324610cbb04/c.html), the release was timed to the fifth anniversary of the Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL), which took effect on November 1, 2021.

The notice, formally titled *Notice on Issuing the Typical Cases of Procuratorial Organs Lawfully Protecting Citizens' Personal Information*, was distributed to provincial-level procuratorates, the military procuratorate, and the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps procuratorate, the SPP said.

## Context: Why Now

The PIPL gave China its first comprehensive statute governing the collection, storage, and transfer of personal data. Its Article 70 authorizes procuratorates to bring public-interest litigation when personal-information harms affect broader public interests—a tool the SPP is now showcasing five years into the law's life. The cases reflect a sustained push, begun with the 2021 *Qinglang* (清朗) "Clean Up Fan Circles" campaign by the Cyberspace Administration of China, to curb celebrity-information trading, chart manipulation, and related online disorder.

## The Doxxing Case at the Center

Case 4, from Chengdu's Longquanyi district, illustrates how far the practice has evolved. A six-defendant group led by Chen Moucheng hacked a provincial affairs platform and other systems, obtaining more than 16 million records and over a billion account and phone entries. They then "opened the box" on 47 victims across the country, including five minors, and five of those victims sought medical care for depression. Sentences ranged from four years and five months to nine months, upheld on appeal on April 30, 2026.

The SPP defined *kaihe* as a "new type of online illegal and criminal behavior in which offenders illegally collect and dig up citizens' personal information online and publicly post it," describing it as "an important source of online violence." The Chengdu case is the same matter CCTV's *Focus Interview* examined under the headline questioning whether the practice is "worse than human-flesh searching."

## Fan-Circle Data Trade

Case 6, from Beijing's Chaoyang district, centers on Shu, an outsourced airline call-center employee and devoted fan who sold celebrities' flight and location data through fan groups. Over 30 celebrities and more than 100 flights were affected, netting more than 10,000 yuan. Shu received a ten-month suspended sentence plus a 30,000 yuan fine. Prosecutors also pursued civil public-interest litigation, appealing to recover illegal gains; the second-instance court ordered recovery on April 28, 2025.

The SPP noted that "chasing stars at airports, airport gatherings, and paid-photo blockades recur despite repeated bans," infringing privacy, disrupting public order, and creating safety risks.

## A Broader Pattern

The remaining cases reveal recurring motifs across the batch: cross-border data transfer, cryptocurrency used to evade enforcement, insider abuse, and targeting of vulnerable groups. In Case 1, an 11-defendant network in Zhejiang trafficked data through overseas encrypted channels and cryptocurrency, with more than 1.98 million yuan involved. In Case 3, a 25-defendant scheme in Henan harvested names, ID numbers, and facial biometrics from more than 20,000 elderly people across six provinces to register and sell payment accounts. Case 8, a civil public-interest suit in Guangzhou, involved web crawlers breaching a cancer hospital's servers to steal more than 3.85 million records; the Guangzhou Internet Court assessed damages at 1.5 million yuan in a ruling dated August 24, 2026.

## Analysis and Implications

The batch signals a shift from criminal prosecution alone toward integrated governance—pairing criminal sentences with civil public-interest damages, as in Cases 6, 7, and 8. The 1.5 million yuan damage award in Case 8 is notable for valuing harm by the sensitivity and scope of the breached data rather than by provable profits, an approach that could reshape how courts assess data-theft damages. The SPP framed its future work as continuing to implement Xi Jinping Thought on the Rule of Law and the "overall national security concept," deepening integrated governance to "root out the soil for crime."

## What's Next

The release raises questions it does not answer: whether the batch will translate into binding prosecutorial guidelines or remain illustrative, and how the CAC's continuing campaigns will intersect with enforcement. A parallel track is already visible—on May 8, 2026, the Supreme People's Court published its own five cases on personal-information crimes. Together, the two releases sketch an emerging judicial consensus that personal data is both an individual right and a matter of public interest.
