# Army to Detonate 5 Tons of Explosives Near Iowa Town

Residents of a small southeastern Iowa town are preparing to evacuate or shelter indoors as the U.S. Army moves ahead with a plan to detonate roughly 5 tons of unstable, bomb-making chemicals — an operation expected to send a plume of lead-containing dust drifting across parts of two states.

The two controlled blasts of degraded lead azide are scheduled for about noon local time Friday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown, a community of nearly 400 people on the Mississippi River near the Illinois border. According to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/iowa-army-ammunition-lead-detonation-2d2d7bfaf8ceacddcbea10ed97b097ce), officials are recommending that some nearby residents begin vacating the area that morning, and that those who stay put keep their windows and doors closed.

## Why the Army says it must detonate in place

The lead azide — a highly sensitive primary explosive used to initiate munitions — had been stored at the plant for more than 20 years in earthen-covered containers, submerged in a bath of ethanol and water to keep it stable. During a routine check earlier this year, workers found that some of the water had evaporated, allowing the material to recrystallize and become far more sensitive to friction and shock.

That change "creat[ed] a highly unstable and dangerous situation," according to an emergency permit approved by the Environmental Protection Agency, as [the Army's public advisory](https://www.army.mil/article/296772) describes the disposal operation. Moving or relocating the material, officials concluded, posed an unacceptable risk of an accidental detonation — a risk that grows in cold weather.

"We're really concerned about the weather situation," Justine Barati, a public affairs specialist with Army Sustainment Command, told AP. "We're really concerned that it will detonate on its own."

The Army said it assembled a panel of experts in munitions chemistry, engineering and environmental safety to design the plan. The 68 barrels are surrounded above, below and on all sides by concrete buried under soil, with sand barriers stacked two stories high. During the blasts, the soil will be sprayed with water and the air will be misted to try to keep most of the lead on Army property.

## A dust plume that ignores state lines

Even with those measures, officials acknowledge the detonation will produce "an air plume with some lead-containing particles" that will disperse in the air and settle on the ground. The dust is expected to linger for no more than about an hour, depending on wind speed, but could break old windows in town.

The EPA approved an emergency permit on Nov. 13 under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, requiring the Army to model lead dispersal and notify the public five days in advance. That produced a Sunday advisory to communities as far as 100 miles away, complete with preliminary plume maps that officials say will be updated as the forecast evolves.

The projected path has already shifted once. An initial map placed Mediapolis, a city of nearly 2,000 residents about 20 miles northeast, in the center of the dust exposure area; an updated map no longer expected it to be affected. Schools and a public library there will still close Friday, and the local fire department planned to hose down playgrounds that could be coated in lead particles.

"This is probably a first for me. I believe it's a first for a lot of our citizens," Mediapolis Deputy City Clerk Amy Stucker said. "The biggest issue we're having is the lack of information."

## Illinois officials demand more testing

The plan has drawn attention well beyond Iowa's borders. In a [joint release](https://dph.illinois.gov/resource-center/news/2026/november/18-release-2.html), Illinois environmental and public health officials said air modeling suggests the plume could reach Mercer County in western Illinois — and criticized the lack of information shared across state lines.

"The Department of Defense has an important responsibility to ensure its operations do not jeopardize the health of Illinois residents or the quality of our environment," said Illinois EPA Director James Jennings. "Air pollution does not respect state lines."

Illinois EPA said it was not notified by EPA Region 7, which permitted the operation, and that the Army's maps did not disclose what conditions the modeling included. The agency is urging the military to conduct comprehensive post-detonation sampling of air, soil and surface dust in affected areas of Illinois and to publish the results.

"There is no safe level of lead in the body, and when inhaled or swallowed, it can pose serious health risks," said Dr. Sameer Vohra, director of the Illinois Department of Public Health.

## Concern focused on children

Lead is a neurological toxicant that can impair brain development, particularly in young children. Patrick O'Shaughnessy, a professor at the University of Iowa's Department of Occupational and Environmental Health, told AP it is encouraging that the Army and EPA are working to minimize the dust, but that it remains unknown how much will ultimately settle in the atmosphere and on surfaces. He said he is especially concerned about toddlers, who tend to put objects in their mouths.

Medical toxicologist Jennifer Sample went further, telling [the Des Moines Register](https://www.desmoinesregister.com/story/news/health/2026/11/18/lead-azide-bomb-detonation-army-plant-plume-maps-middletown-iowa/92841678007/) that "the state of Iowa should close all schools in the plume path."

The Army characterizes the risk as limited and temporary. "It's not lead paint where it's a chronic exposure by children," Barati said. "It's the equivalent of living in a mining town for one hour." The permit requires environmental monitoring on and off site, with soil and surface-wipe sampling to begin within 24 hours in accessible public and consenting private areas. Barati confirmed there are no plans to offer lead blood testing tied to the detonation.

Researchers at the University of Iowa plan to conduct independent monitoring. David Cwiertny, director of the university's Center for Health Effects of Environmental Contamination, cautioned on social media that hosing down lead-containing dust does not mean it is "magically gone."

## What to watch for

Much depends on Friday's weather. The permit bars the operation if surface winds drop below 1 mph or exceed 15 mph, or in the presence of thunderstorms or temperature inversions — and forecasts have called for gusty southerly winds that could complicate the schedule.

For residents, health officials recommend staying indoors with windows closed for 24 to 48 hours, running HVAC systems in recirculation mode, wet-cleaning high-contact surfaces, and removing shoes at the door. Those who can leave are urged to do so during the evacuation window.

Beyond Friday, the central questions are what the soil and air sampling will show, whether Illinois receives the comprehensive testing and notification it has requested, and whether the episode prompts scrutiny of how emergency permits and interstate warnings are handled when a hazardous cleanup can drift across state lines.
