# Neo-Nazi Active Clubs: Belgium's OCAD Watches 'Standby Army'

Belgian security services are monitoring a fast-growing network of neo-Nazi "Active Clubs" that present themselves as ordinary fight and fitness gyms while training members for what they regard as an inevitable race war, according to a joint investigation by VRT NWS's *Pano* and the Dutch programme *Zembla* (BNN VARA). The Coordination Unit for Threat Analysis (OCAD) confirmed to the broadcasters that it is tracking the phenomenon, which investigators describe as a "standby army waiting for activation."

The clubs, which spread from the United States and now operate in at least 26 countries, have established a foothold in Flanders and the Netherlands. Named groups include Wolvenrad, Active Club Dietsland and Active Club Vlaanderen, the last founded in April 2026.

## Hide in plain sight

The investigation, built on an infiltrator, hidden cameras and undercover recordings, [reports](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/17/pano-gevaar/) how the clubs deliberately disguise their ideology behind a fitness-club facade. The tactic is described in a [Danish Centre for Documentation and Counter Extremism (CDE) report](https://stopekstremisme.dk/en/research-and-analysis/active-clubs.pdf) as "hide in plain sight" — using codewords and balaclavas to keep police and the public looking the other way.

"I had to spot white boys in the gym," the infiltrator told Pano. "Once you have contact, you can try to lure them to the club to radicalize them further." Recruits are first helped with training schedules, then gradually exposed to an ideology presented as "extreme right, not Nazi."

"They prepare for what they consider inevitable: the collapse, the race war, the civil war," the infiltrator said. "Hence those trained bodies. They are the standby army waiting for activation."

## Violence, propaganda and a grey zone of legality

The clubs have already been linked to violence. Four members of Active Club Sweden were convicted of hate crimes after beating three men of foreign background in the summer of 2025 in what they called a "hunting event." Members also appeared at demonstrations in Belgium — including a school protest in Kortrijk and a remigration rally in Leuven where someone was beaten, though police never identified the perpetrators.

During a Hitler commemoration, Pano intercepted an alarming remark about targeting politicians: "I still don't understand why there isn't a militia that hunts those people longer. I can hunt politicians with my gun, can't I?"

Fight nights are filmed extensively to produce propaganda; one video of fights in the Dutch town of Zuidwolde has drawn nearly 150,000 views. On Telegram, clubs across Europe urge one another on: "We are the vanguard. We must do things the average person doesn't yet agree with."

Much of this operates in a legal grey zone. The Hitler salute is punishable only in certain circumstances in Belgium, a boundary the clubs appear to test deliberately.

## OCAD: impact 'limited' but deserving vigilance

In its response to the broadcasters, Belgium's OCAD [stated](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/18/reacties-pano/) that it monitors the Active Clubs "as part of the broader right-extremist landscape." The body said the impact in Belgium remains "limited for now," but that the phenomenon "deserves vigilance because the model can also exert an attraction here."

That caution is set against a rising trend. OCAD's [2025 annual report](https://ocad.belgium.be/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/20260909_Annual-Report-2025-English.pdf) recorded that the number of threat reports concerning right-wing extremism more than doubled compared with the previous year.

## Experts warn of rapid growth

Alexander Ritzmann, a specialist in violent extremism, called the pace of expansion unprecedented. "I think it's unprecedented that they have 600 to 700 people in Europe in barely 2 or 3 years — selected to commit targeted violence against their enemies," he said. "It poses an enormous threat to security."

Ritzmann argued the clubs' branding is itself a tactic: "It is specifically designed to mislead police and security services so that they look the other way."

Christophe Busch, a radicalization expert at the Hannah Arendt Institute, framed the danger in political terms. "If the violence ultimately has a political finality to complete a certain ideological end goal or utopian vision of society, then that becomes a problem," he said.

## Analysis: numbers are not the whole story

The investigation suggests the threat posed by Active Clubs lies less in raw membership figures than in the machinery around them. Fitness is the recruiting funnel; fight nights are the propaganda engine; Telegram is the distribution network. As the Danish report notes, the clubs normalize far-right ideas through sport and lifestyle, radicalize young men via online propaganda, and build physical networks of trained activists with international contacts.

For Belgium, several questions remain open. Whether authorities will pursue bans or prosecutions, the precise scope of a reported training location in Brussels linked to Active Club Vlaanderen, and whether the Flemish club's membership grows in the coming months all remain unresolved. The clubs deny being dangerous — Active Club Vlaanderen's members told the broadcasters they focus solely on a healthy youth — but the combination of secrecy, training and glorified violence is precisely what has drawn the attention of the security services.

## What to watch

The Dutch dimension may prove instructive: after the reporting aired, the party Forum voor Democratie expelled two members over attendance at cage fights where the Hitler salute was given and at a Hitler commemoration. Whether similar political spillover emerges in Belgium — no structural link to any party has been established — will be one measure of how far the movement has embedded itself. For now, OCAD's watchful stance signals that the standby army has at least been noticed.
