# Tech Outages Expose Strain on AI-Era Infrastructure

Within a single week in mid-November, a striking cluster of system failures swept through some of the world's most powerful institutions — crippling cloud tools at Microsoft and Apple, halting every commercial flight in the United States, disrupting banking at JPMorgan Chase and knocking a classified communications network at the National Security Agency offline. According to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34501995), the back-to-back failures are now raising a central question: as governments and corporations rush to deploy AI-era technology, can the digital infrastructure they depend on keep pace?

 US officials have since offered a reassuring but uncomfortable answer. The outages were not the work of hackers — they were self-inflicted, the product of frantic maintenance and system upgrades layered on top of aging "legacy" computers.

## A Week of Cascading Failures

The sequence began on **November 12**, when a software vulnerability inside a Microsoft data center hit popular tools including Outlook, Teams and Copilot. At the worst point, more than 80% of Microsoft's network gateway servers were unresponsive. Microsoft later published a preliminary post-incident report explaining that a bug introduced back in July, but never detected, was triggered when the company adjusted Teams traffic routing amid insufficient server capacity — producing a nearly two-day outage response.

The next day, the Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop of all US airport departures for more than four hours. As [RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/world/2650569/) reported, it was the longest halt to US air travel since the September 11, 2001 attacks. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy attributed the fault to switches and routers in the air-traffic-control communications system that malfunctioned under excessive data load, linked to work installing a new router and replacing copper wiring with fiber-optic cable.

On **November 16**, JPMorgan Chase users reported online service anomalies. The bank confirmed a software defect in its local hardware systems had slowed some transaction processing, and said the cause was technical rather than a cyberattack — though it did not disclose whether a software update triggered it. United Airlines also resolved a "widespread" system outage that delayed roughly 150 flights.

## From the NSA to the App Store

The most alarming incident came on **November 17**, when a classified NSA network used for internal communications failed and was classified as a "yellow code" event — the second-highest level in a three-tier alert system. The NSA said it experienced "temporary system performance degradation during scheduled routine maintenance" and that mission-critical capabilities remained fully operational.

That same day, Apple suffered a large-scale technical failure affecting the App Store, cloud services and payments, as well as internal systems including its employee portal and VPN access. Apple said the problem was "not caused by a cyberattack," attributing it to an internal system update. Google's Gmail and Marriott International's online platform also drew user outage reports, though both companies said there was no evidence of a major breach.

On **November 18**, Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton testified before the House Homeland Security Committee that the incidents were "associated with maintenance and technical changes" and were "not a coordinated or state-sponsored attacks," [the New York Post reported](https://nypost.com/2026/11/18/us-news/dni-jay-clayton-affirms-widespread-tech-outages-not-coordinated-result-of-updating-legacy-systems/).

## The AI Patching Paradox

The common thread, officials and experts say, is not a single AI failure or a coordinated attack — it is the strain of modernizing old systems at unprecedented speed. Clayton told lawmakers that many of the problems stemmed from installing software patches to fix major vulnerabilities discovered the previous month, some of them uncovered by powerful AI models now turned loose on corporate and government networks to hunt for weaknesses.

"These models are identifying massive vulnerabilities, and that increases the urgency to patch right away," a congressional official briefed on the matter told the Post. "They're trying to push through a lot of patches all at once — more than the system was designed for."

Senator Mark Warner, vice chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee, made a similar point, noting that AI is helping identify security holes across vast systems at unprecedented speed, forcing firms to patch quickly — and that concentrated updates place enormous pressure on systems never designed for that pace.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin put it bluntly to CNBC: the events are "not cyberattacks, but problems arising from aging tech infrastructure and the upgrade process." Many organizations, he said, are "deploying new tech on old computer systems, which may overwhelm the system."

## A 'Frankenstein's Monster' of Legacy Systems

Industry experts warn the pattern reflects a deeper structural problem. Marva Bailer, CEO of tech consultancy Qualaix, described legacy systems as a "Frankenstein's monster" — AI companies and third-party vendors keep bolting on new components and functions, introducing ever more points of failure. She also pointed to seasonal pressure: with the holiday travel and shopping peak approaching, firms were racing to finish upgrades before December, when consumer demand surges and more back-office staff go on leave.

Joe Slowik, threat research director at risk-intelligence firm Dataminr, called the clustering of so many high-profile incidents in a short window rare and concerning. "We rely too heavily on infrastructure whose stability is already shaky and under-invest in redundancy," he said. "Whether it's an update gone wrong or a cyber event, it can trigger the worst-case scenario."

## Is the Sky Really Falling?

Not everyone is convinced the cluster signals a systemic collapse. As [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-so-many-critical-tech-outages-are-happening-all-at-once/) noted, analysis by the Uptime Institute found that data-center outage rates per site had actually fallen in 2026 for a fifth consecutive year. "If there are 52 major outages in a year, that doesn't mean there'll be one every week," said Laura Nolan, a software reliability engineer at Trace Cognitive Engineering. "It doesn't necessarily mean that it's a shared causal factor."

Yet the publication also highlighted a real and growing hazard: the sheer complexity of modern, interconnected systems means that even routine changes can produce unpredictable outcomes. "When making changes in such systems, the results are often unpredictable," said Nancy Leveson, an MIT professor who studies system safety. Kelly Shortridge, chief product officer at the infrastructure firm Fastly, offered a likely explanation for the uncanny timing — the annual rush to ship software changes before companies freeze updates over the holiday period. "In the weeks before the freeze, teams scramble to release everything at once," she said, describing failures as "a routine change colliding with a certainty that had gone unchallenged long enough to gather dust."

## What to Watch

The immediate political question is resilience and transparency. White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis said there was "no crisis or anything like that for Americans to be worried about," while some lawmakers — including Senator Josh Hawley — pressed for briefings, and former officials urged candor. The deeper issue is structural: whether companies and agencies will invest in redundancy and modernize deliberately, rather than in the last-minute scramble that appears to have triggered this month's cascade. As Nolan put it, software incidents are inevitable — the challenge is making sure the next one doesn't bring everything else crashing down with it.
