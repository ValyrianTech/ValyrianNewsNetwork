# Tencent's AI Buildout Squeezes Profit as Capex Peaks

Tencent Holdings reported third-quarter results on Wednesday that laid bare the cost of China's AI arms race: revenue rose but profit fell, as the company ploughed record sums into AI computing power and guided that heavy spending will persist through 2028. The Shenzhen-based giant said group revenue grew 8% year-on-year to RMB 207.3 billion, but non-IFRS operating profit slipped 6% to RMB 68.1 billion, with its operating margin down to 33% from 38% a year earlier, according to its [official results announcement](https://www.prnewswire.com/apac/news-releases/tencent-announces-2026-third-quarter-results-302943839.html) released after the Hong Kong market close on 18 November.

## The bill comes due

The headline number masks a sharper divergence. Excluding new AI products, non-IFRS operating profit would have risen 8% to RMB 83.3 billion at a stable 40% margin. The roughly RMB 15 billion gap between the two figures is the clearest measure yet of what Tencent's AI push is costing at the bottom line, [Caixin reported](https://www.caixin.com/2026-11-19/102506437.html).

The cash-flow picture is starker still. Capital expenditure payments surged 225% year-on-year to RMB 65.1 billion, driving free cash flow to negative RMB 69.1 billion — or a positive RMB 16.9 billion once large prepayments for compute procurement are stripped out. Over the first nine months of 2026 alone, capital spending reached RMB 166.15 billion, more than double the RMB 79.2 billion shelled out across all of 2025, [Guancha noted](https://www.guancha.cn/CaiJing/2026_11_19_908989.shtml).

Perhaps most telling for a company long prized for its balance-sheet strength: Tencent's net cash position flipped to net debt of RMB 13.7 billion at the end of September, from net cash of RMB 58.2 billion three months earlier and RMB 102.4 billion a year before. Total cash stood at RMB 525.7 billion, and property, plant and equipment swelled to RMB 242.5 billion from RMB 149.9 billion at the end of 2025 — a visible record of the data-centre build-out.

## A strategic pivot, not a cost line

The spending reflects a shift in how China's largest technology company frames AI. Three years ago, Chairman and CEO Pony Ma called AI a "once-in-centuries opportunity" and argued that bringing a lightbulb to market a month early did not matter. At Tencent's 28th-anniversary gathering this month, his language had hardened: AI is "a matter of life and death," and he urged "all businesses to re-start up for the AI era."

Management now describes the outlay as "pre-investment" rather than a recurring cost. President Martin Lau told analysts the company is "at the very early stage of this," adding that realizing AI's benefits across the business requires "a pretty significant reorg of the organization." Tencent duly announced a reorganization to redeploy staff from mature businesses toward AI-driven growth.

The competitive logic is unforgiving. Tencent Cloud CTO Wang Huixing has warned that in the large-model race, "two months without a new version" can push a player off the frontier. Tencent's own model cadence has accelerated in response, with the November launch of its Hy4 family. The company said Hy4 ranked first among Chinese models at launch on the Agent Arena benchmark, while the efficiency-optimized Hy4 flash cuts per-task inference costs to roughly one-twentieth of Hy4.

## Early monetization, and the chip question

The first returns are visible, if modest. WorkBuddy, Tencent's AI productivity assistant, has topped 10 million daily active users consuming more than 100 trillion tokens a day, and the company says it is now China's most-used AI application by token consumption. Cloud services revenue grew 35% year-on-year, and marketing services rose 19% on AI-driven advertising.

Still, two risks loom. First, US export restrictions on advanced chips are a constraint on the training side; Lau acknowledged the curbs "do set everyone back in China," though he argued inference-driven businesses are insulated because efficient models like Hy4 flash can run entirely on domestic silicon. Second, macroeconomic pressure and memory-cost inflation — DRAM prices are up roughly 400% year-on-year — are squeezing the core business, which Chief Strategy Officer James Mitchell said will face those pressures "for the next few quarters, and then move through them."

## What's next

The pivotal question is whether the 2028 guidance holds. Management has set a multi-year arc: capex at peak levels through 2026-2027, declining thereafter, with 2028 delivering "much better" economics as compute efficiency improves and the build-out is fully utilized. Lau likened the current phase to "building a factory" usable for both training and inference.

For investors, the test is timing. Tencent's disclosure is a bellwether for the wider Chinese AI capex cycle, with rivals such as Alibaba and ByteDance facing similar compute intensity and chip-access constraints. The near-term arithmetic is unforgiving — a firm that once grew profit at double-digit rates has seen that growth turn negative. Whether usage converts into profit before depreciation from this spending wave hits margins is the bet Lau is now asking shareholders to underwrite.
