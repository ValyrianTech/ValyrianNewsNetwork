# Union Pacific Merger Promises Cheaper Shipping

Union Pacific says its proposed $85 billion acquisition of Norfolk Southern would create America's first single-service transcontinental railroad and drive down shipping costs for customers. But as federal regulators closed the public-comment window on the deal this week, a broad coalition of farmers, labor unions and manufacturers is pushing back, warning the merger would concentrate power over the nation's freight network and raise prices rather than lower them.

The merger, [under review](https://www.stb.gov/resources/major-railroad-mergers/) in Docket FD 36873 by the federal Surface Transportation Board (STB), would combine the two railroads into a system spanning more than 50,000 route miles across 43 states and linking roughly 100 ports. Public comments on the application were due to the board on Nov. 18, according to [Nebraska Public Media](https://nebraskapublicmedia.org/en/news/news-articles/union-pacific-says-a-merger-would-make-shipping-cheaper-farmers-labor-and-shippers-are-skeptical/), the Midwest Newsroom outlet that first reported the dispute.

If approved, the deal would shrink the number of major U.S. railroads from six to five and produce the largest railroad in the country's history.

## A First-of-Its-Kind Railroad

The U.S. rail network is split roughly along the Mississippi River, with railroads concentrated in the East and West. Midwestern hubs including Chicago, Kansas City and St. Louis serve as interchange points where freight moves from one carrier to another. Union Pacific argues that eliminating those time-consuming handoffs is the core of the merger's value.

"The creation of America's first single-service transcontinental railroad will eliminate time-consuming rail interchanges that will improve service for shippers, reduce costs for consumers and strengthen the nation's supply chain," Union Pacific spokesperson Robynn Tysver said in an email. "A shift from road to rail will ease congestion, reduce taxpayer-funded road maintenance, cut emissions and deliver cargo faster and more reliably."

The companies project that the combined railroad would shift about 2.1 million truckloads off the road annually and generate roughly $2.75 billion in annualized synergies. Mike McClellan, Norfolk Southern's senior vice president and chief strategy officer, said the goal is to make rail a stronger competitor to trucking.

"People use railroads because we're cheap, right?" McClellan said. "Trucks will always present essentially the ceiling on what we can do with pricing." He added: "I will say that we are doing this to grow rail business, not to shrink it."

## Skeptics Warn of a 'Mega-Railroad'

Opponents have coalesced under the banner of the [Stop the Rail Merger Coalition](https://stoptherailmerger.com/coalition-launches/), launched on April 29, 2026. Its members include labor unions, shippers, farmers, transportation groups, the American Chemistry Council and competing railroads such as BNSF. The coalition contends the deal would stamp out competition, drive up costs for manufacturers, farmers and consumers, and inject new weaknesses into the workforce and supply chain.

The concerns center on so-called captive shippers — businesses that have access to only one railroad and cannot easily switch to a competitor or to road transport. Chemical manufacturers and grain elevators are often captive, because their commodities require tanks or hopper cars that do not transfer easily to trucks.

"This is frightening for a captive shipper," said transportation consultant Bob Granatelli, who has worked in the industry since the 1970s. "It will make captivity even stronger."

Jeff Sloan, senior director of regulatory affairs for the American Chemistry Council, framed the risk in blunt terms. "That exposes them to higher rates, less responsive customer service," he said. "Essentially, if you're not afraid of losing your customer, you treat your customer differently."

Elena Prager, an assistant professor of economics at the University of Rochester's Simon Business School, said the pattern is well established. "The typical things that happen as monopoly power rises in a market are increases in price or reductions in service or product quality," she said. "In general, you're looking at higher prices, lower quantities and worse quality."

## Farmers Fear Fewer Options

The American Farm Bureau Federation, which opposes the merger, has warned that farmers would be left more dependent on fewer railroads at a time when their balance sheets are already strained. The group's economists noted that transportation, marketing and storage expenses are projected to hit a record $14 billion in 2026, and that food and farm products make up about 20% of total U.S. rail tonnage, according to a [Farm Bureau analysis](https://www.fb.org/news-release/rail-merger-would-be-costly-for-farmers).

"With fewer routing and carrier options, large portions of the country would be dependent on a single railroad for end-to-end service," the Farm Bureau wrote. "When that pressure disappears, history shows that farmers do not ship less — they get paid less."

Ash Anderson, education director for the Brotherhood of Maintenance of Way Employees (BMWE), said the ripple effects extend to the local level. "If a grain elevator can only ship on one railroad now, and they set the pricing, that's going to affect all of the farmers that get grain to that elevator, and so now those farmers may have to eat more costs," Anderson said.

Clark Ballew, the union's communications director, said the scale of the combined carrier is unprecedented. "If this goes through, they will be a mega-railroad," he said. "Quite literally. Obviously the biggest and largest railroad that's ever existed in the United States. Unforeseen to this point."

## Labor, Safety and a Contested Video

Union Pacific has sought to reassure workers with a "Jobs For Life" promise for union employees on both sides of the merger, negotiated with the International Association of Sheet Metal, Air, Rail and Transportation Workers (SMART), which supports the deal. But the BMWE says it does not trust the agreement and cites it as a reason for its opposition.

Safety concerns loom large in the debate, particularly in light of Norfolk Southern's 2023 derailment near East Palestine, Ohio, where a train carrying hazardous materials caught fire and sent a toxic cloud over the town. Nate Lee, a BNSF rail worker and local union chairman, said the risk is a daily reality for maintenance crews.

"Across the country, you have the potential for an East Palestine-type incident pretty much every single day," Lee said. "It's up to us folks maintaining the railroads to make sure that that doesn't happen." He warned that fatigue could make mistakes more likely: "You can only run a person ragged for so long before it starts to wear on you."

Skepticism intensified this fall after the public release of a video in which Union Pacific CEO Jim Vena threatened to raise rates for businesses that did not support the merger. "I'll probably increase everybody's rates that said they didn't want a seamless railroad by 3 or 4% just to teach you a lesson," Vena said at a March 2026 meeting of the Southwest Association of Rail Shippers in Houston.

Union Pacific sought to keep the recording private, but a federal administrative law judge ordered it released, and the STB directed the company to make it public. The company released the nearly 53-minute video on Nov. 5, 2026, along with a statement. "Let us be clear: Union Pacific does not retaliate against customers," the company said. "A customer's position on the merger will not determine its rates or how we deliver service."

## A Political and Regulatory Test

The merger is being weighed amid heightened political scrutiny. Three of the five STB members are Republicans sworn in by President Donald Trump; one seat is held by a Democrat, and one is vacant following the firing of Robert Primus, the only member who voted against the 2023 Canadian Pacific–Kansas City Southern merger. In October 2025, Union Pacific donated $2.5 million toward construction of the White House ballroom; Vena publicly denied the gift was meant to sway Trump on the merger. Senators Tammy Baldwin (D-Wisc.) and Roger Marshall (R-Kan.) have asked the STB to scrutinize the plan.

William C. Vantuono, editor-in-chief of Railway Age, said investor enthusiasm for the deal reflected hopes that a friendlier regulatory climate could revive large-scale consolidation. He expressed confidence that the board would follow precedent rather than political pressure. "I know most of the Surface Transportation Board members personally," he said. "They are the most ethical people you'd ever come across."

## What's Next

The STB will continue to hear comments from other federal agencies through the winter, with additional hearings planned for the spring. A final decision is expected in late 2027.

Opponents argue that regardless of the outcome, the merger debate has already exposed how much rides on a handful of regulators. "The trickle-down effect is very evident in this merger because we're talking about a transcontinental merger and basically a duopoly," said Paul Bellows, vice chairman of BMWE's Unified System Division. "Every citizen in the United States is going to be affected."

Union Pacific, meanwhile, is betting that regulators will see a more efficient national network as the prize worth the risk. The question before the board is whether that gain for some shippers outweighs the costs borne by those with nowhere else to turn.
