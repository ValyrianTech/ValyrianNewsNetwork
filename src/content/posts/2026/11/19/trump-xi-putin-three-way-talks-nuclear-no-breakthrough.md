# Trump, Xi and Putin Hold Rare Three-Way Nuclear Talks

The presidents of the United States, China and Russia met for the first time ever as a trio on Thursday, gathering at the Kylin Villa in the Chinese tech hub of Shenzhen to discuss nuclear risks, the wars in Ukraine and Iran, and global energy stability. After roughly 70 minutes behind closed doors, the leaders emerged with a modest "peace declaration" but no concrete agreement to reduce their nuclear arsenals, [the Associated Press reported](https://apnews.com/article/trump-xi-putin-nuclear-ukraine-iran-a04dc76ed8e786b087e2c1ea9598783a).

The summit of the world's three largest nuclear powers — held on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) forum — was billed as a historic chance to pull back from the brink. Instead, it produced a narrower set of intentions: reforming the United Nations, increasing fossil fuel output, and curbing the jurisdiction of the International Criminal Court.

## A Stage of Symbols, Not Substance

President Donald Trump entered the meeting seeking to cast himself as a statesman capable of negotiating with America's toughest rivals, flattering both Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin as friends. "We are really making history," Trump said in his opening remarks. "The United States, Russia and China are uniquely positioned to discuss how to reduce the number of nuclear weapons on Earth and also to lessen the risk of those weapons being used."

Beijing's Xi, who chaired the summit, called the gathering one of "groundbreaking significance," urging the three powers to shed a "Cold War mentality." Putin said the three countries "hold joint responsibility for maintaining international peace."

But after the photogenic opening, the leaders settled for a document that [Al Jazeera described](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/19/groundbreaking-trump-xi-putin-summit-five-key-takeaways) as a "peace declaration" reaffirming that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought." It pledged only to hold future "dialogues" on preventing proliferation and minimizing nuclear risks — with no binding commitments, no timetable, and no reduction targets.

## The Nuclear Vacuum Behind the Summit

The absence of real arms-control progress carries particular weight because the last remaining U.S.-Russia nuclear treaty is gone. New START expired in February 2026, and this was the first leader-level nuclear arms control talk since its lapse, according to [Radio Free Asia](https://www.rfa.org/english/china/2026/11/19/trump-xi-putin-apec-summit/).

Trump has said he wants a "new, improved and modernized" deal that would bring Beijing into the fold. The structural obstacle is stark: China is estimated to hold just over 600 warheads, compared with more than 5,000 each for the United States and Russia. Beijing has long argued that Washington and Moscow should slash their arsenals first — a position that leaves little room for a trilateral accord.

"Once you have the nuclear weapons, it's very hard not to have them," Trump conceded during the talks. "So it's not an easy thing to do, give them up."

Daryl Kimball, executive director of the Arms Control Association, warned that symbolism alone will not lower the risk of catastrophe. "We need more than diplomatic theater and photo opportunities between Xi, Putin and Trump," he said. "And one meeting will not likely produce dramatic breakthroughs."

## Ukraine, Iran and the Shadow of Two Wars

The summit unfolded against the backdrop of two active conflicts. The United States launched a war against Iran in February 2026, prompting Tehran to close the Strait of Hormuz and throwing global energy supplies into turmoil. Russia's war in Ukraine continues, with Ukrainian strikes on Russian energy facilities compounding the strain. Both wars were nominally on the agenda — yet neither produced a path to peace.

Notably, a Kremlin spokesperson said Ukraine was not even the "central topic" at the trilateral meeting, though it dominated Putin's separate bilateral talks with Trump — their first encounter since a summit in Alaska last year that failed to broker a Ukraine deal. After the Shenzhen meeting, Trump dialed into a gathering in Berlin between U.S. envoys and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy alongside more than 40 European leaders, telling them he had discussed ending the war with Xi and Putin.

The three leaders also took aim at the International Criminal Court, declaring that officials should enjoy immunity from its prosecutions. All three countries have clashed with the tribunal — Putin faces an ICC arrest warrant, and Washington has sanctioned the court's officials, which makes the joint statement a rare point of genuine convergence.

## A Balancing Act on Taiwan and Chips

Separately, Trump and Xi held their third substantial meeting of the year, covering trade, Taiwan and energy. According to Xinhua, Xi told Trump that Washington should "stick to doing the right thing" on Taiwan, while Trump said the U.S. "does not support Taiwan independence." The White House had not released its own account.

Underlying friction persisted. Chinese officials blocked a Bloomberg reporter from the trilateral meeting after she asked a question at the earlier Trump-Xi session — a pointed reminder that the warm rhetoric masks persistent rivalry over trade, rare earths, semiconductors and artificial intelligence. A trade truce is set to expire on January 10, 2027.

## What's Next

For Trump, the imagery of standing shoulder-to-shoulder with Xi and Putin may carry its own political value, and he announced a White House press conference for Friday. Yet the practical yield is thin, and the risks are real. With no replacement for New START and no trilateral framework in sight, the world's largest arsenals remain unconstrained during a period of acute geopolitical tension.

The next test comes in December, when Trump hosts the G20 summit in Miami and has invited both Xi and Putin. Whether that gathering cements a formal great-power "directorate" — or simply produces another round of photo opportunities — will determine whether Shenzhen marked the start of something durable, or merely a well-staged moment that faded as quickly as the flashbulbs.
