# Iran Widens Gulf Attacks, Hitting US Base in Kuwait

Iran widened its attacks across the Gulf on Wednesday, firing drones and missiles at a US air base in Kuwait and carrying out several strikes on Bahrain, in what appeared to be retaliation for a US strike a day earlier. The escalation breaks a roughly two-month lull in direct Iran–US exchanges and unfolds against an intensifying diplomatic scramble, as American allies in the region press Washington to avoid a return to all-out war.

According to [The Associated Press](https://apnews.com/article/iran-us-kuwait-gulf-attacks-bahrain-b1f0b530cffe951e6fcc1e27b2e1af94), Iran targeted the Ali Al Salem air base in Kuwait and launched several attacks on Bahrain, which hosts the US Navy's 5th Fleet. Kuwait's military said its air defenses intercepted drone attacks and reported no immediate damage. Bahrain's military said it fended off a number of Iranian attacks, with no immediate word on damage or casualties.

## How the Exchange Unfolded

The strikes capped a rapid chain of retaliation. On Tuesday, Iran attacked a US radar installation in Bahrain, prompting a US fighter jet to strike a Revolutionary Guard missile launch site on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. The US described that action as a "self-defence" strike using precision munitions.

Iran said its broader recent attacks — including on other US facilities in Bahrain on Friday and Sunday — were responses to US drone surveillance operations and an attack on Iranian communications towers. According to [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/18/kuwait-bahrain-face-new-iran-attacks-after-us-strike-on-qeshm-island), Bahrain has now experienced four Iranian attacks in six days, ending a weeks-long respite in a war that began with US-Israeli strikes on Iran on February 28.

Kuwait characterized the drone attacks as flagrant violations of its sovereignty and a direct threat to its security. Saudi Arabia's foreign ministry condemned the strikes on Kuwait and Bahrain "in the strongest terms" and denounced a separate attack on a tanker belonging to the United Arab Emirates, urging a negotiated solution. Arab League chief Nabil Fahmy said the repeated attacks revealed "a continuous escalation pattern that threatens to widen the circle of confrontation."

## A Diplomatic Race Against Escalation

Behind the military tit-for-tat lies a struggle over whether the war resumes in full. Government and security officials from the Gulf states, as well as Pakistan and Turkey, have engaged in concerted diplomacy — including phone calls with senior US officials — to avert a renewed war, according to four regional officials briefed on the matter who spoke to the AP on condition of anonymity.

The pressure runs in the opposite direction from Israel. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is reportedly urging the Trump administration to launch a joint air campaign on Iran, and Israel's Mossad chief, Roman Gofman, traveled to Washington and Miami in recent days, meeting CIA Director John Ratcliffe and US envoy Steve Witkoff. Qatar's prime minister met with President Donald Trump at the White House last week as part of the mediation push.

Trump has sent mixed signals about his intentions, saying new strikes are "highly unlikely" while also noting that the military was "locked and loaded." A third US aircraft carrier has arrived in the Middle East, and Adm. Brad Cooper, commander of US Central Command, met Israel's military chief, Eyal Zamir, and Gofman in Tel Aviv on Wednesday.

## The Regional Calculus

The immediate trigger sits on Qeshm Island, at the narrow throat of the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint through which a large share of the world's oil passes. The US has maintained a naval blockade of Iran's ports in the strait since the summer, with US military escorts helping restore passage for some oil tankers. That blockade, and Iran's efforts to raise its cost, form the strategic core of the current phase of the conflict.

The maritime dimension spilled further into the open this week. The UAE state-owned oil company ADNOC said one of its tanker crew members was missing after an attack in the strait on Tuesday, with 11 others injured and receiving medical care — an incident that drew condemnation from Saudi Arabia and the Arab League.

Through August, more than 115 Iranian or Iran-backed strikes had damaged or destroyed structures at 26 military bases and other sites hosting US forces, according to AP reporting. The Ali Al Salem base alone had been struck at least 30 times, damaging or destroying at least 15 aircraft hangars, numerous warehouses and 10 buildings near a residential area. That history underscores how routine, and how dangerous, this latest exchange has become.

Separately, Saudi officials have briefed allies that the conflict with Yemen's Houthis could last another six months, with the Houthis making territorial gains along the Red Sea coast and attacking Saudi infrastructure, including Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport. Trump has so far resisted striking the Houthis directly.

## Calibrated Escalation or Sliding Toward War?

Analysts describe Iran's behavior as a calculated strategy rather than a bid for all-out war. "Iran appears to be pursuing a strategy of deliberate, calibrated escalation," Danny Citrinowicz, a researcher at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies and a former Iran specialist, wrote on X. "The objective is not necessarily to trigger an all-out war, but to steadily increase the military, economic, and political costs of maintaining the blockade."

But that logic carries its own risks. "Even if neither side intends to enter a broader confrontation, the accumulation of military incidents across multiple fronts increases the risk of miscalculation and escalation beyond what either side originally intended," said Hamidreza Azizi, a senior Iran analyst at the International Crisis Group, also writing on X.

Gulf states have reason to fear the worst. A new war, they warn, could be more devastating than the last — potentially hitting oil facilities, power and desalination plants, and undersea communications and financial cables, while drawing in the Houthis to disrupt the Bab el-Mandeb Strait and choke a second global shipping artery.

The diplomatic picture remains divided. A US official has argued that economic pressure is working and that major combat need not restart, a position that aligns with the Gulf states, Pakistan and Turkey. Israel, by contrast, is pushing for renewed strikes on Iran's nuclear and missile capabilities.

## What to Watch

Several questions will shape the next phase. First is whether the US and Iran can pull back from the current exchange before a single miscalculation triggers a wider confrontation. Qatar's foreign ministry spokesman, Majed al-Ansari, has warned that continued escalation without restraint from both sides makes a negotiated solution — or even continued dialogue — harder.

Second is the pace of diplomacy: whether the Gulf-led effort to restrain Washington can offset Israel's push for a joint campaign. Third is the maritime front, where attacks on shipping and the US blockade continue to define the economic pressure campaign on Tehran.

For now, Iran's army chief, Maj. Gen. Amir Hatami, signaled no retreat, saying the armed forces were "monitoring the enemy's movements" and that Iran's response to any misstep by the US and its "backers" would be "even more crushing." Nearly nine months into a war that has oscillated between intense combat, ceasefires and diplomatic stasis, the region is again holding its breath — waiting to see whether this latest round is a message or the start of something far larger.
