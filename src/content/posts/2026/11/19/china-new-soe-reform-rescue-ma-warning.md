# China Launches New SOE Reform, Warns on Rescue M&A

China has entered full implementation of a new round of reform for its state-owned assets and enterprises, with the country's top state-asset regulator declaring that planning is complete and laying down a firm marker that consolidation must be driven by quality and market discipline rather than by bailing out weak firms. At a special seminar on deepening SOE reform held on 18 November, Li Zhen, a member and deputy director of the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (SASAC), said local governments and central enterprises had essentially finished compiling their reform work ledgers — the documents that will guide the next phase. "This marks that all units have completed the strategic layout of the new round of reform," Li said, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/fortune/20261119/673cb5d658824bb183e6033c6cbfa0cf/c.html). "The next step is to focus on full implementation."

The reform is the third major SOE cycle of the decade, following the Three-Year Action Plan of 2020–2022 and the Deepening and Upgrading Action of 2023–2025. With 2026 billed as the "opening year" of the new round, the emphasis has shifted decisively from building modern corporate systems toward what officials describe as higher-quality, more disciplined state capital.

## A Reform Built on Ledgers and Targets

The scale of the planning effort is considerable. According to the [Economic Reference Newspaper](http://www.jjckb.cn/20261119/026257ad101f457ab84b811d3f0b2e45/c.html), which first published the report by journalist Wang Lu, localities have proposed more than 3,300 reform tasks backed by over 9,800 implementation measures, while central enterprises have put forward more than 11,000 reform tasks and 18,000-plus measures.

The new reform blueprint sets explicit requirements on the "three concentrations" of state capital — concentrating it in strategic security and key lifeline sectors, in core functional areas, and in public services and forward-looking strategic emerging industries — as well as on industrial restructuring and the push to reorganize and integrate enterprises. Li stressed that each locality and central enterprise must tailor its choice of industries and approach to its own circumstances.

## The Central Warning: No More 'Big Eats Small'

The most pointed message from the seminar concerned mergers and acquisitions. As an instrument for optimizing the layout of state capital, integration must focus on three specific problems: weak links in industrial chains, dispersed innovation resources, and homogeneous competition. Li said the old "big eats small" approach should be abandoned in favor of integration led by enterprises with strong innovation capacity.

"Be careful to prevent 'rescue-type' M&A and clarify the boundary of bailouts," he said. The warning is significant because SOE consolidation in China has at times been used to absorb failing firms and distressed assets into healthy state groups, distorting market signals and draining resources from stronger enterprises. SASAC's stance signals a shift toward mergers justified by strategic logic rather than by the need to rescue the weak. Restructured enterprises, Li added, must quickly achieve effective integration of systems, management, personnel and safety culture.

## Guarding Against a New 'Involution'

A parallel concern is the risk that the push into strategic emerging and future industries — from new energy to computing and zero-carbon parks — could trigger the kind of cut-throat overcapacity that Chinese policymakers have labeled "involution." SASAC explicitly warned enterprises against rushing in all at once and creating new involution.

"In developing strategic emerging industries and future industries, [enterprises] must combine their own resource endowments and industrial-technological advantages, strengthen investigation and situation assessment, find the right entry and leverage points, and ensure 'good steel is used on the blade,'" Li said. He added that the transformation and upgrading of traditional industries must likewise avoid involution.

Another priority is curbing unrelated diversification: the reform requires a strict ban on incremental unrelated businesses and an orderly exit from existing holdings. SASAC is studying criteria for identifying "unrelated diversification," aimed mainly at business lines that are hard to classify.

## Companies Move From Plan to Practice

Some central enterprises have already outlined concrete measures. China Huadian Group is improving the management system that supports strategic emerging industries, optimizing investment-return standards by industry, advancing integrated new-energy development, and deploying new business formats such as "computing-power-electricity coordination" and zero-carbon parks. China Baowu Steel Group has deepened integration and synergy: its 30 second-tier companies have reviewed more than 300 integration projects this year, shifting from group-level "single-item push" to subsidiary-level "proactive force generation."

## Analysis: Discipline Over Scale

The guiding philosophy behind this round is captured in a policy framework known as the "five prominents," which SASAC's Party Committee articulated in [Qiushi](https://www.qstheory.cn/20250320/973bb88bab43460783cc586db065b502/c.html), the Communist Party's flagship theoretical journal. That framework emphasizes strengthening core functions and core competitiveness, cultivating new quality productive forces, deepening cooperation, and balancing development with security.

The anti-rescue and anti-involution language matters for several reasons. First, it addresses a long-standing criticism that SOE consolidation has functioned as a backdoor subsidy for weak firms, undermining the market's ability to weed out laggards. By demanding that integration be led by innovation-strong enterprises, SASAC is trying to channel capital toward genuine competitive advantage. Second, the warning against herd behavior in emerging industries reflects a broader national push against "involution" that has already reshaped policy in sectors such as electric vehicles, solar and steel. The concern is that a similar rush into new energy and computing could create redundant investment and erode returns.

Third, the crackdown on unrelated diversification targets the sprawling conglomerate model that has at times blurred the line between strategic state investment and opportunistic expansion. Requiring orderly exits from existing non-core holdings could force some groups to shed assets — a potentially significant development for domestic M&A markets and for private partners.

## What to Watch

The immediate test will be whether the tens of thousands of reform tasks and measures actually translate into measurable changes in corporate behavior, or remain ledger-line commitments. Key signals to monitor include the criteria SASAC is drafting on unrelated diversification, how the "bailout boundary" is defined in practice, and whether integration deals increasingly feature innovation-strong enterprises as acquirers rather than healthier groups absorbing the weak. The broader question — whether this round genuinely elevates market discipline within China's state sector — will be answered not at seminars, but in the deals that follow.
