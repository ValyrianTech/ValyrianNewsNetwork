# Brussels Shelter Samusocial Refuses Record 3,396 Families

Brussels' emergency shelter organisation Samusocial refused accommodation to families a record 3,396 times in just two months, turning away nearly nine in ten of those who sought help as the capital's shelter crisis deepens. Between the start of September and the end of October 2026, the organisation received 3,778 requests from families and could not house the overwhelming majority for lack of space, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/17/samusocial-moet-bijna-9-op-de-10-gezinnen-opvang-weigeren/).

"We have 577 places for families in Brussels today, about 100 more than a few years ago," said Samusocial director Sarah De Liamchine. "Yet we have never had to refuse so many families."

The figures lay bare a structural housing and welfare crisis in the Belgian capital, where a scarce supply of affordable homes, long welfare backlogs and a tightening federal asylum policy are converging on families with children as winter approaches.

## A system running at its limits

Samusocial, a Brussels non-profit providing emergency shelter and socio-medical support to homeless people and families, can free up only about 50 family places per week — far short of demand. Over the same two-month window, requests averaged well above 400 per week.

Because the Brussels housing market remains extremely tight, single parents stay in emergency shelters far longer than the short-term stays the facilities were designed for, blocking places for new arrivals. The result is a bottleneck in which families cannot move on to permanent housing and new families cannot get in.

"We are able to free up about 50 family places per week, but that is far too little. As a result we sometimes have to make difficult choices," De Liamchine said. "Which family do we put on the street to be able to shelter an even more vulnerable family? They are terrible decisions that feel like a failure every time. A child should never have to sleep on the street."

Capacity at Samusocial's family facilities has not kept pace with need. When the organisation moved its family emergency shelter centre from Oudergem to Evere in November 2024, capacity in that facility dropped from 375 to 280 places — a net loss of about 100 beds — with families redirected to alternatives such as the French Red Cross Train Hostel and the Colmeia centre in Schaerbeek, [Samusocial](https://samusocial.be/ons-gezinscentrum-verhuist-naar-evere/) explained at the time.

## Voices from inside the shelter

Behind the statistics are families such as Nadine, a single mother of three who asked to be identified by a pseudonym. She became homeless almost overnight after a relationship breakdown and arrived at Samusocial's Evere shelter with her children earlier this year.

"It is almost impossible for a single person with three children to find an affordable home in Brussels. Even for someone like me, who was born here, speaks the language and works," she told VRT NWS. She also carries a sense of guilt about the place her family occupies. "I realise we are occupying a place that is actually intended for families with more urgent needs. The problem is that specialised shelters are also completely full."

Much of the pressure falls on women fleeing abusive partners, who have historically received priority at Samusocial. That priority guarantee has become impossible to honour. "If they flee their violent partner, they can lose their right of residence. Then all that remains is a regularisation application, which can take up to two years," De Liamchine said.

Amira, who fled an abusive partner in 2024 with her three young children and also spoke under a pseudonym, described the shelter as a lifeline. "The most important thing Samusocial has given us is safety. That is even more important than food and shelter," she said.

## Welfare and asylum rules pile on pressure

Samusocial attributes much of the surge to stricter European and Belgian rules. "Residence procedures are becoming longer and stricter," De Liamchine said. Families who lose unemployment benefits sometimes wait months for a guaranteed minimum income (leefloon) because public social welfare centres (OCMW) are overloaded — and some are evicted and end up at Samusocial's door before the paperwork clears.

A further bottleneck concerns people who previously applied for asylum in another country: they may now wait up to 18 months before they can even file an application in Belgium, during which time they have no income or legal pathway to protect their children.

The pressure intensified after a federal law took effect in August 2025 withdrawing material aid — shelter and food — from certain applicants for international protection. Samusocial later said that since that date it had been forced to refuse around 400 families, roughly 1,500 people, about half of whom had never before slept at Samusocial, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/fr/2025/09/09/le-samusocial-de-bruxelles-contraint-de-refuser-l-accueil-a-des/).

The federal government, with N-VA's Anneleen Van Bossuyt at Asylum and Migration, has described its approach as the strictest asylum policy Belgium has seen. Van Bossuyt has argued that people sleeping rough because of the new measures made a "conscious choice" to travel to Belgium and should return voluntarily — a stance strongly contested by humanitarian organisations.

## Cuts to reception capacity loom

Compounding the strain, the so-called Brussels Deal is set to remove reception places that Samusocial relies on. In July 2026 Samusocial warned that the elimination of 1,000 places under the deal would hit its operations directly: nearly 40 percent of the roughly 1,300 places in its emergency shelters are financed through the scheme, [La Libre](https://www.lalibre.be/dernieres-depeches/2026/07/17/asile-et-migration-le-samusocial-redoute-une-crise-humanitaire-apres-la-suppression-de-1000-places-daccueil-K2MKNOPZ7FDNHFCWMYQIGLJZR4/) reported. De Liamchine warned at the time that, absent alternatives, tents and makeshift camps could reappear in public spaces.

The wider trend is stark. Bruss'help's most recent count, in November 2024, recorded 9,777 people in the Brussels region without a housing solution, a rise of 25 percent in two years.

Humanitarian organisations have repeatedly flagged the shortfall. The Humanitaire Hub coalition, which includes Médecins du Monde, BelRefugees, Samusocial, MSF, CIRÉ, Vluchtelingenwerk Vlaanderen and Caritas, has warned that emergency shelter is routinely over capacity, with families and children sleeping outdoors even as authorities commit to sheltering them, [Médecins du Monde](https://doktersvandewereld.be/nieuws-publicaties/overvolle-noodopvang-opnieuw-slapen-tientallen-gezinnen-en-kinderen-op-straat) has said.

## A legal and moral test

Under Belgian law and the International Convention on the Rights of the Child, families with children have a right to unconditional shelter regardless of their administrative status. The record refusals place that obligation under direct strain — and put Samusocial's staff in the position of adjudicating between equally vulnerable families.

"Our call is clear: more affordable and social housing are needed, and smoother access to OCMW benefits," De Liamchine said. The demand splits along Belgium's layered government: social housing is largely a regional responsibility in Brussels, while asylum rules and welfare access sit at the federal level.

For families like Nadine's, the wait continues. "That would have been an ideal interim solution while awaiting a home of our own. I hope I can leave here as soon as possible to make room," she said of the specialised shelters that are themselves full.

## What to watch

With winter approaching, the record refusal figures sharpen attention on whether Brussels and federal authorities will expand shelter capacity, speed up OCMW processing, and reconcile tightened asylum policy with the legal guarantee of shelter for children. The coming months will test whether the system can reverse a pattern that, year after year, has pushed a new cohort of vulnerable families toward the street.
