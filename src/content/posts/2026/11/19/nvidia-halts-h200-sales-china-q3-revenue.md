# Nvidia Halts H200 Sales to China as Q3 Revenue Tops $115B

Nvidia has stopped shipping its licensed Hopper data-center products to China, including the H200 chip, and has stripped all related revenue from its forward guidance — a decisive turn in the AI chipmaker's China business that came the same week the company posted quarterly results far above market expectations.

Interim CEO and Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress disclosed the halt during Nvidia's fiscal 2027 third-quarter earnings call on November 17, confirming that the company will "no longer ship licensed Hopper data-center products to China" and that its outlook no longer includes that income, according to [Caixin](https://www.caixin.com/2026-11-18/102506205.html). The company did not explain the reason for the halt, how long it would last, or whether shipments might resume.

## A Narrow Channel Closes

The disclosure booked a final tally for the special channel: Nvidia sold US$2.4 billion (about RMB 16 billion) in licensed Hopper data-center products to China in the quarter. That figure will not be repeated in guidance, and Kress gave no signal that the business would return.

The H200 was never Nvidia's most advanced chip, but it was among the most powerful AI processors Washington still permitted to be sold to Chinese customers under specific conditions. First launched in November 2023, it carries 141GB of HBM3E memory and 4.8TB/s of bandwidth — a substantial upgrade over the H100 and particularly suited to large language model workloads, according to [International Electronic Business](https://www.seccw.com/Document/detail/id/52558.html).

The channel that carried it was extraordinarily restrictive from the outset. In January 2026, the US government conditionally approved H200 exports to China, requiring that chips transit through the US, undergo third-party security review, and that recipients establish safeguards. Chinese customers' purchase volumes were capped at 50% of what US customers bought, and the US took roughly 25% of transaction value. Even under that framework, H200 shipments never scaled — Chinese regulators approved only small batches, and larger import permits were never opened. About 600,000 H200 chips are reportedly held in warehouses in Hong Kong, theoretically usable for local data centers but dependent on regulatory direction for any onward shipment to the mainland.

## Results Beat Expectations — With China Excluded

The halt landed against a backdrop of extraordinary financial performance. For the quarter ended October 25, Nvidia reported revenue of US$115.8 billion, up 103% year on year, and net profit of US$65.3 billion, roughly double the prior year. Data-center revenue reached US$107.6 billion — more than 90% of the total — up 110% year on year. The company guided to US$130 billion in fourth-quarter revenue, above Wall Street's roughly US$125.3 billion estimate, and its shares rose more than 4% in after-hours trading, according to [TMTPost via NetEase](https://www.163.com/dy/article/LC1N25A305118O92.html).

The customer mix offered a pointed rebuttal to fears of over-reliance on a handful of hyperscalers. Of the US$107.6 billion in data-center revenue, traditional cloud giants contributed US$60.1 billion, while sovereign AI, AI cloud, industrial and enterprise clients accounted for about US$47.5 billion. Sovereign AI revenue more than doubled, and physical AI revenue from robotics and industrial simulation grew nearly threefold, heading toward an annual run-rate of roughly US$25 billion. Nvidia's new Vera Rubin architecture contributed about US$20 billion in its first quarter of delivery, and the company raised inventory from US$31.6 billion to US$39.7 billion to support deliveries.

Still, the results carried warning signs. Non-GAAP gross margin reached 74.9% in the quarter, but GAAP gross margin guidance for the next quarter was cut to 71–72%, reflecting rising HBM memory costs and the cost of ramping Vera Rubin. Nvidia also recorded a US$1.8 billion impairment on non-listed equity investments tied to its "invest-to-buy" funding of AI startups and neoclouds.

## A Governance Shake-Up Behind the Decision

The halt coincided with a profound internal transition. On November 12, Nvidia's board announced that founder Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately, with Kress stepping in as interim CEO. Huang had been the leading advocate for restoring China chip sales and was deeply involved in negotiating the special H200 arrangement. He has since said publicly that he would return if the board judged it in the company's interest, but board chairman Harvey Jones stated on the call that the board unanimously accepted his resignation and had no intention of reinstating him, adding that a formal CEO search had begun.

Nvidia also announced the creation of a National Security and Export Controls Oversight Committee at the board level, with the compliance team reporting jointly to that committee and the interim CEO. Analysts read the moves as a deliberate shift toward a more cautious, institutionalized posture on China.

The policy environment has tightened in parallel. On November 15, the US Bureau of Industry and Security published an interim final rule on remote access to advanced computing chips, extending controls beyond physical exports to compute services delivered to Chinese customers through overseas data centers. Jefferies estimated the rule could cut Nvidia's data-center revenue next year by US$44 billion to US$62 billion; Kress countered that a strong Vera Rubin backlog would cushion the near-term impact.

## Beijing's Muted Response and the Domestic Push

China's official response was terse. Asked about the halt at a regular press briefing on November 18, Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said only that China had "previously and repeatedly stated its principled position on the issue of US chip exports to China," according to [International Electronic Business](https://www.seccw.com/Document/detail/id/52563.html).

The restraint reflects a broader strategy: Beijing's emphasis appears to be on accelerating domestic substitution rather than escalating. Chinese chipmakers have rapidly advanced products comparable to or exceeding the H200. MetaX's XiYun C600 entered mass production in May 2026 with 144GB of HBM3E — above the H200's 141GB — targeting large-model training and inference with a fully domestic supply chain. Huawei's Ascend line has pushed further, with the Ascend 960PR offering 2 PFLOPS of FP8 compute, up to 288GB of HBM and 9.6TB/s of bandwidth, positioned above the H200 SXM, on an annual iteration cadence.

## What to Watch

The closure of the H200 channel marks a milestone in the US-China technology standoff: even a tightly conditioned, narrowly licensed pathway for high-end AI chips is being shut. For Nvidia, the test is whether growth driven by sovereign AI, neoclouds and enterprise demand can outweigh the loss of a US$2.4 billion quarterly line and the potential cost of the remote-access rule.

Three variables will shape what comes next: whether the special H200 channel ever reopens and under what conditions; how China handles the roughly 600,000 chips parked in Hong Kong; and who becomes Nvidia's permanent CEO — and whether the company's posture toward China hardens further under a leader chosen by a board determined to move past its founder.
