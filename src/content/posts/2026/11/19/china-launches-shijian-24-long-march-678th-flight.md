# China Launches Shijian-24 in 678th Long March Flight

China successfully launched the Shijian-24 (实践二十四号) experimental satellite aboard a Long March 2D (长征二号丁) rocket from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center in Shanxi Province early Thursday, adding another spacecraft to the country's expanding and deliberately opaque technology-verification program. The satellite entered its planned orbit and the mission was declared a complete success, according to [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/tech/20261119/cffcaa6be0404352812393d794399944/c.html), which reported the launch at 07:17 Beijing time.

The rocket lifted off at 06:51 Beijing time from Launch Complex 9, carrying the satellite into what international observers describe as a sun-synchronous orbit. The mission marked the 678th flight of the Long March rocket family and the 108th flight of the Long March 2D variant, according to [Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/space/china-shijian-24-mystery-satellite-launch). It was also the 284th Long March mission attributed to the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology (SAST), the rocket's developer.

## A Growth Industry of Unanswered Questions

The Shijian-24 launch is technically routine, but its characteristically sparse disclosure illustrates a tension at the heart of China's space program: remarkable tempo paired with minimal transparency. Xinhua's report confirmed the launch time, the vehicle, the orbit and the flight milestone — and nothing else. China did not disclose the satellite's specific mission objectives, its manufacturer, its mass or its instrument suite.

That information gap is not incidental. The Shijian (实践) series, whose name translates to "Practice," is one of China's longest-running lines of experimental and technology-verification satellites, dating back to Shijian-1 in 1971. According to analysts at the U.S. Air Force's China Aerospace Studies Institute, the Shijian line exists to "develop the best operational practices and optimize the technologies previously tested" on the earlier-stage Shiyan (实验, "Experiment") series — making "put into practice" a more accurate reading of the name than "experiment."

Interest in the series is heightened by precedent. [Shijian-17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shijian), launched in 2016, was China's first satellite fitted with a robotic arm, prompting scrutiny from U.S. Space Command and the Pentagon over its potential to grapple other spacecraft. Shijian-21, launched in 2021 as an on-orbit servicing platform, later towed a defunct BeiDou navigation satellite into a graveyard orbit — a maneuver widely analyzed as a demonstration of possible counterspace capability. In 2025, Shijian-25 docked with Shijian-21 and later separated, possibly completing the first refueling operation in geostationary orbit.

## A Cold-Weather Launch and a Busy Calendar

The Taiyuan launch required adaptations for northern China's cold conditions. Launch teams installed extra thermal insulation on the Long March 2D and heated critical vehicle sections before liftoff, with the insulation separating harmlessly during the initial ascent, according to Interesting Engineering.

The mission is the latest entry in one of the world's most aggressive launch calendars. The November 19 flight was roughly China's 82nd orbital launch attempt of 2026. Earlier in the year, China placed Shijian-31 into a highly elliptical orbit in June, while a January attempt involving Shijian-32 ended in failure. The Shiyan fleet also expanded in March, when two Shiyan-30 satellites and the Shiyan-33 spacecraft entered orbit.

Parallel activity underscores the tempo: Chinese state media reported on November 17 that the Shenzhou-24 crewed spacecraft and its Long March 2F rocket had been moved to the launch pad ahead of a planned crewed mission in the near term.

## What to Watch

The significance of Shijian-24 lies less in the launch itself than in what follows. Observers will watch whether the satellite conducts rendezvous and proximity operations, on-orbit servicing maneuvers or other activities that could carry dual-use implications — the pattern that has drawn international attention to prior Shijian missions.

For now, the public record contains only what Beijing chose to release: a successful liftoff, a correct orbit and another milestone for a rocket family that has become a workhorse of China's space ambitions. The rest, as with much of the Shijian program, remains in orbit and out of view.
