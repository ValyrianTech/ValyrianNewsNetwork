# ElGendy Wins National Book Award, Barred From US

Egyptian writer Abdelrahman ElGendy won the 2026 National Book Award for Nonfiction on Wednesday night for his memoir *Huna: A Memoir of Revolution, Prison, and Becoming* — a prize he accepted from thousands of miles away, after the United States denied him a visa to attend his own ceremony. According to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/19/nx-s1-6037553/huna-writer-wins-national-book-award-for-non-fiction-after-not-being-allowed-into-the-u-s), the $10,000 award was collected on his behalf by his editor, who read aloud a statement in which ElGendy described a dream left "unfinished."

The honor arrived at the 77th National Book Awards at Cipriani Wall Street in lower Manhattan, where 1,912 books had been submitted across categories. But the night's most resonant story belonged to an author who was not in the room — a former political prisoner in Egypt who has become, in exile, a symbol of the collision between American literary prestige and American immigration enforcement.

## A Memoir Born Behind Bars

*Huna* — Arabic for "here" — recounts ElGendy's arrest as a 17-year-old student activist during the 2013 Egyptian military coup, his six years and three months as a political prisoner, and the mechanics of survival and dissent under an authoritarian state. Much of it was written behind bars, pages smuggled out one prison visit at a time, while he rehearsed imaginary acceptance speeches under his duvet.

Released in 2020 after an appeal exposed that he had been wrongfully tried as an adult instead of a minor, ElGendy later earned a full scholarship to the University of Pittsburgh's MFA program and a master's degree from the London School of Economics. His debut was named one of *Publishers Weekly*'s 10 Most Anticipated Books of 2026.

## A Visa Denied, a Dream Unfinished

Earlier in November, ElGendy applied for a U.S. visitor visa from Europe. His consular interview initially went smoothly before ending in "administrative processing"; the next morning he received a formal denial under the Immigration and Nationality Act. The U.S. government provided no written reason.

In his statement, read by editor Helen Thomaides, ElGendy wrote that the moment "should have come full circle," but instead would "remain unfinished, broken by an oppressive regime with a fascist ideology whose concentration camps stand ready to swallow the bodies it deems undesirable."

The episode has a second, contested dimension. Days before the ceremony, [The New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/17/books/national-book-award-nonfiction-elgendy-eligibility.html) reported that ElGendy might not even be eligible for the prize, because he left the U.S. in April 2025 while the award's rules require U.S. citizenship, permanent residency, or a "primary, long-term home" in the U.S. at the start of the awards cycle on December 1, 2025.

Hours before the ceremony, the [National Book Foundation](https://www.nationalbook.org/a-statement-from-the-national-book-foundation-regarding-the-national-book-awards-eligibility-criteria/) disputed that framing. It said its publisher had confirmed ElGendy was "a legal and tax-paying US resident who, in the context of arrests being made by ICE in 2025, fled the United States temporarily in concern for his safety" — a circumstance the Foundation determined satisfied its criteria. It expanded eligibility in 2024 to include authors who "maintain their primary, long-term home in the United States," regardless of immigration status. The Foundation said it had "no concerns" about his eligibility, and awarded him the prize.

## Exile as the Price of Speech

In a [New Yorker essay](https://www.newyorker.com/books/page-turner/i-am-a-2026-national-book-award-finalist-the-us-denied-my-visa) published days before the ceremony, ElGendy linked his situation to the broader immigration crackdown of 2025, including the ICE detention of Turkish doctoral student Rümeysa Öztürk over a Gaza-related op-ed. He wrote that he feared not only detention but deportation to Egypt, where he faced the prospect of "imprisonment for the rest of my life."

"There is a particular cruelty to being forced to outrun the fruits of one's labor, to watch one's work bloom from afar," he wrote. "My book has made it into rooms that I never will."

ElGendy was not the only barred finalist. Syrian author Samar Yazbek, a finalist in Translated Literature, was also denied a U.S. visa. The National Book Foundation called both denials "unjust."

The State Department declined to comment on specific cases but told the Associated Press that the administration "continues to put America and its interests first by fully enforcing US immigration laws," adding that "a visa is a privilege, not a right."

## A Split-Screen American Story

The night's fiction prize, meanwhile, went to Hannah Lillith Assadi for *Paradiso 17*, a novel inspired by her father's escape from the Nakba in 1948 — a parallel the [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2026/nov/19/hannah-lillith-assadi-paradiso-17-wins-national-book-award) noted in its coverage of the ceremony. Brenda Hillman won for poetry, David Levithan and Gabriel Duckels for Young People's Literature, and Vicente Luis Mora and Rahul Bery for Translated Literature. Salman Rushdie received the Foundation's lifetime achievement medal.

Yet the evening's defining image was the empty chair reserved for ElGendy — a writer honored by the country that would not let him enter it.

The episode crystallizes two contradictory American self-images: the nation that bestows its highest literary honor on an exiled dissident, and the nation that bars him from the room where it is given. The Foundation's decision to stand by its winner, and the government's silence on the reasons for the denial, leave the central tension unresolved. What remains is a book that arrived where its author could not — and a question, still open, about who gets to be welcomed home.
