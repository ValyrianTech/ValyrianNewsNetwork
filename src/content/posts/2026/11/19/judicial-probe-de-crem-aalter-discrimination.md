# Judicial probe into ex-mayor De Crem over Aalter bias

Belgian prosecutors have escalated their inquiry into the discriminatory registration policy of former Aalter mayor Pieter De Crem, confirming that a preliminary investigation has become a full judicial investigation led by an investigating judge, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/19/de-crem-aalter-gerechtelijk-onderzoek-pano/). The step moves the case closer to a potential trial and marks the most serious legal consequence yet of a scandal that ended the career of one of Flanders' most prominent Christian Democrat figures.

The East Flanders public prosecutor's office confirmed the escalation, which was first reported by Het Laatste Nieuws. The inquiry was upgraded some months ago after a complaint was filed with a civil-party claim (burgerlijke partijstelling), but the prosecutor has declined to identify who filed it. The investigating judge now has powers including house searches, phone taps and pre-trial detention, and the raadkamer — the chamber council — will later decide whether De Crem must stand trial.

## How the scandal began

The case traces back to March 2025, when the VRT NWS programme Pano, working with the newspaper De Morgen, broadcast the investigation "Welkom in Aalter." It revealed that non-Belgians waited on average roughly nine times longer than Belgians to be registered as residents in the East Flanders municipality. The consequences were severe: without an official address, those affected struggled to enrol children in school, claim child benefits or sign an employment contract — and the municipality withheld the provisional registrations that the law requires.

In September 2025, the Flemish internal audit body Audit Vlaanderen published a forensic report that corroborated the journalistic findings. It concluded that the tightened registration policy was "not adequate, lawful, effective or efficient" and that it had produced a situation in which the equal treatment of citizens was not respected. The audit found the arbitrariness was "almost exclusively" observed among non-Belgians, and that even individual partners in mixed couples were sometimes treated differently.

Audit Vlaanderen dismissed the municipality's long-standing defence that housing-quality inspections were meant to combat slumlords, finding no indication that this was the main objective of the College of Mayor and Aldermen, [according to VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/09/05/audit-vlaanderen-bevestigt-inschrijvingsbeleid-pieter-de-crem-w/). It noted the College took decisions contrary to applicable regulations "notwithstanding the frequent alarm signals" from the federal Home Affairs department and the Immigration Office.

The report also cited maladministration, including a lack of transparent, well-founded motives for refusing registrations — with municipal staff unable to explain what the college's decisions meant. It flagged that possible criminal offences had been committed, widely understood to mean breaches of anti-discrimination law, and referred its findings to the judiciary and the Central Service for the Fight against Corruption. De Crem refused to speak with the auditors.

## Political fallout

Within days of the audit, on 7 September 2025, De Crem resigned as mayor of Aalter and left the CD&V party, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2025/09/07/de-crem-trekt-zich-terug/). In a message to residents, he said the continuity of governance and a return to calm in the municipality were his first concerns. Alderman Mathias Van de Walle succeeded him. Flemish minister Hilde Crevits, a party colleague, launched disciplinary proceedings against De Crem, and party chairman Sammy Mahdi said De Crem had "coloured outside the lines of the law."

At the same time, the East Flanders prosecutor opened a preliminary investigation (opsporingsonderzoek) into the domicile policy and De Crem's role. That exploratory phase has now given way to the judicial investigation.

## Analysis: a test of the rule of law

The escalation is legally significant. A preliminary inquiry allows prosecutors to gather information; a judicial investigation places the matter under the authority of an investigating judge and is a necessary precursor to a potential trial. The shift signals that prosecutors believe there is enough substance to justify coercive investigative powers.

For CD&V, the case has been a profound embarrassment. De Crem was a former federal minister of Defence and a towering figure in Flemish Christian Democracy, and commentators framed the affair less as an internal party matter than as a question of the rule of law and equal treatment. The audit's finding that federal watchdogs repeatedly warned the municipality without effect cuts to a deeper institutional question: how far local authorities can go in shaping who is welcome before the law intervenes.

Much remains unknown. The identity of the civil-party complainant is undisclosed, as is the precise scope of the investigating judge's probe. It is unclear whether other municipal officials may be implicated, or whether searches or seizures have taken place. De Crem's legal defence has not been publicly detailed in response to the escalation.

## What to watch

The next milestone is the raadkamer's decision on whether De Crem must answer the allegations in court. That ruling — and any charges the investigating judge lays out — will determine whether a policy that Audit Vlaanderen called unlawful and discriminatory becomes the subject of a criminal trial. For the residents of Aalter who waited months for an address, the legal reckoning now rests with the judiciary.
