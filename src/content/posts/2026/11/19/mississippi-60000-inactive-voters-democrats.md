# Mississippi's 60,000 Inactive Voters Prompt Demands

Democratic lawmakers in Mississippi are demanding an investigation into a surge of nearly 60,000 voters added to the state's "inactive" list ahead of the November midterms — a spike tied to the state's use of credit-reporting data to verify addresses. The controversy carries extra weight because the state's marquee Senate race was decided by a margin far smaller than the number of flagged voters.

The number of Mississippians listed as inactive rose to more than 230,000 before this year's election, up from 171,000 during the 2024 presidential race, according to [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/mississippi-voters-ballots-democrats-experian-7a9282987612197b43c73cf05f7e7d31). The roughly 60,000 increase followed Republican Secretary of State Michael Watson's 2025 decision to integrate data from Experian, one of the nation's three major credit bureaus, into the voter-roll maintenance process.

## How the Process Works

Mississippi had for years relied primarily on U.S. Postal Service change-of-address data to keep its rolls current. Under the Experian partnership, county election officials gained access to credit and Social Security data to help confirm voters' addresses — a step Watson said was intended to "strengthen the integrity" of state elections.

The state's rules make the process especially consequential. Mississippi law requires election officials to move voters whose addresses are questioned to inactive status at the same time they mail out a confirmation card — meaning many voters may not learn they have been flagged until they arrive at the polls. Other states, including Delaware, Maryland, Oklahoma and Rhode Island, generally give voters time to respond to a notice before their status changes, according to the MIT Election Data and Science Lab.

Inactive voters can still cast ballots, but they must use a provisional — known in Mississippi as an affidavit — ballot, which county election commissioners later review and either approve or reject. The secretary of state's office said 21,484 provisional ballots were cast this year, "nearly identical" to the 21,321 in the higher-turnout 2024 presidential election.

## A Closer Look at the Numbers

Statewide, roughly 75% of provisional ballots were approved for the November election. But in Hinds County — majority-Black and home to the capital, Jackson — the acceptance rate fell to 34%, down from 60% in 2024, according to AP data. It is not clear whether the use of Experian data contributed to that drop.

The arithmetic has become the central political question. Democrat Scott Colom defeated Republican incumbent Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by just over 8,000 votes with 99% of ballots counted — a fraction of the roughly 60,000 newly inactive voters. Colom's victory made him the first Black U.S. senator from Mississippi since Reconstruction.

"We need answers to give to our constituents, because the question is still out there: Did my vote count?" said Democratic state Rep. Kabir Karriem, chair of the Mississippi Legislative Black Caucus, which scheduled a Thursday news conference to demand that legislative oversight committees investigate how credit data was used. "It was disenfranchisement called by another name."

## Watson Defends the Roll Cleanup

Watson has rejected the criticism, framing the maintenance as a core duty of his office. "I don't apologize for cleaning voter rolls," he said in an interview. "That is my job."

A spokesperson for the secretary of state's office, Elizabeth Jonson, said the 230,000 figure was "accumulated over more than five years of maintenance by local officials" and that the state does not classify voters as inactive solely on the basis of Experian data.

Experian spokesperson Scott Anderson said the company has not provided services to Mississippi since early 2025, and that "decisions regarding voter registration policies, procedures and record updates are made solely by election officials in accordance with applicable local, state and federal laws." Watson has since said the state moved to a new vendor, Interactive Data, which draws on information from all three major credit bureaus.

The broader scrutiny of Mississippi's voter rolls has also been documented in national coverage, with [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/06/mississippi-colom-hyde-smith-inactive-voter-registrations) noting that the close Senate race exposed concerns about relying on third-party data to determine where voters live and how states maintain their rolls.

Voting-rights experts say the design of the process — not merely the use of outside data — is the core problem. "We have evidence that some of the data is unreliable because eligible voters were inactivated by mistake and then never received the alert in the mail because it was sent to the wrong address, because the data was wrong," Thessalia Merivaki, an elections researcher at Washington State University, told Democracy Docket. Sydney Saubestre of the Center for Democracy and Technology put it more bluntly: "Credit bureau data was never intended to be used to maintain voter rolls."

The confusion was felt at the precinct level. Clint Lowery, a Harrison County voter told he was not on the roll on Election Day, described the experience to AP: "It kind of blew my mind. ... I used to understand how the system worked, right? And it looks like it's broken."

## A Bipartisan Opening for Reform

Unlike many election fights, this one has an explicit cross-party foothold. State Rep. Cheikh Taylor, chair of the Mississippi Democratic Party, said he intends to introduce legislation in the new year to increase transparency in how credit data is used and to give flagged voters a chance to confirm their identities before being deactivated.

"This is not a partisan issue," Taylor said. "The right to vote and the freedom to vote belongs to us all."

Republican state Sen. Jeremy England signaled that transparency legislation could draw GOP support — while acknowledging the state's history. "I don't think there was anything nefarious involved with it, but look, Mississippi also does have a dark past when it comes to elections," he said. "It's something we definitely need to look at more and make sure that we're doing it in a better way, so that if someone's registered to vote, you know, they're not marked as inactive for a reason they shouldn't be."

## What to Watch

The stakes extend well beyond one race. Mississippi is the only state without a recount law, placing enormous weight on how affidavit ballots are reviewed in close contests. And with statewide elections — including the governor's office, the legislature and the state supreme court — on the ballot in 2027, Democrats are pressing to lock in safeguards while their political momentum is fresh.

Whether Republican legislative leaders schedule the hearings the Black Caucus is demanding, and whether a transparency bill can attract GOP co-sponsors, will determine how quickly the state addresses a process that even some Republicans concede needs a second look. Until then, the central question raised by thousands of flagged voters remains unanswered: how many were eligible, and how many saw their ballots rejected for reasons they never had a chance to fix.
