# New Food Aid Work Rules Upend Early Retirees' Plans

Lisa Reese spent roughly 40 years working — at a liquor store, cleaning apartments, in hospitality, and for 25 years at Marriott — before retiring early to care for her family. At 62, she now looks after her 80-year-old mother in senior living, an uncle recovering from liver cancer surgery, and her 2-year-old granddaughter, whom she watches to avoid the cost of daycare.

When Reese applied for food assistance, she was approved for just $63 a month. The catch, [NPR reports](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/18/nx-s1-5954231/snap-work-rules-retirees), is that she must work, train, or volunteer 80 hours a month to keep it — a new condition that has upended the plans of older Americans and caregivers across the country.

## How the Rules Changed

The requirement stems from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed by President Trump in July 2025, which reshaped the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), the nation's largest food assistance program. The law expanded work requirements to groups that had long been exempt — including able-bodied adults aged 55 to 64 — while cutting roughly $187 billion in federal SNAP funding through 2034, according to [Congressional Budget Office analysis](https://www.cbo.gov/system/files/2025-08/61367-SNAP.pdf).

Those cuts represent roughly a 20% reduction in the program. The CBO estimates the work-requirement changes alone will reduce SNAP participation by 2.4 million people in an average month over the 2025-2034 period. Nationwide enrollment has already fallen sharply: the [Center on Budget and Policy Priorities](https://www.cbpp.org/research/food-assistance/snap-tracker-people-are-losing-food-assistance-as-the-harmful-2025), a left-leaning think tank tracking the changes, estimates that SNAP participation fell by 5.5 million people — a 13% decline — between the law's July 2025 enactment and July 2026, based on the latest month of data from the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Participation has dropped to about 36.1 million people, its lowest point in 17 years.

The expanded rules now reach older adults, parents of teenagers, veterans, homeless individuals, youth aging out of foster care, and some noncitizens including refugees.

## A Caregiver's Bind

For Reese, the policy's central tension is that it recognizes some unpaid caregiving — for someone deemed "incapacitated" — but not the broader role she plays holding her family together. Her uncle did not qualify as incapacitated, so her care for him and others doesn't count toward the 80-hour threshold.

"Daycare was expensive. So I decided to retire to take care of everybody. I don't know if that's crazy," Reese told NPR.

She meets the requirement only because a sympathetic local nonprofit lets her volunteer. Each morning she spends about four hours at Mission of Love Charities in Capitol Heights, Maryland, packing food bags — work she can do while still watching her granddaughter in the afternoons.

"I feel better when they walk past and they wave at me, 'Hey, Miss Mission of Love!' It makes me feel good that they're eating," she said.

The nonprofit's CEO, Adenia Bradley, said the arrangement cannot scale. The organization has already cut its food pantry from weekly to every other week and hired a third caseworker as demand climbs.

"We see it every day, people get cut off," Bradley said, citing both new work rules and administrative errors. "I'm always for a case-by-case basis, but what they're doing now is lumping them all in one pot."

Case workers also describe a difficult labor market. "One of our volunteers, she did three work applications already and she cannot find a job yet," Mission of Love case worker Jatmna Cabrera said — raising doubts about the premise that work is readily available.

## A State as Shock Absorber

Maryland, under Gov. Wes Moore, has tried to soften the impact with streamlined applications, smartphone access, faster eligibility verification, and automatic referrals to job-assistance centers when benefits are terminated. That approach has moderated enrollment declines relative to states such as Arizona, where participation roughly halved between July 2025 and July 2026 — a reminder that state policy meaningfully shapes how the federal cuts land.

## The Administration's Case

The USDA frames the requirement as reasonable and pro-work. "An expectation to work, train, or volunteer for 20 hours per week, as few as four hours per day, is not punishment," the department said in a statement to NPR. "Industriousness and contribution to community should be applauded, not used as scare tactics."

White House spokeswoman Allison Schuster said President Trump's approach has "brought down costs significantly" and argued the program's founding mission to be temporary help had been "a total failure." The legislation, she said, "began the process to halt fraud and ensure that it only serves eligible Americans who need it most."

The White House has also pointed to the broader economy and the removal of ineligible people as factors in the enrollment decline. Critics such as CBPP counter that unemployment stayed roughly flat while food prices rose — arguing the drop reflects policy, not reduced need.

## A Looming Double Whammy

Reese's situation previews a compounding burden: new Medicaid work rules phase in next year. She has relied on Medicaid since retiring and doubts she can sustain 80 hours a month of volunteering given arthritis, sciatica, and high blood pressure.

"I have arthritis in my hand real bad. My thighs hurt so bad, and sciatica, high blood pressure, you name it," she said.

She is now considering finding a part-time job to afford private insurance — splitting the cost with her daughter — until she turns 65 and qualifies for Medicare.

On the politics, Reese draws a distinction between long-term recipients and people like her. "The ones that's been on it for 10, 15, 20 years, yeah, they do need to get off of it," she said. "It's hurting folks like me. I'm sorry if that sounds cruel. But go after them, not the retirees."

## What to Watch

How far the changes go remains contested. Newly empowered Democrats, fresh from the 2026 midterms, want to roll back the SNAP provisions. Meanwhile, fiscal pressure on states intensifies: starting in October 2026, the federal government covers only 25% of administrative costs, down from 50%, and in October 2027 states must share benefit costs and face error-rate penalties.

The caregiving gap — unpaid family care that the rules do not count as work — remains largely unaddressed. For Reese and others like her, the question is whether the safety net can recognize the value of that labor before the Medicaid changes arrive.
