# Three Flu Strains Circulate Together in China, Experts Warn

China's winter respiratory season has taken an unusual turn: three influenza strains are circulating simultaneously, a pattern that breaks from the usual single-strain dominance and could stretch the outbreak longer while raising the risk of repeat infection, respiratory experts warned this week.

Unlike most years, when one strain drives the season, the 2026–27 flu picture now combines influenza A(H1N1)pdm09, A(H3N2) and influenza B(Victoria), [The Paper reported](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34509039), citing a Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital expert.

## A More Complex Season

Dr. Hu Yang, chief physician in the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, said on November 18 that previous flu seasons were mostly dominated by a single strain, but this year "broke that convention," with the two influenza A subtypes and influenza B spreading among the population at the same time. He put current proportions at roughly 47.4% H1N1, 23.4% H3N2 and 29.2% influenza B — while stressing these ratios are not fixed and continue to fluctuate.

Official monitoring tracked by the [China National Influenza Center](https://ivdc.chinacdc.cn/cnic/zyzx/lgzb/202611/t20261105_1842758.htm) confirms the shifting composition. In its Week 44 report — covering the period to November 1 and published November 5 — the agency said nationally 65.4% of positive influenza samples were influenza A and 34.6% influenza B. Within influenza A, A(H1N1)pdm09 accounted for 51.2% and A(H3N2) for 48.8%, with all influenza B belonging to the Victoria lineage. The report logged 106 influenza-like-illness outbreak clusters that week.

The agency also reported reassuring news on vaccine match: antigenic analysis found the circulating strains largely resembled the vaccine strains, with 94.7% of H1N1 similar to A/Missouri/11/2025, and nearly all strains remaining susceptible to neuraminidase and polymerase inhibitors. Only a tiny fraction — 0.2–0.3% of H3N2 and B strains — showed reduced susceptibility to neuraminidase inhibitors.

## No Cross-Protection

The central concern is that infection with one strain confers no immunity against the others. "What needs more vigilance is that there is no cross-protective immunity among the three flu strains," Hu said, explaining that antibodies from one subtype cannot defend against the other two. Because the body needs two to four weeks to develop protective antibodies, vulnerable people "could fully be infected by two or even three different strains within one flu season."

Dr. Li Dongzeng, chief physician in the Department of Infectious Diseases at Beijing You'an Hospital, made the same point in comments carried by [CNR](https://health.cnr.cn/jkgdxw/20261105/t20261105_527863038.shtml): with no cross-protection, people can be infected by different strains in quick succession, the season may lengthen and total infections risk rising. He cautioned, however, that "an earlier start does not necessarily mean the epidemic intensity will significantly increase," noting last year's season also began and ended early.

## Earlier Start, Milder Picture

This year's season began earlier than usual — southern provinces saw positivity rise in late September, with the north following in mid-to-late October. Hu had flagged the early "double-strain" pattern back in late September, when [People's Daily Health Client](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20260928A073HU00) reported that influenza positivity among sentinel-hospital respiratory samples had reached 19.0%, first among all pathogens, and that H3N2 accounted for 94.2% of influenza A.

At the clinical level, the picture remains relatively reassuring so far. Dr. Li Xuan, deputy chief physician in the respiratory department at Shanghai 10th People's Hospital, said on November 17 that although cases after the National Day holiday were mostly younger people, cold waves have brought a clear increase in elderly and chronic-disease patients with flu-like symptoms. "Overall the symptoms of flu patients seen clinically are relatively mild," she said, with pneumonia cases few and no large-scale surge in clinic visits yet.

Severity, experts stressed, is not the same as safety. Flu can rapidly invade respiratory cells, cause systemic symptoms and, in high-risk groups — the elderly, children, chronic-disease patients and the immunocompromised — trigger pneumonia, respiratory failure and, in extreme cases, intensive care.

## Vaccination and Vigilance

The experts' core message is that flu is not a "bad cold" and that vaccination remains the most cost-effective way to prevent infection and severe outcomes. China is using trivalent vaccines this year, which the CDC data indicate match currently circulating strains. Annual vaccination is recommended, and the whole season remains a valid window; priority groups include medical staff, adults 60 and older, chronic-disease patients, children aged six months to five years, pregnant women, and childcare and school staff and students.

Antivirals such as oseltamivir are most effective within 48 hours of symptom onset, though later use can still reduce severe-disease risk in higher-risk patients. They are prescription drugs and should not be self-administered for prevention. Hospitals nationwide have ample stock, Li said, so there is "no need to panic-buy or hoard drugs."

Everyday precautions still matter during multi-strain circulation: masks in enclosed public spaces such as malls and subways, frequent handwashing, ventilation and fewer gatherings. Even those who have recovered from one infection should keep up protection, Hu said, to avoid a second or third infection with a different strain.

## What to Watch

Strain proportions remain a moving snapshot, so the three-way mix could shift as the season deepens. The immediate test is whether the early start translates into a longer or heavier season overall, and whether vaccination uptake among high-risk groups keeps pace. Beyond influenza, other pathogens — rhinovirus and, in southern provinces, RSV — are also rising, meaning this winter's respiratory burden will not be influenza's alone.

For now, the guidance is simple: get vaccinated, seek care early if symptoms are severe, and do not let down your guard after a first infection.
