# Homo Erectus Fossil Found in Guangxi Rewrites Timeline

Researchers have identified the youngest-dated Homo erectus fossils ever found in China—and among the youngest anywhere on the Asian mainland—in two limestone caves in the southern region of Guangxi, a discovery that extends the species' known survival by roughly 200,000 years and upends long-held assumptions about where our ancient relatives could live. The findings were published in two [companion papers in the journal Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11155-6) at midnight Beijing time on November 19, [according to Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/20261119/dbdeff4c01d44b86b580062342fa5022/c.html).

Eight hominin teeth recovered from Naxian Cave and Baxian Cave in Jiangzhou District, Chongzuo City, were confirmed as Homo erectus, dated to between roughly 324,000 and 184,000 years ago. The fossils were drawn from more than 18,000 mammalian specimens collected over about a decade of fieldwork. A ninth tooth, labelled PA1796, could not be assigned to any known species and may hint at the presence of a Denisovan or hybrid population in the same region.

## A Challenge to Two Long-Standing Assumptions

The discovery undermines two prevailing ideas. The first is that Homo erectus disappeared from the Chinese mainland around 400,000 years ago. The second is that the species was a habitat specialist confined to open savanna and woodland, unable to survive in dense, closed-canopy rainforest.

"For a long time, Homo erectus was thought to live mainly in relatively open environments such as savanna and sparse woodland," said Zhang Yingqi, a researcher at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology under the Chinese Academy of Sciences and a co-corresponding author of one of the papers, in comments carried by Xinhua. "By analyzing mammal fossils unearthed with the teeth and isotope information preserved in the teeth, the team found these Homo erectus could survive in dense rainforest with mutually shading tree canopies."

The identification involved two complementary techniques. Researchers examined the bumps and grooves of the teeth—their morphology—and applied paleoproteomics, an analysis of ancient proteins preserved in dental enamel. Four teeth yielded molecular signatures linked to Homo erectus proteins. The age range was pinned down using five independent dating techniques applied to the teeth, cave sediments, and mineral formations.

## A Rainforest Dweller, Not a Grassland Specialist

The ecological finding may prove the most consequential. Adaptation to tropical rainforests had long been regarded as a hallmark of Homo sapiens alone.

"While there has been growing recognition that our own species, Homo sapiens, adapted to rainforests around the world between 100,000 and 13,000 years ago, this is the first clear, direct evidence we have for another hominin living within these habitats," said Patrick Roberts, a professor at the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology in Germany and a coauthor, as [reported by CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/18/science/teeth-homo-erectus-china).

The team also found signs of striking mobility. Strontium isotope analysis—which reflects the local geology where teeth formed during childhood—indicates that individuals ranged across surprisingly large areas, possibly up to 250 kilometers. Ben Utting, an affiliate research scientist at the University of Connecticut who was not involved in the study, told CNN that "journeys of this scale would have required a sophisticated understanding of the surrounding terrain, including regional landscape features and river networks that change with the seasons."

Kira Westaway, a geochronologist at Macquarie University in Sydney and a co-lead author, said the location and timing were both unexpected. "This is a fascinating discovery because it confirms the presence of Homo erectus in a location we were not expecting and at a time we hadn't considered," she told CNN. "This has huge implications for the timeline of Homo erectus as we attempt to understand the causes of its extinction." Westaway was more blunt about the older theory that the species was an inflexible habitat specialist, telling [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/ancient-fossils-reveal-homo-erectus-survived-in-china-some-200-000-years-longer-than-we-thought/): "The idea never really sat very well with me."

## A Mystery Ninth Tooth

While eight teeth fit comfortably within Homo erectus, the ninth stood apart. PA1796 showed no Homo erectus molecular signature and differs morphologically from Homo erectus, Neanderthals, and modern humans. Researchers suggested it could belong to a Denisovan, a Denisovan–Homo erectus hybrid, or an unidentified population.

Ryan McRae, a paleoanthropologist at the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History who was not involved in the research, told CNN that "one of the nine teeth discovered may be Denisovan, or a hybrid, rather than Homo erectus. They don't really commit to calling that, but they certainly hint at it." Chris Stringer, a human-origins researcher at the Natural History Museum in London, said the evidence "increases the likelihood" that interbreeding between the erectus and Denisovan lineages occurred, "at least on occasions."

## Why It Matters

Homo erectus emerged in Africa roughly two million years ago and was the first archaic human to leave the continent and spread across Asia. The species persisted for more than 1.5 million years—far longer than Homo sapiens' roughly 400,000 years—making it the longest-surviving of all human relatives. The youngest Homo erectus fossils known globally come from Java, Indonesia, dated to about 108,000 years ago.

The Guangxi find fills a gap in the fossil record of late Middle Pleistocene Homo erectus in southern China and suggests the species was far more adaptable than its reputation allowed. As McRae put it: "The more we discover about Homo erectus the more we are coming to realize that they are not that different than ourselves."

The research was led by Shandong University's School of Archaeology together with the Chinese Academy of Sciences' IVPP, with partners including Macquarie University, the Max Planck Institute, the University of Copenhagen, and the University of Bordeaux. The two companion papers appear in Nature, published on November 19.

## What to Watch For

Key questions remain. The species attribution of PA1796—Denisovan, hybrid, or something else—awaits further study. Researchers also want to pin down the precise ecological niche of the Guangxi population, its relationship to better-known Chinese groups such as the Zhoukoudian "Peking Man" and Hexian fossils, and what ultimately drove Homo erectus to extinction if not an inability to adapt. If rainforests were never the barrier once assumed, the answer to why our most enduring relative vanished may lie somewhere else entirely.
