# Van Gogh Sells for Record €173 Million After Nazi-Era Dispute

A luminous 1890 Vincent van Gogh canvas sold for $190.4 million (roughly €173 million) at Sotheby's in New York on Wednesday night, setting a new auction record for the Dutch post-impressionist — and closing a dramatic week in which the sale was nearly derailed by a restitution claim tied to its Nazi-era history.

The painting, *Châtaigniers en fleurs* ("Blossoming Chestnut Trees"), was painted in Auvers-sur-Oise in the final months before Van Gogh's death and had not been publicly exhibited for some 60 years, according to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/19/schilderij-van-vincent-van-gogh-bloeiende-kastanjebomen-geve/). It surpassed the artist's previous auction record of $117.2 million, set in 2022 at Christie's New York for *Orchard with Cypresses*.

## A record that almost didn't happen

The sale came after an eleventh-hour settlement between the sellers — the Buenos Aires-based Blaquier-Arrieta family — and the legal heirs of Samuel and Hedwig Fischer, a prominent German Jewish family that once owned the work. The [New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/18/arts/design/van-gogh-painting-auction-sothebys.html) reported that a marathon negotiating session on Monday produced an undisclosed agreement that allowed the auction to proceed on Wednesday.

The Fischer heirs had argued that their family parted with the painting in 1936 under duress, after the Nazi regime devastated their publishing house, S. Fischer Verlag, and forced the family to leave Germany. Their Berlin-based lawyer, Gunnar Schnabel, had pressed Sotheby's to prove the sale occurred at true market value and that the family would have sold it regardless of the political climate — contending, in a statement reported by [NL Times](https://nltimes.nl/2026/11/15/jewish-heirs-challange-180-million-van-gogh-auction-cite-sale-nazi-pressure), that otherwise "no auction may take place."

Sotheby's said its own research had not established that the 1936 sale was made under duress, but that it had chosen to engage constructively with the family rather than push ahead. The auction house announced the settlement on Tuesday, a day before the sale.

## The night at Sotheby's

The Van Gogh was the marquee lot of a 12-work single-owner evening sale from the Blaquier-Arrieta collection, which carried an aggregate pre-sale estimate of roughly $450 million and realized about $412 million. According to the New York Times, the auctioneer, Oliver Barker, spent more than three minutes trying to draw new bidders as Sotheby's controlling shareholder, Patrick Drahi, watched from behind the audience. Ultimately one buyer prevailed: the veteran New York dealer William Acquavella, head of Acquavella Galleries.

The [Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/artanddesign/2026/nov/19/vincent-van-gogh-painting-sells-for-record-190million) reported that the result made the Van Gogh the most expensive work auctioned so far this season. Sotheby's described it as the fourth most expensive artwork ever sold at auction, while the New York Times, adjusting for inflation, placed it as the sixth most expensive painting ever sold.

The painting itself depicts three women in colorful dress walking in the shade of a blossom-laden chestnut tree, with a man in blue work clothes heading in the opposite direction down the street — a quiet, sunlit scene from Auvers-sur-Oise.

## Why the dispute matters

The case placed a spotlight on the contested provenance of art that changed hands during the Nazi era. Under the 1998 Washington Principles on Nazi-confiscated art, current holders and the market are encouraged to seek "just and fair" solutions for such claims. [NL Times](https://nltimes.nl/2026/11/19/contested-van-gogh-painting-auctioned-record-eu173-million) noted that restitution claims for art surrendered by Jewish collectors during the Nazi period have risen again in recent years, with many resolved through private agreement rather than litigation — including a Kandinsky looted from Amsterdam that sold for nearly €32 million in October.

After the sale, Schnabel told the New York Times that the Fischer family hoped the public interest in the case, "and its fast and fair and just resolution, may offer some encouragement to other families who have faced persecution during the Nazi era." He added that the mood in the salesroom was "no Champagne atmosphere."

In a statement reported by the Guardian, the heirs said they were "grateful for the trusting and fair negotiation and collaboration with Sotheby's and the Blaquier-Arrieta family, which make it possible to acknowledge the suffering of the Fischer family during the Nazi era."

## What to watch

Two questions linger. The terms of the settlement remain confidential, leaving open whether compensation was paid. And with a dealer — rather than an institution — as the winning bidder, the painting's next destination is unknown; it could enter a private collection or be resold.

What the sale already demonstrates is a market in which trophy works can still command record prices even as the pool of bidders at the very top remains narrow. For Van Gogh, it was another record. For the Fischer heirs, it was a measure of recognition — and a reminder that the art world's reckoning with its Nazi-era past is far from finished.
