# Democrats' 2026 Midterms: Largest Win in 50 Years

Democrats won back the House of Representatives in the 2026 midterms by a decisive margin — capturing roughly 56% of the national House vote to Republicans' 42% — a 14-point spread that, according to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/19/nx-s1-6045916/democrats-midterm-house-majority), represents the largest win for either party in 50 years, since the post-Watergate election of 1974. [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/democrats-house-control-midterm-election-wins-trump-check-rcna603397) projected that Democrats also flipped the Senate, putting them in charge of both chambers of Congress for the first time in Trump's second term.

## A wave without modern precedent

The scale of the victory sits in a category of its own. Democrats' 2018 and 2006 gains — the latter of which then-President George W. Bush called a "thumping" — were both House margins of less than 10 points, as was the 2010 "shellacking" Democrats suffered at the hands of Republicans. Obama's 2008 election came closest to 2026 at almost 11 points. This year's 14-point spread exceeded them all.

Democrats flipped 46 Republican-held House seats so far, with that number potentially rising to 49 in uncalled races in North Carolina, Minnesota and Tennessee. That marks the second-most flipped seats this century, behind only the 66 seats Republicans flipped in 2010. NBC projected Democrats will hold about 255 seats; NPR estimated the party could reach 260 if all its leads hold — the most since the 102nd Congress of 1991–1993, giving the party its largest majority in a generation.

The result returns House Republicans to the minority status they had not held since before the 1994 Republican Revolution, which ended four decades of Democratic House control.

## A referendum on Trump's second term

The wave was widely interpreted as a rebuke to President Donald Trump and his policies. Driving the losses, according to NBC, were Trump's unpopular policies, his tough immigration crackdown, an eight-month war with Iran, and tariffs on U.S. allies that pushed up the prices of gas, groceries and other goods. Democrats led among independent voters by roughly 20 points.

Trump's approval rating languished throughout the campaign, sinking to roughly 32% in Reuters/Ipsos polling, and became a central anchor on Republican candidates. The results mark the first time in Trump's second term that he will not have full control of Congress.

## A historic leadership line-up

With their majority secured, House Democrats voted to nominate Rep. Hakeem Jeffries of New York for Speaker — a vote that sets him up to become the first Black Speaker in U.S. history when the new Congress convenes on Jan. 3. According to [CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/11/18/politics/hakeem-jeffries-house-democrats-leadership-elections), just 11 Democrats voted against Jeffries in the secret ballot, despite pressure from a bloc of newly elected progressives demanding votes on Medicare for All and a ban on arms sales to Israel.

"Today begins the great American comeback. The American people have spoken all across the land, in the North, in the South, in the East, in the West, in the Midwest, from sea to shining sea. The American people have spoken and made clear they've had enough of the extremism, enough of the corruption," Jeffries said on election night.

"They want a Congress that's going to deliver for the American people, and that is exactly what House Democrats will do as soon as we get into office in January."

Asked about the defectors, Jeffries was terse: "Our job is to fight MAGA extremism, not each other." Senior Democrat Nancy Pelosi downplayed the opposition, noting that 11 no votes were "not so important compared to 250 some votes that he received."

His leadership team will make history on multiple fronts. Rep. Katherine Clark is set to become the first woman to serve as House majority leader, Rep. Pete Aguilar will be the first Latino in the No. 3 job of whip, and Rep. Lauren Underwood will become the highest-ranking Black woman in Congress.

## An agenda of checks and reversals

With the majority, Democrats gain subpoena power and committee gavels, giving them the ability to investigate the Trump administration, its family business ventures and federal agencies. Rep. Robert Garcia, poised to chair the Oversight Committee, has said his panel will focus on "Trump family corruption," abuses by ICE and the Department of Homeland Security, and the Epstein files. On impeachment, Jeffries has said Democrats have "not ruled anything in and not ruled anything out."

Legislatively, Jeffries has signaled a shift toward progressive healthcare priorities — backing a phase-in of Medicare expansion, including lowering the eligibility age, guaranteeing coverage for children, and adding dental, vision and hearing benefits. Democratic priorities also include reversing Trump's Medicaid cuts, extending Affordable Care Act credits, restoring congressional authority over tariffs, and ending the Iran war.

## A redistricting war, unfinished

The outcome capped a turbulent mid-decade redistricting battle. Trump fired the first shot in summer 2025, urging Texas Republicans to redraw maps to pick up as many as five seats; the GOP followed with new maps in Florida, North Carolina, Tennessee, Alabama, Louisiana and Missouri. Democrats countered in California, where voters approved Proposition 50 to enact a Democratic-drawn map, and the Supreme Court blocked Missouri's GOP map.

"This fight is not over; we've just gotten started," Jeffries said before the election. "It will continue into the next Congress until we pass legislation signed into law by a future president that will prohibit mid-decade gerrymandering from ever happening again."

## What's next

The new Congress convenes on Jan. 3, when Jeffries is expected to be sworn in as Speaker and Democrats formally take the gavel. The result will have changed House control five times in the last 20 years — the most churn in 130 years — a sign of how unsettled American politics has become.

For Trump, the wave delivers the first real check of his second term, with subpoena power and legislative leverage now in opposition hands. For Democrats, the challenge will be converting a commanding majority into durable governing results — and deciding how far to press investigations, and potential impeachment, against a president who has just lost his grip on Congress.
