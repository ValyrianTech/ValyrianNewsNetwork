# European Commission Hit by Major AI-Assisted Cyberattack

The European Commission has been targeted by a large-scale cyberattack that disrupted internal HR systems for more than a week, with officials still unable to say whether sensitive staff data was accessed or stolen. The attack, first detected on 22 October, was carried out using a publicly available, AI-powered tool that automates the discovery of weaknesses in digital systems.

The incident, first reported by Politico's Brussels Playbook and covered by [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/19/eu-commission-hit-by-large-scale-cyberattack-with-data-leakage-unknown), has affected platforms including Sysper, the internal HR system used to check payslips, submit timesheets and request leave. Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier confirmed the intrusion and said the Commission had taken "necessary steps" in response.

"We have not detected any evidence of a data breach affecting Commission staff as a whole," Regnier told reporters on Thursday. "The investigation is still ongoing. We are taking any necessary steps based on the findings as they come."

## What Was Affected

The attack disrupted multiple internal systems over roughly ten days. Sysper briefly went offline again on 18 November before recovering, while the Joint Sickness Insurance Scheme (JSIS), the Commission's health insurance system, remained unavailable as of Thursday 19 November. The MyPMO platform, used for processing medical reimbursements, and the Commission's translation services were also affected.

Commission officials told Euronews that most civil servants barely noticed the disruption, and salaries largely arrived as usual despite some routine HR tasks being blocked. The Commission's IT department, DG DIGIT, is still assessing the damage, while the EU's cybersecurity service, CERT-EU, and the European Data Protection Supervisor have been informed.

According to [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/european-commission-cyberattack-politico), the Commission believes it has stopped most of the attack, but the extent of any data exposure remains unclear. The platforms hold sensitive personal details, including officials' names, pay grades, home addresses and contact information — a trove that could fuel far more sophisticated follow-on campaigns targeting individual staff.

Regnier said the attack was based on a publicly available AI tool. CERT-EU identified the framework as ARTEX, an AI-powered penetration-testing tool that automates the identification and exploitation of misconfigurations and software vulnerabilities. It is not yet established whether ARTEX merely aided reconnaissance or enabled the actual intrusion, and the Commission has declined to confirm whether the attack is still ongoing or to attribute it to a specific actor.

## A Pattern of Intrusions

The case is the Commission's third major cyber incident in roughly a year. In January, an attack on the Commission's central mobile-device infrastructure potentially exposed staff names and phone numbers. In March, its cloud infrastructure hosting the Europa.eu websites was compromised, with CERT-EU reporting that around 91.7 GB of data — including names, email addresses and email contents — was stolen. That breach may have exposed data from at least 29 other EU institutions and bodies.

As [Cyber Security News](https://cybersecuritynews.com/european-commission-cyberattack/) reported, neither earlier case has been publicly linked to the November attack. The incidents come amid a broader wave of cyber threats across Europe, including attacks on supermarket refrigeration systems in France and Italy, a breach at Belgium's finance and tax agency, and a pro-Russian hacker group targeting the Italian Foreign Ministry.

The Commission's Corporate Management Board, its top internal crisis group, held an urgent meeting earlier in November, and technology commissioner Henna Virkkunen briefed fellow commissioners on 18 November. The Commission's digital services department ordered extra security checks in response.

## Transparency Under Scrutiny

The handling of the disclosure has drawn political criticism. Dutch MEP Bart Groothuis, of the centrist Renew group, accused the Commission of excessive secrecy. "The problem I have with the European Commission is that they keep the information about it internally and don't disclose it to the public," he told Euronews.

"Each time they don't bring information to the public about intrusions that hit EU institutions, bodies and agencies, they miss an opportunity to explain and sell the economic security policy we set out as the EU against our adversaries," Groothuis said.

As [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/783618/article/2026-11-19/la-commission-europeenne-victime-dune-importante-cyberattaque) noted, the incident could have compromised a large quantity of personal data, with the encoding of employee leave requests among the most visibly affected functions. Regnier said the Commission had no record of a ransom request.

## What to Watch

The central unanswered question is whether the attacker — still unidentified — reached the personal records held in Sysper, JSIS and MyPMO, and if so, which employees and which types of data were exposed. Officials have said that uncertainty about the scope of any compromise was one reason staff received only limited details.

The episode also sharpens a debate over how openly EU institutions disclose cyber intrusions. The Commission introduced a package of new cybersecurity measures in January and has acknowledged becoming a target because of the geopolitical climate. With MEPs pressing for greater transparency and a tool like ARTEX lowering the barrier to automated attacks, how the Commission communicates — and hardens its systems — will be closely watched in Brussels and beyond.
