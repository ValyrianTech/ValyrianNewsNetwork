# Russia and China Build a Polar Silk Road in the Arctic

Moscow and Beijing are quietly stitching together a strategic partnership across the Arctic, linking China's Maritime Silk Road to Russia's Trans-Arctic Transport Corridor in a bid to reshape global shipping — and Western capitals are taking notice. The deepening cooperation, examined in a [geopolitical analysis published by RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/vu-d-europe-dans-le-grand-nord-un-rapprochement-hautement-strategique-entre-moscou-et-pekin-11807719), centres on Russian ports, maritime research and technology exchange, and it is unfolding just as climate change opens previously inaccessible sea routes to commercial navigation.

## Why the Arctic Matters Now

Melting sea ice is transforming the Far North from a frozen wilderness into one of the world's most contested transport arteries. According to USGS estimates cited in the analysis, the Arctic seabed holds nearly one-third of the world's undiscovered natural gas and 13 percent of its undiscovered oil.

The strategic prize, however, is the Northern Sea Route. A voyage from Shanghai to Hamburg takes roughly 18 days via the Arctic, compared with 30 to 35 days through the Suez Canal. As disruptions ripple through Middle Eastern chokepoints and the Suez and Hormuz corridors, the polar shortcut is becoming a more attractive — if still risky and costly — alternative.

## A Deepening Partnership

The recent visit to China by Nikolai Patrushev, an advisor to President Vladimir Putin, illustrated the two countries' intent. According to [Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/2026/10/china-russia-quietly-moving-toward-a-new-arctic-axis/), discussions covered aligning China's Maritime Silk Road with Russia's Trans-Arctic corridor, developing Russian ports, exchanging container-terminal automation technology and conducting joint maritime research.

The economic logic is clear. China is Russia's largest trading partner, and two-way trade exceeded US$200 billion for a third consecutive year in 2025. Russian oil flows through the Northern Sea Route surged by roughly 50 percent year-on-year in 2026, with about 20 million barrels shipped to Asia between July and mid-October, the analysis noted. Putin has suggested cargo volumes could double to 70 million tonnes a year by the end of the decade, aided by Rosneft's Vostok Oil project.

In August 2026, Chinese shipping firm Sea Legend launched the first regular weekly container service across the Arctic, realising Beijing's long-held "Polar Silk Road" ambition. South Korea and India have also signalled interest in the northern shortcut.

## The Military Dimension

Beneath the commercial rhetoric lies a hardening security competition. Russia controls roughly 53 percent of the Arctic coastline, and its Northern Fleet operates from bases concentrated around Murmansk. Moscow has reopened Soviet-era military bases and deployed advanced coastal-defence systems, S-400 air defences adapted to Arctic conditions, and fighters armed with hypersonic missiles, according to the Polish outlet [Defence24](https://defence24.com/geopolitics/cold-war-at-the-top-of-the-world-the-militarisation-of-the-arctic-and-the-sino-russian-alliance-on-the-northern-sea-route).

"Russia is expanding its military footprint in the high north. Moscow has reopened Soviet-era bases and is testing new weapons in the region," EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas warned, citing drone incursions, GPS jamming and the sabotage of undersea cables as threats to Arctic security.

Western intelligence is watching China's role closely. Thomas Nilsson, head of Swedish military intelligence, told The Times that Beijing will "use" Russia to gain a foothold in the Far North — an assessment the [East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/21/china-adapts-to-a-colder-arctic-reception/) frames more cautiously, noting that China's military capabilities in the region remain limited in the near term even as its partnership with Russia proves closer than earlier forecasts suggested.

## The Western Response

The icebreaker gap looms large over Western strategy. The United States operates just two ageing icebreakers, while Russia has more than 40, including powerful Arktika-class nuclear vessels, Defence24 noted. "Without the physical capability to operate in ice-covered waters, Washington's declarations in favour of freedom of navigation in the Arctic remain mere diplomatic notes."

NATO has moved to strengthen its northern flank. The accession of Finland and Sweden means seven of the Arctic Council's eight members now belong to the alliance, and the Arctic Sentry initiative, launched in February 2026, unifies maritime and air patrols, intelligence-sharing and military exercises.

The European Union, meanwhile, unveiled a new Arctic strategy in October 2026 that reorients its priorities from climate to security, as [Le Monde](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2026/10/20/eu-unveils-new-arctic-strategy-in-face-of-us-russia-threats_6760261_4.html) reported. "The Arctic is one of the places where the tectonic plates of geopolitics are colliding," said European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen. The plan includes expanding the EU's icebreaker fleet, boosting critical-infrastructure protection, intensifying monitoring of Russia's "shadow fleet" and creating a new Centre for Maritime Security.

## What to Watch

The Arctic is unlikely to replace Suez any time soon: ice, high insurance premiums, specialised vessels and a short operating season all constrain the route. But the direction of travel is clear. As Asia Times put it, "whoever controls the software, data, equipment and logistics architecture of ports can shape trade without ever firing a shot." The question now is whether the West can match that architecture with physical capability — and whether Moscow's growing dependence on Beijing will ultimately cost it influence in its own strategic backyard.
