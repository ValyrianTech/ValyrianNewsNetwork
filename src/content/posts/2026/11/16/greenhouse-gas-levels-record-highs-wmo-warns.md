# Greenhouse Gas Levels Hit Record Highs, WMO Warns

Atmospheric concentrations of the three most important long-lived greenhouse gases linked to human activity all reached new record highs in 2025, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) [reported](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/greenhouse-gas-levels-once-again-reach-record-high) on 27 October 2026, warning that the accumulating heat would intensify extreme weather for decades to come.

Global average carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration reached 426.8 parts per million (ppm) — 153% of the pre-industrial level of 1750 — while methane (CH4) hit 1,948 parts per billion (ppb) and nitrous oxide (N2O) reached 339.1 ppb. The findings, published in the WMO's annual Greenhouse Gas Bulletin No. 22, were timed to inform negotiators gathering at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye, which ran from 9 to 20 November.

## 'Another Year, Another New Record'

WMO Deputy Secretary-General Ko Barrett captured the gravity of the findings in a blunt assessment. "Another year, another new record. Greenhouse gases are trapping an unprecedented amount of heat in the atmosphere, providing energy for more extreme weather and associated risks to our economies and societies," she said, according to [ESG News](https://esgnews.com/wmo-greenhouse-gas-record-2025/). "The past 11 years have been the warmest on record and this trend is set to continue."

Barrett stressed that CO2's longevity means today's concentrations will shape the climate for generations. "CO2 has a very long life-time and so will affect the global climate not just for years but for decades and even centuries."

The bulletin underscores a distinction that is central to understanding the crisis: it measures accumulated atmospheric concentrations, not annual emissions. Even if emissions were slashed immediately, roughly half of the CO2 emitted each year remains in the atmosphere, committing the planet to further warming. According to the report, CO2 growth rates have tripled since the 1960s, rising from an annual average of 0.8 ppm per year to 2.4 ppm per year during the 2011–2020 decade. When the bulletin was first published in 2004, the annual average CO2 level stood at 377.5 ppm — a rise of roughly 49 ppm in about two decades.

## A Temporary Slowdown, Not a Reprieve

The year-on-year increase from 2024 to 2025 was +2.8 ppm — above the decadal average but slightly below the exceptional surge recorded in 2023/2024. WMO senior scientific officer Oksana Tarasova attributed the more modest figure to a recovery of land carbon sinks and reduced fire emissions after a strong El Niño.

"However, a 2.8 ppm annual increase is still a substantial addition of carbon to the atmosphere from fossil fuel burning," she cautioned. "We can expect to see a further increase in concentrations in 2026 because of fossil fuel burning and the development of a powerful El Niño which will impact land carbon sinks as it did in 2023/2024."

The WMO also flagged emerging concerns from the energy transition itself. The 2025 increase in sulphur hexafluoride (SF6) — used as a spark suppressant in electricity distribution and with a atmospheric lifetime of over 1,000 years and a global warming potential roughly 24,300 times that of CO2 — was a record high. Hydrogen concentrations rose 5.3% between 2010 and 2024; while not itself a greenhouse gas, hydrogen has a strong indirect warming effect, and leakage from production and infrastructure poses a growing concern.

NOAA's Annual Greenhouse Gas Index shows that radiative forcing by long-lived greenhouse gases increased 56% from 1990 to 2025, with CO2 accounting for about 81% of that increase.

## Pressure Builds on COP31

The bulletin was released as an authoritative scientific input for negotiators at COP31, which aims to "turn climate commitments into concrete actions," according to the [summit's official site](https://cop31.tr/). Preliminary WMO data presented at the summit indicated that 2026 is set to rank among the four warmest years on record, [Qazinform](https://qazinform.com/news/2026-set-to-rank-among-4-warmest-years-on-record-wmo-says-7dd2c0) reported.

Barrett acknowledged the rapid expansion of renewable energy but urged caution. "The energy transition is essential and welcome, but as with every transition, it has to be done in a smart way and informed by environmental observations," she said.

The report also carries a starker message: the risks of reduced CO2 uptake by the ocean are increasing as the climate changes, threatening the natural carbon sinks that currently absorb roughly half of humanity's emissions.

## What to Watch

With a powerful El Niño developing and fossil fuel burning continuing apace, the WMO expects concentrations to climb further in 2026. The key question for negotiators in Antalya and beyond is whether emissions cuts can outpace the inertia of a climate system that scientists say is now running hotter than historical baselines prepared societies for. As Barrett put it: "Every additional part per million matters because it traps additional heat. In effect, we are supercharging the ocean and the atmosphere."
