# China Revises Rules Governing Local Party Committees

The Central Committee of the Communist Party of China has issued a revised set of regulations governing how the country's local party committees operate, make decisions and are held to account, in a move aimed at tightening centralized control over governance at the provincial, city and county levels. The full text and an accompanying implementation notice were published by [People's Daily](http://politics.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1116/c461001-40834175.html) on its front page on 16 November, after Xinhua released the document the previous evening.

The revision — the first major overhaul of the rules since 2015 — was reviewed and approved by the 24-member Politburo, chaired by Xi Jinping, on 27 March 2026, and formally promulgated by the Central Committee on 7 November, according to [the Chinese government portal](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/202611/content_7086080.htm). It is the implementing measure for a decision adopted at the Fifth Plenum of the 20th Central Committee in late October, which called for the strengthening of local party committees' functions.

## What local party committees do

Local party committees form the vital middle tier between the Party center in Beijing and grassroots organizations. As of the end of 2025, China had 3,202 such committees nationwide — 31 at the provincial level, 396 at the city or prefecture level, and 2,775 at the county level, according to a [People's Daily commentary by Yin Li](https://cpc.people.com.cn/n1/2026/1109/c64094-40830296.html) published ahead of the release. In their jurisdictions, they exercise what the Party calls "overall leadership" — setting direction, managing the big picture, making decisions and ensuring implementation.

China first issued trial rules for these bodies in 1996, followed by a full version approved in 2015. The new text, running to seven chapters and 34 articles, retains that foundational structure while adding several sharp-edged provisions designed to address recurring problems in local governance.

## A negative list of prohibited conduct

Perhaps the most consequential addition is Article 27, a new "negative list" that enumerates 10 categories of prohibited decision-making behavior. It bars local committees from distorting or selectively implementing central directives; from pursuing quick, debt-funded projects to burnish short-term reputations; from abandoning predecessors' unfinished projects — the phenomenon the document calls "new officials ignoring old debts"; and from illegally adding hidden local government debt.

The list also prohibits "local protectionism" and the creation of policy barriers that obstruct the construction of a unified national market — tying party-disciplinary rules to a core economic policy objective of dismantling local fiefdoms that fragment China's domestic economy.

According to the accompanying notice, the regulations are intended to strengthen the function of local party committees and improve their operating mechanisms, the report said. It directed local committees to resolutely implement the Central Committee's decisions and ensure that its orders are carried out without obstruction.

## Constraining the top leader

Several provisions target the unchecked authority of local party secretaries, the top leader of each committee. Article 18 bars the secretary from riding above the organization, from practicing "one-man rule" or "patriarchal style," and from requiring subordinates to uphold personal authority. Article 23 introduces a "last-to-speak" rule at standing committee meetings, under which the secretary speaks only after other members have offered their views — a mechanism designed to prevent the leader from setting the tone and suppressing dissent.

The revised text also caps the size of standing committees: 11 to 13 members at the provincial level and 9 to 11 at city and county levels. Article 17 stresses that matters requiring collective decision must be decided collectively, with no individual or minority permitted to decide unilaterally.

## Lifetime accountability

The regulations' most striking enforcement innovation appears in Article 30, which establishes lifetime accountability for major decision-making errors. Where a standing committee causes a serious misjudgment, the secretary and the members who participated in the decision face investigation and accountability long after they leave office or retire. Members who recorded an explicit objection or reservation at the time may be exempted from liability — an incentive for officials to speak up rather than rubber-stamp collective decisions.

## Analysis: a rulebook for implementing central control

The revision codifies themes that officials had already been signaling. In her commentary, Yin Li described local party committees as the "middle link" in implementing central decisions, and said they must obey the Central Committee's leadership "without deviation, without distortion." That framing lays bare a central concern: the transmission gap at the regional level, where local officials might dilute or reinterpret Beijing's policies.

The negative list turns a set of political slogans into enforceable rules. By naming specific pathologies — "image projects," "performance projects," policy "flip-flopping" and hidden debt — the document targets the tendency of incoming leaders to abandon predecessors' unfinished work and chase flashy, debt-funded megaprojects for short-term credit. The lifetime-accountability provision, meanwhile, gives the discipline inspection system a long-term lever over officials who might otherwise prioritize rapid results over sustainability.

The emphasis on collective leadership and the last-to-speak rule respond to recurring anti-corruption findings in which local secretaries dominated decision-making and sidelined their committees. These are procedural tools rather than punitive ones, aimed at reshaping how decisions are made before errors occur.

Ultimately, the regulations function as an operating manual for 3,202 committees. Article 32 requires each committee to draft its own work rules based on the regulation, which means the practical effect will depend on how strictly those rules are implemented and enforced by higher-level oversight.

## What to watch

No official side-by-side comparison with the 2015 text has been published, leaving some uncertainty about exactly how much has changed in substance. Enforcement mechanisms beyond the general reference to discipline and law are also sparse in the text, meaning the real test will come through the discipline inspection and inspection systems that the regulations invoke. For now, the document signals a clear direction: tighter procedural discipline, longer accountability horizons and sharper limits on local leaders' personal authority.
