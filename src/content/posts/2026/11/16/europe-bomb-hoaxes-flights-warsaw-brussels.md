# Bomb hoaxes disrupt dozens of European flights

A wave of bomb threats forced emergency landings, fighter-jet escorts and security sweeps across Europe on 5 November, disrupting at least 27 passenger flights — including a LOT Polish Airlines service from Warsaw to Brussels that landed safely at Brussels Airport in Zaventem after the alert. No explosives were found on any aircraft, and investigators have yet to identify who is behind the alerts.

The Brussels-bound flight was among the first alerts of the day. According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/05/bommelding-vlucht-warschau-naar-brussel/), the plane landed normally and Belgium's federal police carried out a risk analysis of the aircraft. "After investigation by the federal police, no additional measures proved necessary," the airport's press service said, adding that police are examining whether the incident is linked to earlier, similar cases.

## A continent-wide wave

The disruption extended far beyond Belgium. On the same day, a Ryanair flight from Charleroi to Pescara was briefly held after a note bearing the word "bom" was found in the aircraft toilets; the alarm was lifted and the plane departed for Italy. Another LOT flight, bound for Paris, was stopped during taxiing and searched, but no suspicious package was found.

Elsewhere the response was more dramatic. An Aer Lingus Airbus A320 flying from Dublin to Rome was diverted to France's Évreux military air base and intercepted by French fighter jets before its 157 occupants disembarked safely. A Finnair service from Helsinki to Rome was escorted by Italian jets into Fiumicino airport, while a Wizz Air flight from Milan to Warsaw was diverted to Vienna. Bulgaria saw three Ryanair flights affected, and Czech police reported threats against four aircraft bound for Prague.

As [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/live/cwjdmnrg94ezt) reported, at least 21 flights had been impacted by the afternoon, a tally that continued to climb toward the end of the day. Britain's Heathrow and Birmingham airports also said they had received threats, though Heathrow described its alert as "not credible."

## Scale and cost

By the EUobserver's count, at least 35 planes faced threats across the week's three days of alarms, with the scare affecting 20 airports in 16 countries — Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Finland, France, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Serbia, Spain and the UK. Citing Eurocontrol, [EUobserver](https://euobserver.com/248019/eu-commission-to-host-security-talks-after-week-of-bomb-hoaxes-that-hit-20-airports-and-dozens-of-european-flights/) reported that the hoaxes affected less than 0.5 percent of European flights on any of those days and did not create network-wide delays.

The financial toll, however, is far from trivial. Eurocontrol data puts the cost of each affected flight at about €55,000 on average, sometimes rising to as much as €220,000 per flight according to aviation expert Philippe Baum of Coventry University — adding up to millions of euros in lost time, fuel, security resources and passenger disruption.

The 5 November wave was the second in a matter of days, following threats against at least seven flights on 3 November. A day later, on 6 November, bomb alerts reached newsrooms, forcing Ireland's RTÉ to evacuate and suspend programming.

## Who is behind the threats?

As of mid-November, no responsibility had been publicly established, and authorities cautioned against drawing premature conclusions about whether the incidents share a common source. EU transport commissioner Apostolos Tzitzikostas said it was "too early to link the incidents to each other," while stressing that Europe remained at "full operational readiness" with passengers' safety and the smooth functioning of European aviation as the priority.

Some messages invoked the names of misogynistic groups. An email to airport contacts in Italy claimed to come from "Fighters of the Male State," apparently referring to the Russian anti-feminist movement Male State. Its founder, Vladislav Pozdnyakov, denied any involvement. Such claims have not established who is actually responsible.

The timing — set against a backdrop of cyberattacks on supermarket refrigeration systems and other malicious disruptions in Europe — has fuelled speculation about hybrid warfare, but no hard evidence has yet tied the hoaxes to a state actor. Security specialists have floated several possibilities: coordinated disruption, individuals acting on personal grievances, and simple copycat behaviour.

## Copycat threats and the policy response

The pattern widened well beyond aviation. Italian schools had already received threatening messages in late October and early November, and on 11 November roughly 6,300 schools received emailed bomb warnings, with further waves hitting some 3,778 Italian schools the next day — about 77 of which were evacuated. Similar threats reached schools in Scotland, Northern Ireland and Ireland, and a bomb alert briefly suspended rail travel at the Calais-Fréthun station. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni said those sending the threats sought to generate fear and disruption, while checks found no concrete danger.

In Ireland, Taoiseach Micheál Martin said the Garda Síochána, the Defence Forces and other authorities were working with European counterparts to identify those responsible, and that it had not been determined whether the threats came from individuals or a coordinated group. Tánaiste Simon Harris described threats against the media as a threat to democracy.

The European Commission responded by convening a meeting of EU aviation security experts on 11 November, which also included Norway, Switzerland and Iceland, to review the situation and discuss whether extra measures are needed.

## What to watch

Two questions now dominate the response. The first is attribution: whether the flight, media and school waves share a single source — or, as officials suggest, are the scattered work of opportunists and copycats. The second is resilience. Each hoax consumes scarce security, military and economic resources, and analysts warn that a flood of false alarms risks breeding complacency, in which a genuine threat could one day be overlooked.

For now, European authorities are treating the bulk of the incidents as hoaxes while keeping their guard up — a posture defined less by any single arrest than by the continent's ability to absorb a strange, expensive and still unexplained campaign of disruption.
