# New Mexico's Free Childcare: Promise and Pitfalls

A year after New Mexico became the first U.S. state to offer free childcare to every family regardless of income, the program stands as a closely watched national experiment — one that has delivered life-changing relief to many parents and a measurable lift to the state's labor market, while exposing deep structural weaknesses in capacity, workforce and financing.

Nearly 47,000 children are now enrolled in the universal program, and the state's Early Childhood Education and Care Department (ECECD) projects that figure will climb to 58,000 in the coming year [according to NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/15/nx-s1-6040194/new-mexico-free-universal-childcare-families). With roughly 67,000 children age five and under in the state, officials say about half are covered by the universal program, with others served by New Mexico PreK and Head Start.

## A program built on oil and gas

New Mexico's path to universal care began in 2022, when voters approved a constitutional amendment dedicating a slice of the Land Grant Permanent Fund to early childhood education. Much of that fund is fed by oil and gas taxes and royalties. In September 2025, Gov. Michelle Lujan Grisham announced the state would lift income eligibility thresholds altogether, and the program launched on Nov. 1, 2025. It was written into law in March 2026 as the Child Care Assistance Program Act. To qualify, families must be New Mexico residents, and adults in the household must be working, looking for work, in school or unable to work.

"I can't go anywhere in New Mexico… where I don't have a parent tell me that this is life-changing and transformative," Lujan Grisham told NPR. "We need to keep growing."

Parents echo that sentiment. "I'm really grateful for universal childcare because I wouldn't have been able to send her," said Judyn Martinez, a Santa Fe parent. Veronica Ramirez, who lives in Ohkay Owingeh Pueblo, said consistent care allowed her to expand her nonprofit work and pursue a bachelor's degree. "Now I'm able to work more and make more money and support my family a little bit better," she said.

The labor-market effects appear real. A new Arizona State University paper found that lower-income mothers in New Mexico saw full-time employment rise by 23% after the program was enacted [as NPR reported](https://docs.iza.org/dp18968.pdf). ECECD's own 2026 family survey found that 93% of participating families report a positive impact on their well-being, with direct cost relief cited most often.

## Demand outpaces capacity

The central weakness is physical capacity. The state lacks enough providers and classrooms to meet demand. Kids Campus in Santa Fe, a sought-after center with room for about 130 children, has a waitlist of nearly 700. One parent, Lilia Doyle, was on six waitlists for her two-year-old son; in October she was told a spot would not open until August 2027.

"I have great belief in this program, but as it currently stands, I think the rollout needs a lot, a lot of help," Doyle told NPR.

Providers say the problem is structural. "A provider can't simply add another classroom because demand increases," Daisy Lira, founder of The Hive Education in Sunland Park, told lawmakers at a September hearing [covered by Source New Mexico](https://sourcenm.com/2026/09/10/new-mexico-childcare-providers-struggle-as-demand-waitlists-increase/). "They have to hire qualified staff. They need equipment and supplies. They need insurance."

Rising business insurance costs have become a particular flashpoint. "We have public funding available, families waiting for care and providers willing to create more slots. But if that provider cannot obtain the coverage needed to operate, those seats or slots never get created," said Ray Jaramillo, director of Alpha School for Young Children in Las Cruces.

Rural geography compounds the challenge. Nearly half of U.S. children ages five and under live in a licensed childcare desert, and New Mexico is highly rural. "It's not a lack of funding, it's a lack of providers," state House Speaker Javier Martinez, a Democrat, told NPR. "We have communities that are miles from any type of population center."

ECECD set a goal of 12,000 new childcare slots in the program's early years and says it is more than halfway there.

## The solvency question

The program's funding model has become its most consequential question. The Early Childhood Education and Care Fund was created in 2020 with about $300 million and has grown to roughly $11 billion, largely from surplus oil and gas revenues. The universal childcare law allows up to $700 million to be drawn from it over five years.

But that draw, combined with a $1.9 billion diversion in 2025 to Medicaid and Behavioral Health trust funds, has pushed the fund's payouts above its returns. Ismael Torres, the Legislature's chief economist, told lawmakers in October that without adjustments the fund will shrink at an "exponential rate" starting in 2031 and be empty by 2048 [Source New Mexico reported](https://sourcenm.com/2026/10/22/course-correction-needed-for-new-mexico-universal-childcare-fund-legislative-economist-says/). He recommended finding other revenue sources, such as the general fund, and slowing withdrawals.

State Rep. Doreen Gallegos, a Las Cruces Democrat who co-sponsored the fund's creation, said lawmakers "are busy patting ourselves on the back" about the first-in-nation program, but "for us to be sitting here…looking for solutions for solvency is not good."

The program's current operating budget is almost $600 million, and some lawmakers say it is projected to run about $50 million over budget this year. Separately, ECECD's chief financial officer filed a whistleblower lawsuit in July alleging an $83 million budget shortfall and retaliation; the department denies the allegations. Those fiscal disputes remain unresolved.

## Who should get free care?

The program's design — free care for all, including wealthy families — has drawn criticism. "By calling it universal, all we've done is add the wealthier folks," said state Sen. Steve Lanier, a Republican who sued unsuccessfully to stop the program. "But somebody that's making $450,000, $600,000, do they really need that assistance?"

Supporters frame childcare as a public good. "Nobody questions a wealthy family sending their kids to public school and it should be the same for early childhood," said Catron Allred, who oversees Kids Campus and the early childhood program at Santa Fe Community College.

That tension has now become concrete. The annual Consumer Price Index rose above 3%, triggering co-pays for families earning at least six times the federal poverty level — about $163,920 for a family of three and $198,000 for a family of four. Proposed co-pays range from $15 to $99 a month for one child and $22.50 to $148.50 for two or more [per Source New Mexico](https://sourcenm.com/2026/11/10/new-mexico-could-soon-require-co-pays-for-high-earning-families-using-universal-childcare-program/). ECECD says the change will affect about 10% of enrolled families.

Lujan Grisham opposes the co-pays, and ECECD will hold a hybrid public hearing on the proposal on Dec. 3 in Santa Fe and online.

## A national test case

New Mexico is not alone in moving toward expanded care — Oregon, Connecticut, Washington, D.C., New York City and Vermont have advanced their own approaches, with Vermont tapping payroll taxes. But New Mexico is the only state offering universal care now, and its rollout is instructive for others.

New York City is expanding free care more cautiously, scaling capacity "in parallel" with access rather than all at once. "Both the biggest challenge and opportunity in New York City is our scale," Emmy Liss, executive director of the Mayor's Office of Child Care and Early Childhood Education, told NPR, adding that her office has learned from New Mexico but that "our realities are different."

Cynthia Osborne of Vanderbilt University's Prenatal-to-3 Policy Impact Center said New Mexico is "going to provide lessons that the rest of the states are going to have to pay attention to." "If New Mexico can show us how to build the infrastructure, I think other states will say that's an investment that we should make," she said.

## What's next

The program's future will be shaped by two converging forces: the Dec. 3 co-pay hearing and the January 2027 inauguration of Gov.-elect Deb Haaland, a Democrat who supports the program and has said she hopes to spare families from co-pays "to the extent that we can."

On the fiscal side, the next legislative session will likely confront the trust fund's solvency, weighing a shift toward general-fund revenue and slower withdrawals. On the capacity side, the open question is whether provider growth can catch up to the 58,000 projected enrollees.

For families still stuck on waitlists, those decisions cannot come soon enough. "We'll buckle down, and we'll make it through," Doyle said, "but… it's not ideal."
