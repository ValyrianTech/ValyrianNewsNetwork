# ULA's Vulcan Fails, Destroying 40 Amazon Satellites

A United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket malfunctioned during its climb to orbit on Sunday, destroying all 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites it carried and dealing a heavy blow to both companies. It was the first outright mission failure for ULA since the Boeing-Lockheed Martin joint venture was formed in 2006 — a two-decade reliability record the company had long made its calling card.

"Based on data received by our Launch Director, we confirm that the Vulcan vehicle launched from Space Launch Complex 41 [at the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station] suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit," ULA said in a statement roughly 50 minutes after liftoff, as [CBS News reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/vulcan-rocket-40-amazon-leo-internet-satellites-launch-mishap/). "We will work closely with our customer and other appropriate organizations to find the cause, determine the appropriate corrective action and return to flight as quickly as possible."

The cause of the failure is not yet known. ULA said it had no specific data yet to explain what occurred and pledged "full transparency with our customers," who it said would have visibility into the investigation.

## A rocket dependability was built on

Vulcan Centaur is ULA's next-generation heavy-lift vehicle, designed to replace the workhorse Atlas V. The 214-foot-tall rocket is powered by two methane-fueled BE-4 main engines built by Blue Origin and up to six Northrop Grumman strap-on solid rocket boosters, producing some 3.8 million pounds of thrust at liftoff. Its hydrogen-fueled Centaur upper stage does the final work of reaching orbit.

Sunday's flight was only the fifth for Vulcan and its first in nine months, following a hiatus while ULA and Northrop Grumman worked to resolve problems with nozzles on the strap-on solid boosters that marred two earlier flights — one in 2024 and another in February 2026. Those missions still delivered their payloads. The boosters appeared to function normally on Sunday.

For ULA, the stakes extend well beyond a single commercial contract. The US Space Force had put Vulcan launches of national security payloads on hold pending further analysis of the earlier booster issues, and Sunday's Amazon mission was widely viewed as a key step toward recertification. That approval now looks considerably further away.

## Ten minutes to failure

The launch itself was eventful even before ignition. A Friday attempt was scrubbed after problems knocked out a gaseous nitrogen plant serving multiple launch providers, and a Saturday attempt was delayed by network problems between the Vulcan and ground systems. On Sunday, controllers called a hold at the T-minus 20-second mark over an unexplained technical issue; the problem was resolved, and the rocket lifted off at 12:40 p.m. ET at the end of its launch window. It is not yet known whether the countdown issue played any role in the subsequent failure.

The strap-on boosters burned out and fell away about a minute and 40 seconds after liftoff, as planned. But according to [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2026/11/15/ula-suffers-first-total-mission-failure-with-loss-of-vulcan-rocket-carrying-40-amazon-leo-satellites/), the first stage appeared to burn longer than the company's pre-launch timeline called for, stage separation occurred later than expected, and telemetry-driven animation showed the Centaur upper stage making unusual orientation adjustments early in its burn. The Centaur had been scheduled for a 12-minute, 15-second firing. Something went wrong during that phase.

That divergence has left analysts weighing two competing explanations. One points to a failure in the Centaur upper stage itself. The other suggests the first stage — and potentially its BE-4 engines — underperformed, forcing the Centaur to operate outside its intended envelope. ULA has not confirmed either.

The BE-4 engines are already under scrutiny for a separate reason. In May 2026, Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket exploded on its launchpad, and the investigation traced the cause to an oxygen valve in one of the vehicle's BE-4 first-stage engines. Blue Origin subsequently developed a retrofit kit, and the BE-4s on Sunday's Vulcan had been modified before flight, ULA spokesperson Jessica Rye told Reuters in August. Whether those engines contributed to Sunday's loss remains an open question.

## A constellation falling behind

The lost satellites were the first of 38 Vulcan flights Amazon has booked for its Leo constellation, its answer to SpaceX's Starlink. Amazon has also contracted 24 launches on Blue Origin's New Glenn, 24 on Europe's Ariane 6, and 13 on SpaceX's Falcon 9, alongside eight already-completed Atlas V missions. More than 3,300 satellites are needed for full coverage; Sunday's flight would have brought the total launched to 436.

The schedule pressure is acute. As [Engadget noted](https://www.engadget.com/2317283/ula-vulcan-rocket-failure-destroys-40-amazon-leo-satellites/), the Federal Communications Commission required Amazon Leo to have 1,600 satellites in orbit by July 31, 2026 — a milestone it did not meet, with only around 390 in service at the time of the failure. Amazon still aimed to have more than 3,200 satellites deployed by mid-2029. According to [Reuters](https://www.jpost.com/international/article-915000), just 398 Leo satellites have been deployed across 14 launches since 2025 — roughly 12 percent of the plan.

Amazon struck a measured tone. "There is risk with any launch, and that's especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01," the company said in a post on X. "ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they'll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan." Amazon has previously acknowledged that no operator could have anticipated that all three of its core heavy-lift launch programs would suffer repeated, concurrent scheduling slips severe enough to exhaust its built-in buffers.

## Fallout across the launch industry

The failure ripples well beyond Amazon. Blue Origin's New Glenn remains grounded following its May explosion, so the two vehicles meant to carry the bulk of Amazon's constellation are now both out of service simultaneously. Arianespace's Ariane 6 and SpaceX's Falcon 9 are the only booked launchers currently flying, and SpaceX — Amazon's chief competitor — is among them.

For ULA, the commercial and reputational damage is significant. The company had reportedly taken out a substantial loan to cover losses from the earlier Vulcan grounding, and a second extended stand-down threatens revenue further. Vulcan now faces a likely multi-month investigation before it flies again.

There may be implications for NASA as well. The agency plans to use a Centaur upper stage on its Space Launch System rocket for the Artemis 4 and Artemis 5 lunar landing missions later this decade. If the anomaly is ultimately traced to the Centaur, that plan could come under review; if it stems from the first stage or BE-4 engines, Blue Origin's return to flight could be affected instead. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman publicly offered the agency's help: "The team at @NASA is available to assist in all matters related to the investigation and remediation effort," he wrote on X. "Artemis, Moon Base, and the nation need ULA. We are in your corner and here to help."

## What to watch

Three questions will shape the months ahead. First, what failed — the Centaur upper stage or the first stage — and how long the fix takes. Second, whether Vulcan's grounding further erodes Pentagon confidence in the vehicle at a time when national security launches have been shifting toward SpaceX. Third, whether Amazon can recover enough schedule to satisfy regulators and close a competitive gap with Starlink that is already measured in thousands of satellites.

What is already clear is that a company whose identity rested on never losing a payload has now lost 40 of them at once — and the rocket meant to secure its future is grounded while investigators work out why.
