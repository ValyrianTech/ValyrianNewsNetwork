# Hoax Bomb Threats Hit 6,300 Italian Schools

Around 6,300 schools across Italy received identical hoax bomb-threat emails on Wednesday, prompting the evacuation of several hundred buildings, while hundreds more schools in Scotland and Northern Ireland—and a "generic communication" to schools in the Republic of Ireland—received similar warnings. Authorities in every affected country quickly assessed the messages as bogus, but the sheer scale and apparent coordination alarmed governments and triggered cyber-crime investigations.

Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni said checks carried out so far had found "no element that would suggest there is a concrete danger," describing the threats as a strategy intended to ["generate alarm and concern"](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2026/nov/11/bomb-threats-hoax-schools-scotland-northern-ireland).

## A Wave Across Europe

According to [VRT NWS](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/11/bommeldingen-scholen-europa/), the original Dutch-language source, the hoaxes were monitored at government level and prompted an emergency meeting, with Meloni consulting the intelligence services. The [BBC reported](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwz0k79pr8z2o) that a single email appeared to have landed in the inboxes of 6,300 of Italy's 7,476 state schools, with private schools unaffected.

![Bomb alert at schools across Italy from an anonymous email (ANSA)](https://www.ansa.it/webimages/news_base/2026/11/11/77e55bf5158f388ab7c4b80d1be2b66e.jpg)

In the United Kingdom, Police Scotland said about 1,000 schools—from Dumfries in the south to Aberdeenshire and Orkney in the north—had received the warning. At least 300 schools were affected in Northern Ireland, and the Republic of Ireland's Garda Síochána confirmed a similar "generic communication" reached schools there, though it assessed the threat as "not credible" and called no evacuations.

## No Credible Threat

Police forces moved quickly to reassure parents. A Police Scotland spokesperson said inquiries had established "nothing to suggest that they represent a credible threat," adding that there was "no requirement to evacuate schools or keep children away from education based solely on the content of the emails." Northern Ireland's PSNI described the risk to the public as "low."

Italy's Education Minister Giuseppe Valditara characterised the incidents as probably a "cyber-attack, not a genuine risk," linking them to a broader wave of threats that has swept Europe in recent weeks, targeting flights, supermarkets and even hospitals.

## The "Mikhail Andropov" Signature

According to Italian press reports, the email contained the phrase "we have come to annihilate you" and was signed by "Mikhail Andropov, leader of the Vilayat of Misoginistan"—a Russian-sounding name used earlier in the month in threats to European newsrooms, including a bomb warning that forced the evacuation of Ireland's public broadcaster.

The BBC noted that the version sent to Scottish schools used similar wording but named the same figure as leader of a "Red Owl" project. The fabricated group name blends "vilayat," an Islamist administrative term, with "misogyny"—a construction analysts say appears designed to sow confusion rather than signal a genuine organised threat.

## Confusion, and Some Comic Relief

Not every recipient was rattled. Massimo Nunzio Barrella, a Milan headteacher, told the Adnkronos news agency that he realised the email was spam the moment it arrived at 08:59 and decided not to evacuate his school. "The text seemed almost comical," he said. "It was like a message from Martians: 'We have come to annihilate you.' I even had a bit of a laugh."

Antonello Giannelli, president of Italy's national association of headteachers, said the threat was "clearly fake" and pointed to a deeper problem: "This episode shows how much irresponsibility, when combined with the extraordinary communicative power of the tools we have today, can cause damage and disruption on a large scale."

## Attribution Remains Unclear

No country has directly attributed the campaign to a specific group or state. Some European governments suspect Russia or Iran, but Italy's Defence Minister Guido Crosetto cautioned against jumping to conclusions. "It is not always the work of other countries or hostile entities," he said. "There are also attention-seekers and madmen who, in times like today, cannot wait to sow terror in a city or region."

The episode fits a pattern that has unsettled Europe through early November, with dozens of false flight bomb alerts and threats to newsrooms forcing evacuations and disruption. An extremist misogynistic group that had been linked to some earlier threats later denied any involvement.

## What to Watch

Police Scotland's cyber and fraud unit is liaising with security and law-enforcement bodies in the UK and overseas to identify who is behind the messages. The key question now is whether the campaign is the work of a coordinated network, a state actor seeking to test Europe's response, or isolated hoaxers exploiting the low cost and high reach of mass email. For now, the practical lesson for schools may be the one their own headteachers are drawing: cheap threats, sent widely, can still empty buildings and dominate a news cycle.
