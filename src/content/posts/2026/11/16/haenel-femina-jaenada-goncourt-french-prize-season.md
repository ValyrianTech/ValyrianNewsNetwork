# Haenel Takes Femina as French Prize Season Draws Fire

Yannick Haenel won the Prix Femina on Wednesday for *La solitude des professeurs est infinie* (*The Solitude of Teachers Is Infinite*), a novel about teaching in the Paris suburbs, one day after Philippe Jaenada claimed France's most prestigious literary honour, the Prix Goncourt. The back-to-back awards capped an autumn prize season that has been dominated less by the books themselves than by a fierce argument over who gets to win them.

Haenel, 59, received the Femina at the Dior headquarters in Paris, according to [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-le-femina-a-ete-decerne-a-l-ecrivain-francais-yannick-haenel-pour-la-solitude-des-professeurs-est-infinie-11810576). The twelve-member, all-female jury preferred him to Emma Marsantes (*N'efface pas mes cercles*) and Lucie Rico (*Mais que fait ce sang sur le pull de Jennifer ?*), who also drew votes.

## A Novel of Classrooms and Solitude

Published by Gallimard in August, *La solitude des professeurs est infinie* follows Jean Deichel, the author's fictional double, as a young trainee French teacher arriving at a middle school in Mantes-la-Jolie, a working-class suburb west of Paris. Haenel, who taught in the 1990s before turning to writing, dedicated the book "to the teachers, to the *lycéens* and to the *collégiens*" at a moment when students were protesting in the streets.

He said he felt the need to write about the isolation of teachers after the murders of Samuel Paty and Dominique Bernard, two French educators killed in separate attacks that shook the country. The novel dwells on the small victories of the classroom—the moment "when a face lights up fleetingly, when a pupil shows understanding"—but also on the raw reality of the job, describing time spent "calming things down, defusing, neutralising: whatever the pedagogues say, it is violence that comes first in middle schools."

It is a second major accolade for Haenel, who won the Prix Interallié in 2009 for *Jan Karski* and the Prix Médicis in 2017 for *Tiens ferme ta couronne*. He is also a columnist, including for the satirical weekly *Charlie Hebdo*. The Femina's foreign-novel prize went to the American writer Laura Kasischke for *Baignade surveillée*, and its essay prize to Jennifer Tamas for *Barbe Bleue contre-enquête*.

## Jaenada's Criminal Investigation

The Goncourt, announced the previous day from the steps of the Drouant restaurant in Paris as tradition dictates, went to Jaenada for *L'Inconnue du quai de Javel*. The 62-year-old author's novel investigates the murder of a young Breton woman in the late 1940s. The nine members of the Académie Goncourt gave him six votes to three for Haenel, with Clémentine Mélois and Sylvain Prudhomme also among the finalists, [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/goncourt-2026-le-prestigieux-prix-a-ete-attribue-a-philippe-jaenada-pour-son-livre-l-inconnue-du-quai-de-javel-11809864).

"He makes visible a woman who had been made invisible, a victim, a young victim whom the police did not much care about," said Philippe Claudel, president of the Académie Goncourt, in announcing the winner.

Jaenada has built a devoted readership on sprawling books that probe historical criminal cases—*Sulak* (2013), *La Petite Femelle* (2015) and *La Serpe* (2017)—combining deep archival research with an immersive, novelistic style. He had already won the Femina in 2017 for *La Serpe*. "When you win a literary prize, it's wonderful, it's joy," he said. "Because it is one of the important ways of making your book known, of selling books and of having readers." He succeeds Laurent Mauvignier, whose *La Maison vide* won the 2025 Goncourt and became a major bookselling success.

The Renaudot, handed out moments after the Goncourt in keeping with tradition, went to François-Henri Désérable for *Le palais*, a novel set in the world of wine, published by Gallimard. The book follows Arun Kumar, a character born in a slum by the Ghazipur landfill in New Delhi and raised in Burgundy wine country, who can detect and memorise hundreds of aromas in a glass. "I didn't want to give the reader the impression of a 400-page oenology course," Désérable [told RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/le-prix-renaudot-2026-attribue-a-francois-henri-deserable-pour-le-palais-11809873). The Renaudot marked its centenary edition in 2026.

## A Season Overshadowed by Controversy

For all the literary distinction on display, the 2026 season will be remembered for two controversies that cut to the heart of how France anoints its writers.

The first is the clean sweep of all five major autumn prizes—the Goncourt, Renaudot, Femina, Médicis and the Académie française's Grand Prix du roman—by male authors. That outcome prompted more than 200 women writers, including Nobel laureate Annie Ernaux and Virginie Despentes, to publish a tribune denouncing what they called the "sexism" of the prize system, as [RTBF reported](https://www.rtbf.be/article/prix-litteraires-en-france-plus-de-200-autrices-denoncent-dans-une-tribune-le-sexisme-du-systeme-11811966).

"We express our anger at this system of literary rewards whose workings—peculiar to France—seem to us utterly incapable of reflecting the richness and diversity of the literature being written today," the signatories wrote. "The juries accomplished the heroic feat of rewarding only men," they added, noting that the season had been billed as an open one, with many acclaimed books by women. They called for "rotating, gender-balanced juries representing a genuine editorial diversity," arguing that the closed, co-opted, largely lifetime nature of the grand-prize juries keeps a "old patriarchal insider system" running at full tilt.

Claudel defended the Goncourt, which has awarded only 13 women in its 124-year history. "I read texts, and I don't read sexes," he said, noting that he has worked to increase parity on a jury that has gone from two to four women out of ten members. "The prize has been moralised," he added, pointing to rules barring an Académie member from simultaneously being paid by a publisher.

The second controversy concerns Thélyson Orélien, whose heavily favoured novel *C'était ça ou mourir* was removed from the Goncourt selection and eliminated from other prizes over plagiarism accusations and suspicions of extensive use of artificial intelligence. The affair has raised uncomfortable questions about eligibility and AI detection at a time when the boundaries of authorship are being redrawn.

## What to Watch

For Haenel and Jaenada, the near-simultaneous wins sharpen attention on two very different books—one a meditation on the classroom, the other a cold-case investigation—and on the curious fact that Haenel was also the Goncourt runner-up. The season is not quite over: the Prix Interallié is due on 18 November, followed by the Femina des lycéens and the Goncourt des lycéens later in the month.

But the deeper question is whether the tribune's call for reform will translate into concrete change at the Goncourt, Femina, Renaudot and beyond. France's literary prizes remain among the most powerful kingmakers in world publishing, capable of turning a quiet novel into a national event. Whether they can broaden who gets to be crowned—and how—may prove the more enduring story of the 2026 season.
