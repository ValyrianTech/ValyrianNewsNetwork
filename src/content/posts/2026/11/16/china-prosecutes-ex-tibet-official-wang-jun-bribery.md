# China Prosecutes Ex-Tibet Official Wang Jun for Bribery

Chinese prosecutors have filed a public indictment against Wang Jun, a former deputy head of the Tibet Autonomous Region's legislative body, on charges of accepting bribes, escalating one of the country's most closely watched anti-corruption cases to the courtroom stage. [Xinhua News](https://www.news.cn/legal/20261116/a8b578d0107a4105aaecb3ec1cd0de94/c.html), citing the Supreme People's Procuratorate, reported on November 16 that the case had been transferred to the Zhangjiakou Intermediate People's Court in Hebei Province for trial.

Wang, who held sub-ministerial rank as deputy secretary of the Party leadership group and deputy director of the Standing Committee of the Tibet Autonomous Region People's Congress, is accused of using her positions to seek benefits for others in exchange for property. The amount involved was described in the indictment as "particularly huge" (数额特别巨大), a designation that under Chinese law signals a serious sentencing range if she is convicted.

## From Disciplinary Probe to Courtroom

The prosecution is the culmination of a process that began nearly a year ago. Wang was placed under disciplinary review and supervisory investigation on December 24, 2025, while still in office—a status Chinese media often describe as being "investigated on the job" (任上被查). According to reporting compiled on her biography, she had led a delegation of National People's Congress deputies on a pre-session inspection in Nyingchi just days earlier, underscoring how abruptly senior officials can be removed from public life.

The investigation was concluded by the National Supervisory Commission, China's top graft-fighting body, which transferred the case to procuratorial organs for review. The Supreme People's Procuratorate then issued a formal arrest decision and designated the Zhangjiakou Municipal People's Procuratorate to handle the prosecution—moving a Tibet-linked case to Hebei, a standard practice known as designated jurisdiction intended to reduce local interference in sensitive prosecutions.

Prosecutors allege that Wang exploited the conveniences of her offices—including deputy commissioner of the Ngari (Ali) Prefecture Administrative Office and director of the Tibet Autonomous Region Department of Land and Resources—along with her broader authority and status, to secure benefits for others and illegally accept property. During the review stage, Xinhua reported, procurators informed Wang of her litigation rights, interrogated her, and heard the opinions of her defense counsel.

## A Career Built in Tibet's Key Posts

Wang, born in February 1964 in Pingliang, Gansu Province, spent decades as a Tibet cadre. She served in the Ngari prefecture administration before being appointed in 2008 as deputy party secretary and director of the region's Department of Land and Resources—a post with jurisdiction over land allocation and mining rights, areas historically vulnerable to graft. In 2016 she was elected deputy secretary of the Tibet Autonomous Region Discipline Inspection Commission, and in 2018 she was elevated to vice chairman of the regional People's Congress Standing Committee.

Her prosecution follows a broader pattern of scrutiny directed at Tibet-linked officials in recent years. She previously worked alongside Ji Guogang, a former colleague on the same Standing Committee who was investigated in November 2022—described in reports as the "first tiger" of Tibet after the 20th Party Congress—and sentenced in December 2024 to 13 years in prison for accepting 43.98 million yuan in bribes. Reports indicate that six centrally managed officials from Tibet have been investigated since the 20th Party Congress in October 2022.

## The Wider Anti-Corruption Calculus

Wang's case illustrates the multi-stage machinery Beijing uses to pursue senior officials accused of graft: disciplinary review by the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection, supervisory investigation by the National Supervisory Commission, procuratorial review, and finally criminal trial. The process is designed to project both rigor and procedural legitimacy, and each stage is typically announced publicly through state media.

The timing also reflects an intensifying campaign. Reporting indicates that 2025 saw a record number of centrally managed officials placed under investigation—more than 60—with Wang reported by one outlet as the 63rd such official investigated that year. For the leadership, high-profile prosecutions serve both as deterrent and as a signal that the anti-corruption drive, launched more than a decade ago, continues to reach the upper tiers of the bureaucracy.

## What's Next

The Zhangjiakou Intermediate People's Court has not yet announced a hearing date. The precise total of the alleged bribes remains undisclosed, with the indictment offering only the "particularly huge" characterization. Comparative cases suggest the severity band: Ji Guogang's 13-year sentence for a 43.98 million yuan bribe offers one benchmark. It also remains to be seen whether Wang will contest the charges and whether other officials connected to her tenure will face action.

For now, the case stands as another marker of how China's anti-corruption campaign moves from investigation to courtroom—and of the political and legal machinery that carries it there.
