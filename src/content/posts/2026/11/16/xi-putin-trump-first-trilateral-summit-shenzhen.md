# Xi, Putin and Trump to Hold First-Ever Trilateral Summit

China's Foreign Ministry announced Monday that President Xi Jinping will hold a joint heads-of-state meeting with Russian President Vladimir Putin and US President Donald Trump in Shenzhen on November 19, the first time the leaders of the three major powers have gathered face to face. The three-way encounter, set on the margins of the APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting, is being framed by Beijing as an effort to coordinate on major international issues — though the agenda remains sharply contested.

The terse announcement, carried by [Xinhua](https://www.news.cn/politics/leaders/20261116/6e79955146d14fad9397d0eac92bce27/c.html) and the [Chinese Foreign Ministry](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/web/wjdt_674879/wsrc_674883/202611/t20261116_12059654.shtml), stated simply that "on November 19, President Xi Jinping will hold a China-Russia-US heads-of-state meeting with Russian President Putin and US President Trump in Shenzhen." It offered no agenda, no listed deliverables and no hint of what the three men intend to produce.

## A meeting months in the making

The convergence did not happen overnight. According to reporting summarized by [BBC Chinese](https://www.bbc.com/zhongwen/articles/cwe85djzkkv5o/simp), Putin first floated the idea of a US-China-Russia leaders' meeting in a phone call with Trump in early October, reportedly to discuss Ukraine. Through mid-October the Kremlin pushed for a trilateral on the sidelines of the Shenzhen summit, drawing immediate expressions of concern from the European Union and Ukraine.

On October 19, Trump publicly said he was willing to meet Xi and Putin, pointing to a focus on "denuclearization." Beijing stayed silent for nearly a month. Then, on November 13, a White House spokesperson confirmed to reporters that the trilateral would take place during the APEC summit — setting the stage for Monday's formal confirmation from China's Foreign Ministry.

The venue is itself a statement. Shenzhen, China's technology capital and home to Tencent and Huawei, is hosting the 33rd APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting on November 18–19, organized around a theme of technology and innovation. Host cities are chosen to project a national image; for Beijing, Shenzhen offers a face that is modern, forward-looking and unmistakably Chinese.

## Three leaders, three agendas

If the announcement was short, the gap between the participants' priorities is anything but. BBC reporting indicates the three sides remain far apart on what the meeting is for.

The White House has signaled that Trump wants to focus on nuclear weapons and disarmament, and on ending the war in Iran. Trump has publicly downplayed Ukraine, saying the leaders "can meet to discuss nuclear weapons and nuclear disarmament." His political standing entering the summit is weakened by Republican losses in the November midterms, a factor analysts say could shape both his leverage and his appetite for a dramatic win.

Moscow, by contrast, wants to use the format to advance a Ukraine ceasefire on terms favorable to Russia. Putin aide Yuri Ushakov said "the Ukraine issue will inevitably be raised in some form," while cautioning that only "preliminary ideas" exist for such an "unusual format."

Beijing's calculus is the most delicate. According to analysts cited by the BBC, China is cool toward discussing Ukraine without European participation, wary of appearing to back Russia in the war, and skeptical of nuclear-arms talks — preferring to keep its own smaller nuclear program opaque. Beijing is seen as eager to project a mediator's image but reluctant to make concrete concessions or bear real costs.

## Echoes of Yalta

The comparison that has dominated commentary is to Yalta. In February 1945, Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin met on the Black Sea coast to shape the post-war order — a gathering that in Eastern Europe became shorthand for great powers carving up smaller nations' fates over their heads.

Putin has done little to discourage the parallel. On November 4, he was photographed pausing before a Yalta-themed sculpture in Moscow and telling schoolchildren that the three leaders "met to discuss how the post-war world should be arranged" — a remark widely read as a signal.

But scholars caution against the analogy. Joseph Torigian of American University noted, per the BBC, that "for the Chinese, Yalta is not a fond memory" — Stalin's gains in Manchuria were a deep grievance for Mao Zedong and a factor in the later Sino-Soviet split. Marcin Kaczmarski of the University of Glasgow added that Russia is the only one of the three still seeking an explicit division of the world into spheres of influence; privately, he said, Chinese elites do not regard Russia as a power equal to China, reserving true peer status for the United States.

Alicja Bachulska of the European Council on Foreign Relations warned that even a photo-op with no substance could backfire on Beijing — by providing the venue, China may reinforce perceptions that it is helping the US and Russia bypass Europe on Europe's fate.

## Europe and Ukraine watch warily

That concern is loud in European capitals. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has insisted that Europe must be involved in any discussions on Ukraine, while Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky has warned against a "second, third or fourth Yalta" that would divide the world into spheres of influence.

Eduardo Pedrosa, executive director of the APEC Secretariat, stressed to the BBC that APEC is not a venue for signing agreements or resolving hard geopolitical problems, but a platform for exchanging views under a non-binding framework. The three powers, he noted, "could meet anywhere" — a reminder that the summit's significance lies less in any agreement than in the act of meeting itself.

## What to watch

The meeting itself is scheduled for the second and final day of the APEC leaders' gathering. Whether it produces a joint statement, a ceasefire framework, or merely a handshake will define how history remembers it.

Several questions hang over Shenzhen: whether the format sidelines Europe, a point of acute concern in Kyiv, Berlin and Brussels; whether Trump's disarmament framing gains any traction with a skeptical Beijing and a Moscow focused on Ukraine; and whether hosting the summit boosts Xi's global-statesman image or entraps China in a spheres-of-influence narrative that damages its ties with Europe.

A side storyline is also in play. Chinese and Japanese ties are frozen over Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's "Taiwan contingency" remarks, and analysts widely expect no formal Xi-Takaichi bilateral — though Tokyo is reportedly seeking at least a customary handshake.

For now, the announcement alone has made its mark. Three leaders who have spent years managing rivalries in bilateral settings will, for the first time, share a table. What they say there — and whom the arrangement leaves out — may matter far more than the words of a one-line statement issued on a Shenzhen morning.
