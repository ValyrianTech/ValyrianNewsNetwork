# US data centers out-pollute Denmark as COP31 touts clean AI

The 400 largest data centers in the United States emitted more heat-trapping gas in a single year than the entire nation of Denmark — and that was before the artificial intelligence boom truly took hold, according to a Harvard University study published Monday.

The findings landed the same day that negotiators at the United Nations [COP31](https://apnews.com/article/cop31-climate-data-centers-ai-pollution-a1b338e7f781976f89260d172a7c65e2) climate talks in Antalya, Türkiye, launched a voluntary pledge urging companies and countries to power the world’s data centers with clean energy and to be more transparent about their power, water and emissions footprints. Together, the two developments crystallized the central tension of the AI era: a technology hailed as a potential climate savior is also becoming a formidable source of planet-warming pollution.

## A footprint two to three times larger than earlier estimates

Harvard researchers examined 403 of the largest U.S. hyperscale data centers — the massive facilities that train and run AI models — between May 2024 and April 2025. Using available data and modeling, they estimated that the centers’ electricity use produced roughly 28.3 million U.S. tons (25.7 million metric tons) of carbon dioxide, according to the study published in [Nature Sustainability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-026-01985-7).

The study’s central scenario put electricity demand at about 82 terawatt hours — enough to power nearly 7 million homes for a year, and roughly 1.8% of total U.S. electricity consumption. More than half of that power, about 54%, came from fossil fuels, the primary driver of global warming.

The emissions estimate is two to three times higher than what previous studies had shown, said study senior author Francesca Dominici, a Harvard biostatistics professor. She likened the total to the annual carbon output of an entire U.S. state such as Michigan or “a small European country,” and warned that plans for some 2,000 additional U.S. data centers make the trajectory more troubling.

“We are going backwards toward the goal of controlling CO2 emissions,” Dominici said.

The findings drew measured support from outside experts, who said the reliance on modeling was reasonable given how little companies disclose about their power consumption and pollution.

“We cannot manage environmental impacts effectively without reliable measurements and disclosure,” said Fengqi You, a Cornell University energy systems scientist who was not part of the study. “Ultimately, the environmental footprint of AI is not fixed. It depends on where we build data centers, how efficiently they operate, and how we supply their electricity.”

The study arrives against a backdrop of rapid expansion. The biggest technology companies’ electricity consumption rose 240% between 2022 and 2025, according to a report by the advocacy group Climate Action Against Disinformation, pushing several of them off their own climate targets.

## A voluntary pledge, and its limits

The “Antalya Artificial Intelligence Pledge,” launched by COP31 host Türkiye and the [International Telecommunication Union](https://www.itu.int/en/mediacentre/Pages/PR-2026-11-16-COP31-Antalya-AI-Pledge.aspx), called for data centers to use clean power and efficient energy and water systems. It is built on four pillars: sustainably powering and building AI infrastructure; designing and procuring AI responsibly across its lifecycle, including circular e-waste management; supporting standardized assessment and reporting of AI’s environmental impacts; and strengthening financial and technical support for AI-driven climate action in developing countries, least developed countries and small island developing states.

Signatories — an initial coalition of governments, companies, research institutions and civil society groups — may also add commitments on green data-center requirements, AI skills and computing capacity. Implementation will be advanced through ITU’s Green Digital Action initiative and supported by ITU standards, including what the agency describes as the world’s first international guidelines for measuring AI’s full environmental footprint. Progress is to be tracked annually at the UN climate talks through 2030.

UN climate chief Simon Stiell used the launch to deliver a pointed warning about the gap between promises and present-day pollution.

“We cannot ask people to accept higher bills, more planet heating pollution and deeper inequality today in exchange for promises of a better tomorrow,” Stiell said. “Claims about future emissions savings cannot excuse rising pollution now, making climate disasters even worse.”

COP31 President Murat Kurum framed the pledge as an effort to bring the technology sector into the climate fold. “Industry and technology must be part of the climate solution,” he said. “We need to ensure that the rapid growth of AI is powered and deployed responsibly, and used for the benefit of climate actions.”

ITU Secretary-General Doreen Bogdan-Martin said the pledge “galvanizes international action towards sustainable, accountable AI innovation that benefits everyone, especially the most climate-vulnerable nations.”

## The debate: climate villain or climate tool?

The deeper question the twin announcements raise is whether AI’s environmental harms will outweigh its potential benefits.

Former U.S. Vice President Al Gore, one of the world’s most prominent climate advocates, told the Associated Press that while data centers must run on carbon-free energy, he is less worried about AI’s emissions than others are. He cited a study by British economist Nicholas Stern predicting that by 2030 advanced AI could drive a 6% to 9% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions by making the use of materials and energy far more efficient.

Caleb Max, president of the National Artificial Intelligence Association, argued the concerns may be overstated. “AI will solve not only the energy usage issues that data centers face, but unlock numerous currently unknown advances in science and technology that will address climate change,” he said.

Skeptics are unpersuaded. “We have hope in one hand and then harms in the other,” said Philip Newell, a spokesman for Climate Action Against Disinformation. “They’re selling us hope while hiding the harms from us.”

## Waste heat: an overlooked lever

Beyond cleaner power, one mitigation pathway is gaining attention: capturing the waste heat that data-center equipment vents into the atmosphere and putting it to use.

Danish engineering firm Danfoss calls waste heat “the world’s largest untapped energy source,” noting that reusing it can displace natural gas as a heating fuel. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centers could supply enough heat for around 10% of European homes by 2030 if that heat were fully integrated into district heating systems that supply heat and hot water to residents.

Speaking during COP31, European Commissioner for Energy and Housing Dan Jørgensen said Europe must replace imported fossil fuels by capturing heat that is currently going to waste and by tapping geothermal energy. Industrial development firm BaRupOn, meanwhile, is building a manufacturing campus in Texas designed to recapture heat to generate additional on-site electricity, and is planning a North Dakota data center that could feed heat to the surrounding community, chief executive Balaji Tammabattula said.

University of Colorado Boulder professor Gregor Henze said the pairing is a natural fit, because modern data centers that rely on liquid rather than air cooling provide a fairly constant source of heat at the right temperature for thermal energy networks.

## What to watch

The Harvard study’s authors acknowledge significant data limitations: because facility-level records are protected by data-use agreements, the estimates rely on modeling, and some industry voices dispute the urgency of the concerns. That opacity is itself part of the problem the new pledge seeks to address — its transparency pillar may prove the most consequential, and the most difficult to enforce, given that compliance is voluntary.

Two numbers will define the next phase of the debate. The first is how quickly the roughly 2,000 planned U.S. data centers come online, and whether they are sited and powered in ways that bend the emissions curve downward. The second is whether AI’s efficiency gains materialize at the scale optimists predict. Until then, the pledge’s annual progress checks through 2030 will offer the clearest measure of whether the AI boom becomes a climate liability or a genuine part of the solution.
