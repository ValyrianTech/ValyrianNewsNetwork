# Berkshire Net Profit Falls 28% as Buybacks Hit Record

Berkshire Hathaway reported a 28% drop in third-quarter net profit on Saturday, even as the conglomerate accelerated share repurchases to a single-quarter record, remained a modest net seller of equities, and watched its cash reserves climb further. Net profit attributable to shareholders fell to $22.11 billion from $30.80 billion a year earlier, according to [The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_34429766), the Chinese outlet that first reported the results.

The headline decline was driven almost entirely by a steep fall in investment gains, not by deterioration in the operating businesses Warren Buffett built. That distinction sits at the heart of how investors are reading the third quarterly report under new chief executive Greg Abel.

## What the numbers show

Berkshire's investment gains fell roughly 46% to $9.41 billion from $17.31 billion a year earlier, the dominant reason net profit slid, as [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/07/berkshire-hathaway-q3-2026-earnings-buybacks.html) reported. Operating profit, the metric Buffett long treated as the truest gauge of the underlying businesses, fell a milder 5.8% to $12.71 billion, or about $8,954 per Class A share, from $13.49 billion.

Revenue, however, rose about 11% to $105.65 billion, the second consecutive quarter of double-digit growth, even as several consumer businesses, including Fruit of the Loom and Forest River, softened.

The quarter was the third since Abel, 64, succeeded Buffett as chief executive. Buffett, 96, remains chairman emeritus and Berkshire's largest individual shareholder, while his son Howard Buffett became chairman of the board in September, Reuters reported via the [New York Post](https://nypost.com/2026/11/08/business/berkshire-hathaway-speeds-up-stock-buybacks-though-profit-falls/).

## A record buyback, and a signal

Berkshire repurchased about $9.6 billion of its own stock between July and September, more than double the roughly $4.5 billion bought in the second quarter and narrowly surpassing its previous single-quarter record of about $9.3 billion set in 2020. The company added about $2 billion more in October, bringing total 2026 buybacks to roughly $14.4 billion. Berkshire bought essentially no stock in 2025.

The scale of the repurchasing is the most telling figure in the release. Berkshire has no fixed price limit on buybacks, having scrapped a 20%-over-book-value cap in 2018, and repurchases only when the CEO, after consulting the board chairman, judges the stock to be below conservatively calculated intrinsic value, without letting cash fall below $30 billion.

As [The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/11/09/warren-buffett-s-rule-for-stock-buybacks-is-very-specific-berkshire-spent-a-record-usd9-6-billion-on-them-last-quarter/) noted, Buffett spelled out that philosophy in his 2011 shareholder letter: "Charlie and I favor repurchases when two conditions are met: first, a company has ample funds to take care of the operational and liquidity needs of its business; second, its stock is selling at a material discount to the company's intrinsic business value, conservatively calculated."

Berkshire bought most heavily as prices rose through the quarter, roughly $3.4 billion in July at an average of about $496 per Class B share, about $847 million in August at about $504, and about $5.4 billion in September at around $507. Analysts read the record as a two-part message from Abel: that Berkshire's own stock looked undervalued, and that attractive external targets meeting Berkshire's standards remain scarce.

## A cautious stance beneath the buyback

Despite the aggressive repurchasing, Berkshire was a small net seller of stocks in the quarter and let its cash pile grow, a defensive posture in a richly valued, AI-charged market.

Cash and equivalents stood at $377.4 billion at the end of September, up about 3% from $365.5 billion at mid-year, per CNBC. Reuters, using a narrower definition that appears to net out certain items, reported cash of $356.9 billion, down from $380.2 billion six months earlier. Excluding rail and utility cash along with short-term Treasury payables, CNBC said, cash holdings totaled $352.3 billion, down 1.9% from the prior quarter.

Berkshire's five largest equity holdings were unchanged: Alphabet, American Express, Apple, Bank of America and Coca-Cola, together about 65% of the fair value of its equity investments, a portfolio valued at roughly $335.5 billion at Sept. 30. The Alphabet stake, built in late 2025 and enlarged through a $10 billion commitment in June 2026, has been a drag: the shares have fallen about 25% since June, weighing on investment gains.

## Where the weakness really lies

The genuine soft spot is insurance, particularly auto insurer Geico. Insurance underwriting profit tumbled about 43% to $1.35 billion, and insurance investment income fell about 14% to $2.73 billion. Geico's pretax profit dropped roughly 45% as its loss ratio rose to 76.6% from 71.5%, reflecting a deliberate willingness to charge lower premiums to win back customers lost during an earlier push to tighten underwriting.

CFRA Research analyst Cathy Seifert, who rates Berkshire "hold," called Geico's results weak relative to peers Allstate and Progressive, and warned that Berkshire's famously limited disclosures become a liability when performance turns. "Limited disclosures are fine when everything goes in the right direction, but become a point of vulnerability" when they do not, she said, according to the New York Post.

Elsewhere the picture was brighter. BNSF railroad profit rose about 19%, helped by fuel surcharges and higher volumes. Berkshire Hathaway Energy profit climbed about 8%, and the manufacturing, services and retail group posted operating profit of $4.04 billion, up about 12%.

## Analysis: a headline that flatters the negative

The 28% net-profit drop is, in large part, an accounting artifact. Under U.S. GAAP, Berkshire must mark its sprawling equity portfolio to market each quarter, so unrealized gains and losses flow straight through net income, making the figure highly volatile. Berkshire itself cautions that investment gains or losses in any single quarter are "usually meaningless" and can mislead investors who do not grasp the accounting rules.

Strip that out, and operating profit fell less than 6%, and less than 1% once foreign-currency effects on Berkshire's debt are excluded, per the Motley Fool. The more meaningful story is capital allocation: a record buyback plus continued net selling plus rising cash amounts to caution and confidence in equal measure. Abel is willing to deploy the cash hoard on Berkshire's own shares, but he is in no rush to chase an expensive market.

Markets have rewarded that posture. Amid a sharp rotation out of AI and technology stocks, Berkshire Class A shares are up more than 12% over the past month while the S&P 500 has fallen 5.5%, CNBC reported, a striking reversal for a stock that had lagged since Buffett announced in May 2025 that he would step down. That outperformance suggests investors' long-standing anxiety about a post-Buffett Berkshire is, for now, easing.

"Investors may be rotating into less volatile, high-quality names such as Berkshire," Seifert said. "The question is how much investors will accept short-term shortfalls in exchange for owning a company with a strong balance sheet and which successfully transitioned to a post-Buffett era."

## What to watch

Berkshire flagged "considerable uncertainty" about the economy and geopolitics, including tariffs and wars, and said several retail businesses are seeing "sluggish" demand as consumer confidence sags, even as the U.S. economy grew at a 3.4% annualized rate in the third quarter.

For the next quarter, the key questions are whether Abel sustains the buyback pace now that the Class B shares trade near $570, about 12% above Berkshire's September average purchase price, and whether Geico's loss ratio stabilizes as it prices to recapture customers. How the Alphabet bet performs, and what management says about tariffs and consumer demand, will shape whether Berkshire's defensive appeal holds through the broader tech selloff.

For now, the conglomerate widely viewed as a barometer of the U.S. economy is sending a clear message: in a market it considers expensive, it would rather buy itself than almost anything else.
