# Euroclear Chief: No 'Miracle Solution' on Russian Assets

The head of clearing house Euroclear has warned that there is still no "miracle solution" for the roughly €185 billion in frozen Russian central bank assets held on its balance sheet, as the European Union weighs how to use the money to support Ukraine without destabilising its own financial system.

In an interview with the Belgian daily [Le Soir](https://www.lesoir.be/782489/article/2026-11-13/la-patronne-deuroclear-au-soir-sur-les-actifs-russes-il-ny-toujours-pas-de) published on Friday, 13 November 2026, CEO Valerie Urbain said any scheme to transfer the assets to the EU — or to a new vehicle — would not eliminate the risk of bankruptcy for Euroclear, the risk of damaging the eurozone's reputation, or the risk of destabilising Belgium and its taxpayers.

## The Scale of the Freeze

EU countries immobilised approximately €210 billion ($236 billion) of Russian central bank assets after Moscow's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, according to [Reuters](https://live.euronext.com/en/financial-news/no-miracle-solution-frozen-russian-assets-euroclear-ceo-tells-soir). Euroclear, the Brussels-based clearing house, holds about €185 billion of that total — more than 80% of its balance sheet.

The concentration is what makes the debate so sensitive. Some European nations want the assets disbursed on behalf of Ukraine, while Belgium, where Euroclear is based, has opposed the plan. Urbain told Le Soir that transferring only the assets and not the corresponding liabilities would bankrupt Euroclear.

Any transfer, she argued, would require permanent EU member-state guarantees until the war ends or Euroclear's repayment obligation is extinguished.

## 'Europe's Biggest Bargaining Chip'

Urbain's most pointed intervention reframes the assets not as a funding source but as leverage in peace negotiations. "Russian assets are Europe's biggest bargaining chip," reads the headline of the Le Soir interview. The newspaper's photo caption quotes her saying, "The best use of the Russian assets is to integrate them into peace negotiations."

That framing marks a notable shift. For more than a year, the EU debate has centred on a so-called "reparations loan" under which the immobilised assets would be channelled to Ukraine as a zero-interest credit line, with Kyiv asked to repay only if Moscow agreed to compensate for war damages. Amounts discussed have ranged from roughly €90 billion to €165–210 billion, with the reparations loan and joint EU borrowing as competing options.

So far, only the interest has moved. Euroclear's shareholders have agreed to pass the interest generated by the assets — nearly €8 billion to date — to the European Commission for use in Ukraine, Reuters reported.

## A Reputation Risk for the Eurozone

In comments carried by [Bankingnews.gr](https://www.bankingnews.gr/diethni/articles/910868/euroclear-slams-europe-over-russian-assets-amid-fears-of-investor-flight), Urbain warned that confiscating the assets could trigger deposit outflows and threaten the reputation of the European financial system.

"During my travels, I observe that many investors followed last year's discussions and are now wondering whether Europe is still capable of upholding the rule of law," she said. She noted that investors have so far trusted European markets because of the predictability of legislation, property protection and adherence to legal rules — trust that, once lost, is dangerous to forfeit while Europe needs investment in defence, digital transformation and the challenges of an ageing population.

On the financial exposure, Urbain was blunt: no level of equity capital would allow Euroclear to withstand a unilateral confiscation or forced transfer of the Russian funds, which account for roughly 80% of its balance sheet. In a severe crisis, she said, shareholders would have to step in first; because the Belgian state is among them, recourse to taxpayers would be a "last resort."

Urbain also said Euroclear has not been targeted by a cyberattack so far, despite a recent wave of cyber incidents affecting several European countries, including Belgium.

## A Political Standoff

Belgium holds a de facto veto because Euroclear sits on its territory. Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever has called the reparations loan "fundamentally wrong" and riddled with "multifold dangers," warning of multi-billion-euro losses in court. He has said he would approve such loans only if EU leaders provided, in writing, sweeping guarantees covering the Russian assets and all associated risks — coverage that could vastly exceed €185 billion.

At the EU summit of 18–19 December 2025, leaders failed to reach a decision on the reparations loan. Instead, they approved a €90 billion loan to Ukraine for 2026–2027 financed by joint EU borrowing rather than Russian assets, with repayment envisaged only once Russia compensates Ukraine. Hungary, Slovakia and Czechia refused to participate, and the reparations loan was left on hold.

The EU also moved to freeze the Russian assets indefinitely, using the Article 122 urgency procedure and a qualified majority, removing the need for six-monthly unanimous renewals that Belgium feared could lapse and expose it to Russian claims.

## Legal and Geopolitical Peril

The stakes extend beyond Brussels. Russia's Central Bank has filed a lawsuit against Euroclear in a Moscow court demanding roughly $230 billion in compensation; if successful, Moscow could seek to seize Euroclear assets in other jurisdictions. The European Central Bank, under Christine Lagarde, has repeatedly stressed compliance with international law. The United States has reportedly discouraged EU capitals from using the assets, arguing they are needed for a peace deal.

Russian intelligence has also reportedly targeted Euroclear's CEO and senior executives with intimidation tactics, underlining how the financial dispute has become entangled with the wider confrontation between Moscow and the West.

## What to Watch

The interview does not resolve the central dilemma: whether the EU can structure guarantees acceptable to Belgium and to Euroclear, and whether the assets will ultimately be used for a loan, held as leverage in peace talks, or released. Russia's litigation against Euroclear remains a live threat, and investor confidence in European financial infrastructure — built on the promise that property rights are sacrosanct — is now part of the bargaining itself.

For now, Urbain's message is one of caution: the assets may be Europe's most powerful card, but playing it carries risks that no accounting trick can make disappear.
