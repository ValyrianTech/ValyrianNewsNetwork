# Flanders to Test Guard Dogs Against Wolves After Attack

Flanders' nature agency will launch a pilot project deploying livestock guardian dogs to protect sheep flocks from wolf attacks, a shift prompted by a mass killing of sheep in the province of Antwerp. The Agentschap voor Natuur en Bos (ANB) announced the plan on 16 November, framing the dogs as a "second protective layer" around large grazing areas where wolf-proof fencing alone has proved insufficient, [VRT NWS reported](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/16/pilootproject-honden-tegen-wolven/).

"Following that incident, we are considering laying a second protection layer around large nature areas with grazing," ANB spokesperson Jeroen Denaeghel told VRT NWS. "The plan is to develop a pilot project for herd protection with dogs."

## The Attack That Triggered the Plan

The decision follows an attack earlier this autumn on the Groot Schietveld, a military and nature domain in Brecht, where wolves killed 35 sheep and injured 17 more from a flock of 450; three others went missing. The [attack](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/13/wolven-bijten-35-schapen-dood-op-militair-domein-groot-schietvel/) occurred in early October but only became public in mid-November. DNA analysis by the Instituut voor Natuur- en Bosonderzoek (INBO) confirmed wolves were responsible, though the samples were too degraded to identify individual animals.

Herder Bert Plasmans, who discovered the flock, described the aftermath bluntly. "This is absolutely not what you want to encounter, as any farmer who works with animals will tell you," he said. He added that the losses meant more than numbers: "They are animals you work with, you experience births with them, so this does affect you."

The grazing area was ringed by a temporary flexible fence, but the uneven terrain likely allowed the wolves to find a way in. INBO notes that properly installed and maintained wolf-proof fencing stops wolves in about 99 percent of cases — which is why the failure at Groot Schietveld has focused attention on a gap that fencing alone cannot close on such large, uneven domains.

## A Policy Reversal in the Making

The announcement marks a significant shift in Flanders, where deploying such dogs on public domains has until now been effectively prohibited. As [VRT NWS detailed](https://www.vrt.be/vrtnws/nl/2026/11/13/wolf-schapen-kuddebeschermingshonden-brouns-herder-brecht/), the cabinet of Environment Minister Jo Brouns (CD&V) had cited safety and liability concerns, asking who would be responsible if a child were attacked at an unguarded moment.

"The Flemish government currently forbids its shepherds to use livestock guardian dogs," said Jan Loos of Welkom Wolf, an advocacy group pushing for coexistence with the wolf. "There is not a single Flemish official who dares to take responsibility for deploying these dogs."

Loos argues the risks are manageable. "Those dogs are raised on a farm. They are preferably socialised there with children and with people. If you choose the right dogs, there is no problem," he said. He also points to a welfare dimension: the dogs stay with the flock around the clock, in all weather, which he says is possible precisely because they are working animals rather than domestic pets.

## Much Still to Be Decided

ANB is careful to stress how little is settled. "We still have much to investigate. Which dogs do you deploy, how many, where and under what conditions?" Denaeghel said. "There is also no Flemish legislation on this yet. Nor on the animal welfare of the dogs that are outside day and night."

He suggested the first trial would likely take place in a fully enclosed domain, to prevent guardian dogs from pursuing a walker's pet. "The good practices must then be translated to densely populated Flanders. That will not be simple," he said. On timing, ANB points to the start of the next grazing season in spring 2027.

## Precedent Already Exists Abroad

The strongest argument for the pilot is that workable models already exist elsewhere in Europe. In the Netherlands, [Staatsbosbeheer](https://www.staatsbosbeheer.nl/wat-we-doen/flora-en-fauna/wolf/kuddebeschermingshonden) runs guardian-dog projects at the Veluwe, De Meinweg and the Strabrechtse Heide. Dutch herder Michiel Poelenije, who works in the Dwingelderveld national park in Drenthe, told VRT NWS that the dogs transformed his operation. "After a wolf attack in broad daylight, we decided there had to be more security for the sheep, but also for the border collies that drive the sheep and for the shepherd," he said. "I no longer go out without those dogs."

Loos says his organisation has already sourced working-line livestock guardian dogs from wolf and bear territory in Spain; they are held in the Ardennes and, he says, "could be deployed tomorrow in Kalmthout and surroundings, if the regulation comes."

The Flemish Sheep Farming association has separately started its own pilot to study what is possible with guardian dogs, with a first meeting planned this month.

The broader driver is a westward shift in wolf activity. For years attacks were concentrated in Limburg; now they increasingly fall on Antwerp, which has far fewer fenced pastures. "Limburg has lived with the wolf for a number of years already," INBO's Koen Van Muylem said. "In the beginning there were also many damage cases, but there people learned that wolf-proof fencing really does work."

## What to Watch

The central question is no longer whether Flanders will allow guardian dogs, but how. The liability framework — who is responsible, whether insurance is required, and what rules govern breed selection, socialisation and signage — will determine whether the pilot stays confined to enclosed test sites or reaches the open landscapes where farmers like Plasmans say they need help. "We work according to the government's rules, but the protection now turns out to be insufficient," Plasmans said. "So we would like to work with dogs to guard the sheep." With the wolf itself strictly protected under European law, the dogs may prove the most practical answer available.
